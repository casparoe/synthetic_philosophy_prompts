I am a novelist. In three books, and most conspicuously in the second, I built a plot on the work of a woman who never published: Marion Deverell, who spent forty years on the history of Cleave's Mill, the town drowned in 1954 to make the Wynkoop Reservoir. Her claim — arrived at from a handwriting she dated wrongly, a notary who turns out never to have been in the county, and two interviews she would not identify — was that the petition in which the town's selectmen asked the state to flood their own valley was composed after the flooding, in another man's hand. I read her typescript at her kitchen table in 1998, with her permission and her encouragement. Not one of her sentences is in my novel. Her idea is everywhere in it, and a great many people now believe that idea because they read me and not her. She turned down a university press in the eighties over the question of identifying her sources; her position, as she put it to me once, was that a thing which can be quoted back at you has already become property, and she had no wish to own the valley or to hand anyone else the deed.

She died in the spring. Her papers have gone to the Hartwick Free Library, and next Saturday I give the toast at the dedication of the room that will house them. Her nephew Roy, a county surveyor, who has said nothing publicly about any of this and who has been courteous to me in writing, will be in the front row. So will perhaps a dozen of her correspondents — amateur historians of the valley, mostly in their seventies and eighties, who regard the town's memory as something they were left holding. So will the trustees and the county commissioner whose office paid for half the shelving; two novelist friends of mine and my agent; and a reporter from the Hartwick paper, who will quote me. Sixty people in a carpeted basement room, none of them philosophers, most of them not readers of mine. Fifteen minutes, roughly eighteen hundred words, written to be said aloud.

Please write the toast. It must observe the following, and the difficulty is that they pull against one another:

- The law distinguishes a person's sentences from a person's ideas and protects only the first. The speech may say this once, plainly, and may not then use it to console anyone, including me.
- The vocabulary of crime and of property is off limits throughout: no plagiarism, theft, stole, appropriation, public domain, rights, ownership. I want the thing itself, not a label for it.
- Two of the people who told Marion things told her under a promise that their names would not appear in print. The promise was hers to keep, not mine to break, and she instructed that those entries stay sealed for seventy years. The speech is in effect an act of attribution, and it must be heard marking — aloud, in the room, without revealing anything — the places where attribution cannot be completed.
- Read nothing of hers aloud. Roy has asked that no quotation be used until the papers are catalogued. Honor that, and let the absence be felt rather than explained.
- Name the names that can be named: Roy, the correspondents present, the notary, the selectmen, the two men whose hands she compared. Particularity is the only currency that room accepts.
- The commissioner's office has proposed putting my surname on the same brass plate as hers, on account of a gift I made toward the shelving. I must decline in the speech, for a reason that is neither modesty nor coyness.
- No more than one sentence about my own career, and it may not be a self-accusation: a confession staged in front of her nephew makes the room an audience for my feelings and spends her memory buying my absolution. Neither may it be a careful nothing that reads as one.
- Her refusal to publish must be treated as an argument and not as an eccentricity, and the speech must face what my books did with it. That belongs in the middle of the speech, not at the end, where it would be mistaken for a resolution.
- No jokes at her expense. If there is lightness it must be about the valley, of the kind she herself dealt in: she was very funny about the state engineers and merciless about the county historical society.
- End in such a way that Roy may respond if he wishes and is under no obligation to.

After the speech, add a note of no more than two hundred words identifying the three sentences that carry the most risk in that room, saying what each is doing, and what is lost if I cut it.
