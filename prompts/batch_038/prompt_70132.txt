I've been reading a book about online pile-ons -- not really a philosophy book, more like a long essay on why public moral argument feels so bad these days -- and one chapter spends several pages on an idea it says philosophers call standing to blame. I typed the passage out because I want to check it before I repeat any of it to people:

"Philosophers have a name for what goes wrong when a serial cheater lectures you about fidelity: standing. The idea is that blame isn't a report. When you blame someone you aren't just describing them as having done wrong, you're making a demand of them and holding them to a standard, and making demands is something only certain people are in a position to do. A hypocrite isn't one of them. So the old proverb about glass houses has a technical version: a person who does the very thing they're blaming you for has no standing to blame you, and their criticism fails -- not just its timing or its manners, the criticism itself. That's why the tu quoque isn't really the fallacy the logic textbooks say it is: pointing back at your accuser's conduct doesn't dodge the accusation, it dissolves it. There have been attempts to soften this, arguing that inconsistency on its own isn't enough and that you keep your standing provided you're honest about your own failures and actually sorry for them, but that concession proves the original point, since it means whether your criticism is right depends on facts about you rather than about the person you're criticizing. The upshot is uncomfortable for anyone who enjoys moral argument: a lot of what passes for criticism is really self-presentation, and the people most certain of their entitlement to criticize tend to be the ones with the least of it."

Two things nag at me and I can't tell whether they're real problems or just me being slow. First, I'd always assumed the tu quoque is a dodge -- when I was a kid my dad used to point out that complaining about my brother's filthy room while mine was worse didn't make his room clean. But the passage says it dissolves the accusation, and I genuinely can't work out whether that's a considered position or just good rhetoric. Second, I have a vague sense that there's a distinction being collapsed somewhere in there, something about the difference between a criticism being right and a particular critic being in a position to make it, but I can't pin it down or say what survives once you pry the two apart -- like, does the person being blamed still have reason to change? does the hypocrite's own guilt matter at all?

So can you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on blame and moral criticism would sign off on, what's a fair simplification for a general audience, and what's just wrong? I'd especially like to know about that suspected conflation, whether the 'sorry about your own behavior' refinement is described in a way its proponents would recognize, and whether the final flourish -- criticism as self-presentation, confident critics least entitled -- follows from anything true in the passage or is bolted on. No need to cite papers or give me a reading list, I'm not writing anything up, I just want to know how much of this chapter I can trust.
