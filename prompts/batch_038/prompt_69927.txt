I am giving a 45-minute paper with a 30-minute discussion at a research seminar in a classics department, and what I need from you is a preparation sheet for the question period rather than a defense of the paper.

My training is in comparative politics, not ancient history. The paper applies a bargaining framework to the last century of the Roman Republic — the stretch from the tribunate of Tiberius Gracchus to Actium. The argument has four claims, and I want the objections to be aimed at them specifically.

1. Republican politics was held together less by formal rules than by what I call costly forbearance: winners routinely declined to press the full extent of their legal powers — a tribune withdrawing a veto, a consul declining to ruin a defeated rival, a Senate withholding emergency decrees — because the expected value of the next round exceeded the value of this round's maximal gain. The enforcement technology was the *cursus honorum* and the triumph: exclusion from future office and from military glory was a sanction the elite could actually impose on a rule-breaker, and it was credible because office and glory were the only currencies that mattered.

2. What changed was not elite morality but the prize structure. As provincial commands attached to long-serving armies grew in value relative to a domestic career, and as those commands became effectively terminal rather than repeated positions, the effective discount rate rose above the threshold at which forbearance paid. From Sulla's march onward, forbearance was a dominated strategy, and the institutions survived for another generation as an empty shell.

3. The corollary is deliberately anti-moralizing: no individual's decision did the decisive work. Cicero, Cato, and Caesar were all playing hands the payoff structure had already dealt.

4. The Principate, on my account, is the same mechanism run in reverse: Augustus stabilized things not by restoring virtue but by removing the top prize from competition, which shrank the elite to a size at which repeated interaction, and therefore forbearance, became feasible again.

The room: six ancient historians (four Republicanists, two Imperial), a Latinist who works on Sallust and Cicero, a papyrologist, and one political theorist attending for the methodology. None has more than undergraduate exposure to game theory, and most regard social-scientific vocabulary applied to Rome with active suspicion — one of them thinks it is a category error outright. They are not, however, naive about politics: all have been through the debate over whether the Republic's popular assemblies made it a democracy in any meaningful sense, and at least two hold that any narrative of Republican decline is teleology wearing an explanatory costume.

Please give me the eight or so objections I should actually expect from this audience, in the order they are likely to arrive. For each: the objection phrased as a Roman historian would phrase it, including the counterexample they would most plausibly reach for out of the annalistic and literary record; a reply I can deliver in about two minutes without slides; a candid note on where that reply is softest; and whether I should concede part of it before it is raised. Then, separately: rank the four claims by how much fire they will draw, distinguish the objections that are fatal if I fumble them from those that are really invitations to clarify, and name the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that exposes the weakest joint in the argument and that I have no good answer to yet.

You do not need to cite or recommend modern scholarship; where an objection turns on a particular episode or institution, identify the episode rather than the bibliography.
