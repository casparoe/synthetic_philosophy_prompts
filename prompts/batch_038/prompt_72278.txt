I spent 34 years doing process control in refineries — tuning loops, worrying about dead time, measurement lag, plants that drift on you. Retired three years ago and fell into philosophy and the history of economics, mostly reading at night with a notebook. Lately I've been a trustee for a regional federation of worker co-ops, and we also run a small community land trust attached to it. Nine of us on the board, no economists, we meet monthly, and each agenda item gets about twenty minutes before a vote.

Every couple of months somebody brings a motion that amounts to: let's allocate this thing by an internal price instead of by queue, or seniority, or a meeting. Shared CNC machine time in the fabricaton workshop. Slots on the two delivery vans. The common purchasing budget. The four affordable units in the land trust's new build. Care hours in the co-op's home-care arm. Sometimes the answer feels obvious and sometimes it doesn't, and we go around in circles and then just vote, and its obvious to me afterwards that half of us were arguing about different questions.

I've been reading around the socialist calculation debate — Mises, Hayek, and Lange's reply with the central board that iterates shadow prices and tells its plant managers to produce out where marginal cost equals the administered price and to hold average cost down. And here's the thing that got me: to an engineer, Lange's scheme is an integral-only control loop with no feedforward, closing on a plant with long transport lag and drifting dynamics. That is precisely the configuration that hunts, oscillates, or goes unstable. Meanwhile Mises reads to me like a claim about whether the measured variable even exists in the absence of a certain ownership structure — no signal, not a bad controller. And Hayek reads like a claim about sampling rate and sensor placement: the information is real but it's local, transient, and mostly not written down anywhere, so by the time it reaches the board it's stale. But I could be reading my own trade into all three of them, and I'd rather be told plainly if I am.

So: write me a one-page decision procedure this board can actually run on any "let's price it" motion. I want the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure stops with a no, and the points where it hands the item back to deliberation because it's a values question wearing a technical costume. Keep Mises's objection and Hayek's objection separate — I think they bite on different things and a proposal can fail one and pass the other, and if I'm wrong about that say so. Tell me where the capital-goods / consumption-goods distinction actually changes the verdict, because I suspect we've been pricing consumption-ish stuff fine and kidding ourselves about the capital-ish stuff.

Then run it on three of ours: CNC machine time in the shared workshop, the delivery van slots, and the four affordable units. I've been arguing for pricing the van slots for a year now, so if the procedure says no to that one, let it say no — I'd rather have a procedure that beats me than one that flatters me.

Mark every step that is really a judgment call dressed up as a test, and say what the procedure can't do and where it will misfire. Our general manager signs off on anything that moves money, so flag which outputs need her and which the board can just act on. Don't bother with references or a reading list, I can't chase them down and it'd eat the page.

Last thing, and it matters: commit. I don't want a survey of what Mises-side and Lange-side people each say with the choice left to me. Take a position — on whether there's a real winner on the sub-questions that bear on our decisions, on which of my three cases should be priced, on whether my control-theory gloss is insight or category error — and give me your reasons, and tell me the strongest thing someone on the other side would say back and why it doesn't move you.
