I write novels about people in uniform, and the independent bookstore in my town is doing an evening on my last one — an hour, me talking for a bit and then discussion. Same thing happens every time. There's a scene where a young soldier puts two rounds into a guy who's half-raised his hands and half-reaching for something behind him, and the room splits into people who think he's a murderer and people who think he did the job he was sent to do, and then they just trade those two verdicts at each other for forty minutes and nobody moves.

So this time I want to spend the first fifteen minutes getting one idea into the room before we go anywhere near the scene. The idea: that enemy soldiers are equally permitted to fight each other, and equally not to blame for doing it, even when one side's war is flatly unjust — and then the newer argument that this is really a legal convention dressed up as morality, that what you may do depends on whether your cause is the just one. I've read around this enough to know the shape of it, but I've never taught anything to anyone and I don't want to wing it in front of forty strangers, some of whom will already have opinions about the armed forces.

Can you plan the fifteen minutes for me? Not explain the debate to me — I want the sequencing. What has to be in place before what, given that nobody in the room has ever heard there's a seperate question of whether you may start a war and a question of what you may do inside one, and that the gap between those two is doing most of the work here. Which single scenario should carry the whole thing: I was going to use my own scene, but I suspect thats a trap because they'll argue about my character's nerves instead of the principle — so give me something else and tell me what it needs to have and what it must not have. And the misconceptions in the order they'll actually arrive: I'm braced for the one that hears this as saying both sides were equally right about the war, for the one that concludes every soldier in every war is therefore a war criminal, and for whoever perks up the second following orders gets mentioned. Where in the sequence do I head each of those off rather than mopping them up at the end?

Then tell me what I should openly refuse to get into at this level — I'm guessing treaty law and anything about a war that's currently on the news, but you decide — and give me one question I can put to the room at minute fifteen that'll show whether the idea landed or whether I've just made forty people feel bad about a novel they enjoyed. No reading list, please, I'm not sending them home with homework.
