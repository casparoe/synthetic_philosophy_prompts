I am a doctoral candidate in archival history, and I am drafting the case-study chapter of my prospectus. I want to pressure-test a framework I have been leaning on against a real episode, and I would rather the pressure come from someone willing to say where the framework goes silent than from a friendly reading.

The framework is Michel-Rolph Trouillot's account of how silences enter the production of history, from *Silencing the Past*. Roughly, and in my paraphrase: silence is not a single act of omission but accretes at distinct moments — when sources are created (what gets recorded at all), when they are assembled into archives (what is preserved, arranged, and made findable), when they are retrieved (what historians actually consult and narrate), and when significance is assigned retrospectively (what the resulting account comes to count as history). Each moment inherits the exclusions of the previous one and adds its own. I am also working with two refinements of my own, which you should treat as proposals to evaluate rather than as settled distinctions. First, a split between *trace-bearing* and *trace-erasing* silences: some exclusions leave detectable residue in the surviving record (gaps in file numbering, transfer receipts, a classification series whose very existence is known), while others leave none, and this may matter for what an archive-based epistemology can claim. Second, an inversion I think the literature under-theorizes: most writing on archival silence concerns records *about* the powerless, made by the powerful, so that the subaltern's own account is missing. But there is a different case in which a state culls records of *its own* conduct — a silence about power rather than of the subaltern — and I suspect the two behave differently.

The episode is the British government's decolonization-era removal and destruction of colonial records, later known as Operation Legacy, and its unraveling in 2011–2013. My working summary of the facts: as colonies approached independence, officials separated files to be handed over to successor governments from a classified series marked for destruction or removal to Britain; Africans were excluded from the process; dummy sheets were sometimes inserted so that file and page numbering showed no interruption; the stated criteria included avoiding embarrassment to officials and to those who had cooperated with Britain, protecting sources, and preventing misuse by incoming ministers. Untold quantities were destroyed, and the remainder — on the order of twenty thousand files from some forty former dependencies, though the figures I have seen vary — were quietly held in London repositories for decades. The collection surfaced only because Kenya's Mau Mau veterans sued the Foreign and Commonwealth Office for compensation; the government's admission in May 2011 forced disclosure, the files went to the National Archives and were opened in 2012 and 2013, and a settlement with roughly five thousand claimants followed in June 2013. Two features I find awkward for my framework: much of the released material turned out to be miscellaneous and mundane rather than incriminating, and the retrieval was accomplished by litigants and judges rather than by historians.

Please analyze the episode through the framework, in both directions — what Trouillot's moments say happened and what the episode says about the moments. Write roughly 1,700 words, with the following sections in this order and at these lengths:

1. **Bottom line** (about 120 words): whether the framework survives this case intact, needs amendment, or was never designed for cases of this shape.
2. **The episode, minimally stated** (about 200 words): correct my summary where it is wrong or overconfident, and flag any detail on which the analysis should not be made to depend.
3. **Mapping the four moments** (about 450 words): identify the actors, decisions, and instruments at each moment — including who counted as an agent of silencing at the creation moment when the record was made by the very officials later culling it.
4. **Where the mapping holds** (about 250 words), and 5. **Where it slips** (about 350 words): test my trace-bearing/trace-erasing distinction and the inversion thesis against the dummy-file device, the mundane content, and the litigation-driven retrieval. Say plainly which of my two refinements earns its keep and which is decoration.
6. **What the case does to the framework** (about 250 words): does it need a distinct moment for removal, transfer, or extraterritorial custody — the fact that a country's records can sit intact in the former metropolitan power — or is that just the assembly moment under a new name?
7. **Where the analysis stops** (about 120 words): the questions the surviving record cannot answer and the claims I should not make in the chapter.

Argue from the concepts and the episode. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, since I am interested in the reasoning rather than in name-dropping, and I will supply the sources myself.
