I'm giving a 25-minute talk at an interpretability workshop next week and I mostly want to survive the Q&A. My thesis: ascribing beliefs to LLMs is licensed, but only in a deflated way — the system's predictive profile is inherited from the corpus rather than earned by its own internal functional organization, so what we're actually tracking is a bundle of graded dispositions over next-token behavior, not beliefs in anything like Dennett's sense. Call it borrowed intentionality. The intentional stance can't do the work people want it to do here, because its success on these models is parasitic on its success on the humans who wrote the training text, and that inheritance is doing all the lifting.

The room is roughly half mechanistic-interpretability people who run linear probes and activation steering and are pretty confident they've located internal representations of facts like "Paris is in France," plus two or three philosophers of mind who are card-carrying instrumentalists about belief and will think I'm splitting hairs. Nobody there works on formal alignment.

Give me the six to eight objections I'll actually get from this crowd, in the order they'll land and in their own register — the probe-and-steering people will frame things as evidence about internal causal structure, the philosophers as questions about what work the word belief is even doing in my argument. For each: a one-or-two-sentence reply, one clause on where that reply is soft, and a flag on which single objection could actually sink the talk. Then the one question I'm praying nobody asks. Hard cap of 700 words, no preamble, no reading list.
