You are a philosopher of notation and type with substantial hands-on experience in handwriting-recognition data practice. Think carefully before answering, and begin with a short, explicitly labeled brainstorming pass in which you weigh the candidate criteria I set out below, state which ones you will operationalize and which you will discard, and say what is lost by each discard. Work from reasoning rather than references: I do not want a bibliography, and you do not need to name sources.

I run annotation quality at a company that builds training and evaluation sets for handwriting recognition. Our largest current contract is a children's literacy corpus: roughly 40,000 segmented character images per week, from writers aged five to eleven, mixing manuscript and cursive hands, some of it from degraded classroom scans. For each cropped image an annotator must decide whether it is an instance of a target letter, most often the letter a. The decision has to be made in 25 to 40 seconds by workers with no training in linguistics or typography, and it has to be made the same way by all of them: the client audits quarterly and expects inter-annotator agreement above 0.85. Both error directions are expensive. A mark labeled a that is not an a contaminates a class permanently, because bad exemplars propagate through the trained model and are almost impossible to trace back to their source image. A mark refused when it genuinely was an a wastes collection money we have already spent and thins exactly the difficult region of the space the client is paying to cover. Anything a first-pass annotator cannot settle goes to a senior reviewer, but that tier can absorb at most about five percent of volume, so the procedure has to terminate on its own almost every time.

Here is the difficulty that off-the-shelf labeling guides do not solve, and the reason I want something built from the concept rather than from a rule list. The question of what makes every a an a has at least four respectable answers, and they come apart on real items:

- Function: the mark is being used as an a by the writer, and would be read as one by users of the writing system.
- Feature: the mark carries the diagnostic features that separate a from its rivals in this particular hand. The rival set is what matters, and it shifts with writer and style; for a the nearest neighbors are o, u, e, d, the digit 4, and the single-bowl form.
- Genealogy: the mark is reachable from a canonical exemplar by a continuous deformation that never crosses into a rival's diagnostic territory, so that the letter's spirit survives even where individual features are lost.
- Recognition: a competent reader given the image alone, with no word context, identifies it as an a at a rate well above chance.

Our current guide collapses all four into the single word legibility, and annotators then disagree because they are silently applying different criteria to the same image. I also have one fixed complication: in a second orthography we collect, the single-bowl form is a letter in its own right rather than a stylistic variant of a, so the same shape can be an instance of a in one corpus and a different letter in another. Treat that as a given parameter of the job, not as something to argue about.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure my annotators can actually run, delivered in these parts:

1. An ordered sequence of tests. For each: the question the annotator asks, what counts as a sufficient answer, and the exit — label, reject, or escalate. Include the stop conditions and the definition of the indeterminate tier, with the volume cap respected.
2. Language a worker with no linguistics background can follow. The four criteria above are for you; they should not appear in the annotator-facing text.
3. An explicit rule on word context: when an annotator may use the surrounding word to resolve a mark, when they may not, and how a context-assisted decision is recorded, since context-rescued labels cannot enter the shape-only subset we sell separately.
4. A rule enforcing rival-relativity, so that the operative question is never whether this is an a but whether it is closer to a than to each member of its rival set in this hand.
5. A one-page annotator card condensing all of the above.
6. A list of the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and the categories of item the procedure will systematically get wrong.

Then run it on three cases:

- An isolated crop showing a closed bowl with a vertical stem on its right, where the upper right of the bowl fails to close by a gap of roughly a fifth of the x-height, and nothing else is visible.
- That same shape appearing as the middle mark of a word that is plainly cat, written by the same child.
- A display-style capital A from a worksheet header, drawn with the crossbar omitted and the apex left open, in an otherwise consistent hand. On this third case, note in advance that I want display letterforms kept in the corpus. If your procedure throws them out, say so plainly rather than bending the rule, and tell me what keeping them would cost.

Close with two things. First, a short account of what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether it preserves the question about letter identity or quietly substitutes a labeling convention for it, and where a reader who cared about the concept rather than the pipeline would say the substitution bites. Second, a confidence assessment: rate each of your major design choices high, medium, or low with one sentence of justification, and single out the one point in the whole procedure you are least sure about, saying what evidence or argument would settle it.
