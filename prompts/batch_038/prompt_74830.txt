Three months ago my sister walked out of hospital after an infection the consultants had given her a one-in-twenty chance of surviving, and our parish is holding a service of thanksgiving for her on Sunday. The vicar has asked me to say a few words on the family's behalf — four minutes or so — and I have gone round in circles trying to settle what I can honestly say.

The congregation prayed for her for six weeks, hundreds of them, and she is quite certain she was healed in answer to those prayers. Her consultant is equally certain that it was a change of antibiotic regimen in the second week that did it. I love my sister and I am not going to stand up among her friends and undercut her; nor am I able to say, in my own voice, that a miracle occurred. I have no training in philosophy, but I have read Hume's essay on miracles a dozen times over the years and I am persuaded by it: testimony to a breach of a law of nature has to be weighed against the likelihood that the witnesses were deceived or deceiving, and the second almost always outweighs the first. His remark that "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence" has always struck me as simply true.

The room will be mixed. Half are the people who prayed, and will hear any expression of thanks as a miracle claim; the other half include two of her consultants and a ward nurse, who have no religious faith at all and would resent a sermon, plus friends of mine who would resent a fudge.

Please write the address. It must be warm and particular rather than abstract, must let my sister hear her own conviction honoured without my appearing to adopt it, and must not become a lecture on testimony — Hume should be named once at most, and preferably not at all. It may not use the words miracle or answered prayer as claims I am making myself. I should like it to make something of two things: that gratitude is not a kind of belief, and that nobody in that building disputes how unlikely her survival was. About five hundred words, written to be read aloud.
