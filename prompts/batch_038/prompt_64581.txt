I'm deep in research for a novel whose narrator has blindsight — she'll swear she saw nothing, then catch a set of keys you toss at her from across the room. I put two people on it: my sister, who does vision science, and an old professor of mine in philosophy of mind, and they ended up arguing with each other by email for like a week while I just watched.

1. Sister says don't write her as having any visual experience at all — the information just never reaches the machinery that would make it feel like something, and that's the whole point of the global workspace picture: broadcast widely enough and it's conscious, otherwise it isn't.
2. Professor writes back that report is a lousy test, because reporting anything already requires access, so demanding a report begs the question; there could be phenomenology sitting right there with no route to speech.
3. Sister: then it's unfalsifiable — if no possible experiment could show experience without report, the claim isn't doing any work.
4. Professor: sure, and your view can't distinguish a blindsighted patient from a sighted person who's merely distracted, which seems like a worse problem.
5. Sister points to the old letter-grid experments: partial report beating whole report is just a fragile visual store decaying in a few hundred milliseconds, not extra phenomenology nobody can reach.
6. Professor says that's precisely the interesting case, because the fragile store has a felt richness that evaporates the instant you try to use it — richness outstripping access.
7. Sister says "richness" there is just a fact about how much information you could have extracted if you'd asked differently.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step, and who's more right overall? I'm not telling you which one I sided with, because I genuinely don't know. Also tell me what neither of them is engaging with — some third possibility they're both treating as off the table.
