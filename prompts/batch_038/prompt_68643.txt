I write about science and ideas for an independent monthly magazine, and I also sit on the five-person working group designing a standing readers' council: a rotating body of readers who would meet quarterly to advise the editorial team on how we cover contested subjects — artificial intelligence, vaccination policy, climate adaptation. The group votes next Thursday on the recruitment rule, and the memo I circulate beforehand needs to settle it.

Three proposals are on the table. First, pure sortition: a stratified random draw from a commercial address file, matched to census margins, with an honorarium and no screening of any kind, and decliners replaced by the next name drawn in their stratum. Second, an open call: volunteers, with the editors selecting for regional, age, and educational balance and excluding anyone who holds party office or works for an advocacy organization. Third, a hybrid — two-thirds drawn by lot, one-third recruited from applicants — on the reasoning that the applicants are the ones who will actually do the reading between sessions.

One piece of internal evidence matters. A pilot council recruited by open sign-up last spring drew 214 volunteers, of whom 61 percent had already written to us about a story they loved or loathed. Its recommendations, arrived at unanimously, amounted to a request that we publish more of what we already publish.

Please write the memo: a recommendation with the parameters filled in — size, term length, honorarium, treatment of decliners, whether members may publish dissents — and reasoning my four colleagues could actually argue with. It must answer the objection I expect from the deputy editor, which is that the case for sortition does not transfer to our situation at all: a lottery confers standing precisely because the chosen are nobody's agents, answerable to no constituency and unreachable by lobbyists, whereas our council would be advisory only, would be handed its questions by the very editors whose work it reviews, and could be overruled by silence. If that objection lands, say so. If it lands only partly, identify which of the arguments for selection by lot survive the move from a legislature to a magazine's advisory panel and which do not. Address separately whether excluding partisans and advocates is a legitimate protection or a form of epistemic gerrymandering, and whether the answer differs under the rule you recommend than under the ones you reject. Close with the strongest case for your losing option, stated in terms its proponents would recognize. No footnotes or citations — I will handle attribution — but please do not invent studies or survey findings to fill gaps.
