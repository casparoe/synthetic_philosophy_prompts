I am working through Bertrand Russell's lecture "Vagueness" (delivered as an address and published in the *Australasian Journal of Psychology and Philosophy*, vol. 1, 1923), and I would like help understanding a connected set of claims in it rather than a general survey of the sorites literature. The four excerpts below are taken from an online transcription of the text; the transcription preserves a few obvious typographical slips, which I would ask you to read through rather than treat as meaningful.

Excerpt 1 (from the opening):

> Vagueness, which is my topic tonight, illustrates these remarks. You will no doubt think that, in the words of the poet: "Who speaks of vagueness should himself be vague." I propose to prove that all language is vague, and that therefore my language is vague, but I do not wish this conclusion to be one that you could derive without the help of the symbolism. I shall be as little vague as I know how to be if I am to employ the English language.

Excerpt 2:

> There is a certain tendency in those who have realized that words are vague to infer that things also are vague. We hear a great deal about the flux and the continuum and the unanalysability of the Universe, and it is often suggested that as our language becomes more precise, it becomes less adapted to represent the primitive chaos out of which man is supposed to have evolved the cosmos. This seems to me precisely a case of the fallacy of verbalism --- the fallacy that consists in mistaking the properties of words for the properties of things. Vagueness and precision alike are characteristics which can only belong to a representation, of which language is an example.

Excerpt 3 (the sentence immediately preceding this passage introduces the example of the word "red"):

> It is perfectly obvious, since colours form a continuum, that there are shades of colour concerning which we shall be in doubt whether to call them red or not, not because we are ignorant of the meaning of the word "red", but because it is a word the extent of whose application is essentially doubtful. This, of course, is the answer to the old puzzle about the man who went bald. It is supposed that at first he was not bald, that he lost his hairs one by one, and that in the end he was bald; therefore, it is argued, there must have been one hair the loss of which converted him into a bald man. This, of course, is absurd. Baldness is a vague conception; some men are certainly bald, some are certainly not bald, while between them there are men of whom it is not true to say they must be either be bald or not bald. The law of excluded middle is true when precise symbols are employed, but it is not true when symbols are vague, as, in fact, all symbols are.

Excerpt 4:

> This vagueness exists also, though in a lesser degree, in the quantitative words which science has tried hardest to make precise, such as a metre or a second. I am not going to invoke Einstein for the purpose of making these words vague. The metre, for example, is defined as the distance between two marks on a certain rod in Paris, when that rod is at a certain temperature. Now the marks are not points, but patches of a finite size, so that the distance between them is not a precise conception. Moreover, temperature cannot be measured with more than a certain degree of accuracy, and the temperature of a rod is never quite uniform. For all these reasons the conception of a metre is lacking in precision. The same applies to a second. The second is defined by relation to the rotation of the earth, but the earth is not a rigid body, and two parts of the earth's surface do not take exactly the same time to rotate; moreover all observations have a margin of error. There are some occurrences of which we can say that they take less than a second to happen, and others of which we can say that they take more, but between the two there will be a number of occurrences of which we believe that they do not all last equally long, but of none of which we can say whether they last more or less than a second. Therefore, when we say an occurrence lasts a second, all that it is worth while to mean is that no possible accuracy of observation will show whether it lasts more or less than a second.

Could you please work through the following questions with me? I would like the discussion to stay close to what Russell actually writes; where you find it helpful to name a modern position (epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree-theoretic or many-valued semantics, contextualism), please say explicitly which words in the excerpts license that label and which do not. You do not need to cite or recommend any secondary literature.

1. What exactly is being denied in Excerpt 3? The clause "there are men of whom it is not true to say they must be either be bald or not bald" seems capable of at least three readings: (i) that the disjunction is *false* of such men; (ii) that the disjunction is neither true nor false, i.e. that there is a truth-value gap; (iii) that the vague symbol fails to determine one definite proposition at all, so that there is no single disjunction with a determinate truth-value to assess. Which reading sits best with Excerpt 2's doctrine that vagueness and precision belong only to representations? What does each reading commit him to, and which of them is consistent with his insistence that nothing here is a fact about the men themselves?

2. He calls this "the answer to the old puzzle about the man who went bald." Please reconstruct the sorites argument in the form you think he has in mind — the conditional form, the induction form, or both — and identify precisely which premise, rule, or step his reply is supposed to block. Does abandoning excluded middle for vague predicates by itself stop the hair-by-hair march, or does he need some further thesis in addition (about the conditionals, about discriminability, or about what proposition a vague sentence expresses)? To what extent is his "answer" an explanation of why the argument fails, and to what extent is it simply a redescription of the phenomenon the argument exploits?

3. Is the metre-and-second case in Excerpt 4 the same phenomenon as the baldness case? He argues that because the marks on the standard rod are "patches of a finite size," the distance between them "is not a precise conception." Yet there would seem to be a perfectly determinate physical distance between any two specified points on that rod; the difficulty looks rather like the failure of a stipulation to single out which points are meant, or the impossibility of finding out the answer. Are vagueness proper, incompleteness of stipulation, and practical unmeasurability three distinguishable things here? Does Excerpt 4 run them together, and if so, does that undermine the inference from the scientific case to the claim that "all symbols are vague"?

4. The closing gloss of Excerpt 4 — that to say an occurrence lasts a second is only worth meaning that "no possible accuracy of observation will show whether it lasts more or less than a second" — looks like an analysis of content in terms of possible observation. How does that square with Excerpt 3? If the content of the claim is fixed by what observation could settle, then for that content an excluded-middle principle seems to hold (either some possible observation would show more, or none would), whereas the baldness passage withholds excluded middle. Is he moving between a claim about truth and a claim about warranted assertibility, and does anything in these excerpts entitle him to that move? What would he have to surrender in order to keep both claims?

5. Excerpt 1 concedes that if all language is vague, then his own language is vague. If his thesis-statements are vague in the sense of Excerpt 3, do they themselves fall outside the scope of excluded middle, and does the position thereby undercut itself? Or does his framework leave room for a vague symbol to state something true — for example because every acceptable sharpening of it is true — and if that resource is available, why does it not equally restore excluded middle for the very sentences about bald men that he wants to exempt from it? Is there a stable position between self-refutation and self-limitation here, or must he settle for being, in his own phrase, "as little vague as I know how to be"?
