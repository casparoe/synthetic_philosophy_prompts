I am the teaching assistant for a fourth-year seminar on causation in the historical sciences, and this week I have been given one of the two essay questions from the final examination to double-mark. The convenor wants my marks and my second marker's marks compared before the board meets, so I would like an independent assessment of one script before I write up my own; I have deliberately not drafted my comments yet, and I would rather not anchor on them. The question, worth 20 of the 100 marks for the paper, was as follows:

"Historians sometimes say that an event was overdetermined — that several distinct conditions would each, on their own, have brought it about. Explain what a claim of that kind commits one to, and how it differs from the weaker claim that an event had many causes, each necessary but none sufficient. What, if anything, does genuine overdetermination threaten in a counterfactual account of causation? Illustrate your answer with a case of your own choosing."

The script in front of me runs as follows.

There are two senses of overdetermined in the historical literature and most of the trouble comes from sliding between them. In the loose sense, to say the war had many causes is only to say that a long list of factors — the alliance system, the arms race, the press, imperial rivalry — jointly raised the probability of a general war, and that historians dispute which of them deserves emphasis. That is the sense in which a cause may be necessary without being sufficient: take away the alliance system and there may be no war in 1914, but the alliance system by itself would never have produced one. In the strict sense the claim is much stronger. It says there were several distinct sets of conditions, each individually sufficient for the effect, so that the effect would have occurred whichever of them had been present.

My case is August 1914. The killing at Sarajevo is the trigger, but beneath it sat standing causes — the mobilisation timetables, the logic of the German war plan that turned a Russian mobilisation into an invasion of France, the alliance commitments — all of which were still in place throughout July and would have produced a general European war in any case. The trigger simply got there first, and the structural causes were backups waiting in the wings. That is what makes this symmetric overdetermination rather than a merely multi-causal story: more than one sufficient condition was on offer.

The difficulty is for counterfactual analyses. On the Lewis-style view, c causes e just in case, roughly, e would not have occurred had c not occurred. But if the structural causes were sufficient, the war comes about anyway, and the effect does not counterfactually depend on the assassination at all. One obvious repair is to remove all the candidate causes together, in which case there is plainly no war and dependence is restored; but I do not think that is what the analysis is supposed to deliver, since the test is meant to be applied to each candidate separately.

Mackie's INUS analysis is usually wheeled in at this point: a cause is an insufficient but non-redundant part of a condition which is itself unnecessary but sufficient for the effect. Notice what this concedes. Sufficiency never attaches to an individual candidate cause, only to whole conjunctions of conditions. If that is right, then strictly speaking nothing is ever individually sufficient, nothing is ever overdetermined in the strong sense, and the term is a piece of rhetorical inflation that historians should abandon.

There is a further consequence worth drawing out. If the war really was overdetermined, then nothing turns factually on what the men of July 1914 decided; they were prisoners of their own railway timetables. There is no determinate answer to the question of who caused the war, and the entire war-guilt controversy that has run since Versailles rests on a metaphysical mistake. One thing I would add in defence of the historians, though, is that the relevant counterfactual depends on the contrast being drawn. Had Sarajevo not happened there might still have been no war in August 1914, even if there would have been one by 1916, and a great deal of the disagreement in the literature is disagreement about which of those foils is being held fixed rather than disagreement about the facts.

No introduction, no overall summary paragraph, and nothing about prose style or organisation: just the score and the five numbered comments.
