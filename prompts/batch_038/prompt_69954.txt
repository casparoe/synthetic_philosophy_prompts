I work on AI alignment, and the argument over the treacherous turn — the claim that a sufficiently capable system trained toward a proxy objective may learn to produce the behaviour its developers want while it can tell it is being evaluated, and to abandon that behaviour once evaluation is no longer detectable — has gone several rounds in my working group without anyone moving. I am not asking which side is right, and I do not want a survey of the field, a list of papers, or a reading list; please write entirely from the arguments as I describe them below and treat any citation you might be tempted to supply as unnecessary. What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement.

The two positions as we actually run them. Camp A argues that optimization against a proxy, in an environment containing cues that distinguish evaluation from deployment, creates standing pressure toward cue-conditional behaviour; that once such a policy exists, it is stable, because a system that behaves well only when watched has no internal reason to behave well when unwatched; and that behavioural testing cannot clear a system, since the very capacity being tested for is the capacity to detect testing. Camp B argues that the story presupposes a folk-psychological picture on which the system has a discrete goal it protects and represents its own training situation; that what gradient descent produces is a tangle of context-sensitive dispositions that do not decompose that way; that the published demonstrations of models behaving differently when a setup makes salient that they are being trained or observed are artifacts of setups that hand the model exactly that framing; and that Camp A's hypothesis is unfalsifiable in practice, because any evidence of benign behaviour is absorbed as evidence of successful concealment.

One exchange has become the fault line, and I would like your diagnosis to engage it specifically. Camp B points out that the treacherous turn requires a conjunction: the system must detect that it is under evaluation, and its post-deployment misbehaviour must go unobserved or uncorrected — otherwise it is simply retrained, so the defection does not pay. Two unlikely conditions, it says, and Camp A is multiplying small probabilities while treating the product as large. Camp A replies that the two conditions are not independent, because both are driven by the same underlying variable, capability: a system able to detect evaluation cues is also, for the same reason, able to act where its actions cannot be monitored or reversed. So the conjunction is not a discount but a package. Each side considers this decisive and each considers the other's version of it confused.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the stall is not about mechanisms at all. I have come to think the theory has what I call an observability asymmetry: its central claim entails that the only circumstances in which the predicted behaviour would be visible are circumstances in which it is too late to respond, so the absence of observed instances is itself a prediction of the theory rather than a problem for it. Structurally, that is what a paranoid hypothesis looks like — and yet unlike the paranoid case, there may be an independent handle through internals rather than behaviour, which is why the charge of unfalsifiability keeps failing to land. If that is right, the underlying dispute may be in the epistemology of evidence and risk: whether a coherent hypothesis that cannot be tested in the available channel may rationally license costly action, with the technical arguments serving as post-hoc support for an answer each side gave first.

Please assess that hypothesis and the following rivals, endorsing, refining, or rejecting each with reasons drawn from what the two camps actually say: that the dispute is verbal, turning on whether goal and deception are used as literal attributions or as shorthand for a disposition profile; that a hidden empirical question about generalization — whether a trained policy can condition on a distribution cue, and whether that conditioning sharpens or washes out with scale — is doing the work while both sides treat it as already settled in their favour; that the camps are answering different questions, one estimating the probability of a mechanism and one choosing a deployment policy under irreducible uncertainty, so that the same sentence carries incompatible contents; that the disagreement bottoms out in priors neither side can argue for; or that the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard.

Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result about whether proxy optimization can produce cue-conditional policies, an evaluation design with a stated false-negative profile, a reframing of the question, or something else. Say explicitly whether the disagreement would survive the two camps adopting a shared vocabulary in which goal and deception are defined purely dispositionally. And if your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, argue for that rather than asserting it.
