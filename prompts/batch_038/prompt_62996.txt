Been going round in circles on the acts and omissions doctrine and I keep coming back to the English conjoined twins case, Re A, the Manchester one from 2000 where the Court of Appeal authorised separation against the parents' wishes. I know the broad shape of the story and I don't want another summary of it. What I want is the case actually run through the doing versus allowing, killing versus letting die distinction, in both directions, with the mapping done properly.

Here are the facts as I have them so you don't need to go hunting. The twins — the court used the pseudonyms Jodie and Mary, and I'll do the same — were joined at the lower abdomen. Mary had severe brain damage, a heart that barely functioned and no usable lungs of her own, and was alive only because oxygenated blood reached her from Jodie through a shared artery. Jodie's heart and lungs were doing the work for the pair of them and straining under it. Left joined, the medical evidence was that Jodie's heart would fail within a few months and both would die. Separated, Jodie had an excellent chance of surviving to a fairly normal life; Mary would die almost immediately. The parents were devout Catholics from Gozo and refused consent to the operation even accepting that both girls would die. The hospital trust went to court for a declaration that separation would be lawful. At first instance the judge said it was not a killing at all — he treated it as analogous to Bland, so withdrawing support, an omission, the passive sort of thing. The Court of Appeal reached the same result but rejected that analysis. Two of the three judges treated what the surgeons would do as an intentional killing of Mary needing a special justification, one finding it in something like self-defence exercised on Jodie's behalf, the other in necessity; the third rested more on what the surgeons' intentions actually amounted to. The operation went ahead in November 2000, Jodie lived, Mary died as predicted.

So, first the mapping, and be disciplined about it rather than gestural. Who is the agent whose doing or allowing is in question — the surgeons, the trust, the parents, Jodie herself? What is the act and what is the omission, and is clamping a shared artery one event or two depending on how you slice it? What is the baseline state of affairs that letting die is supposed to be measured against here, and can you state one that isn't already smuggling in a view about who is entitled to whose circulation? Then what does the distinction predict about how the case ought to come out, and does it explain why the appellate court threw out the letting-die characterisation rather than the reverse — my suspicion is that the characterisation was doing justificatory work, that calling it an omission means you never have to find a defence at all, and if so that's a problem for the distinction, not just for the judge at first instance.

Then the part I actually care about: which features of the case does the distinction get wrong, and which does it just never touch. I keep coming up against at least these. Mary was going to die either way, so the question is not dying but dying some months earlier, and whether those months were worth anything to a child with her brain damage — the distinction has no obvious handle on that. It may be that the two of them are one organism rather than two people for the purposes of counting harms, in which case there is no separate victim to be killed or let die. The parents refusal is itself a doing versus allowing question, since refusing consent is how both girls come to die, and yet the whole apparatus seems to have nothing to say about who gets to decide. And what the three judges were really fighting over looks to me less like doing versus allowing and more like whose body the artery belonged to — a claims and bodily integrity question wearing the costume of an agency question.

Tell me where each of the standard positions lands on the case. The Rachels-style bare difference argument and whether this episode is just another bathtub case with more stakes. The view that the distinction is real but not for the reasons people usually give, Bennett's line about positive and negative agency and what it does with a surgeon who has to move his hands in a particular way to stop a supply. The double effect route, and whether the intention question can carry the weight the third judge seemed to want it to carry. And whatever survives of the distinction once the victim is physically plumbed into the beneficiary, which is a configuration none of these arguments were built for. Which of them actually explains the outcome the court reached, and which merely happens to be compatible with it? Is this case a confirmation of one of them, an anomaly for all of them, or a freak that the theory was never meant to cover — and if it's a freak, say so plainly rather than forcing a reading.

And where the literature is just silent, say that too. I would much rather be told the distinction has nothing to say about the timing question or about parental authority than have something plausible invented for me. No need for references or a reading list, I've got the judgment and the papers — I want the argument.
