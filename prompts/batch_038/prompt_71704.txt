(b) (9 points) Let Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} with the uniform prior. Agent A's information partition is {{1,2,3}, {4,5,6}}; agent B's is {{1,2}, {3,4}, {5,6}}. Let E = {1,3}. Compute each agent's posterior probability of E at ω = 1; determine whether each agent knows the other's posterior at that state; and say whether the situation is consistent with the agreement theorem, explaining why.

(c) (8 points) Does the common prior assumption follow from the requirement that each agent be individually Bayesian-rational — that is, from the Savage-style requirement that an agent's preferences over acts admit a subjective expected utility representation? Explain.

**Student's answer.**

(a) The common prior assumption says that agents who look as though they have different priors should really be modeled as starting from a single prior over a state space that already contains their private information, and then conditionalizing on what each of them learned; apparent differences in belief are just differences in information. Aumann's agreement theorem says that if two agents share a common prior, are commonly known to be rational and to update by conditionalization, and their posterior probabilities for an event E are common knowledge, then those posteriors are equal. The hypothesis that has to be common knowledge is the posteriors themselves: it is not enough that each posterior merely be true, nor even that each agent know the other's.

(b) At ω = 1, A's cell is {1,2,3}, which has prior probability 1/2, and E ∩ {1,2,3} = {1,3}, which has prior probability 1/3; so A's posterior is 2/3. B's cell is {1,2}, prior probability 1/3, and E ∩ {1,2} = {1}, prior probability 1/6; so B's posterior is 1/2. A knows B's posterior: whichever of ω = 1, 2, 3 obtains, B's cell — {1,2} in the first two cases, {3,4} in the third — contains exactly one state in E, so B's posterior is 1/2 either way. B, however, does not know A's posterior: B considers ω = 2 possible, and if the state were 2 then A would know only that the state lay in {1,2}, which would give A a posterior of 1/2 rather than 2/3. So the two posteriors are not common knowledge, and the example does not contradict the agreement theorem. The theorem never said that agents with a common prior cannot hold different beliefs; it said only that they cannot commonly know that they do.

(c) Yes. Savage's representation theorem shows that if an agent's preferences over acts satisfy the axioms, those preferences can be represented by a probability function that is unique, together with a utility function unique up to positive affine transformation. A rational agent's beliefs are therefore not a free parameter; they are pinned down by rationality itself. Two agents whose priors differ must consequently differ in their preferences over acts as well, and since the axioms fix what a rational ordering over acts looks like, at most one of the two can be fully rational. On this view the common prior assumption is not an extra hypothesis layered on top of Bayesian rationality but a consequence of it, and the agreement theorem needs to state it separately only because the theorem is meant to cover agents who may fail Savage's axioms.
