I am a high school senior writing a research paper for a political rhetoric seminar, and I want to test Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" against a case that I suspect is genuinely awkward for it, rather than against the examples the essay itself offers. Please work through the analysis with me.

The case is the renaming of the CIA's coercive interrogation program as "enhanced interrogation techniques" between 2002 and 2009. So that you are not guessing at the record, here are the facts I am working from: after Abu Zubaydah's capture in 2002, the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel produced a memorandum defining torture so narrowly — physical pain comparable in intensity to that accompanying organ failure or death, mental harm lasting months or years — that waterboarding, prolonged sleep deprivation, and binding detainees in stress positions fell outside the definition; the memo was written in answer to CIA officers' question about which methods they were permitted to use. It is composed in lucid technical prose: numbered sections, defined terms, active verbs, very little figurative language. The label then propagated into official documents, briefing materials, court filings, and news copy, and it survived the exposure of the program — the Senate Intelligence Committee's 2014 report, which condemns the practices, still uses the term and its acronym throughout.

First, map Orwell onto the episode mechanism by mechanism rather than in general: the claim that political writing is largely "the defence of the indefensible" and therefore has to consist of "euphemism, question-begging and sheer cloudy vagueness"; the claim that the underlying driver is insincerity, a gap between one's real and declared aims, which pushes a writer toward long words and exhausted idioms; the claim that such phraseology lets people discuss atrocity "without calling up mental pictures of them"; and the claim that the cycle is self-reinforcing yet reversible by individual effort, with the closing list of writing rules as the remedy. For each, tell me which feature of the case it fits exactly, which it fits only loosely, and which it does not touch at all. Where Orwell is silent, say so plainly instead of stretching him to cover the gap.

Second, the puzzle that made me choose this case, and a hypothesis I want evaluated. Orwell's diagnostic markers are absent: the crucial documents are concrete, precise, and grammatically plain, and they nevertheless did the work he assigns to bad prose. My hypothesis is that his catalogue — dying metaphors, operators, pretentious diction, meaningless words — is missing a category for misdirected precision, exactness lavished on a threshold while the thing the threshold excludes goes unnamed. Take a position on whether that is a real addition to Orwell's account or merely a relabeling of question-begging, which he already had.

Third, the audience problem. Orwell seems to assume the euphemism operates on readers. Here the readers — journalists, judges, senators, the public — were mostly not fooled, and the term persisted anyway. Work out what the euphemism was actually for if not deception, and whether the insincerity account can absorb that answer.

I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I have read the essay and I know the chronology, and I cannot check sources you supply. What I want is a verdict, argued for: does Orwell's theory emerge from this case intact, intact but narrowed, or broken? Commit to one of those and defend it rather than presenting the readings as equally available. And if you think I have misstated a fact, or built a question into my framing, say so at the top rather than quietly working around it.
