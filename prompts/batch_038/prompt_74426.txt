A chapter in a trade-press book I'm reading on the history of tolerance devotes several pages to Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals." Its account runs as follows: "Montaigne is the founder of cultural relativism. Persuaded by his reading and his conversations that every people measures the world by its own habits, he concludes that 'barbarian' has no content beyond 'not us.' He therefore declines to judge the Tupinambá: their cannibalism is for them no more objectionable than our own dietary prohibitions, and there is no common standard by which one practice could be ranked against another. He goes further and inverts the verdict, casting them as uncorrupted children of nature whose society is morally superior to ours — the first noble savage. But this strands him. If no standard exists outside a culture, Montaigne cannot make the criticism that occupies most of the essay, namely that Europeans who torture the living and massacre whole villages are worse than the people they call savages. His relativism is thus self-undermining, and the essay survives as travel writing rather than as moral thought."

I have only skimmed the essay, and what I do recall is a remark to the effect that we may call these people barbarous with respect to the rules of reason, though not with respect to ourselves, who exceed them in every kind of barbarity — which sounds like a ranking rather than a shrug. Is the book's account fair? Please go through it claim by claim and tell me what a Montaigne scholar would accept, what is a defensible popular simplification, and what the essay itself contradicts. In particular: does the sentence I half-remember carry the force the book's argument needs, and does the charge of self-undermining survive once the summary is corrected?
