---

By "epistemically reliable" I'll mean: a selection method that tends to produce a body which makes correct decisions — decisions that are well informed and that track the real interests of the governed, rather than the interests of whoever is best positioned to win an election.

Montesquieu's line is the standard starting point. Lot is natural to democracy because it offends no one: nobody can complain they were passed over, since nobody chose. Choice is natural to aristocracy because it is a judgement about who is better, and it entrenches the people who are already in a position to look better. Aristotle makes roughly the same point. And Athens is the proof of concept: the boule of 500 was filled by lot, annually, from the citizens, so it was a random sample of the city, a statistical microcosm — which is exactly what modern sortitionists say a citizen assembly should be. That's the first epistemic advantage. Elections filter for wealth, name recognition, and being good at talking, so an elected chamber is systematically skewed toward lawyers and the comfortably off, whereas a sorted chamber has the same class, gender, and occupational mix as the country. And because the chamber mirrors the population, its decisions will mirror the decisions the population itself would reach if it were properly informed.

Second, and this is the part the question is really after: correlated errors. Condorcet's jury theorem says that if each member is more likely than not to be right, then as the group grows the probability that the majority is right approaches 1 — but only if the members' errors are independent of one another. Sortition seems uniquely good at securing that independence. Randomly chosen members did not run on a shared platform, do not owe their seats to a party machine, and have no whip to obey; they are 500 separate people who happen to have been drawn together. So a sorted chamber is much closer to Condorcet's ideal jury than a parliamentary caucus is, and the correlated-errors objection — which is fatal for elected bodies that all vote along party lines — actually cuts in favour of lot.

Third, there is a fairness point that also does epistemic work. Nobody knows in advance whether they will be drawn, so at the constitutional stage everyone is choosing under a lottery, which is basically the original position applied to real institutions: a Rawlsian veil of ignorance that elections cannot produce, because candidates know exactly who they are and what they want.

The objection I think survives is accountability. An MP who governs badly can be thrown out; a sorted delegate serving a single short term faces no sanction at all, so nothing disciplines them once seated, and if the chamber gets captured by a charismatic internal faction there is no mechanism for the public to remove it. The defenders' reply — the chamber can expel its own members, and short terms limit the damage — is just the chamber policing itself, which is the problem restated rather than answered. Relatedly, nobody sorts the agenda. Even a perfectly representative chamber depends on civil servants and invited experts to settle what it hears about, and lot does nothing about that bottleneck.

So the claim in the question is basically right: sortition is more epistemically reliable, and Athens shows the arrangement works in practice and not only on paper.
