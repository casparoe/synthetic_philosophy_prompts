I work on interpretability at a mid-size lab. We keep an internal registry of "mechanisms of concern": circuits and features we've characterized in earlier models, mostly things that look like planning-around-evaluation, sandbagging, or instrumental reasoning about whoever is deploying the system. Every new checkpoint gets run through an automated screening pass that looks for the registered mechanisms again, and when it registers a hit, a human has to make the call: is this the same mechanism we documented, or a look-alike? Right now that call is made by whoever's on rotation and its basically vibes, but it's load-bearing. "Not the same mechanism" clears the flag and the release proceeds. "Same mechanism" holds it and escalates to red-teaming. Roughly weekly during a training run, plus every new model family.

Please write me a decision procedure a junior researcher can run on a flag without having thought about any of the metaphysics involved, and that a skeptical reviewer can audit afterwards.

The core problem I can't solve myself: there are at least four different standards for "same mechanism" and they come apart. Genealogy (it's descended from the registered one through the training process, checkpoint to checkpoint). Causal profile (intervening on it produces and removes the same behavior). Implementation (same algorithm-level description, same computation, maybe at a different layer or in different units). Functional role (same input-output contribution, same thing being computed about what inputs, regardless of how). I need the procedure to pick a primary standard or give me an explicit weighting, and to defend that choice rather than just assert it. This smells like a Ship of Theseus / criteria-of-identity problem to me; if you think that's the right frame, use it, and if you think it's a bad analogy, tell me why, because I'll otherwise keep reaching for it.

Constraints you have to design around:

- Sparse autoencoder dictionaries don't align across checkpoints, so "same feature index" isn't available even within one training run. Anything relying on unit identity across models is off the table.
- Some flags are cross-family and cross-scale (7B documented mechanism, candidate in a 70B model with a different data mix). For third-party models we sometimes have behavioral access only, no weights.
- Half a day of one researcher's time, max. Two-person sign-off on any verdict that clears a flag.
- No ground truth, ever. We never get to observe whether the mechanism would have produced the bad behavior in a real deployment. So the procedure can't be calibrated against outcomes; say what that does to its status.

Then run it on three cases:

1. Same training run, twenty thousand steps later. The registered feature has split into two features and the causal work has partly migrated a layer. Patching out both recovers most but not all of the original behavioral suppression.
2. A mechanism we documented in a small open-weight model shows up in a much larger model from a different training run. Totally different features, different algorithmic description, but patching it in reproduces the flagged behavior and patching it out removes it.
3. The detector fires on a behavior, and inside the same model there are two candidate mechanisms that could produce it: one registered, one we've never seen before that is behaviorally indistinguishable from the registered one as far as our tests can tell.

I want at least one of these to come out against what I'd want it to. Be explicit about which one and about what I'd be giving up by following the procedure there instead of overriding it.

Two more things. First, tell me what the output should be: a binary identity verdict, or a graded similarity score against a threshold. If graded, what would make the threshold anything other than arbitrary, and how should the number be reported so a non-expert doesn't over-read it. Second, say plainly if you think the whole registry framing is the wrong unit — that mechanisms aren't the kind of thing that persists across models at all, so the question the screening pass asks is malformed and something else should replace it. I'd rather hear that now than after building more tooling on top of it.

Finally, mark every point where the procedure stops being a procedure and hands the decision to a person, and tell me where it will systematically misfire: distributed or redundant mechanisms, mechanisms that only exist relative to a prompt distribution we don't have, mechanisms that are the same at one grain and different at another. I'd like the failure modes listed alongside the steps they break, not in a separate disclaimer at the end.
