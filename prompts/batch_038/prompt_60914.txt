I'm a third-year philosophy undergraduate, currently enrolled in a seminar on consciousness. Our department's student philosophy society has asked me to run one session in their open series, which is aimed squarely at people who have never taken a philosophy course; the last one drew about twenty, mostly engineers and psychology students, with a few literature majors. I have chosen the question of whether dreams are experiences: Malcolm's case that the received view rests on a confusion, and Dennett's case that dreams are not experiences either, though for reasons that run in something like the opposite direction.

I have read both papers and I am reasonably confident about what they say. What I need is not a restatement of them but a plan for explaining them, because I have never taught anything before and I can already feel the shape of the difficulty.

First, the order. I want the dependency structure worked out rather than a table of contents. My guess is that the received view has to be in the students' hands before Malcolm's conclusion can even be stated — and that the received view has to be stated as a claim about where experiences sit in time, not merely as a claim that dreams are vivid. Correct me if that is wrong. I also need to know which of the two arguments should come first. Dennett first seems to turn Malcolm into a straw man; Malcolm first risks the room concluding that he is simply obtuse about his own dream life and switching off. Tell me how to resolve that, and what must be true by the time the second argument is introduced for the contrast to register.

Second, the example. I had planned to open with my own recurring dream in which a telephone rings and, on waking, turns out to have been my alarm. I want to know whether that case has what the argument needs or whether it quietly sabotages it, and if it is the wrong vehicle, what kind of case should replace it: what the example must contain, and what it must conspicuously not contain, if the two positions are to be separable inside a single scenario.

Third, the misconceptions, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I can predict three: that they will hear this as Descartes' dreaming argument and start arguing about whether we can know anything at all; that they will hear dreams are not experiences as dreams are not real, or as nobody actually dreams; and that they will hear Malcolm and Dennett as agreeing, differing only in tone. I expect there are others I cannot see from inside the debate — something, I suspect, about what memory is being asked to do, and something about whether a report is a description. Name them and place them.

Fourth, two of the attendees have done a rotation in a sleep laboratory, and I am certain one of them will raise the lucid-dreaming work in which dreamers emit pre-arranged eye movements during verified REM. I want a designated place in the plan for that intervention, and I want to know what to concede. Does it actually reach Dennett's argument — I take his hinge to be the idea that what happens after a moment can help settle what the experience at that moment was — or does it reach only a popular caricature of it? I would rather be precise about the limits of my reply than bluff in front of them.

Fifth, the check. Give me one question or one short case to put to the room at the end that would tell me whether they have grasped the disagreement rather than merely followed me politely. I would prefer one where the answer is not legible from the phrasing.

Finally, the omissions. What should I cut and announce that I am cutting — the broader verificationist commitments behind Malcolm, presumably, and perhaps anything downstream of the multiple drafts model — and where exactly does the line fall at this level? Please deliver the sequence with rough timings, the carrying example, and the misconception map. I do not want a bibliography; the two papers are all I intend to use.
