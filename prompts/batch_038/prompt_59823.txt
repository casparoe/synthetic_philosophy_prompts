For a school independent-study project on minds and machines, I read a chapter of a popular book about the mathematics of consciousness that I borrowed from our library. The chapter's summary of the Lucas–Penrose argument goes like this:

'Gödel's theorem proves that no machine can ever match the human mind. Any system running on fixed rules — and a computer program is just that — will contain statements the system itself can never prove, though we, standing outside it, can see that they are true. So take any program offered as an account of a mathematician, write down its Gödel sentence, and reason your way to the truth of that sentence. The program cannot do this; you can. Human reasoning therefore is not algorithmic, and that is why we have free will and why Penrose goes on to argue that the brain must be exploiting quantum-gravity effects inside the microtubules of its neurons. The usual objection — that we cannot know a human being is consistent, so we cannot apply Gödel's theorem to ourselves — is a dodge. The argument needs only that we see the truth of one particular sentence, and consistency is precisely what we do see. Nor does it help the mechanist to say the program could be repaired by adding that sentence as a new axiom: we simply form the Gödel sentence of the improved program and repeat. That settles it.'

I want to know how much of this to believe, and I am not asking for references or a reading list — I cannot chase down sources for this project anyway, so please just explain. Go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a mathematical logician or a philosopher of mind would sign off on, which are the sort of simplification a popular book is entitled to, and which are distortions. In particular: does the incompleteness theorem by itself license the step from "the program cannot prove this sentence" to "we can see that it is true," and what exactly does that step require beyond consistency? Is the remark about free will part of the argument, or a separate thesis that has been welded onto it? Does the microtubule proposal follow from the incompleteness reasoning, or is it an independent hypothesis that could turn out false even if the argument succeeded? And when the passage announces that human reasoning "is not algorithmic," is that really what Lucas and Penrose concluded, or is it stronger than what their reasoning supports? One more thing I noticed but could not evaluate: the passage says a human recognizes truths "that no algorithm produces," yet the sentence being recognized is itself produced mechanically from the program, so I suspect something is being slid past me there.

I should say where I am coming from. I am inclined to think the argument works, because I cannot picture a program genuinely recognizing that a sentence is true rather than merely printing it. But a classmate says the whole line was refuted decades ago by the consistency objection, and that anyone still pressing it is ignoring the fact that human mathematicians believe plenty of false things about arithmetic. I suspect both of us are overclaiming. So please finish by telling me what the most defensible version of the argument looks like once the distortions are stripped out, what that version would and would not show about artificial minds, and which single step a serious opponent should concentrate their attack on.
