So I'm six months into my fourth novel and I've got a character who's carrying more intellectual weight than I planned for him. Ray is the narrator's uncle — eleven months as an army medic, then nine years driving an ambulance, now he runs a small hospice and has self-published a forty-page pamphlet he calls *The Toll*, which the family pretends to have read. Chapter fourteen is supposed to turn on Ray laying his argument out at a kitchen table while the narrator tries to punch a hole in it, and my two beta readers both came back with the same note: the hole punches itself, and Ray sounds like a man arguing against himself without noticing. Which might be great, if it's deliberate. Right now I can't tell. Here's the thing as I've got it sketched, and it's Ray's voice more than mine, so forgive the pulpit:

(1) You act wrongly whenever you impose on someone — without their say in the matter and with no way for them to get out of the way — a substantial risk of being killed or maimed, for the sake of ends that are yours rather than theirs. (2) Every war unavoidably does exactly that, including a defensive one, including one fought against an army that is already killing people: your bombs and your blockade land on the other side's civilians, and your own nineteen-year-olds mostly have no more say in the decision than those civilians do. (3) So every war is wrong to fight in, and that's pacifism, and it doesn't need a single empirical claim about how wars are actually conducted. (4) But look at what (1) is doing: it never mentions war. Put a city next to a chemical plant. Route a highway past a school. Fly a route over a town. Run a vaccination campaign. Raise a levee that saves your street and puts two extra feet of water in somebody else's basement. Send a fishing fleet out under a forecast that everyone has read. Each of those imposes a non-consensual, non-exitable, substantial risk of death or maiming on identifiable people for ends that belong to someone else — a commuter's, a ratepayer's, a developer's, a shipping company's. (5) Not all of those are wrong; Ray drove an ambulance for nine years and will fight you on the traffic one in particular. (6) So (1) is false, risk-imposition isn't the thing that makes war wrong, and what Ray has left is "war is very bad and it kills enormous numbers of people" — which isn't a philosophy, it's a weather report.

What I need from you is a diagnosis I can put in a mouth, and I'd rather you skip the names and the bibliography — I haven't read the just war literature and Ray certainly hasn't, so anything that only works as a footnote is useless to me. Tell me where the chain actually breaks, or whether it doesn't break at all and Ray is just being asked to pick a premise to lose. I suspect one clause in (1) is smuggling — something about who the risk is reciprocal with, or who shares in the benefit, or the difference between a risk that's dispersed over a hundred thousand drivers and one that's concentrated on a single block — but I don't know if that's a real philosophical distinction or a novelist reaching for a get-out. If I have to choose I'd rather Ray keep (3) than give up (5), because (5) is his whole life and losing it turns him into a crank instead of a man with a problem. So: which step is the bad one, why did it look perfectly fine when I wrote it, and what does each of the standard positions — the liability people, the double effect people, the ones who say war is only contingently wrong — have to pay to reject it? And finally, be honest with me about whether a smart reader with no philosophy training would catch this before page 300, because if they would, I need to give the narrator the line.
