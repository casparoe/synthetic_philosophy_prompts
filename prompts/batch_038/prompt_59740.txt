So, I write novels, and the one coming out in spring has a protagonist who feels pain perfectly well and simply doesn't mind it. I built him out of the real condition where a lesion takes the awfulness away but leaves the location and the intensity intact — people say yes, that hurts, it just doesn't bother them, and apparently some of them laugh at the pinprick. Pain asymbolia, I think its called.

The library in my town has asked me to do an evening talk about the research behind the book. Forty minutes, a flip chart, about thirty people: my usual book-club regulars, a lot of retirees, three or four nurses from the hospice, and one woman who's had fibromyalgia for twenty years and writes me letters about it.

Don't explain the condition to me, I've got that far enough. What I want is a plan for how to explain it, because every time I rehearse it I blow it somewhere different. Give me the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what — does the "there's a sensory part of pain and a separate suffering part" distinction have to be on the table before I tell them about the patients, or does putting it there first make the case land as a rigged demonstration rather than a discovery? What's the one scenario the whole thing should be built around, and what does that scenario have to have and have to not have? Right now I'm using a thought experiment I made up: a pill that switches off the minding while leaving the nerves firing exactly as they were before, and I already suspect it's smuggling in something — tell me what, and whether the real cases can carry the weight instead.

Then the misconception map, which is the part I actually need. The ones they'll walk in with and the ones they'll manufacture out of what I say. I'm expecting "so the pain was never really there" and "that's the same as being unable to feel pain at all," but the one that keeps me up is the nurses going home thinking I've argued that suffering is optional and a matter of attitude — which in that room, with that woman, would be close to unforgivable. Where in the sequence does each of those get headed off, exactly, before it forms rather than after?

And tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — the brain plumbing, the argument about whether these people are still being harmed, whatever else — and how to say I'm skipping it without sounding like I'm hiding something. Finish with one question I can ask at the end that separates people who actually got the distinction from people who just found the story creepy. No reading list, I won't get through one before Tuesday.
