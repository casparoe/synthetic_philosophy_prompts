I am a high school junior in an independent research seminar, and my semester project concerns how international organizations measure national well-being. I have been working through the World Happiness Report's methodology materials, including the FAQ in which the editors answer objections, and I have run into a philosophical puzzle I cannot resolve on my own. I have not studied theories of well-being formally, so please define any technical term the first time you use it, and please write your answer in American English. I am not looking for sources or a bibliography, since I have the documents in front of me; I would instead like you to distinguish, throughout, which of your claims are things the report actually says and which are inferences you are drawing about what its practice assumes.

Here is the practice, as precisely as I can state it. The annual ranking rests on a single survey item, the ladder question, in which respondents imagine a ladder with steps numbered from 0 at the bottom to 10 at the top. In the FAQ's own wording: "The top of the ladder represents the best possible life for you and the bottom of the ladder represents the worst possible life for you." National scores pool responses over three years. The editors explain that they deliberately did not build a weighted index out of many indicators, because one question lets people apply their own sense of what matters most regardless of their culture or background. They also explain that they prefer this life-evaluation measure to measures of daily positive and negative emotion, on the grounds that evaluations vary more between countries and are better explained by people's life circumstances, whereas emotions largely reflect the events of the day asked about. Elsewhere the FAQ states that "the ultimate good is the wellbeing of present and future generations," and that other goods such as health, wealth, and relationships are good "because they help us to feel good." Meanwhile, the chapter presenting the rankings explains each country's score by reference to six variables — GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption — benchmarking every country against a hypothetical country assembled from the lowest values of those variables, which the report calls Dystopia, and reporting whatever is left over as a residual. Those six variables do not enter the ranking score itself.

Please reconstruct the theory of well-being this arrangement presupposes, and assess whether its parts presuppose the same one. In particular:

1. For the ranking to mean what readers take it to mean — a comparison of how well people's lives are actually going — what has to be true about the relationship between a person's considered evaluation of their life and their well-being? Is the practice committed to a mental-state theory, a judgment or preference-satisfaction theory, an objective-list theory, or a hybrid? The FAQ's stated value theory sounds experiential, but the thing measured is a reflective judgment rather than a feeling, and the editors explicitly prefer the judgment to the feelings. Is that judgment doing constitutive work (well-being consists in, or is fixed by, what people judge) or evidential work (judgments are the best available evidence of a good that exists independently)? Do the rankings survive equally well under both readings, or does one of them make the enterprise unintelligible?

2. Because the ladder's endpoints are defined relative to each respondent's own best possible life, the same number may express different standards in different people and places. What assumption must the ranking make to treat the numbers as comparable across countries, and is that assumption a claim about well-being or a claim about survey response behavior? Then consider the direction in which it might fail: if development, schooling, and wider horizons raise people's sense of what the best possible life for them could be, a country could grow richer, healthier, and freer while its average ladder score falls. Given that the editors justify preferring evaluations precisely because they track circumstances, can the practice dismiss that drop as an artifact, or is it committed to calling it a real decline in happiness? What does each answer cost it?

3. Naming the low benchmark Dystopia and explaining scores through six selected variables looks like an objective list, even though the report's reason for using one question was to avoid imposing one. Is that six-variable apparatus merely explanatory, treating those things as causes of well-being, or does it smuggle in a constitutive claim that they are components of well-being? And what does the Dystopia label presuppose about the residual — that it names a kind of badness, or only unmodeled variance?

4. Which rival views of well-being does this practice quietly rule out, and which is it merely compatible with while sitting awkwardly beside them? I have in mind views on which outsiders can assess a life against standards the person living it does not endorse, views on which well-being is not a single quantity that can be averaged across persons, and views on which well-being is a way of living rather than a state to be rated. For each, say whether the practice excludes it or tolerates it, and say what would concretely have to change — the wording of the question, the pooling and averaging, the decision to publish a ranking at all, or how governments respond to it — if the presupposed view were replaced.

5. Finally, what does publishing this as a ranking presuppose about the purpose of measuring well-being? For "move our country up the list" to be a coherent policy aim, what would a government have to believe about the nature of well-being and about whose job it is to promote it — and does the practice give a government any reason to work on people's evaluations rather than on their lives?

Please be candid about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license exactly what the report does. I would rather learn that than have a single reading forced onto the material.
