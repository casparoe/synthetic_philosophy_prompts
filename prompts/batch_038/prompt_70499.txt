You are an expert in incentive and measurement design who has run a great many experiential workshops for professional analysts, and you are also a careful philosopher of social science. Before writing anything, work through the mechanics privately: run the arithmetic on your own design and confirm that the numbers really do produce the effect I describe below, and adjust the parameters until they do. Then write up only the finished design.

**Context.** Once a year I train the incoming cohort at a municipal Office of Performance Management: 25 analysts who will spend their careers building KPI dashboards for city agencies. Mixed background — some statistics, some longtime program staff, no philosophy. Two hours. A room with five tables of five, two whiteboards, paper, pens, several decks of index cards, and dice. No participant laptops, no real money, and I want to run it exactly as written without improvising a single rule.

**What I want them to feel.** Not that metrics can be gamed — they already believe that, and it is the least interesting thing about the topic. I want them to feel that a metric can be honest, unbiased, well-validated on historical data, and defended by people with perfectly aligned incentives, and still fail catastrophically the moment real optimization pressure is applied to it. At the reveal I want the room's dominant reaction to be that the metric in the game was a *good* metric, and that this is precisely what is alarming. I also want each of them to have personally chosen the more aggressive threshold at some point, and to notice that they would choose it again.

**The conceptual spine.** Build the activity on four distinct mechanisms by which a proxy and its underlying goal come apart: (i) a *regressional* mechanism, where the proxy is an unbiased but noisy measure of the goal, so selecting hard on the proxy also selects for the noise, and the gap widens in the tails; (ii) an *extremal* mechanism, where pushing the system into a regime in which the historically observed proxy–goal relationship simply no longer holds; (iii) a *causal* mechanism, where the intervention that raises the proxy itself interferes with the goal; and (iv) an *adversarial* mechanism, where an agent with different aims exploits the gap deliberately. Your activity must instantiate at least three of these in a way the participants can afterwards sort into the four boxes. Do not use these labels, the term Goodhart, or the word proxy in anything participants read or hear before the final reveal — they should hit the mechanisms first and receive the vocabulary afterwards.

**Structural requirements.**
- A cover story: participants are told the session is about resource allocation under uncertainty. They must not suspect it is a lesson about measurement.
- One segment in which every participant behaves honestly and competently and the goal still deteriorates, and a separate segment in which someone exploits the measure deliberately. In the moment, the two must feel identical.
- The measured indicator must rise throughout, visibly, with a public running tally on the whiteboard, so that the room has cause to feel it is succeeding.
- A structurally blind role: a player who sees only the indicator, is fully rewarded for raising it, and has no access to the goal.
- Everything must be reproducible with cards and dice. Specify a fallback in case the random draws refuse to cooperate on the day.
- Payoffs without money: individual standing tied to the indicator, and a shared group outcome tied to the goal, revealed only at the end and attached to a small real consequence you propose.
- A mid-activity decision point at which tables can tighten their selection threshold, with most of them expected to do so.

9. A frank statement of which feature of the phenomenon this activity cannot reproduce, and what I must supply verbally to cover the gap.
