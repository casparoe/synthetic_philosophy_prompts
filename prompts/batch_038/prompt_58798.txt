I have just taken over as chair of our museum's acquisitions and attributions committee, and I need to write the memo on the panel described at the end. First, the committee's record over the past decade, as I have reconstructed it from the minutes:

1. A seventeenth-century Dutch panel exposed as a forgery painted in the 1930s on a period canvas with period pigments. Our own curators rated it as good as anything in the Dutch gallery. The committee struck it from that gallery, retained it in the collection, hung it in an exhibition on forgery labelled as a 1930s work, and wrote its value down to a small fraction of the purchase price.
2. A Renaissance altarpiece: the master's design, much of the execution by assistants, his hand visible in the faces and drapery highlights. Catalogued as "master and workshop," hung in the main gallery, insured near full attribution value.
3. A bronze cast from a sculptor's own mould, pulled by her contracted foundry after her death, within the numbered edition and expressly authorised in her will. Catalogued and displayed as a work by the sculptor, on the same footing as the lifetime casts.
4. A medieval panel of which roughly 85% of the paint surface was laid down in a documented nineteenth-century campaign; original ground, underdrawing and a few passages survive. Attribution retained, but the committee refused a loan whose label would have called the panel intact, and our wall text gives the restoration percentage.
5. A violin from a celebrated eighteenth-century workshop with replaced top, neck, bass bar and varnish, in unbroken use under its original name for two centuries. Accepted as a bequest, catalogued under the original maker, every replacement noted in the condition report.
6. A large contemporary installation fabricated entirely by a workshop from the artist's drawings and instructions, then assembled and signed by her. Treated as autograph; the committee rejected a proposal to label it "after the artist."
7. A drawing bought as a study by a famous master, which scholarship now assigns to his brother working in the same household, unsigned, with no evidence that anyone intended a deception. The committee declined the famous attribution, catalogued and displayed it under the brother's name, and held it at the brother's market level.

The new case. In 1926 a student copyist, under an open commercial commission, painted a full-size copy of a famous altarpiece for a provincial church; she signed her own name on the reverse and the commission stamped COPY on the stretcher. The original altarpiece burned in 1948 and survives only heavily altered and overpainted, so her copy is now the best record of what it looked like. In the 1990s a dealer scraped off the stamp, doctored the support, invented a provenance, and sold it abroad as a lost autograph work; the fraud has since been exposed, the dealer convicted, and the panel has come to us as a donation with the fraudulent paperwork intact in the file. Our conservators judge it the finest thing in the copyist's small known oeuvre. Her family asks that it hang under her name.

Please write the memo. I want: the candidate principles jointly consistent with all seven precedents, including ones the committee has never articulated; what each implies for the new panel on three separate questions — whether we acquire it, under whose name it is catalogued, and where it hangs; a single best guess at how the committee will vote, with the reason for preferring that principle to its rivals; which precedent is the closest analogue and whether it genuinely decides the case; and one further hypothetical case that would tell the leading principles apart. Where a choice is underdetermined by our record, say so plainly and state what principle each option would commit us to.

Finally, address one suspicion of mine: that the record is not tracking a single property but two — what a work is worth, and where it may hang and under whose name — and that the committee has been sliding between them. If you agree, identify which verdicts force the split.
