I am reading a widely sold popular history of physics, one chapter of which devotes a couple of pages to Newton's bucket. The relevant passage runs as follows:

"Newton's bucket is supposed to prove that absolute space exists. Hang a bucket of water by a twisted rope and let it unwind. The surface is flat precisely when the water is moving relative to the bucket and concave precisely when it is at rest relative to it, so the curvature cannot be caused by motion relative to anything nearby: it must be caused by motion relative to space itself. Newton therefore concluded that space is a real container, existing whether or not anything is in it, and that rotation is absolute even though uniform motion is not. Mach exposed the flaw two centuries later: the water is really rotating relative to the distant stars, and if the bucket's walls were miles thick their mass would produce the curvature. Einstein adopted this insight. General relativity makes the geometry of spacetime a dynamical thing produced by the matter in it, and satellites have now measured the Earth dragging spacetime around as it turns. The verdict of modern physics is therefore clear: substantivalism was a gratuitous metaphysical add-on, and physics eventually came over to the relationists, just as it earlier dispensed with the luminiferous ether."

For what it is worth, I find the relationist conclusion attractive, which is part of why I want to know whether the case made for it here is any good. Could you give me an accuracy audit? Please work through the passage claim by claim rather than in summary, and for each element distinguish what a philosopher of physics or a historian of the period would accept as stated, what is a defensible popular simplification, and what is a substantive misstatement. Four points I would especially like you to address. First, the passage moves from the observation that rotation is detectable to the claim that Newton took rotation alone to be absolute: is that his position, or does it collapse a distinction he was at pains to preserve? Second, is the description of the experiment itself accurate, including the stated correlation between the water's motion relative to the bucket and the shape of its surface? Third, does the summary of Mach represent what he actually claimed, or does it convert something more tentative into a positive mechanism? Fourth, does general relativity settle the matter in the way described — in particular, does making the metric field dynamical amount to a vindication of relationism, or could a contemporary substantivalist argue that it cuts the other way — and is the closing analogy with the elimination of the ether apt or misleading?
