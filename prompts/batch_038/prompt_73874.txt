Ok so this blew up at our lab's reading-group-ish thing last week and I still dont know who's right. Two senior people, I'll call them R and M. The context: we were hashing out whether to sign off on a model for a deployment where it does something like intake triage — decides which cases get escalated to a human and which get auto-closed. Not life or death, but real consequences for the people on the wrong end of it.

R opened by saying the eval bundle settles it: the model makes the correct escalation call on ~97% of the combined red-team plus production-mixture set, and expected harm across the traffic it'll actually see is the only quantity we can bound anyway. Ship it, because the status quo is human triagers who are measurably worse and much slower.

M said that's the wrong object entirely. The 97% is an average over a distribution we picked, and averaging is exactly what breaks when the failure isn't "slightly wrong" but "wrong in a correlated way" — some region of input space where the model systematically escalates everything, or systematically closes everything. What you'd want instead is something worst-case-shaped: no reachable input region where behaviour degrades past some stated bound.

R: worst-case guarantees are unobtainable in practice, and if M applied that standard consistently nothing would ever ship — including the hospitals own triage software, which was validated statistically and nobody blinked at it.

M: disanalogy. A queueing system or a brake controller fails one unit at a time, roughly independently. A deployed model is a single artefact copied across every instance, so any tail is a shared tail — if some input pattern breaks it, it breaks everywhere at once, and there are motivated actors actively hunting for that pattern. Independence is what licenses averaging, and you don't have it here.

R: fine, grant that. But then the right quantity still isn't the worst case, it's expected harm under the distribution of adversaries and inputs that actually exist in the wild. Against an unbounded adversary every guarantee is vacuous — you can always cook up another prompt. So "worst case" isn't doing any methodological work, it's just encoding that you're more risk-averse than me.

M: it does do work, it shifts the burden. Average-case thinking asks "have we observed enough failures to be worried yet?" Worst-case thinking asks "can you exhibit a reason this failure mode can't occur?" Those are different epistemic demands, not different appetites.

R: which is a prior about which errors are worse, wearing a methodology costume.
