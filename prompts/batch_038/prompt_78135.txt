I am preparing a handout for a social ontology seminar in which I set out my own position on conventions, and I would like a consistency audit before I circulate it. Here is what I hold.

1. Lewis's analysis in *Convention* is basically right: a convention is a regularity in behavior, in a recurrent situation, sustained by the parties' own preferences and expectations, and it satisfies his six conditions — conformity; expectation of conformity by others; roughly similar preferences over combinations of actions; a conditional preference that others conform given that almost everyone does; a parallel conditional preference for some alternative regularity; and common knowledge of all of the foregoing.

2. Conventions are arbitrary in Lewis's sense: for any convention there is an alternative regularity that would have served nearly as well, and nothing intrinsic to the one in force recommends it.

3. Conventions are solutions to coordination problems — situations of largely coincident interest. A regularity sustained where each party would gain by defecting given everyone else's conformity is not a convention at all, but a moral or legal norm, and I want to keep those categories apart.

4. My paradigm examples nonetheless include the rota in my department by which whoever makes the last of the coffee cleans the pot, and the standing practice in my building of leaving umbrellas in the unlocked hall stand. In the second case, taking one is treated by residents as wrong rather than merely inexpedient, including by people who have never met the taker and lose nothing themselves.

5. Common knowledge does no explanatory work beyond first-order expectation: like Sugden, I think an agent can follow a convention by reasoning from her own experience of it, and the infinite hierarchy is decoration.

6. Conventions characteristically emerge and persist through imitation and selection rather than through anyone's deliberation; Skyrms-style dynamics account for the same phenomena without attributing the relevant concepts to the agents.

7. Lewis's central application — a language as a convention of truthfulness and trust in a population — is not viciously circular, despite the account's need for common knowledge.

Which of these genuinely conflict, which merely appear to, and where is the cheapest repair? Please rank the conflicts by severity rather than treating them alike; for each, say what would have to be given up and what that costs me. I am also interested in the pairs you judge to be compatible: say why, since I cannot tell from the inside which of my worries are real. Do not assume I would rather abandon any particular item.
