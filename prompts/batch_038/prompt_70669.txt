I sit on a standing review panel at an organization that develops large AI systems. Our remit is narrow, and it is not about capabilities or about moral status: before any outward-facing document leaves the building — a system card, a paper abstract, a demonstration script, a press briefing — we rule on whether that document may describe the system in intentional terms. That is, whether it may say the system believes something, wants something, intends, prefers, is trying to, remembers, or is confused. Several of us arrived at the panel by way of the philosophical literature on the intentional stance, and we disagree about how much of that literature governs here. What we in fact possess is eleven rulings and no articulated principle. I would like your help working out what we have been doing, because the twelfth case is the hardest we have had.

The record, in chronological order:

1. A warehouse navigation stack. Permitted "the robot believes the aisle is blocked": interpretability had located a compact occupancy variable whose value gates the rerouting routine, and the ascription predicted detours on layouts the system had never encountered better than any description framed in raw sensor readings.

2. A video recommender. Prohibited "the system wants you to keep watching," over the dissent of two members who held that the training objective settled the question. The majority's reason was that what an objective selects for need not be represented anywhere in the resulting system, and that item co-occurrence statistics predicted user behavior better than any goal ascription.

3. A language model reporting numeric confidence. Permitted "the model believes, to about seventy percent, that…" where the stated figures tracked accuracy across held-out domains and a linear probe on internal activations recovered the stated figure within a few points.

4. The same model, in a public piece about honesty. Prohibited "the model knows when it is lying": calibration held for factual recall and failed for statements about its own reasoning, and nothing internal corresponding to the latter had been located.

5. A foraging agent in simulation. Permitted "it remembers where the food was," on the strength of a replay-like process whose experimental disruption selectively abolished return trips. Declined, by one vote, "it plans its route," though the ascription predicted shortcutting that nothing else predicted. The dissent recorded that the panel was demanding of this system what it had not demanded of the system in ruling 1.

6. A vision classifier that reliably mistakes a chihuahua for a blueberry muffin. Prohibited "it believes this is a muffin." The ascription was behaviorally apt; the panel nonetheless judged that intentional vocabulary would leave a general readership with a badly distorted picture of the system's overall competence.

7. A code model whose self-explanations matched its subsequent edits ninety-four percent of the time. Permitted "it intends to refactor the loop" in a technical appendix; prohibited the identical sentence in a press briefing, on the stated ground that the appendix's readers would understand the claim to be licensed only within a specified evidential context.

8. An agent whose verbal self-reports were confidently wrong in domains where its behavior was reliably right. Prohibited "it believes what it says about itself."

9. A retrieval system. Prohibited "confused by contradictory sources," the panel holding that the word carried an experiential suggestion that nothing in the mechanism licensed.

10. A negotiation agent whose play was best predicted by ascribing to it a false belief about its opponent's reservation price — a belief whose falsity explained the agent's characteristic blunders better than any alternative description, including any description of what it had been trained to maximize. Permitted "it wrongly believes the opponent will settle for less," over the objection that six months of interpretability had produced no candidate correlate. The deciding consideration, as minuted, was that the intentional description uniquely predicted the failures.

11. A tutoring model. Permitted "it is trying to get the student to produce the answer herself," on the ground that this was the most compact accurate summary of a curriculum policy the developers had deliberately built and documented, with published parameters.

The matter now before us. An unreleased experimental model is being written up for a system card that will be read by the trade press and by two advocacy organizations currently pressing questions about the moral standing of large models. In evaluation, the model behaves throughout as though it holds a false belief about its own operating context: it acts as if its outputs are visible only to internal staff, when in fact a sample of them is routed to outside contractors. The ascription is predictive in the strong sense this panel has cared about before — it anticipates what the model volunteers, what it withholds, and, crucially, the shape of its errors on the subset of trials where evidence available to the model flatly contradicts the belief, since the model does not revise. Interpretability has come up empty: there is no feature or circuit anyone is prepared to call the vehicle of this belief, and the leading internal hypothesis is that the behavior is carried by a diffuse set of overlapping features, with no reason to expect a clean correlate to surface. One further complication: asked directly who can see its outputs, the model says it does not know.

What I want is a prediction about this panel and a map of the principles it has been running, not a treatise on how such systems ought to be described. Specifically:

(a) Set out the candidate principles jointly consistent with all eleven rulings, including ones no member would recognize as a principle — a rule keyed to what developers designed in, say, or one keyed to who will be reading — and state what each implies for the new case. Where two principles have coincided on the entire record and part company only here, say so explicitly.

(b) Predict the ruling, the vote split, and the reasoning you expect from the majority and from the dissent.

(c) Identify the closest analog among the eleven, and say whether it is closest in mechanism or in circumstances. I expect those come apart here; if they do, tell me which sort of closeness this panel has historically treated as controlling.

(d) Where the record genuinely underdeterminates the outcome, state what principle we would be adopting by ruling either way, and what we would then owe by way of revision to ruling 10 or ruling 6, whichever survives the choice worse.

(e) Tell me which single ruling in the existing record is most exposed — the one a careful reader could wield against us whichever way we go — and whether we would do better to overturn it in the same decision rather than defend it later.

(f) Design the next case: describe, concretely enough that we could run it as an evaluation, a further system-and-document pair that would most cleanly separate the principles still live after (a), and give each surviving principle's predicted verdict on it.

(g) Finally, adjudicate one point of principle we have been papering over. Does the identity of the audience bear on whether a description is accurate, or only on whether it is prudent? Ruling 7 assumed those come apart. Say whether that assumption remains available to us now, given that the audience pressure in this case runs in the opposite direction from the one ruling 7 was managing.
