I'm a philosophy-of-biology PhD student spending the year embedded in a systematics lab. The lab is revising a genus of montane herbs — sexual diploids, a handful of obligately apomictic lineages, several contact zones — and the monograph has to be internally consistent with the delimitation decisions my co-advisor, the empirical half of the pairing, has already published. His verdicts, as I have them from the lab files:

1. Three populations arrayed around an arid basin. Each adjacent pair exchanges pollen where they meet; the two terminal populations, where they finally come into contact on the north side, yield no hybrids in 200 plants scored. He keeps the whole ring as one species.
2. Three apomictic lineages growing side by side with their sexual progenitor, each fixed-different at several nuclear loci and each confined to a distinct substrate (scree, seep, meadow), with no gene flow possible between any of them. He recognizes all three as species.
3. Two forms sharing a valley, three weeks apart in flowering and served by different pollinators, with 12% hybrids along a 400 m transect but each holding a tight cluster at nine nuclear loci. Two species.
4. Two well-differentiated taxa joined by a hybrid zone hundreds of kilometres wide: steep clines at every locus, no clusters anywhere, alleles moving freely. One species.
5. An autotetraploid lineage that has arisen at least four separate times from the same diploid parent within the last few centuries, instantly isolated from it by chromosome number, morphologically intermediate, restricted to disturbed ground. One species — all four origins folded into a single taxon, polyphyly notwithstanding.
6. Two sky-island populations separated by roughly a million years, ecologically interchangeable so far as anyone has tested, with fixed differences at three nuclear loci and reciprocal monophyly. He splits them, and does so without having attempted crosses.

The seventh case is mine to write up. A single ridge carries a narrow endemic that is diagnosable against its lowland relative (fixed differences at six of fifteen nuclear loci, reciprocal monophyly), grows on a different substrate, and flowers two weeks earlier. Until about thirty years ago the two were separated by an uncultivated lowland stretch; irrigation removed it. Since contact they have hybridized freely: F1s and backcrosses fully fertile, no clustering left anywhere in the contact zone after three generations, the endemic's diagnostic alleles down to 10–20% frequency, and a demographic model projecting complete assimilation within roughly ten more generations. Crosses on the few remaining pure ridge plants show no intrinsic barrier of any kind. The isolation was always the gap.

Predict his verdict, and give me the reasoning rather than just the label. Specifically: which candidate rules are compatible with all six published verdicts, and which of the familiar species concepts, if any, does each correspond to — I expect at least two of them to fit everything up to now, so don't collapse them prematurely. Where those rules diverge on the ridge population, and which of them his own precedents, as opposed to his temperament, actually favour. Whether the answer shifts depending on whether delimitation is read synchronically or diachronically, and whether a revision can avoid committing to one of those readings. A listing petition turns on the verdict, so tell me what we should recommend if the goal is coherence with his own six decisions, which of the six is the closest analog, and what principle we would be adopting whichever way we go. Finally, name the one further case that would discriminate between the candidate rules, and say what result on it would settle the matter.
