I have unexpectedly inherited a second-year course, "Minds, Machines and Doubt," cross-listed between our philosophy department and the computer science school, and I am writing its final examination for the first time this term. The cohort is about 140 students, and the computing contingent is large enough that week five's seminars drifted toward rendering pipelines and floating-point limits rather than toward the inference itself. The department's regulations are unhelpfully precise: the paper must contain one essay question worth 25 marks, answerable in 45 minutes, closed book, and — after last year's external examiner objected that the questions rewarded reliable recall of a named argument rather than any understanding of it — my head of department has asked that each stem carry all the material a student needs, so that no outside reading is required or rewarded. My topic is the simulation argument, and my difficulty is that I wrote a credulously enthusiastic undergraduate essay on precisely this material; marking this term's formative work has shown me the two responses I feared. Roughly two thirds of the cohort writes some version of the claim that because we cannot rule out being simulated we probably are, treating the impossibility of detecting a difference as itself a reason for the conclusion and never noticing that something further is needed to travel from a claim about proportions of observers to a claim about this particular observer. Another fifth writes that the hypothesis is unfalsifiable and therefore not a real question, then stops — apparently unaware that this concedes the premise the argument most depends on. A handful get further, but no one has yet put a finger on the weak joint.

So please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme. The crux I want is the step from "most people are simulated" to "you are probably simulated": the step that silently requires a principle about self-locating belief, and whose choice of reference class ends up doing all the work. I want the stem to hand the students a compact numbered version of the argument — computing capacity in a mature civilization grows without limit; such a civilization will run many detailed simulations of its own past; each simulation contains vastly more conscious people than the original history did; from inside, a simulated person cannot tell which kind of person they are; therefore you are almost certainly simulated — and then to complicate it with a stipulated twist that has to be reasoned with rather than waved away. The twist I have in mind: suppose the question stipulates that the simulating civilization runs its simulations lazily, so that background people are instantiated only while someone is attending to them and otherwise are not conscious beings at all, in the manner of a game engine declining to draw what is off-screen. The students should be asked what this does to the argument — whether it renders the argument unsound, or merely shifts the weight onto an assumption about the economics of simulation to which we have no internal access — and whether the stipulation is even coherent given the purpose the simulators are supposed to have in running the thing at all, since a civilization that wanted a faithful history would need exactly the population the stipulation deletes.

For the marking scheme, give me five named moves whose marks sum to 25, and under each a one-line note on the characteristic way a competent but unexcellent answer mishandles it. It should say plainly what separates an A from a B: as I read this year's work, a B answer correctly identifies that some sampling principle is required and then treats the lazy-rendering twist as a straightforward refutation, whereas an A answer sees that the twist changes which premise is load-bearing without by itself disposing of the conclusion, and can say why. Please build in that no marks are available for naming a philosopher, a paper, or a label, and that the unfalsifiability line earns nothing unless accompanied by a reason why it blocks this specific inference rather than any inference about the external world. Keep the model answer free of citations as well; it should read as though written by a very strong student working only from the stem, and it should resist the temptation to end by announcing that the question is unanswerable — my students need to see what a finished piece of reasoning about the argument actually looks like, not a licence to stop early.
