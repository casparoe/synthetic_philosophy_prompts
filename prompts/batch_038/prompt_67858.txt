My flatmate's got a dissertation chapter that's supposed to kill off imprecise credences and I can't find where it breaks. Roughly, in his numbering:

(1) Your credal state is a set of probability functions, and your credence in p is imprecise just in case the values {P(p): P in the set} have non-zero width.

(2) Learning is narrowing. You learn from evidence E only if, after conditionalising the whole set on E, the width of your set for the hypothesis you care about is strictly smaller than it was before. He calls this the Narrowing Criterion and thinks it's just what "learning" amounts to, not a substantive claim.

(3) If your prior set is the totally vacuous one — every probability function on the algebra — then for any finite E, the conditionalised set still has width 1 for anything E doesnt entail.

(4) So an agent with a vacuous prior never learns anything about the next toss, the next raven, the next anything, however much data piles up.

(5) And vacuity is the imprecise Bayesian's own default rather than a strawman, because of what he calls the Fidelity Principle: your credal width has to match the specificity of your evidence, so evidence that's silent about a coin's bias obliges you to sit at width 1 about the next toss.

(6) Hence the imprecise Bayesian is committed to agents for whom evidence makes literally no difference.

(7) A theory on which more evidence never changes your state isn't a theory of learning, it's a refusal to have one.

(8) So imprecise credences are never rationally required, and precision wins by default.

He thinks (5) is the load-bearing one and says he'll happily defend it. I want to keep (1) — I think the set-of-priors picture is right, and it's what makes things like dilation and credal inertia worth worrying about in the first place — and I'd also like to keep (7), since I agree that a learning theory that never learns is worthless.

So: which step actually fails, and why did it look fine going in?
