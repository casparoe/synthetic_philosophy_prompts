I chair an interdisciplinary team assembling a public database of gene-product annotations for a newly sequenced extremophile. My collaborator, a molecular biologist, and I have reached an impasse over what we are permitted to write in the column headed "function."

Her working rule is purely dispositional: if purifying the protein and putting it in a test tube shows that it accelerates some reaction, or if removing it changes some measurable capacity of the organism, then that is a function, and the database should say so. She is indifferent to where the gene came from. I keep objecting that this will license entries that are true of the machinery but false of the organism's economy — capacities that exist without being exercised, side effects that no one would call what the item is for, and ancestral leftovers that now do nothing worth reporting. My preferred rule is historical: an item has the function it does because of what its ancestors were retained for, and we should record that, not merely what the item can be coaxed into doing.

Three entries have brought matters to a head:

1. A duplicate of a heat-shock gene retains weak activity under our assays, but knockouts of the duplicate are indistinguishable from wild type under every condition we have tested, and the population-genetic data suggest the duplicate has been drifting neutrally since the duplication event.
2. A gene acquired by lateral transfer, whose ancestors were retained in the donor lineage for a purpose we can identify fairly confidently, but whose product in our organism currently shows only faint traces of that activity and is robustly expressed in a different tissue.
3. A structural protein that reliably binds a certain heavy metal at concentrations the organism never encounters in its habitat, an activity that is reproducible in vitro and that my collaborator wishes to record as a detoxification function.

Our funder's guidelines state that the resource must remain neutral on theoretical disputes and should describe only what the systems do, which my collaborator cites as settling the matter in her favor.

Please advise me on how to resolve this. I would like you to begin with a brief statement of your recommendation, and then continue under the following four headings:

1. The Dispute Beneath the Disagreement — around 250 words identifying, with their standard names and lineage, the two conceptions my collaborator and I have been gesturing at, and saying why the funder's appeal to neutrality does not in fact leave the question open.
2. The Three Entries — around 500 words taking the cases in order, giving each conception's verdict, and explaining why the verdicts come apart in each case rather than merely differing in emphasis.
3. The Strongest Case Against My Position — around 300 words pressing the best objections available to my collaborator, including any known thought experiments or empirical results that tell against a purely historical rule.
4. A Working Compromise — around 300 words proposing a concrete annotation policy that a mixed committee could actually adopt, and stating what my collaborator and I must each give up to live with it.
