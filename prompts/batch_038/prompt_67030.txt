I run a grad seminar on accuracy-first epistemology and last week the students built an argument I can't kill — partly because I half-believe it. The setup is the standard one: finite opinion space, Brier score, so the rule is strictly proper and every probability function expects itself, and only itself, to be the most accurate. Here's what they handed me, roughly in their words.

(1) Epistemic rationality is nothing over and above accuracy. A doxastic state is permissible just in case it isn't accuracy-undermining in the sense spelled out at (2), and nothing else — coherence for its own sake, evidential fit, epistemic humility — is separately relevant to whether it's allowed.

(2) A doxastic state is impermissible iff some alternative available to the agent is guaranteed to be at least as accurate come what may and strictly more accurate in at least one world.

(3) So every non-probabilistic credence function is impermissible, since some probability function dominates it. Fine, no complaint there.

(4) Now extend the scoring rule to imprecise states. We scored a credal set by its least accurate member, on the grounds that a set is only as good as it's worst part. Under that convention any set with more than one member is dominated by one of its own members. So imprecise credences are never permissible — not even in the ambiguous-evidence cases where everyone thinks they obviously are.

(5) Finally, take any coherent credence function c that isn't the one your evidence recommends; call that one c_E. There's an alternative available to you, namely c_E, that you expect to be strictly more accurate than c.

(6) So by (2) and (5), exactly one credence function is permissible for you given your evidence, and by (4) it has to be precise.

(7) Which also means rational peers can never rationally disagree, and that no rational agent is ever uncertain about what her own evidence is, since (5) applies again one level up.

(7) is obviously too strong, and (4) makes me queasy, but I can't locate the step that's actually broken — every line reads like something I'd have signed off on in September. So: which premise or inference do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? For what it's worth, my own sympathies are that I'd much rather give up the scoring convention in (4) than give up (1) or (2), but I suspect I can't have both, and I want to know which of them is doing the load-bearing work.

Give me the diagnosis step by step, and then tell me what would change your mind about it: which of these lines you'd defend if a student pushed you hard, and where you think your own reading of the argument is most likely to be off.
