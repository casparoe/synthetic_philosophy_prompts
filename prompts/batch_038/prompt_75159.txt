I sit on a working group at a UK university's museums service, advising on claims for the return of objects taken from the royal palace at Benin City in 1897. Across our last two meetings the argument has been carried almost entirely by two analogies, and I would like both audited rather than adjudicated by instinct.

The first was offered by a colleague pressing for return. A plaque from the Oba's palace is stolen property; the museum's position is that of someone found in possession of a bicycle taken from your shed decades ago. Once the taking is granted, he argued, everything else — limitation periods, the good faith of later purchasers, the absence of a claimant at the time — is special pleading that the ordinary law of theft and conversion already knows how to discount.

The second was offered by a colleague resisting it. The museum is not an owner but a trustee: it holds the collection in custody for the public, present and future, which is why disposal is restricted by statute (section 3(4) of the British Museum Act 1963) and why returns must be negotiated rather than simply handed over.

For each analogy, set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of owner, taker, purchaser, remedy, beneficiary class — and then separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the use it was put to from those that are merely decorative. I am particularly interested in what the trustee analogy costs its user: a trustee who acquires the property by wrong stands in a very different relation to it from one who receives it from a settlor, and it is not obvious who the settlor or the beneficiaries are here, or whether a trust so constituted is anything a lawyer would recognise. Say also what follows from the fact that both analogies borrow from the same body of law, and pull in opposite directions from it.

Then tell me which conclusion, if either, survives. Two further matters. First, when we turned to ancestral human remains — where UK national museums hold a specific statutory power of transfer under section 47 of the Human Tissue Act 2004, limited to remains of persons who died within the preceding thousand years, and where discussion proceeded almost entirely outside the vocabulary of ownership — the property framing became much harder to sustain. Explain what that contrast shows about whether the theft analogy was ever doing the argumentative work. Second, if neither analogy can carry the weight placed on it, propose a better one and state precisely what it licenses and what it refuses to.

A table of correspondences is welcome where it clarifies. Please write throughout in British English.
