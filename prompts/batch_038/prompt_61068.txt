I audited a departmental methods workshop last month on quantifying long-run historical dynamics, and during the discussion period two of the participants — Nadia, a quantitative sociologist, and Emil, an economic historian — got into a dispute about the cliodynamic research programme, specifically the structural-demographic model of political instability and the famous 2010 forecast of a decade of rising instability in the United States and Western Europe. I did not take part and I have no stake in either side, but I found the exchange unusually well-constructed and I could not decide who had the better of it. It went roughly as follows.

(1) Nadia opened by saying the curve-fitting charge against cliodynamics has been answered empirically: the model was calibrated on data ending before 2000, the forecast was published in 2010, and the decade that followed delivered the predicted deterioration — mass protest waves, an assault on a legislature, historically low institutional trust. Retrodiction is cheap, she said; this was a risked, dated, ex ante call, and it landed.

(2) Emil replied that "it landed" does a lot of unearned work. The forecast specified no magnitude, no error bar, no scoring rule, and no condition under which the authors would have conceded failure. A claim that a large, politically strained polity will experience "growing instability" at some point over ten years has a very high prior probability of being true regardless of the model. Worse, the outcome variable is a constructed index of events — riots, assassinations, strikes, terror incidents — whose coding decisions were made by people already committed to the theory, so part of the confirmation lives in the operationalization.

(3) Nadia said that if constructed indices disqualify a forecast, then nothing in historical social science predicts anything: GDP, unemployment, and mortality series are all constructed too. And the leading indicators — popular immiseration in real wages, elite overproduction measured as the number of aspirants per available elite position (proxied in the American case by the growth of professional and advanced degrees relative to high-status openings, and by top-end income shares), and state fiscal strain measured in public debt — were all measurable in 2010 from data that predated the outcome. The mechanism variables were not reverse-engineered from the event, she said, and the same index and the same model were used across many cases: eighteenth-century France, imperial Rome, Ming China, Russia before 1917, the antebellum United States. A single consistent apparatus applied to a dozen polities cannot be tuned to any one of them.

(4) Emil said she had quietly swapped in a stronger claim — cross-case validity — to defend a weaker one. The number of genuinely independent long-run cases is small, and each case carries its own adjustable elements: where the cycle begins, how long its integrative and disintegrative phases run, how the nested roughly fifty-year oscillation is aligned inside a two- or three-century cycle. With several dials per case and a handful of cases, fit quality is uninformative. He added that turning points can only be identified decades after the fact, so on exactly the historical cases where the theory can be checked, it is checked at a lag that no living researcher can be held to.

(5) Nadia said the relevant comparison is with the alternative, not with an ideal: a conventional regression with twenty controls estimated on a couple of hundred observations has far worse parameters-per-datapum than a three-state-variable mechanistic model fit to two centuries of annual data. And the model is not merely "instability has many causes." It commits to a conjunction — instability should spike when elite numbers are high *and* state finances are strained *and* popular living standards are falling, and not from any one alone. A mechanism that can be wrong in specific configurations is falsifiable in a way a factor list is not.

(6) Emil's last move was that this relocates the problem rather than solving it. He asked her to name an observation the programme is committed to treating as a failure. When a forecast comes true it is counted as confirmation, he said; when a polity with heavy elite overproduction stays calm for thirty years, the response is a missing variable, an unmodelled institution, or a phase lag. A framework that absorbs both outcomes by adding a term has not been tested by either, and the 2010 success — real as it may be — does not by itself establish that the programme risks anything.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the six, give a rating on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify the rating in a few sentences, attending to the specific sub-claims inside each move rather than to its overall thrust. Where you think a move is partly right, say which part survives and which does not. Then answer three further questions: whether any move commits a nameable error of argument (goalpost-shifting, special pleading, proving too much, trading on an unstated base rate) and who commits it; which of the two is more right overall, and whether the margin is large or small; and what both of them missed — I have in mind things like what a fair scoring rule for a decade-ahead social forecast would actually look like, whether the prior probability of the 2010 outcome being directionally correct is high enough to drain the forecast of evidential value, whether "the model" and "the research programme" are being conflated, and whether either side ever said what evidence they would accept in advance. Finally, if some single piece of evidence could break the deadlock in a way both would have agreed to beforehand, describe it; if none could, say so and explain why.
