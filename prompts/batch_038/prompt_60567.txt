I'm giving a 40 minute talk at a joint medieval-philosophy / metaphysics seminar in a few weeks and I want a Q&A prep sheet, basically — not a defense of my view, I just want to know what's coming at me.

The thesis. Al-Ghazālī's attack on causality in the seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut gets sold one of two ways: either as radical scepticism about causes, or as an anticipation of Hume. I want to argue for a third thing, namely that his real target is the *modal status* of the causal connection rather than the connection itself. On my reading Ghazali is perfectly happy that fire and cotton are reliably conjoined, that we can investigate it, and that we can predict it; what he denies is that the connection holds of necessity in virtue of the natures of the relata. So the habit of God (ʿāda) does all the work a law of nature does epistemically, but it's contingent in a way the falāsifa's natures aren't. That makes the dispute with Ibn Rushd a dispute about what it would take for a causal claim to be *demonstrated*, not about whether induction works.

And here's the part I'm most exposed on, so aim some fire at it: I want to say this puts him structurally closer to a best-system Humean about laws than to a sceptic about causes, with one crucial difference — his regularities are held in place by a continuing personal choice rather than by nothing at all. My claimed payoff is that this gives Ghazālī something the Humean can't have: an account of why the habit is *projectible* into the future (someone is holding it there, and remembers it), without making it necessary. I know that looks like I'm paying for projectibility with a metaphysically expensive promissory note, and I know a Humean will say the note doesn't cash: if the habit is just God's regular behaviour so far, then nothing about it determines which regularity continues, and I've reinvented the new riddle of induction with an agent in it. I want that objection stated better than I can state it.

The audience, three groups:

1. Four or five people who work on falsafa and kalām. They'll think I'm smuggling Hume into a text that isn't doing that work, and they'll press me on whether the Tahāfut even states Ghazālī's own settled view or is arguing dialectically against the philosophers on their own premises. One of them will insist the whole thing only makes sense against the atomism and the doctrine of accidents, which I barely mention.

2. Two or three contemporary metaphysicians who work on laws of nature — one a dispositional essentialist who thinks powers and natures are exactly what Ghazālī is throwing away, and one a best-system Humean who will ask whether God's habit is doing any explanatory work at all or is just a redundant layer on top of the regularities.

3. A couple of Ashʿarite theology specialists who care about the miracles problem: if the habit can be broken, what makes a break a miracle rather than just the habit being other than we thought, and how is anyone supposed to know which is which.

Give me the eightish objections I'll actually get from these people, in roughly the order they'd come up, each phrased the way that group would phrase it — the medievalists' version of the so-what complaint and the powers metaphysician's version are not the same objection and shouldn't read alike. For each one: a reply I could actually deliver out loud in about ninety seconds, and a blunt note on where the reply is soft or where I'm quietly conceding something I don't want to concede. Tag each as an exegesis objection or a metaphysics objection. No bibliography, no citations, no invented studies, and don't name scholars unless you're certain — I'd rather have the objection in their own vocabulary then a reference list. Then finish with the one I'm hoping nobody raises and why it's the one I can't answer.
