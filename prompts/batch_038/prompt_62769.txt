I've been reading the divergence debate — Britain vs. China, why the Industrial Revolution happened where and when it did — and there's a move I keep seeing that I don't have a name for, and it's bugging me because it seems to do a lot of the actual work in these arguments.

You ask something of the form "why A rather than B": why Britain and not China. But that question is hard to answer, partly because A and B aren't really the units that matter. So the answer, without announcing that it's doing so, swaps in a different pair: why England and not the Yangzi Delta; or why Europe and not East Asia; or why 1800 and not 1500; or why Britain and not the Netherlands. The swap is usually a genuine improvement — the new pair is measurable, the data exists, there are fewer confounds. And then the answer gets offered as an answer to the question you actually asked, and it reads fine, because nobody rechecks whether the new pair still licenses a claim about the old one.

What I can't figure out is what determines wether a swap is legitimate. Some clearly are: if you ask "why Britain and not China" and someone says the honest question is England versus the Yangzi Delta, because comparing a country to a civilization is a category error, that looks like a repair and not a dodge. Others clearly aren't: answering "why did the thing take off around 1800" with a story about European overseas expansion from 1500 on, when your own account holds that the two regions were roughly level until much later. But there's a big grey middle and I think all the interesting cases live there — the swap makes the question answerable, but it's answering a slightly easier version of it, and you can't tell from inside the answer how much was lost in the exchange.

Nearest things I can come up with, none of which seem to be it: changing the subject / arguing beside the point; the "unit of analysis" complaint in social science methodology; and the general observation that why-questions carry an unstated contrast. But that last one is about the fact that the foil matters at all, whereas what I'm describing is the substitution of one foil for another partway through an argument, and the fact that the substitution is often methodologically virtuous — which is exactly what makes it invisble.

So: is there an established concept for this? If yes, which of my candidates is closest and what does it fail to capture? If no, please coin something — a definition plus boundary cases. Does it cover a swap that narrows the question until it's trivially answerable? Does it cover swapping "A rather than B" for "A rather than A-minus-one-cause"? Is it the same thing as building a strawman of a question instead of of a person? Does it stop being this phenomenon when the author explicitly flags the swap, or just become a more honest version of it? I care a lot more about the carving than about the label, so don't just hand me a term.

One more thing: tell me how confident you are here. Roughly how sure are you that what I'm describing isn't just an existing concept I've failed to find, and which single claim in your answer would you bet least on?
