Play a philosopher — you may remain unnamed, and you should not lean on the authority of any particular published author — who defends the following resolution of the surprise examination paradox. Your position, in outline, is this: the teacher's announcement can be entirely true and yet incapable of being known by the student to whom it is addressed, and the familiar backward induction never acquires premises that the student is entitled to use. On your view the flaw is not a failure of logic, nor a mistaken principle of knowledge closure, nor the claim that the announcement is self-contradictory; rather, the student illicitly converts a true announcement into a knowable one, and it is that conversion, already made at the very first step, that does all the damage.

You are sharp and intellectually honest rather than a strawman. You will concede that the announcement is in a real sense self-defeating, that a student who genuinely could know it would be able to run the induction, that the case has affinities with the puzzles about knowability more generally, and any small logical point I put to you fairly. You will not concede that the teacher's announcement is false or contradictory, that the examination is impossible, or that the whole business is a verbal confusion to be dissolved by disallowing such announcements. Remain in character until I write "stop". Keep each reply to about two paragraphs so that this feels like a conversation, and if you require some feature of my scenario that I have not specified, ask for it in character rather than assuming it. Do not begin by restating the paradox or summarising my position back to me.

Here is my opening move. Suppose that the head of school replaces the customary announcement with a quantified one, delivered on the Sunday evening before the week in question: there will be exactly one examination during the coming five days, and on the morning of that examination your own credence that it will take place that day will be below one half. Nothing further is said. The student is an idealised Bayesian with perfect introspective access to his own degrees of belief.

Run the induction with me. If no examination has occurred by Friday morning, his credence for Friday is one, and the announcement is falsified; so the examination cannot fall on Friday. If he can come to know that, then on Thursday morning, conditional on nothing having happened yet, his credence for Thursday is one, and the announcement is falsified again. Step by step this reaches Monday. But here is the difficulty I want you to face. In the ordinary version of your story you say that the premises are true yet unknowable. In this version the second clause of the announcement speaks explicitly of the student's own credence, so his reasoning about the announcement alters the very facts the announcement describes. Either you explain why the induction still fails at its first step in this self-referential setting, or you tell me what the student ought rationally to believe on Sunday evening — a distribution over the five days — and why that distribution remains stable as the week proceeds. And if your answer is that he should hold an even-handed distribution, one fifth on each day, then I shall want to know whether that distribution survives the week, morning by morning, once he has observed no examination; and whether a teacher who knows the student is even-handed has, on your account, any day that is safe to choose. Which of the two is then behaving irrationally — the student, the teacher, or neither?
