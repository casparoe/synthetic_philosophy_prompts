I sit on the research ethics service of a mid-sized teaching hospital — seven of us, three clinicians, two lawyers, and two lay members — and for two years we have been splitting the same hard protocols three votes to four. What frustrates me is that I no longer think we disagree about the cases; I think we are each working with a different picture of what makes an agreement count, and none of us would be able to say which. Before our next meeting I want to hand every member a short self-diagnostic they can complete in ten minutes: five concrete vignettes of the sort of protocol we actually see, each with two or three answer options, such that a person's pattern of answers reveals which family of views about the validity of consent they are committed to. It has to work on people who have never read the literature, so no question may contain a theory name or a term of art — nothing like performative, uptake, voluntariness, baseline, authenticity, or materiality — and no question may ask the respondent what they think consent is in general. Only what they would rule in these cases.

Please cover, one per item and in this order: a participant who says the right words and signs the form in front of the coordinator but inwardly intends no such thing and has simply decided she could never refuse a doctor out loud; a disclosure that is accurate about everything on the protocol's risk schedule yet omits something the recruiter knows this particular participant would care about enormously and that no reasonable-person standard would flag, such as the downstream commercial use of her tissue; a large payment offered to someone who has no other way to afford something she badly needs, where accepting strictly improves her situation and nobody threatens her; a competent young adult consenting to an intervention that everyone on the team agrees will permanently change what she cares about, including possibly her view of the intervention itself; and a fully informed, unpressured consent to take part in something whose value depends on many others consenting too, or whose costs fall partly on people in her household who did not consent. Build the instrument from the logical space rather than from a list of doctrines: work out first which answers to which cases are jointly available, then let some of your options be combinations rather than single commitments, and make sure the leaves are families of views about validity — the outer-act view, the inner-authorization view, hybrid accounts, the restricted versus unrestricted accounts of what deception undoes, the threats-only versus background-circumstances accounts of pressure, the at-the-time-capacity versus higher-order-identification accounts of competence, and the view that a valid yes does not by itself license what it is a yes to. Name each leaf by describing the view, never by philosopher or book; I want no attributions anywhere. Where a cell of the space is unoccupied — a combination of answers no live position endorses — mark it and diagnose in one clause whether it would be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. And if two of your leaves turn out to be indistinguishable across all five of my cases, as I suspect the pure inner-authorization view and an outer-act view with a demanding recognition requirement may be, say so plainly rather than pretending the tree separates them.

Deliver it as exactly five numbered items with no heading, no preamble, and no closing remark. Inside each item use four labeled lines: CASE, giving the vignette in at most three sentences and phrased so a layperson can answer it without knowing where the answer leads; OPTIONS, two or three lettered choices stated in the words someone might actually say out loud; KEY, one line per option saying what that option commits the respondent to and which leaf or later item it points them toward, so the members can score themselves; and GAP, one clause naming what this node cannot distinguish, or the word none if it separates cleanly.
