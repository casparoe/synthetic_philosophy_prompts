I am a novelist, and this autumn I am leading a ninety-minute session for eighteen adults at a residential writing school — mostly would-be novelists, two of them working translators. I want them to meet the predicament of radical interpretation as an experience rather than a lecture: the interpreter who has nothing but a speaker's behaviour and the circumstances of that behaviour to go on, and who finds she cannot fix meanings without assuming beliefs, nor beliefs without assuming meanings, and can make sense of her speaker at all only by presuming broad agreement — so that wholesale error turns out to be unintelligible while particular errors remain something one can discover.

Design a game that brings them to this wall unaided, before anyone names it. One participant is the informant, speaking only in invented syllables applied to real objects in the room; the rest work in small teams building a lexicon and making predictions. Give me the roles, the materials, a token scoring scheme that needs no money, a minute-by-minute plan, and two engineered crises: a point at which the informant's settled use of a word shifts and the teams must decide whether her meaning, her beliefs or her accuracy has changed; and a point at which two teams hold incompatible interpretations that her subsequent behaviour cannot separate. Then a debrief mapping what happened onto those entanglements — the philosophers' names may appear here and nowhere earlier — an honest account of what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how I should handle each, and the one feature of the real predicament the game cannot reproduce that I shall have to supply by talking. British English, please. Close by telling me how confident you are that the design delivers what I have described, and singling out the element you are least sure of.
