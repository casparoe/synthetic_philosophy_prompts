I teach a philosophy of religion seminar — 22 students, mostly juniors and seniors, one 80-minute session, in a room with movable tables and no budget for props beyond paper and index cards. The session is on karma as a theory of moral desert: the view, roughly, that the moral quality of what you do determines the quality of what happens to you, and that this runs as a law of the world rather than as a verdict handed down by anyone — though in some traditions the law is said to be supervised by a deity, and in others it simply operates on its own. I want to work at the level of the theory rather than any particular tradition's doctrine, and several of my students hold karma as a live religious belief, so nothing in the activity may be played for laughs at the expense of the view itself.

The distinction I care about is one my students reliably miss when they read it. Call it the ledger reading: karma is a running balance, acts post credits and debits to it, and the balance fixes the quality of your subsequent experience — desert is quantitative, external, and administered by the mechanism. And call it the formation reading: what your acts actually produce is you. Your choices accumulate into dispositions, and your subsequent experience is fitting to you not because a balance was settled but because you have become the kind of person for whom those experiences are the natural continuation. On the first reading, suffering is payment; on the second, suffering is expression. They sound like paraphrases of each other in a textbook and they behave completely differently once anyone has to act on them.

Three further problems should surface in play, in this order, and I have labels for them but I don't want the labels used during the game. First, what I call the opacity problem: no one can read anyone else's balance, so the ledger reading licenses no interpersonal moral judgments at all — a student who notices this will have found the reason the theory is so hard to argue with and so easy to misuse. Second, the intervention problem: if a bad outcome is deserved, then relieving it obstructs justice, yet the act of relieving it is itself good — the ledger seems to pay you for defeating its own distribution. Third, the continuity problem: for the balance to deliver desert, whoever suffers must be the one who acted, and the moment you try to say what that requires, the ledger reading starts to collapse into the formation reading, because causal continuity of disposition is what's actually doing the work.

Here is what I need from you. Design the activity, and design it so that these three things happen to the students rather than being told to them.

Specific constraints, mostly earned from a bad run last year. My previous version gave each student a visible karma counter and resolved outcomes by drawing from a shuffled deck; within five minutes it had become arithmetic plus a coin flip, nobody felt the desert intuition at all, and one student correctly pointed out that a shuffled deck made it a game about luck, which karma is precisely supposed to not be. So: no dice, no shuffled outcome decks, no random number of any kind — every outcome in the game must be generated by somebody's choice, so that moral luck cannot smuggle itself in. Also, at least one phase must involve a student's account passing to a successor who is not that student, so the group can feel the difference between "the same person pays" and "someone downstream pays." And at least one point must permit a student to relieve another's bad outcome at some cost to themselves, with the rules conspicuously silent on whether that is allowed or how it counts.

The core device I want you to build, and the one I'm least sure is workable: I want the written rules to be genuinely ambiguous between the ledger and the formation readings, in the rules text itself rather than hidden by me, so that different table-groups resolve it differently in play and the conflict only becomes visible at a forced interaction point you design. Students must be able to look back at the handout and see that it really did permit both, so nobody feels tricked. If you think this requirement can't be met without turning into a gotcha, say so plainly and give me the closest thing that can be met.

Finally, I have a bias to guard against. I think the formation reading is the only one that survives scrutiny and the ledger reading is incoherent, and I do not want the game rigged to produce my conclusion — I want at least one honest route by which a table could end up defending the ledger reading and being right about something. Several of my students come in persuaded by the ledger version, and if the activity is stacked they'll notice.

Deliver this in two parts. First, in prose: the setup and materials list; the full text of every handout and card the students will hold, written as they should actually be printed; the phases with timings summing to 80 minutes; the debrief, as an ordered list of questions with notes on what a good student answer sounds like and what to do if nobody produces one; and a short closing note on what the activity cannot stage. On that last point, address explicitly a worry of mine: any simulation of karma has a designer and a facilitator resolving outcomes, so the game may unavoidably stage karma as administered by an intelligence, which is the one thing the impersonal-law version denies. Tell me whether that ruins the exercise or whether it can be turned into material for the debrief.

Second, after the prose, append one JSON object — no prose inside it except where a field calls for it — with exactly these fields: `activity_name` (string); `target_insight` (string, one sentence, what students should be able to say afterwards that they could not before); `materials` (array of strings); `phases` (array of objects with `phase`, `minutes`, `participants_do`, `intended_realization`); `load_bearing_mechanics` (array of objects with `mechanic`, `philosophical_claim_staged`, `what_breaks_if_removed`); `debrief_sequence` (array of objects with `step`, `question_to_ask`, `good_answer_sounds_like`, `if_nobody_gets_there`); `predicted_failures` (array of objects with `failure`, `how_it_will_show`, `fix_in_the_moment`); `cannot_reproduce` (array of objects with `feature`, `why_the_game_cannot_stage_it`, `what_i_say_instead`); and `rigging_check` (string: where this design still risks stacking the deck toward the formation reading, and the specific change that would reduce it).
