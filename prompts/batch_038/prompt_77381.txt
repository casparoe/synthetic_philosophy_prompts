First session of a metaethics seminar I'm teaching, on evolutionary debunking arguments. Problem: I've read maybe fifteen of these papers and I can't tell you which ones are actually arguments and which are just a genealogy story plus a shrug. So before I assign anything else I want the spec, not the survey.

Write the desiderata — what an argument from the evolutionary origins of our moral intuitions to some flavour of moral skepticism has to deliver before it's earned its conclusion. Standard of adequacy: good enough to move a realist who already accepts every word of the relevant evolutionary psychology and doesnt dispute the science. Converting people who were already skeptics doesn't count.

For each requirement, give the consideration or case behind it, and phrase it so I could hand it to a grad student as a question to put to a paper. About 8–10, fewer if some of mine below collapse together.

Here's what I'd expect on the list. Keep what's worth keeping, cut what isn't and say why, add what I've missed:

- the genealogy has to be more than a plausible story — what does it take for a selection history to do epistemic work, as opposed to merely be true?
- a robustness clause: does the argument survive if the causal details come out differently — cultural group selection rather than kin selection, or the dispositions being a spandrel of general intelligence plus language rather than an adaptation in their own right? I suspect this one fights with the previous one, and if it does I want to know what that costs.
- process vs content: is the target the reliability of the faculty, or the truth of the particular beliefs it outputs? most of these slide between the two without noticing.
- retail vs wholesale: if it debunks, say, disgust-based norms or intuitions about incest, does that license anything about morality as a whole? what would the upgrade require?
- a parity constraint. My seminar's fixed points are that modus ponens is reliable, that 7 is prime, and that pain is bad for the creature feeling it. Say which of the three the debunker can keep, which she loses, and whether losing the third is a fatal self-exception or just where the argument was always headed.
- no self-undermining: if it generalizes to all normative belief, including the epistemic ones its own premises run on, is that a reductio or merely an uncomfortable bill?
- the counterfactual — that we'd have had different moral beliefs had our ancestors been selected differently — plus an account of why that isn't the genetic fallacy with extra steps.
- who owes the explanation of the fit between our moral beliefs and the moral facts. Requirement on the debunker, or on the realist she's attacking? sort that out, I think half the literature has it backwards.

Then tag every requirement as non-negotiable, negotiable but expensive, or a camp prejudice wearing a requirements costume. Then the bit I most want: is there a subset that can't all be met at once? My guess is robustness plus empirical support plus parity is the tightest squeeze, but I could be wrong — show me the smallest unsatisfiable set, or tell me the list is jointly satisfiable and name what the cheapest view gives up instead. Last, one line on which single requirement eliminates the most existing replies, and a line each on how the dilemma-shaped versions and the ones aimed at the third-factor style realist replies come out against the list. No full scorecard, we'll do that in class.
