And here's Maya's essay:

"Plan A looks like it harms nobody, and the reason has to do with timing rather than with air quality. Which specific person you are depends on an absurdly delicate chain of events — which sperm meets which egg, and that depends on when your parents met and conceived, which depends on where they were living and working at the time. A two-year difference in when a project like this finishes moves thousands of people around, and that changes who meets whom. So the children who grow up in the Eastside under Plan A are, almost all of them, not the same children who would have grown up there under Plan B. Under Plan B those particular people would never have existed at all.

Harm is normally comparative: to harm someone is to make them worse off than they otherwise would have been. For the Eastside kids there is no such alternative. Their only alternative was never existing, and since their lives are worth living, Plan A is better for each of them than the alternative was. So they have no complaint — you cant say 'you ruined my life' to someone whose life was only possible because of the thing you did. And this is basically what the person-affecting view says anyway, which is what utilitarianism amounts to once you apply it to individual people: an act is wrong only if its worse for someone. If nobody is worse off, Plan A isnt wrong.

I dont think thats the end of it though. Even if no one has a complaint, the world under Plan A is worse than the world under Plan B — same number of people, worse lives — and Parfit's whole point is that we need a theory of wrongness that doesnt have to run through harm to particular people. Thats the right move for (a).

For (b), the already-conceived kids are a different case but I dont think it changes much. The reason Plan A seemed harmless was that there was no fact about who the future people would otherwise have been. Thats still true in a way here: if the council picks Plan B those kids still exist, but the specific details of their lives would be different, so theres no clean comparison to make. The air is worse under Plan A, but 'worse than what' is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and once you start comparing whole possible lives instead of single events the objection runs out. So (b) is really just (a) again.

For (c), the wider principle seems built to get us the verdict we want. It lets you set the Eastside kids' actual lives against the lives the Plan B kids would have had, even though theyre different people. The cost is that it stops being person-affecting in any real sense — youre ranking outcomes now, not protecting anyone — so you might as well be a straight utilitarian, and then you inherit whatever problems utilitarianism has with population."
