I have spent six years as a neuropsychology technician, first in an epilepsy surgery program where I set up and ran the lateralized testing on patients who had had their corpus callosum sectioned for intractable seizures, and for the last two in a movement-disorders clinic. I am now trying to write a thesis proposal on the unity of consciousness, and before I commit to any framework I have been auditing my own case-by-case intuitions, because I suspect they will not survive contact with each other and I would rather find that out now. Here they are. In the testing room, a word is flashed only to the left visual field, the patient's left hand reaches behind a curtain and correctly retrieves the object named, and the speaking hemisphere, which saw nothing, immediately supplies a plausible reason for the reach; I want to say there are two subjects in that room for those few seconds. Five minutes later, the same patient folds a towel with both hands, complains about the hospital coffee, and answers my questions; I want to say there is plainly one. Nothing structural changed between the two situations except what was on the table, and yet I am unwilling to give up either verdict. When a callosotomy patient's two hands work at cross purposes — one buttoning the shirt, the other unbuttoning it, sometimes with the patient watching in mild amusement — I take that as the strongest evidence available for two wills sharing one body. But when a patient of mine in the movement-disorders clinic, whose corpus callosum is entirely intact, has a hand that does exactly the same thing because of degenerative damage to the frontal and parietal circuitry, and she is visibly distressed by it and tells me it is not her, it is the hand, I insist that there is one person there with a malfunctioning part. Note the perversity: the patient who disowns the action is one subject on my view, and the patient who claims it, or shrugs about it, is two.

The rest of the list makes things worse. A child who loses an entire hemisphere to control catastrophic seizures and grows up with one intact hemisphere, a full personality, and a normal life is one person, and I refuse to describe the operation as a killing; but if the isolated hemisphere of an adult split-brain patient is a subject, as I said above, I cannot see why the same tissue in a child is not, and my only candidate answer — that the infant hemisphere is not yet specialized enough to be subject-eligible — gives me no line I could defend. Dreamless sleep and general anesthesia leave the count at one, on my view; the number of minds in a bed should not fall to zero at three in the morning. If instead I imagine a future drug or magnetic intervention that blocks the commissure for ninety minutes and then lets it resume, I lean toward saying one person under temporary suspension, and I cannot explain why permanence should add a mind. Suppose — I have read that something like this exists, but let us simply stipulate it — a pair of conjoined twins joined at the head who share a thalamic bridge, so that each can reportedly see what the other sees, while keeping separate bodies, separate speech, separate memories, and separate preferences: two persons, clearly, on my reckoning, even though the shared channel that I lamented in the split-brain case is present here and its absence there produced exactly the same number. Finally, when I myself am torn about taking a job, one part of me pulling one way and another part resisting, I am one person, and I do not want any account that makes ordinary ambivalence a case of multiple occupancy; but then I need to be told why motor autonomy changes the count when motivational conflict does not, and whether my scruple is anything more than squeamishness about surgery. The rough principle I keep reaching for, which I want you to treat as a hypothesis to be tested rather than as my conclusion, is this: two streams belong to one subject when they are forced to co-author a single output — one voice, one body moving through one space — and neither can produce that output without the other's participation, so that disagreement has to be negotiated rather than bypassed; and a subsystem that can act on the shared output unilaterally, or that has an output of its own which the others can neither see nor veto, is a separate subject. I add that this is meant to be a standing arrangement rather than a momentary state, which is how it is supposed to keep the count at one during sleep.

What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of competing candidates, that best organizes all of this. State each one precisely enough that a colleague who has never met me could apply it to a novel case and reach the verdict you would reach, and for each clause tell me which of the cases above forced it in, since I suspect several clauses are there only to protect a verdict I should abandon. Then run it on three cases I have not listed and have no intuition about: an octopus, whose arms carry a large share of its neural machinery and can solve local problems while the animal is attending to something else; a person with severe dissociative identity disorder, where I do not know whether I am looking at one badly integrated subject or several; and a split-brain patient fitted with a device that shows each hemisphere, within half a second, whatever the other is currently seeing, so that an external prosthesis does the commissure's job — does the count drop back to one, and if it does, what did the surgery ever change? Most importantly, be honest about the cost. Tell me which of my verdicts the recommended principle must overturn, and in particular which of the two pairs I cannot hold — the testing-room pair, where the count swings with the task, and the pair in which the disowned rogue hand belongs to one subject while the claimed one belongs to two. If you think a single principle can preserve both the two-subject verdict in the testing room and the one-subject verdict during dreamless sleep, show me exactly what additional machinery that requires and say whether the machinery is principled or a patch. I would also like a brief answer to a question underneath all of this, which is whether how many subjects there are is a fact to be discovered or a decision about how to count, because I sit on my unit's ethics consult list, and the question stopped being academic for me the afternoon we had to decide whose assent counted when a callosotomy patient's left hand pushed away a cup his mouth was reaching for. I am not looking for names, references, or a bibliography; I cannot check them and they would not settle anything here.
