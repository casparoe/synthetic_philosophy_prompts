I belong to a monthly reading group at my local library — mostly retired teachers, two graduate students, and me. I work in public health statistics rather than history, so when this month's theme turned out to be how historians explain the conduct of ordinary perpetrators, I leaned on a much-praised one-volume history of the twentieth century that several members own. Its chapter on the Holocaust spends two pages on what it calls the great argument of the 1990s, and I have transcribed the passage as carefully as I can:

"Christopher Browning studied a battalion of middle-aged reserve policemen from Hamburg who were ordered to shoot Jewish civilians in occupied Poland. What made his book a landmark is that he showed these men were not Nazis in any meaningful sense: they were ordinary family men with no particular hatred of Jews, and they killed because they were ordered to and because no one wanted to look weak in front of his comrades. Daniel Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners*, published a few years later, argued the opposite. Germans, he claimed, had been raised for centuries on a uniquely German 'eliminationist' antisemitism, so the killings were practically inevitable once the Nazis took power: his men shot Jews because they wanted to, and Browning's policemen count as 'ordinary' only if you assume — wrongly — that ordinary Germans were more or less like us. Professional historians rejected Goldhagen almost immediately, and Browning's situational account is now the standard explanation. Read together, the two books teach that character counts for very little. Put almost any ordinary person inside the right structure of authority and peer pressure and he will do terrible things, which is why the proper object of study is institutions rather than individuals."

One member recalled, hazily, something about the battalion's commander offering his men the chance to step out of the firing line before the first massacre; the passage does not mention it, and I cannot tell whether it matters. A friend who is a history graduate student said only that the passage is sloppy in the way popular writing about this debate always is, and refused to elaborate. Since I cannot extract more from her, I would like an accuracy audit I can bring back to the group. Please go through the passage claim by claim and sort it into three piles: what a specialist in this literature would accept as stated, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a substantive distortion. Then settle four questions in particular. First, is it right that Browning's men killed because they were ordered to, and how much room does his account leave for antisemitism or for having been raised under the regime — is he really offering a single-factor situational explanation? Second, is Goldhagen's claim about Germans a claim of degree (more antisemitic than their neighbours) or of kind (a distinctive variety of antisemitism), and does the practical inevitability attributed to him follow from anything he actually argued? Third, the passage says Goldhagen argued the opposite of Browning; given what each of them says about whether the killers were coerced and about whether they could have refused, is that the right shape for the disagreement, or does it relocate the dispute? Fourth, does the closing generalisation — that character counts for very little and that institutions, not individuals, are the object of study — follow from either book, or from the two read together, and is the claim about professional rejection and about what is now standard a fair characterisation of the reception or a compression of something messier?

I would rather not get a both-sides survey, and I do not need a reading list; I want your own assessment and a definite verdict. Tell me plainly whether the passage is, on balance, fair, and where exactly my friend is right. If you think its concluding moral-psychological lesson is nonetheless true — that the perpetrators' conduct tells us more about situations than about character — say so, and argue for it separately from whether these two books support it, since I suspect the passage may be smuggling in a conclusion it has not earned. Finally, flag the two or three places where your own confidence is weakest, so that the group knows which parts of your audit rest on your recall of these books and which on reasoning about what the claims would have to mean.
