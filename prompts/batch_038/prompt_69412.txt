Year abroad in the US and I've turned into the annoying guy about two things: I won't tip, and I'll cross against the light when the street is empty — though not when there's a kid on the corner watching what adults do. Two different people have now ended the argument with the same proverb: "when in Rome, do as the Romans do."

Can you take that line apart for me? I want the separate claims it could be making, from the near-trivial ones (you can't know the local rules better than the locals do, and breaking them reads as arrogance even when the rule is arbitrary) up through courtesy, all the way up to a full moral obligation to conform. For each candidate reading: is it a claim about prudence, etiquette, or morality; is it true, false, or contested; and would it actually get my critics their conclusion? I suspect the answer isn't the same for the tipping case and the jaywalking case, since in one the convention mainly transfers money from me to someone and in the other it coordinates a bunch of strangers, but say where I'm wrong about that. And finally, does any reading survive the thought that the local convention might be one I think is unjust rather than merely pointless — because that's the move I actually want to be able to make about tipping without sounding like a tourist.

Menu, not a verdict on whether I'm right.
