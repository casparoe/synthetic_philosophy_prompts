I am in my final year of high school and, with a teacher's supervision, I have been carrying out an independent study in philosophy of mind. I intend to submit a short paper to our school's philosophy review in the spring, and the first thing my supervisor asked me to do was to write down, as precisely as I can, everything I actually believe about the question "is the brain a computer?" so that she can attack the whole package at once rather than one sentence at a time. I would like a second opinion before I hand it in, because I suspect the package is not as unified as I have been assuming, and I genuinely do not know where it starts to come apart. I want a consistency audit, not a tutorial: please do not explain the standard positions to me, do not tell me what various philosophers think, and do not end with a reading list, since I cannot obtain most of what would be on it.

Here is the package, in my own words.

1. Physicalism. Two worlds that are physically identical are mentally identical; nothing about mentality floats free of the physical facts.
2. Computationalism, taken literally. The brain is a computer, not merely a system that we may profitably *describe* as one. Cognitive processes are computations over representations.
3. Substrate independence. If some non-biological system reproduced the relevant causal organization of my brain, it would have mental states of the same kind as mine.
4. Computation is objective. Whether a physical process performs a given computation is a fact about the process, not about anyone's choice of description. I am unmoved by the Putnam-and-Searle line that any open system can be mapped onto any finite-state program, so that the wall in my bedroom is running my word processor.
5. Anti-instrumentalism about ascription. Whether a system has a particular belief, or is in a particular pain, is not relative to a level of description or to what happens to serve our explanatory interests.
6. Naturalized content. Representations inside a system are about things in the world in virtue of causal connections to those things, not because somebody interprets them; there is no inner audience doing the reading.
7. The lookup-table machine. A robot whose every output is read off an astronomically large precomputed table of possible inputs and responses, and which therefore reproduces my conversational behavior exactly, would have no mental states whatsoever.
8. The distributed simulation. A billion people passing signals by radio so as to reproduce, step for step, the causal structure of my neurons — even if it takes them a year to run what takes my brain an hour — would have mental states, and they would be the same kind as mine.
9. Method. Whether the brain is a computer is an empirical question, to be settled by neuroscience and computer science rather than from the armchair.

Three specific things I want from you. First, go through the pairs and separate the genuine tensions from the ones that only look like tensions: I expect that some of these will turn out to be compatible on inspection, and I would rather be told that plainly than have everything flagged as equally worrisome. Please rank the genuine ones by severity rather than listing them in the order I gave them. Second, for each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair then costs me — which of the other commitments it forces me to weaken or abandon. Third, and most importantly, I want a verdict on two questions I could not answer myself. Is there a criterion for when two systems perform the *same computation* that is neither a criterion about the material they are made of (which (3) forbids) nor a criterion about what interests us (which (5) forbids), and that nonetheless returns "no mentality" for the machine in (7) and "mentality" for the crowd in (8)? If there is no such criterion, tell me which of the two verdicts I have to surrender, and what I lose with it. And given (4) and (5) together, do I have any principled way to explain why the wall is not running my word processor without letting description-relative considerations back into the individuation of computations through the back door? Finally, tell me which single revision to the list would buy me the most consistency, since I would rather make one large change than nine small ones.
