Background: second-year polisci PhD (comparative institutions), and before grad school I spent six years as a distributed systems engineer. The systems vocabulary is still the one I actually think in, and I want to know how much of the presidentialism vs. parlimentarism debate survives being restated in it. I'm fluent on the engineering side and only intro-course fluent on the institutions side, so don't go easy on either.

Translate the standard case against presidentialism — dual democratic legitimacy, the rigidity of a fixed term, the winner-take-all stakes of a single executive office — and the standard case for parliamentarism into terms like leader election, leases, failure detectors, split brain, safety vs. liveness, graceful failover, restart. Then work through the sub-cases rather than stopping at the slogan: what is a confidence vote, mechanically? What is Germany's constructive no-confidence, where the assembly has to elect the successor in the same motion that ousts the incumbent? Caretaker governments? Snap elections? Semi-presidential systems where a president and a prime minister each claim a mandate?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want: which claims carry over cleanly, which ones you had to bend to fit, and which have no counterpart in the systems vocabulary at all. Be blunt about whether the translation smuggles — e.g. does treating a government as something that has to converge presuppose there's a correct value to converge on, which politics cant have? And end with your verdict on whether the residue exposes a limit of the engineering framing, or something the institutions debate has left vaguer than it looks.
