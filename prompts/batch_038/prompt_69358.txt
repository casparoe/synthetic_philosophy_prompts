I am not an academic, and I have no formal training in economics or in the history of philosophy. For eleven years I have kept the books for a community land trust in a small town, and for the last four I have also kept the ledger for a mutual-credit scheme in which members pay one another in units that exist nowhere but in that ledger and are understood to be settled eventually in hours of work. Someone left a copy of George Berkeley's *The Querist* in our office, and I have been reading it slowly ever since.

What unsettles me is how familiar it sounds. Almost everything our members say at meetings is in that book: that the units are not themselves wealth but counters or tokens for recording who may claim whose labour; that a greater quantity of them is worth nothing unless it sets idle hands to work; that hoarding metal money is a confusion; that a district short of coin would do better with a paper currency issued by a public bank and secured on the land of the district; and that the amount in circulation is a matter for deliberate public management rather than something to be left to find its own level. Berkeley put these points as several hundred numbered queries, nearly all of them opening with the same word — whether — while he was Bishop of Cloyne in the 1730s.

I would like a piece of satire built out of that collision: a parody launch whitepaper for an invented land-backed community token, say the acre-token of the Kestrel Valley Land Bank, written entirely in Berkeley's interrogative manner. About 800 to 950 words.

The requirements I care about:

1. Use the furniture of a real whitepaper — executive summary, the problem, mechanism, collateral, governance, roadmap, risk factors, frequently asked questions — but let no section answer anything. Every line of body text is a numbered query beginning with whether. A whitepaper's whole job is to reassure, and the form should refuse to do so.

2. The queries must carry the doctrine accurately, as I have set it out above. Do not turn Berkeley into a crude inflationist: his concern is industry and employment, not the pleasure of printing. Exaggerate what his view actually implies, never a position he did not hold.

3. Include one boxed interjection, three sentences at most, from the project's appointed sceptic, in ordinary declarative prose — the only place in the document where anyone says anything. The queries that follow must absorb those objections and convert them into further whether-questions, which is where I want the real joke to sit.

4. In the risk factors section, include the collateral problem as a sidebar: who appraises the land, what happens when land values fall, what happens when the people whose future labour is supposed to settle the tokens move away or decline to work. Let these land on the reader without ever being labelled objections.

5. Somewhere in the roadmap, the token's hard supply cap should quietly cease to be treated as a constraint, since on the token theory the only real limit is administrative. Do not comment on this. Let a reader notice.

6. End the frequently asked questions with a single query that raises whether a town whose units are only counters has actually created anything, or merely recorded an intention — stated as a question, with no moral attached.

Two prohibitions. Do not put any sentence in quotation marks as though it came from Berkeley's text, and do not attach real query numbers or citations to it; I cannot check them and I do not want invented ones. Write pastiche in his manner and attribute everything to the invented bank. And do not break the deadpan: no author's note, no gloss, no wink at the reader explaining the joke. I want a specialist in the history of economic thought to recognise that the author knows the material and to find the satire fair, and a neighbour of mine to laugh at it without knowing who Berkeley was.
