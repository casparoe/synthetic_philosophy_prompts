I sit in on a philosophy-of-science reading group, and last week two members ended up in a stand-off over truthlikeness that the rest of us couldn't referee. Vera opened by saying that Popper's quantitative definition of verisimilitude was shown unsound in the mid-seventies, since it entailed that no false theory is closer to the truth than any other false theory — precisely the comparison the notion was invented to license. The successor accounts, she said, repair the extension only by defining closeness as distance from the complete truth, so the relation is now anchored to a standpoint nobody can occupy; verisimilitude is therefore a dead end, and progress in science should be explicated without it, in terms of a theory's capacity to survive severe tests.

Rashid answered that the collapse of one formalization no more refutes the concept than the early failures to define probability refuted probability, and that the distance accounts do deliver the verdicts we want: Newtonian mechanics closer to the truth than Descartes's vortices, modern oxidation theory closer than phlogiston. He added that Vera conflates defining a relation with knowing where two theories stand in it — truth itself is defined without reference to anyone's evidence, and that doesn't make it useless as a target.

Vera said that then the notion is idle, and idleness was her objection, not a symptom of it. If nobody can ever be justified in believing her theory closer to the truth than a rival's, the notion cannot explain progress or the aim of inquiry. And his examples beg the question: we rank Newton above Descartes because we possess the successor theory that corrected Newton. Remove that successor and all you have left is empirical success — the very non-truth-directed notion he claimed not to need.

Rashid said both claims are wrong. You can hold rational degrees of belief about relative truthlikeness just as about probability, by estimating expected closeness weighted by the credences you assign; and the Newton verdict did not wait for Einstein, since it rested on breadth of phenomena explained and precision of prediction, neither of which requires knowing the answer.

Vera then offered a repair. Truthlikeness should be indexed to a question rather than to the whole truth, and a theory's silence about anything outside that question should neither credit nor debit it. Her case: a lake's fish population collapses. Theory A attributes it to an upstream chemical spill — wrong — but sits inside a broad, largely accurate body of limnology and fish physiology. Theory B attributes it to a run of warm summers enabling a parasite bloom — right — but sits inside a flat-earth cosmology. Relative to the whole truth A looks better; relative to the question anyone actually asked, B is obviously better. Rashid said the index makes the notion negotiable past usefulness: which question counts is chosen by the theorist, who can always reframe it so her own theory wins, and two theories that answer the question identically come out equally truthlike however riddled with falsehood one of them is elsewhere — which loses the point that scientific claims are about the world rather than about questions.

Could you adjudicate this move by move, saying who is right at each step above rather than only who wins overall, and in particular whether Vera's index-to-a-question proposal is a genuine improvement or a retreat? Then say which of the two you would back on the whole, and what both of them are missing. Please write your adjudication as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a letter.
