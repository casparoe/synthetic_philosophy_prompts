Next month my third-year seminar on the philosophy of probability opens with Stoppard's *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead*, and I want to hand the students a reading of its coin-tossing material that is answerable to both the play and the actual debate between frequentist, subjectivist, and propensity interpretations. The material I have in mind is the opening: the two of them are spinning a coin and calling it in an unspecified landscape, it comes up heads seventy-six times running, the caller wins every toss and bags the coin, Rosencrantz is the caller and is entirely unbothered, treating the streak as a personal record, while Guildenstern is the one made uneasy and works through candidate explanations for why the laws of probability seem to have been suspended, including divine intervention and the possibility that a single moment is being replayed. The streak then continues when they take on the Tragedians at the same game, strip the company of its money, and oblige the players to act out their debt. Both men, of course, arrive with no usable memory of anything prior, including how they came to be there.

Please give me the reading. Which interpretation does each man's behaviour actually model, and does it matter that they differ in this way rather than merely in intelligence — I want to know whether Rosencrantz can be read as a naive finite frequentist who simply identifies the observed relative frequency with the probability, and whether Guildenstern's distress is best understood as a propensity theorist's distress at finding no stable disposition behind the arrangement, or whether both readings are projections on my part. Then take up the harder question, which is whether the scene's alarm is available to a consistent subjectivist at all. On an exchangeability-style analysis, seventy-six heads is remarkable but perfectly admissible, and the correct move is a decisive revision of one's expectation about the next toss rather than any metaphysical shudder; so a Bayesian in the audience ought to feel only the mild frisson of learning a bias parameter, and would in fact end up forecasting the next toss far better than the man doing the philosophizing. Tell me whether that defuses the scene, whether the play's dread therefore presupposes that chances are objective features of the world, and whether Stoppard is inadvertently vindicating the character he appears to be mocking. I have been calling the setup the seventy-six flip problem for my students, meaning a sample large enough to be wildly improbable under any antecedently plausible hypothesis but generated by an arrangement the agents know nothing about, cannot inspect, cannot repeat on demand, and will not live long enough to see converge; say whether that is a fair characterization of what the play stages or whether I have overstated it.

Two further things I want addressed. First, the second streak, against the Tragedians, where the participants and the stakes change and the run persists anyway: what does that do to a propensity reading on which the disposition belongs to the whole repeatable experimental arrangement, and does it push the reader toward some fourth generator, namely the fact that these two are inside a script whose ending Hamlet has already fixed? Second, the end of the sequence: they die, so there is no long run for them, ever. Is the play therefore a dramatization of the single-case and finite-frequency difficulties rather than of induction in general, and if so what does that say about which of the three interpretations it is really in tension with?

I would also like you to be candid where the reading strains. Stoppard is writing theatre, not philosophy of science; the coin may be doing comic and menacing work that has nothing to do with the interpretation debate, and a critic who turns Guildenstern into a failed Bayesian may simply have missed the point. Take seriously the possibility that treating the play as a commentary on frequentism versus subjectivism versus propensity is itself a category mistake, and tell me where you land. Finish by saying what the play shows about probability that argument alone does not.

Please write this to me as continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter rather than a document: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italicized emphasis, no tables. Paragraphs are welcome. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or page references, and please do not quote the play, since neither of us has the text in front of us here; work from the scenes as I have described them and paraphrase freely. Naming views and their principal advocates where it sharpens the discussion is very useful; sending me off to look things up is not.
