I'm writing a paper for my school's philosophy seminar on Ryle's distinction between knowing how and knowing that, and I want to defend a thesis I'll call the pervasiveness claim: there is no instance of knowing that p which does not also involve some instance of knowing how, where the ability partly constitutes the propositional knowledge rather than merely following from it. I currently sit at about 0.7; my seminar leader is nearer 0.15 and thinks most of our gap is verbal.

I'd like your credence in the claim, but with the derivation shown rather than a bare number. Please assign rough probabilities to at least the following terms: (a) whether knowing how to apply a concept and draw inferences with it is knowing-how in the sense at issue, or only a disposition that an intellectualist can absorb into knowing-that under a practical guise; (b) the force of pure testimony cases — suppose Ida knows that her town's water contains lead at fifteen parts per billion because she read it in a report, yet cannot test the water, would not recognize a lead pipe, and has no idea what to do with the information — is that a genuine counterexample, or does she retain enough minimal ability to survive the claim; (c) whether "ability" and "propositional knowledge" are determinate enough that the claim has a truth value at all, rather than turning on how broadly one stipulates the word "know"; and (d) whether the constitutive relation the claim requires is available, or whether know-how could only ever be causally or developmentally prior to know-that.

Then combine those into a single figure, tell me which term the result is most sensitive to, and say which of the two positions — mine at 0.7 or my seminar leader's at 0.15 — is most likely resting on a disagreement about (c). Finally, name the one argument or case that would move your number furthest.
