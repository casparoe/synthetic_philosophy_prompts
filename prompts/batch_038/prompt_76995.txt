For several months now I have been conducting an argument by email with a friend of mine — we both did our undergraduate work in physics and neither of us has ever sat down with Spinoza's Ethics — about whether Einstein's self-declared Spinozism amounts to anything more than a dignified label for an aesthetic reverence toward physical law. The argument began at a conference dinner after a talk on the cosmological constant and has never really stopped. His position is that Einstein merely borrowed a metaphysician's vocabulary to give weight to what is really a physicist's aesthetic emotion, and that nothing in the Ethics survives the borrowing. Mine is that the overlap is doctrinal, not decorative. I would like you to reconstruct both positions in their strongest forms and then adjudicate between them, since we are clearly incapable of settling it ourselves.

Please structure the comparison along three axes, and be willing to state in a sentence or two any Spinozist doctrine you rely on, because your audience is scientifically trained readers who have never opened the Ethics. First, the object of reverence. Spinoza's God is a single substance with infinitely many attributes, of which everything that exists is a mode, and thought and extension are two attributes of one and the same thing; Einstein, in the short cable he sent back to a New York rabbi who had asked him by telegram whether he believed in God, characterized his God as the one who reveals himself in the lawful harmony of the world rather than in the fate and doings of human beings. Does Einstein's reverence pick out Spinoza's God, or only Spinoza's God considered under a single attribute, or only nature as already-produced rather than nature as producing — and would Spinoza classify Einstein's cosmic religious feeling, described in his popular essays and addresses of the 1930s and 1940s, as an instance of the second kind of knowledge or a candidate for the third? Second, intelligibility. Einstein treated the comprehensibility of the world as the one thing about the world that he found genuinely miraculous, whereas Spinoza's parallelism seems to guarantee in advance that the order of ideas is the order of things, leaving nothing to be astonished at. Decide whether Einstein's wonder is a residue of the personal deity he had just finished rejecting, or whether it is something Spinoza's system cannot accommodate. Third, freedom and blame. Einstein denied free will outright, endorsed Schopenhauer's maxim that a man can do what he wills but cannot will what he wills, confessed that he nevertheless felt compelled to act as though freedom existed, and took determinism to mitigate resentment and retributive punishment; Spinoza also denies freedom of the will, but preserves a robust notion of freedom as acting from one's own adequate causation, and builds an entire ethics of increasing power and joy on top of it. Is Einstein's determinism Spinozist, or is it a stoic resignation wearing Spinoza's clothes — and is the move of acting as if free even available inside Spinoza's system, or does it confuse imagination with intellect in a way Spinoza would have diagnosed ruthlessly?

Finally, I want you to test a distinction I have been using, which may well be worthless. Call an attitude of reverence 'substantialist' when it is directed at that from which everything follows, and 'structuralist' when it is directed at the order that the things one happens to study exhibit. My claim is that Einstein's is structuralist and Spinoza's is substantialist, and that this single difference generates nearly all of their divergences on the three axes above. Tell me whether the distinction does real work, whether it collapses under pressure, or whether it needs refinement — and if it needs refinement, refine it and use the refined version. Then commit to a verdict on each axis rather than hedging that both thinkers were subtle; say specifically where the two views coincide, where Einstein truncates Spinoza, and what the truncation costs him, including whether he can still ground his rejection of retributive punishment without Spinoza's account of freedom. I would also like you to say what Spinoza would have made of the blunt private letter of Einstein's last years, in which he wrote far more dismissively about the word God and about scripture than he ever did in print: vindication of the Ethics from inside physics, or a category error about a book Spinoza himself had already dismantled. Aim for roughly a thousand words, argue rather than survey, and do not pad the response with a reference apparatus.
