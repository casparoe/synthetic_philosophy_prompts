I direct a small family foundation. My training is in hospital administration, not research, and I want to be upfront that I lack the vocabulary for what is going on in our boardroom this year. One large grant has split our five trustees, and I can feel that the two loudest arguments are each missing something I cannot name. I would like help thinking it through.

The proposal comes from a professor who builds mathematical models of long stretches of history. He has assembled data going back centuries on wages, food prices, population growth, how many young people complete higher education relative to how many desirable positions are open, and how much money governments have left after their debts are serviced. From these he claims to identify a recurring shape: societies go through long periods of broad improvement and then long periods of squeeze, and the squeezes arrive when ordinary living standards have been sliding for a while, when too many ambitious people are competing for too few places at the top, and when the public purse is exhausted. He says these conditions are currently lining up in the same configuration that preceded civil breakdown many times before, that his model identifies particular regions where serious instability is likely within the next ten years, and that our money would run the model forward, publish the forecasts, and brief governments and aid agencies in the places it flags.

One trustee, a retired engineer, says this is astrology with spreadsheets. His argument is that history happens once. There is no second run of the same society to compare the forecast against, so nothing the professor asserts could ever be shown to be wrong, and a claim that cannot be wrong is not a claim at all. He adds that predicting trouble somewhere on earth over the next decade is a safe bet in any year, and that he would rather fund a food bank.

Another trustee, a former judge, says the engineer is proving far too much. You cannot rerun last Tuesday either, and yet weather forecasting improved; medicine acts on mechanisms it understands well before it can run the decisive trial; and if there is a mechanism operating now, waiting for a clean test means waiting until people are hurt. She also notes that the professor's previous book named several countries, some of which did blow up and some of which did not, and that publishing your misses strikes her as the opposite of what a charlatan does.

A third trustee raised something that unsettled me most of all. If a forecast of this kind is published and believed, the people positioned to act on it will act, the crisis will be averted or shifted elsewhere, and the model will look wrong precisely for having been right. She wants to know whether a forecast that undermines itself by being taken seriously is worth funding at all.

Here is what I would like from you.

1. Sort out what the engineer's objection really amounts to. Is the non-repeatability of history a decisive objection to this kind of work, or does it fail to separate this from other forecasting we take for granted? If something genuinely is different about a subject matter that happens only once, say what the difference is and what it does and does not rule out.

2. Tell me what would let me distinguish a forecast from a story that has been adjusted until it fits the past. I want things I could actually put to the professor across a conference table: whether the flagged regions were committed to in advance and in writing, how precise the claims are, what he would accept as counting against him, what he does with the places he flagged where nothing happened, whether the model was tuned on the same history it is now said to explain, and anything else you consider a fair question that I have not thought of.

3. Judge the mixed track record. Does naming several countries and being right about some of them carry any weight at all, and what makes such a record worth more or less?

4. Deal with the self-undermining forecast. Does it show the work is worthless, or is a warning that gets heeded and then appears to fail actually the success condition? Be clear about what, on that reading, we would be buying with our money and how anyone could tell whether we got it.

5. Give me a recommendation: fund, refuse, or fund with conditions, and if with conditions, which ones and why those rather than others. Then tell me what I can say to the engineer and the judge so that each understands the part of their own objection that survives the decision.

Please also state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of, along with what would most change your mind about it.
