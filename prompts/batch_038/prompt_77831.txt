I am the registrar at a mid-sized museum of modern and contemporary art, and I have just spent a fortnite reconciling our accession register against our insurance schedule and the customs declarations for our outgoing loans. The exercise has left me with a puzzle that museum vocabulary cannot resolve, and I should like a philosophical reconstruction of what our paperwork actually commits us to. Here are the practices, stated as plainly as I can.

First, instruction-based works. When we acquire a wall drawing of the kind associated with Sol LeWitt, what we buy, and what the accession number attaches to, is a signed certificate together with a diagram and a set of instructions. The wall itself is painted by hired drafters working from specifications supplied by the artist's estate, photographed for the record, and painted over at the end of the run. We open no condition report for the wall; we carry the work at its full purchase price on the insurance schedule in years when no instance of it is installed anywhere; and the estate may authorize further instances, each of which requires a newly countersigned certificate. Nobody in this building thinks we own a copy of something.

Second, attribution. Our catalogue grades run autograph, studio of, circle of, follower of, after. The market and our own acquisitions committee treat those grades as tracking something real: we will pay a very large sum for a panel in which the master's hand is discernible in a fraction of the surface, and almost nothing for a workshop product that no visitor could tell from it. Provenance research asks who touched the object, not what the object looks like.

Third, customs. The tariff headings that admit works of art duty-free are defined by medium and by hand — paintings and drawings executed entirely by hand, original sculpture in any material, photographs printed by or under the artist's supervision and numbered within a limited edition — and anything else travels under the heading for its material. So a fabricated steel piece crosses a border as an article of base metal unless the crate is accompanied by a signed certificate and a letter from a curator. The statute in force during the most famous such dispute required, for a sculpture to enter free of duty, that it be original, that it be the work of a professional sculptor, and that it serve no practical purpose: in effect a legislative definition of arthood. When Brâncuși's *Bird in Space* reached New York harbor in 1926, customs assessed the polished bronze as a manufactured metal object at forty percent of its value and released the crates on bond under the heading for kitchen utensils and hospital supplies; only in 1928 did the Customs Court hold it to be an original sculpture and duty-free. The appraiser who defended the original classification, reporting the opinions solicited for the government, quoted one of them as saying, "If that's art, hereafter I'm a bricklayer."

Fourth, conservation. In the paintings lab every decision about cleaning, varnish removal, and inpainting is argued in terms of that canvas's own history and its maker's materials, on the assumption that the work simply is this physical object. Two floors up, faced with an installation of perishable components or a faded painted wall, we authorize re-fabrication from the artist's specifications and record the result in the register as the same work, not as a replica.

Fifth, deaccessioning. Our accrediting bodies permit the proceeds of a sale to be spent only on acquiring other works, so the collection functions as a closed economy in which one holding is exchanged for another; we will sell a work we have come to think mediocre and buy one we think better. Yet we would never sell the certificate of an instruction-based work while an instance of it is on view, and we continue to list works whose only instance has been destroyed.

What theory of what an artwork is does this cluster presuppose? I would like the reconstruction to do the following. Identify which of the five practices commits us to treating a work as something capable of indefinitely many instances and able to outlive all of them, and which commits us to treating a work as a particular physical object essentially tied to the hand that made it; then say whether those commitments are genuinely contradictory or whether a single account can license both, and what that account must say about the ontological status of the certificate. Say which rival accounts the practice rules out — for instance, an account on which something is art in virtue of the experience it is designed to afford, one on which arthood is conferred by the artworld, one on which a work is identical with a particular act of making — distinguishing for each whether our practice is merely unfriendly to it or flatly incompatible with it. Be scrupulous about separating what the paperwork strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and about separating philosophical commitment from administrative motive: several of the things I have described, notably carrying an uninstalled work at full insured value and writing the certificate as the accessioned object, may be driven by indemnity, liability, and tax treatment rather than by any view about the nature of art, so tell me how to tell those cases apart and which of the five cannot be explained away that way. Finally, suppose the museum were persuaded to adopt a rival view — that a work is essentially the event or experience it generates, or that arthood is something we confer rather than discover. What would have to change in the register, the insurance schedule, the conservation mandate, the loan and customs paperwork, and the deaccession policy, and which single change would be hardest to make and why?
