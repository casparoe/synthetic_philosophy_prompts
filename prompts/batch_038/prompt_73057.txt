I'm in a reading group on the paradoxes of infinity — three of us, one of whom is a physics TA — and we have spent two sessions on Thomson's lamp without getting anywhere. Let me fix the case so that we are all discussing the same thing. A lamp is on at one minute before noon. A switch is then flipped at half a minute before noon, at a quarter of a minute before, at an eighth, and so on, so that the flips accumulate at noon and there is no last flip. The standing question is whether the lamp is on or off at noon. My friend has constructed the following argument, which he says shows not merely that the question is unanswerable but that the situation described is impossible — and, worse, that the impossibility generalizes further than any of us is comfortable with.

> (1) A system is in a given state at a given time only if something makes it so: either there is an interval ending at that time throughout which it was in that state and nothing disturbed it, or some event occurring at or before that time brought it into that state.
> (2) An event brings a system into a state only at the moment it occurs; its effect persists until some later event changes the state, and no event can first produce a state at a time later than itself.
> (3) In the lamp case there is no interval ending at noon throughout which the lamp is undisturbed, since every such interval contains a flip.
> (4) So by (1), if the lamp is on at noon, some event brought it into that state; and by (2) that event must be one of the flips, whose effect begins when the flip occurs. But every flip that leaves the lamp on is followed by a flip that leaves it off, so no flip's effect survives to noon.
> (5) Therefore nothing makes it so that the lamp is on at noon. The same reasoning, mutatis mutandis, shows that nothing makes it so that the lamp is off at noon.
> (6) A switch is either on or off. So the lamp is in no state at noon, which is a contradiction: the schedule describes an impossible situation.
> (7) Nothing in (1)–(6) mentioned lamps, switches, or anything else particular. What was used was (1), (2), and the fact that there were infinitely many discrete acts accumulating at a time with no last one. Hence no system can complete an ω-sequence of discrete acts within a finite interval.
> (8) But crossing a room is, on Zeno's decomposition, the completion of an ω-sequence of discrete acts — crossing each remaining half-distance — and Achilles's overtaking the tortoise is another. By (7) neither is possible.
> (9) Likewise a ball dropped onto a floor that rebounds to a fixed fraction of its previous height performs infinitely many bounces in a finite time and is at rest at the accumulation instant, in the idealized model everyone is taught. By (7) that is impossible; and by (1), (2), and (4), nothing makes it so that the ball is at rest then, since there is no last bounce and no interval before the accumulation instant during which it lies undisturbed at rest.

We are stuck, and I want a diagnosis rather than encouragement. I am not willing to give up the ball or the room, so I take the conclusion to be false; what I cannot see is which step to blame, since each of (1) through (7) looked entirely innocent when we wrote it down. My friend says that if I want to keep (1) — and I do, very much, because it looks like the least a physical explanation can assume — then I owe him an account of what makes it so that the ball is at rest at the accumulation instant, given that it is not any bounce and not any undisturbed interval. Someone else in the group says the whole problem dissolves once you notice that Thomson never showed the lamp to be impossible, only that his specification of the states before noon leaves the state at noon unsettled; but that strikes me as conceding (1) rather than answering it, and I cannot see how it bears on (9) at all. A third suggestion was to build the switch as a contact spun by a motor whose rate doubles at each stage, on the grounds that this replaces infinitely many acts with a single continuous motion; that appeared to make things worse rather than better, and none of us could articulate why.

So: identify the step or steps to reject, and explain for each why it seemed unobjectionable in advance. I want the diagnosis specific enough to account for whatever asymmetry there is between the lamp and the bouncing ball — or, if you think there is none, to say so and explain what follows. Then price the alternatives for me: what it costs to retain (1) but restrict it, and how the restriction is to be stated without mentioning infinity at all; what it costs to deny that (5) and (6) jointly yield a contradiction; and what it costs to accept that the state of the lamp at noon, if there is one, is a fact with no explanation. Tell me which of these you would take and what you would have to surrender elsewhere in order to live with it, and finish by saying whether the spinning-contact variant rescues (7), sinks it, or shows that (7) was never about acts in the first place.
