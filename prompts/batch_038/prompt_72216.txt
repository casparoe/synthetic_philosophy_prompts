Thirty-eight years as an atmospheric physicist, and the last dozen of those were spent writing assessments for committees and then sitting in the room while people with no idea what an error bar was decided what to do with them. I'm retired now, I've finally read Thucydides properly cover to cover, and honestly it reads like my old job with better sentences. So I want to get into the modern literature on expertise and democracy, epistocracy, all of that. But before I buy a pile of books I want the spec, and I don't want a reading list from you — I can find the books myself. I want to know what I should be demanding of any of them.

So: write me the requirements a theory of expert advice to a political body has to meet, where the body holds the actual decision power and the expert has no authority beyond being listened to. What does such a theory have to explain, which cases does it have to get right, which distinctions can it not collapse, what does it have to be consistent with? Give me the case or the consideration behind each requirement, and mark which are genuinely non-negotiable and which ones you suspect are just the prejudices of one camp or another — including possibly mine.

I want the motivating cases drawn from the three Athenian failures, because between them they seem to me to cover failure modes the modern discussion doesn't treat as one problem:

- Mytilene: Cleon gets the assembly to decree the slaughter of the whole adult male population, they sleep on it, reopen the debate the next day, and Diodotus wins the reversal by a hair — but wins it by refusing to argue the Mytileneans deserve mercy and arguing only that a massacre is bad policy. Then they execute a thousand or so of the ringleaders anyway. And the reversal only works at all because a second trireme physically outruns the first one.
- Sicily: Nicias is asked to advise on the expedition, opposes it, and when opposing it fails he tries to kill it by demanding an enormous force — and the assembly votes him a bigger one. He ends up commanding a war he argued against, with his chief rival pulled off the campaign to answer charges at home, and at the end he takes his diviners' word about a lunar eclipse and sits still for the better part of a month while getting out is still possible.
- Socrates: tells the court the plain truth, declines to parade his weeping family, offers free meals at public expense as his counter-penalty, and the margin for death gets wider, not narrower. And here too a ship's schedule decides when the sentence is carried out — the execution waits on the sacred voyage to Delos.

Requirements I'd expect to see, and tell me where I'm wrong: does the theory have to handle irreversability — i.e. should the burden of proof an expert insists on scale with how hard the decision is to undo, and is that an epistemic requirement or just a temperamental preference of careful people like me? Does it have to handle the credibility discount, the fact that in a room which assumes every speaker has an angle, the plain truth only gets believed if it's dressed up as interest — that's basically Diodotus's complaint about his own assembly? Does it have to say what an expert does when the experts a body actually defers to are soothsayers, and how a body is supposed to notice that its recognized experts have a bad track record in time for the noticing to matter? Does it have to handle second-order effects, the way an honest warning changes the audience and makes the outcome worse? Does it have to distinguish being right from being effective, and if so, say which of the two the expert is on the hook for? Does it have to account for the physical logistics of reversing a decision already in motion — the trireme, the Delos ship — or is that engineering detail rather than philosophy? My suspicion is it isn't detail at all, but argue with me.

And the bit I most want: if some subset of these requirements can't be met together, say so and show me exactly where the squeeze is. My own hunch is that sincerity, effectiveness and not-manipulating-your-audience is a pick-two situation, and that Diodotus, Nicias and Socrates each give up a different corner and each pay for it in a different currency. If you think my trilemma is sloppy, knock it down — thirty-five years of peer review left me with no ego about this sort of thing.

Purpose, since what counts as adequate depends what the theory is for: I want a spec good enough to hand to a young scientist in my old lab who is about to start testifying to a state legislature that does not want to hear what she has to say, and where the votes happen weeks later behind closed doors. A theory that only describes how experts function, with no grip on what she should do when the room is hostile and the clock is running, is worthless to me.

A table is fine for the requirements themselves, but I want the conflicts in prose.
