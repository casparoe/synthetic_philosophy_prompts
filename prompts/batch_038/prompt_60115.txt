ok so I just spent like three hours arguing with a friend about Turing's "child programme" bit at the end of the 1950 Mind paper — you know, where he says instead of trying to simulate the adult mind you should build something like a child's mind and then put it through an education process, with the teacher supplying rewards and punishments, some kind of curriculum, and then this residual category he brings in for random elements / other experience. My friend's take is that the whole thing is just a design methodology in disguise: a plea for search over architectures plus a credit-assignment signal, and the "education" talk is decorative. My take is that the analogy is load-bearing, and that it's actually a claim about a *relation* — education is a two-place (or three-place, once you count the randomness) thing, and it matters who is on which end of it.

I want you to settle this by running the analogy against a real episode, hard. The episode: New York City public schools — biggest district in the country — blocked ChatGPT on district devices and networks in the first week of January 2023, a few weeks after launch. The stated reasons were negative impacts on student learning plus worries about safety and accuracy of the content; it wasn't a total prohibition, schools could request access for staff and students. Then about four months later, in mid-May 2023, the chancellor publicly reversed course in an op-ed, said something to the effect that students are going to work in a world where understanding generative AI is crucial, and the district started pushing teacher toolkits and examples of classroom use instead. Lots of other districts did the ban-then-flip thing around the same time. If you want other facts about the episode, flag that you're assuming them rather than inventing specifics.

So: analyze the NYC ban-and-flip as a case of Turing's child-programme-and-education picture, and then tell me where the picture stops applying. Be disciplined about the mapping before you get interpretive. Who is the teacher in this episode, who is the child, what plays the role of the curriculum, what plays the role of the rewards-and-punishments signal, and what — if anything — is the "other experience"/random element? For each of those slots I want you to say straight out whether it has a real referent, a strained one, or none at all. There's a term I've been using in the argument that you should pressure-test rather than adopt: I call it the frozen child problem — the idea that the district was handed something that looks exactly like an educable pupil but whose dispositions can't actually be changed by the person doing the educating, so what's left isn't teaching, it's handling. Is that a genuine disanalogy with Turing's scheme, or does Turing's own picture already contain the resources to absorb it (the unpredictability of a well-designed learning machine, the fact that he's not promising the educator control)?

Then run it the other direction. Does the child-programme reading *predict* anything about why the January panic happened and why it collapsed by May — e.g. that treating a partly-educated, still-being-shaped system as a finished adult mind generates exactly the cheating-and-accuracy panic, and that the panic dissolves once the adults realize the thing is being updated underneath them by someone else? Or is that just a story I'm telling after the fact, and the real explanation of the flip is mundane (pressure from vendors, fear of being seen as behind, a new chancellor's PR instincts)? Say which. And be honest about the asymmetry that's bugging me: the actual educating in 2023 was happening somewhere else entirely — a vendor, using a huge distributed workforce giving thumbs up and down, doing precisely the reward-and-punishment shaping Turing described, while the classroom teacher got a read-only artifact and a lesson plan. Does that make my friend right that Turing's education talk reduces to a training methodology, or does it make the point sharper, because the relation is real but the school was locked out of one end of it?

Finally, and this is the part I care most about: Turing's scheme has no slot for a *pupil*. The machine is the child and the human is the teacher, full stop. In the NYC case there's a third human being in the room who is simultaneously being educated and doing the educating, and the thing in the middle is the machine. Reconstruct what Turing's picture would look like with that third term added — who teaches whom, and what counts as the reward signal when the outputs are being consumed by a kid — and then tell me whether the district's eventual policy (toolkits, examples, "embrace it") is a coherent answer to that four-way arrangement or just a gesture. End with an actual verdict on my argument with my friend: decorative metaphor or load-bearing relation, and which pieces of the episode decide it. Don't hedge into "both readings have merit" unless you can show me the specific feature of the case that has merit on both sides.
