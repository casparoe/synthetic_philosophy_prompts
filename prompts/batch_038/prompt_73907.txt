our seminar ends next week and instead of a summary i want to close the term on hindsight bias / the narrative fallacy with a poem. the bit i most want to land is that hindsight bias isn't one thing but three — memory distortion (I swear I said it would turn out this way), inevitablity (it had to), and foreseeability (anyone paying attention could have seen it coming) — and that the last two are what make the past read like a plot instead of a pile of accidents.

form: two sonnets back to back, 28 lines total, both about sarajevo on 28 june 1914.

first sonnet, the day from inside: present tense, no plot, just the farce. the bomb earlier that morning bouncing off the folded-back hood of the archduke's car and going off under the car behind; the archduke insisting he's still going to the hospital to visit the men it injured; nobody telling the drivers the route has changed; the lead car turning up Franz Josef Street anyway; the shout from the back seat, the stop, the car reversing and jamming; a nineteen-year-old standing four or five paces off at the corner by the delicatessen because he'd more or less given up an hour earlier. all contingency, no meaning, and if parts of it are faintly comic that's fine — the day really was that shape.

second sonnet, the same day as retold by a textbook in, say, 1934. here's the constraint i actually care about: the second sonnet must reuse the first sonnet's fourteen end-words as its own end-words, in the same order. identical material, re-emplotted; the rhyme scheme is the plot getting imposed on it. and the historian is permitted to get two of them wrong — one end-word comes back slightly altered, as though he's misremembering his own earlier line, and one comes back as a near-homophone or the other sense of the same word. that's the memory-distortion leg of the triad and i want it audible, not explained or footnoted in the poem.

one more thing: the volta of the second sonnet should be a grammatical event rather than a change of subject — the moment the verbs slide out of what happened and into the modals of necessity (had to, was bound to, could only have, would inevitably). creeping determinism as something you hear in the mood, not something the poem announces.

and the second sonnet has to be the better poem. more coherent, more moving, better shapen. that's the whole argument: the seduction isn't that the tidy version is ugly, it's that it's lovely.

please don't resolve the tension and don't tack on a couplet about how we're all biased — the last line should just be the historian doing his job well, and leave it there.
