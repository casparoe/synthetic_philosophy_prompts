I am revising a section of a thesis chapter on the principle of indifference, written for readers who have already met Bertrand's chord problem and know the three classical selection procedures and the three answers they yield. The section advances a claim I am no longer confident I can defend, and I would like a careful conceptual account of where it fails, or of how it might be repaired.

The claim is this: Bertrand's paradox is an artifact of infinite idealization. In any genuinely finite version of the problem the principle of indifference delivers a single determinate answer, so the paradox cannot tell against the principle itself; it tells only against applying the principle to uncountable outcome spaces in the absence of a specified measure.

To support this I constructed three finite analogues. In each, the circle has radius R and an equilateral triangle is inscribed in it, so that the target quantity is fixed in advance: a chord strictly longer than a side of that triangle, hence longer than √3 R.

(A) Polygon chords. Inscribe a regular n-gon and choose an unordered pair of distinct vertices uniformly from the C(n,2) available pairs; the chord is the segment joining them. For n = 6m the probability that the chord is strictly longer than √3 R works out to (6m − 3)/(18m − 3) — so 1/5 for the hexagon, 3/11 for the dodecagon, tending to 1/3 as m grows.

(B) Radial grid. Choose one of n equally spaced radii uniformly, and independently choose one of m equally spaced positions along that radius uniformly; the chord is the one perpendicular to that radius at that position. For any n, the probability tends to 1/2 as m grows.

(C) Lattice midpoints. Take the square lattice of spacing h and choose uniformly among those lattice points falling inside the circle; the chord is the one whose midpoint is the chosen point. As h → 0 the probability tends to 1/4.

My thought was that each of these is a finite sample space to which indifference applies uniquely and unproblematically: nothing in the statement of the problem distinguishes one vertex pair from another, one radial grid position from another, or one interior lattice point from another. Yet the three limits reproduce the three classical answers exactly, and the schemes disagree with one another at fixed finite parameters as well as in the limit.

Please explain the conceptual situation, in roughly a thousand words, assuming elementary probability but no measure theory and no familiarity with the secondary literature. Specifically:

1. Say precisely where the indeterminacy has gone. Has it been eliminated, or merely relocated into the choice of a finite sample space? If relocated, explain why that relocation does not rescue my thesis — or, if it does, identify the further assumptions the thesis needs.

2. Distinguish two possible grounds for applying indifference to a finite set: a symmetry ground, on which some group of transformations of the sample space permutes the outcomes and thereby forbids unequal weights; and an ignorance ground, on which the absence of differentiating information is itself the license. Then assess, scheme by scheme, which ground is actually available. Be concrete: in (A), what does the dihedral symmetry license — equal weight to all pairs, or only equal weight among pairs sharing a separation, leaving the relative weight of different separations open? In (C), what remains of symmetry once the disc has been replaced by a point set whose exact symmetries are only those of the lattice?

3. Address whether my three schemes can even be assessed against an invariance-based response of Jaynes's kind, which singles out the random-radius procedure by demanding scale and translation invariance of the chord distribution. I suspect they cannot, since each scheme destroys precisely those invariances; I want the reasoning spelled out, and I want to know whether that renders my construction irrelevant to the invariance debate or, on the contrary, makes it a clean test of indifference understood as a rule about ignorance rather than symmetry.

4. Distinguish two disagreements visible here: the disagreement among (A), (B), (C) at fixed finite parameters, and the disagreement among their limits. Are these the same phenomenon wearing different clothes? In particular, does the behaviour of (A) at n = 6 — where chords of separation n/3 have length exactly √3 R and so fail the strictly-longer test, dragging the answer down to 1/5 — carry any conceptual lesson, or is it purely a finite-size artifact I should suppress in the chapter?

5. Explain in what sense, if any, each scheme answers a question other than the one Bertrand asked, and whether the limit of answers to a sequence of surrogate questions can ever settle the original. I want this made vivid without machinery: the reader should come away able to say why taking limits and fixing an outcome space do not commute here.

6. Finally, state plainly which of my formulations above are false, question-begging, or merely loose, and give the strongest version of the thesis I can still defend — including exactly what must be conceded to the classical response that the chord problem is well posed only once a method of selection has been specified.

No references, suggested readings, or bibliography; I want the argument, not the citations.
