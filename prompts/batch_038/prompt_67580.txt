I am preparing a session for my graduate social-ontology seminar on the metaphysics of generations, and I want an independent judgment before I commit my own view to a handout. The question I am putting to the seminar is narrow: when we say that Millennials or Boomers are a generation, what sort of thing, if anything, are we talking about? Not whether the labels are useful in journalism or marketing — I grant that they are — but what the world has to contain for those labels to be more than noise.

Please score and rank five candidate ontologies. I will describe them, and I will also describe the four tests I want them scored against, because the tests are mine and I would rather you apply them than substitute better ones of your own.

The candidates:

A. Natural kind. Generations are mind-independent divisions in the population, with birth windows tracking genuine discontinuities in demographic and historical exposure, in something like the way geological periods track discontinuities in the strata.

B. Interactive social kind. Generations are real, but their reality is sustained by our own classificatory practices: the label alters the dispositions and self-descriptions of the people it collects, and the kind persists in virtue of that feedback loop.

C. Historical individual. A generation is not a kind at all but a particular — a bounded, temporally extended entity whose members are bound by causal-historical continuity rather than by resemblance, on the model of those who hold that a species, a war, or a city is an individual rather than a class.

D. Statistical aggregate. A generation is a bookkeeping device over birth years, no more ontologically loaded than an income quintile; everything real is said of individuals, and the cohort term merely collects them.

E. Ideological posit. Generation talk is not just dispensable but does specific work: it re-describes period effects and class position as the intrinsic property of an age group, so the correct treatment is elimination rather than reduction.

The tests, each to be scored 0–10:

1. The Recruitment Test. Mannheim held that mere chronological contemporaneity is not enough to make a generation, and that a shared location in the historical process is required — with the consequence that not every cohort acquires a distinctive consciousness at all. Can the account explain why someone born outside the window who underwent the same formative events does not thereby become a member, or, if it does admit such a person, why the admission is principled rather than arbitrary?

2. The Quiet Window Test. Take a twenty-year stretch in which nothing formative occurs and social change is slow. Does the account still deliver a generation there? If it does, it owes us an explanation of why a generation with no distinctive profile is the same sort of thing as one with a vivid profile; if it does not, it owes us an explanation of why the demographer's continuous series of cohorts nonetheless tracks nothing.

3. The Feedback Test. Once a label circulates, the people it picks out begin to answer surveys and describe their own biographies in its terms. Which accounts can absorb this without making the identity conditions of the kind hostage to the reach of the label?

4. The Bookkeeping Test. Cohort is a linear function of period and age, so age, period, and cohort effects cannot be separately estimated without further assumptions; and the classic sociological picture has members of a generation internally stratified by class, region, and culture. Does the account claim more explanatory work for generations than these constraints can license — or, in the case of E, does it claim too little, given that stratification is itself a claim about the distribution of properties within a cohort?

Finally, an Overall score 0–10 for each candidate, answering: how well does this account answer the seminar's question, all things considered? Then give me a strict ranking of A through E with no ties.

Please organize the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

Verdict (about 120 words). The strict ranking, one sentence per candidate, plus your Overall scores.

Scorecard (a table only, no prose). Rows A–E, columns for the four tests and Overall. Halves are permitted; do not round everything to the middle of the scale to be safe.

Justifications (about 150 words per candidate). For each, where it loses points and on which test it loses them — not a general appreciation of the view.

Objections to my rubric (about 250 words). Which of the four tests is ill-posed, question-begging, or redundant with another; which candidate I have described unfairly or as a straw man; and whether a single ranking is the right output at all. If you think the exercise rests on a confused premise — that "generation" does not pick out one thing across these sentences, so that scoring ontologies against a shared rubric is a category mistake — say so plainly here and tell me what you would rank instead. Do not score politely along with me and bury the reservation.

What would settle it (about 150 words). The one consideration that would demote your first-ranked candidate below your second: an argument or a finding, not another intuitive example.

I am not asking for sources, references, or a reading list — I know the literature and cannot check anything you cite, so please argue from the substance alone.
