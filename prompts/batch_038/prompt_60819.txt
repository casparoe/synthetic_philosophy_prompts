For something like fifteen years I have read the rationalist blogs, forums, and comment threads, and one cluster of results has always done heavy lifting there: Aumann's agreement theorem, together with the Milgrom–Stokey result that under a common prior and common knowledge of rationality no speculative trade is possible. The standard use is normative. Two people who have genuinely shared all their evidence cannot rationally keep disagreeing; therefore persistent disagreement is a tell of bias, of unequal information, or of bad faith; therefore the remedy is to pool evidence and update toward one another. I have watched this reading get operationalized in argument norms, dispute-resolution schemes, and prediction-market advocacy.

I think it is backwards, and I would like you to make the strongest possible case for the reverse reading. The thesis: those theorems are not norms governing disagreement but characterizations of the preconditions under which disagreement has determinate content at all. Where agents do not share a prior, one party's announced belief is not a claim about the world that the other can coherently fold in as evidence about the world; it is evidence about the other's prior, and treating it as the former involves a category mistake. On this reading the theorems discipline no actual arguer. They instead explain why disagreement among agents without a common prior is not merely permitted but structurally stable, why evidence-sharing fails in predictable places rather than by accident, and how two fully rational Bayesians can be driven further apart by exchanging true information.

Please argue for this, and in doing so:

1. Identify precisely which assumption carries the normative weight, and show why relaxing it leaves the mathematics untouched while removing its availability as a standard for real disagreement.
2. Confront the obvious reply: that an appeal to differing priors is an excuse available to anyone, and so a reading on which it is always available cannot be the correct one. Say whether the thesis can be formulated so that it genuinely risks something, and specify what.
3. Reconcile the thesis with the classical convergence result showing that two Bayesians in a finite state space who take turns announcing their posteriors about an event must end up agreeing, and must know that they agree. Explain why that process, when it succeeds, is not a counterexample.
4. State what a person holding this view would actually do differently in an argument, as against what the standard reading tells them to do.

I would rather have an argument that survives its best objection than a confident one, so mark clearly where you think the thesis overreaches.
