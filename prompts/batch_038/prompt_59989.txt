I chair the ethics committee of a regional transplant center, and next month I am giving the six-minute principal address at a ceremony honoring a non-directed living kidney donor: a thirty-four-year-old high school science teacher who donated to a stranger fourteen months ago and has since declined every media request. About two hundred people will be in the room — the transplant surgeons and coordinators, her husband and two teenage daughters, her mother, the recipient, who speaks immediately after me, and a dozen people from development and donor recruitment who are paying for the dinner. Two others in the room were approached about donating within the last three years; one was ruled out on medical grounds and one declined without ever saying why. Neither should be able to tell that they are being addressed.

I did a year of graduate work in ethics before moving into administration, so I have the vocabulary. The problem is that the vocabulary is precisely what has to be kept out of the room. Please write the speech, treating what follows as load-bearing constraints rather than preferences.

1. The speech must make it unmistakable that nothing she did was owed — by her, or by anyone else in that room — and must not convert that into an implicit demand. There is a puzzle I assume you know: if she did the best thing available to her and could do it, it is hard to see how it was not required; and if it was not required, it is hard to see how praise amounts to more than a report of our taste. The speech cannot argue about this, but it must not land on either horn. It may not say that any decent person would have done the same, and it may not retreat into the safe formulation that this was simply her personal choice — that is what the draft from development said, and she crossed it out.

2. She insists the sacrifice framing is false in her case. She was not frightened, she was back in the classroom in three weeks, and she has told me twice that this was the easiest good thing she has ever done and that she would find a week of jury duty more unpleasant. Most of the praise given to acts like hers lives in the cost, and she has taken the cost away. Find something to praise that survives that. She has also forbidden the words hero, selfless, and angel, and forbidden the construction "not everyone could do this, but…" — she says that sentence either shames the room or lets it off the hook, and she wants neither done to it.

3. The recipient has written to me that she cannot bear another year of being told she owes something she can never repay, and that she has begun to dread other people's gratitude. My speech expresses the room's gratitude, and hers follows mine within a minute. Give her something she can stand up and receive without being handed a debt — and do not do it by pretending the gratitude is not there.

4. Her mother's opposition was about risk: the operation might have killed her daughter, and her granddaughters might have lost their mother. I have been turning over whether some of the credit belongs to the people who absorbed that risk and let it happen anyway, and whether an act can be counted as beyond duty when part of its cost falls on others who never chose it. If you can handle this in a sentence or two of ordinary language, do. But she has asked that the evening not become about anyone's permission, so do not promote her mother to co-honoree.

5. A colleague at the head table has argued to me in writing that this whole way of talking is confused: beneficence is a duty that leaves you latitude about when and toward whom to discharge it, so what we are celebrating is not moral surplus but an ordinary duty discharged spectacularly well by someone who happened to have the means and the temperament for it. I do not think he is right and the speech cannot debate him. But include one line that makes clear the distinction is being drawn deliberately rather than by accident — a line he would have to argue with, not merely correct.

6. Development wants a call to action and a pointer to the registry. She has refused to be recruited with. Find something true they can live with: an acknowledgment that other people can help and that helping looks different for different people, without becoming an instruction to anyone.

Six minutes, so roughly eight hundred and fifty words, written for the mouth rather than the page. No terms of art: the words duty, obligation, and supererogation should not appear, and the distinction has to be carried entirely by concrete sentences about what she did and what nobody asked of anybody. She is funny, and the room knows it — eleven years of teaching have left her unable to resist a joke at her own expense — so a speech with no humor in it will read as a monument rather than as an evening. After the speech, add no more than a hundred words telling me which two of these constraints pulled hardest against each other and what you gave up to satisfy them; I want to know that before I read it aloud.
