Thirty-eight years in a geophysics lab, retired four now, and I read philosophy instead of journals these days. I've been writing down my gut verdicts about when distrust of an official account is actually warranted, and I want to know what principle, if any, fits them.

- The company that builds the sensor publishes a paper showing the sensor hits spec. I discount it. Warranted, I think.
- A lab with no stake reanalyzes the same raw data and gets the same answer. Now I believe it.
- The county says the water is fine, three households say it tastes like pennies, a university lab finds lead. Siding with the households over the county: warranted.
- Someone says that university report is cooked up to win grant money, on no evidence except that grants exist. Not warranted.
- Nobody ever publishes the trials where the drug did nothing, so I infer from the missing half of the record. Warranted.
- Someone else infers from a missing record too: no whistleblower ever came forward, so there must be nothing to blow the whistle on. I say not warranted, but honestly I can't articulate why these two absences differ.
- Lots of people would gain if the consensus on warming were wrong, and I still trust the consensus. But I also tell my nephew that dismissing it because "they all fund each other" is lazy. That pair feels inconsistent in me.

My own attempt at a rule, which I've taken to calling the coordination-cost test: the more separate people who'd have to keep a story straight, and the longer they'd have to keep it straight, the less believable a cover-up becomes, so warranted distrust should fall as the required conspiracy grows. Trouble is it seems to cut against my water case and my grant-money case, where I do distrust a pretty wide circle.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I havn't thought of — try it on a former student of mine who's convinced the journal that rejected his paper did it to protect a rival's priority claim. And tell me straight which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, rather than bending the rule to save them.
