Three weeks of my second-year philosophy of mind seminar have been spent building the case against physicalism — Mary, zombies, causal closure — and about half the class has arrived at epiphenomenalism with real enthusiasm. Thursday's eighty-minute session is on the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and what I want from you is the plan for it, not the explanation itself. The claim I need them to feel is that if experience makes no causal difference, the neural machinery that produces my sentence about my pain would have produced that same sentence had the pain been absent; so either the accuracy of such reports is a standing coincidence, or the reports are not really about the experience at all — and the premise doing this work is the very one that powered the zombie argument they have just embraced.

Tell me what has to be in place before that can be felt rather than merely assented to (I suspect something about what fixes the content of a report, and about the gap between reliability and non-accidental reliability), which single case should carry the session and what that case must have and must lack — Mary and the zombie are both spent currency by this point — and where in the sequence to intercept each response I expect: the brain state caused the utterance so nothing is missing; this is just causal closure renamed; we are being pushed into scepticism about our own minds; therefore qualia are illusory. Close with one question that would show whether they have grasped the paradox rather than a refutation of it, and tell me which of the standard escapes (acquaintance, phenomenal concepts, the metaphysics of causation) I should cut and whether to announce the cut.
