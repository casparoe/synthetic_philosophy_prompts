I'm co-teaching a law-and-philosophy seminar this term with a colleague over in criminology, and we've hit a wall on shaming sentences — you know the sort, the judge who orders a defendant to stand outside the post office with a sign around his neck, or the publicity-and-registry models of sanctioning. I wanted to put Bernard Williams and Ruth Benedict on the schedule and spend two weeks on whether a community can deliberately cultivate shame as a moral resource. My colleague thinks that whole line is romantic nonsense, and his argument runs through an analogy he's extremely attached to. Guilt, he says, is a courtroom; shame is a crowd. A courtroom has rules of evidence, a defense, a tariff of proportionate penalties, and above all an ending — you serve the sentence and the matter is closed. A crowd has no procedure, no counsel, no scale of proportionality, and no exit: it doesn't adjudicate what you did, it settles what you are, and it goes on looking. From that he draws two conclusions he treats as a single package: (i) guilt is the better moral emotion, because only guilt's tribunal is fair to the person in it; and (ii) a modern society couldn't choose shame as its register even if it wanted to, because a crowd presupposes a thick face-to-face community, and anonymous mass society has dissolved the audience shame depends on. Hence shaming sanctions are both unjust and unavailable, and policy should speak only to guilt.

My problem is that the analogy feels rigged at both ends and I can't quite articulate where. On the guilt side he's picturing a well-run court, but the actual tribunal of conscience is famously the opposite: no defense counsel, no statute of limitations, no proportionality review, and a judge who is also the accused — the psychoanalytic picture of a conscience far crueler than any court it imitates seems to me to cut exactly against him, and Williams himself is happy to concede guilt can be excessive and irrational. On the shame side he's stipulating an actual, hostile, external audience, whereas the internalized-other move — the idea that shame's spectator can be imagined, internalized, or idealized, somebody you have never met or the community you'd like to belong to — is supposed to block precisely that stipulation. And then there's the awkward fact that courtrooms are spectatorship devices: the gallery, the public trial, the mugshot. So I don't know whether he's got a real disanalogy or just a flattering description of guilt and an unflattering one of shame.

Could you audit it for me properly? I'd like an explicit correspondence table — what in the crowd/courtroom pairing plays the role of the judge, the audience, the evidence, the sentence, the acquittal, the fact that guilt is act-focused while shame is self-focused — then the disanalogies sorted into the ones that actually matter for the use he's putting it to and the ones that are just decorative, plus a clear statement of which features of the source domains the analogy quietly imports that the targets don't have. Then: do (i) and (ii) survive separately, once the disanalogies are on the table? He sells them as a package and I suspect they come apart — it's perfectly possible for one to fall and the other to stand, and I want to know which. If you think there's a better analogy for the same territory (something that keeps the audience point without the kangaroo-court problem), give me that too. Also please tell me how confident you are in the main claims, and single out the one point in your answer you're least sure of — I want to know where you're extrapolating rather than reasoning. End with a last line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of HOLDS, HOLDS-PARTLY, or FAILS, then a semicolon and one sentence of ten words or fewer saying what does or doesn't survive.
