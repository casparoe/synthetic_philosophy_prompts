Third year of a politics PhD here, writing up the chapter on the royal prerogative, and there's a thought experiment I cant stop circling. Suppose Bagehot — The English Constitution, 1867, the dignified vs the efficient parts, the sovereign keeping the right to be consulted, to encourage and to warn — got dropped into the present and had to rule on five things that all happened long after he died. I'll spell the cases out because I don't want you half-remembering them:

1. Belgium, April 1990: King Baudouin said his Catholic conscience wouldn't let him sign the law liberalising abortion, so the government invoked the constitutional clause on the impossibility of reigning, the ministers took over his powers for roughly 36 hours and promulgated it themselves, and parliament then declared him able to reign again.
2. UK, September 2019: the Queen prorogued Parliament for about five weeks on Boris Johnson's advice, and the Supreme Court unanimously held the prorogation unlawful and of no effect. Nobody seriously argued she had any discretion to refuse the advice.
3. UK, reported in 2021: under the Queen's Consent procedure the palace gets advance sight of bills touching the prerogative or the monarch's private interests, and it emerged that her own lawyers had persuaded ministers to redraft a transparency bill so her private landholdings wouldn't have to be disclosed. More than a thousand bills have gone through the procedure.
4. Japan, 2016–2019: the Imperial House Law has no abdication provision and the Emperor is barred from making political statements, so Akihito went on television and dwelt on his age and failing health in a way everyone read as a request to step down; the Diet passed a one-off law and he abdicated in April 2019, the first abdication since 1817.
5. Sweden since 1974: the monarch was taken out of government formation entirely — the Speaker of the Riksdag proposes a prime minister, the king's functions are purely representative, and the government just keeps him informed. So he doesn't even hold Bagehot's three rights as a matter of constitutional design, and yet the monarchy stays very popular.

Here's the thing I actually want pressure-tested. I've been arguing in seminar that Bagehot's three rights are an epistemic claim and not a moral one — the sovereign gets a hearing because he has sat through forty years of state papers and has no electoral skin in the game, not because he has a specially good conscience or any virtue of office. If that's right, then Belgium and the Consent business fall clean outside his framework, since both turn on the monarch's private self, his conscience and his wallet, and Bagehot supposedly has nothing to say about either.

So give me exactly five numbered points, one per case, one or two sentences each, saying what Bagehot would actually make of it and which bit of his own theory makes that answer awkward for him. No introduction, no summary, no closing paragraph. And if a case breaks my epistemic-rights thesis, say so right inside that point instead of saving it up.
