I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and a friend of mine — call him D. — has spent the last three years working on insect welfare. He has sent me an argument that he says demolishes the machinery I have spent fifteen years defending, and after a week of staring at it I still cannot locate the slip. I would like a diagnosis rather than reassurance.

The argument as he gave it to me. The numbers are his own back-of-envelope; he insists they are placeholders and that nothing turns on their exact size, and I have kept them because I want to know whether that insistence is honest.

1. An act is permissible only if no available alternative has strictly lower expected moral disvalue. Expected disvalue is the sum, over every individual the act affects, of the probability that the individual is sentient multiplied by the disvalue its suffering or death carries, with no discount for phylogenetic distance and no ceiling on how many small quantities may be summed.

2. Let p be the probability that a given insect is sentient, and w the disvalue of an insect's death as a fraction of the disvalue of an adult human's death. Any assignment that is not obviously deranged gives p and w small but strictly positive values; D. suggests p ≈ 10⁻³ and w ≈ 10⁻⁵, hence about 10⁻⁸ human-life-equivalents per insect killed.

3. A single broad-spectrum application on a row-crop field kills on the order of 10⁶ to 10⁸ arthropods per hectare. One application on one hectare therefore carries between 10⁻² and 1 human-life-equivalents of expected disvalue, while the entire benefit of that application — one hectare's protected yield for one season — is worth much less than a human life; call it 10⁻² in the same units. At no point in the range is there any margin.

4. Nor is there a margin in principle. For any positive p and w, however tiny, there is a number of insects N large enough that N·p·w outweighs the benefit of sparing them, and agriculture, which treats hundreds of millions of hectares, always supplies such an N. Consequently no assignment of small positive weight to insect sentience can leave insecticide use permissible.

5. The arithmetic reaches well beyond the field. A large commercial black soldier fly plant — the sort that turns out ten or twenty thousand tonnes of fresh larvae a year for poultry and aquaculture feed — goes through something like 10¹¹ individuals annually, which on his own numbers is a thousand human-life-equivalents a year, and there are dozens of such plants with more under construction. Global cropland insecticide use kills on the order of 10¹⁵ insects a year, which is some 10⁷ human-life-equivalents annually, against a world homicide count in the hundreds of thousands.

6. Hence insecticide use and insect farming must stop immediately and globally. Since that cessation would cost billions of human lives through crop failure, and since no one can in any case comply with a demand of that scope, premise 1 is false: expected-value ethics is a machine for generating obligations no one can meet, and it mugs itself.

7. He offers two corroborations, which I find harder to swallow than the conclusion. First, the entomologist who lethally collects a few thousand specimens a year in order to estimate p has, on these numbers, a moral record comparable to a serial killer's — and her work is precisely what would improve our estimate of p. Second, the consumer who has bought organic for twenty years has been making things worse, because organic yields are lower per hectare and organic standards still permit Bt, spinosad, and pyrethrins; the argument cannot distinguish her from the conventional buyer and in fact ranks her below him.

What I want from you. I cannot accept 6, and there are two things I would rather keep than lose: the comparative expected-value test in 1, which is load-bearing for the whole multiply-and-compare way of thinking that governs how I give money and spend my time; and the claim embedded in 5 that insect farming and insecticide use are grotesquely underweighted in ordinary moral arithmetic, which I believe is true even if the reductio built on it is not. I am prepared to pay for keeping them. I want the bill itemized in advance.

My reading group proposed five exits, and I want each priced rather than merely listed: deny that insects are sentient; cap the aggregation, so that small harms stop summing past some point or insect interests sit lexically below human ones; hold that the sign of w is itself unsettled, since r-selected lives with near-total juvenile mortality may be net-negative and death may not be a harm; insist that the alternative to spraying is not zero insect deaths but a different and possibly larger number of them, spread across more land and more generations; and abandon expected-value reasoning in ethics altogether in favour of something that refuses to multiply by tiny probabilities. If you think we have missed an exit better than all five, add it and price it too.

Please answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** — two to four sentences: the step you reject and the name of the error, or, if you judge the chain valid, the premise you would give up.
2. **Diagnosis** — about 300 words. Walk the chain and say where it fails, separating the moves that are sound from the ones that are not, and tell me explicitly whether D. is right that the specific numbers do no work.
3. **Why it looked innocent** — about 200 words. Explain the pull of the step you rejected, and state what further premise would have made it valid.
4. **Repair options and their prices** — about 500 words. Take the five exits above, plus any you add, and for each say what else I would have to surrender in order to adopt it.
5. **Does the reductio land?** — about 250 words. Whether 6 follows from anything that survives your correction, and how his case compares with the mugging structure he thinks it shares.
6. **Where I land** — about 150 words. What you think I should actually believe and do about insecticides, and which single empirical or philosophical question, if settled, would most change your answer.
