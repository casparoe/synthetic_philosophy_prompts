I am drafting an objection to L. A. Paul's argument about transformative choices, and I want a purpose-built case to test the objection before I commit it to paper. The objection is that the two conditions she bundles together do independent work. For this purpose, understand the conditions as follows: an experience is *epistemically* transformative when it teaches the agent something about what the experience is like that she could not have acquired in advance by testimony, description, imagination, or inference from her other experiences; it is *personally* transformative when it changes her core preferences, her values, or her sense of who she is. Paul's discovery problem holds that when both conditions are met, the agent cannot rationally make the choice by ordinary expected-utility reasoning, because she cannot know the utilities she will have. My objection is that the epistemic leg alone can generate the failure, and that the personal leg is doing nothing distinctive. I need a scenario that either shows this or shows me that it doesn't.

Build me a single decision case, facing an ordinary adult, with the following four properties.

1. The choice is epistemically transformative: there is a fact about what the outcome would be like that the agent cannot get in advance, and — this matters — the missing fact bears directly on how she would evaluate the outcome. Do not build the gap out of an incidental sensory detail. The standard cheat here is to put the inaccessibility in what something tastes like or what a landscape looks like, where the gap changes nothing about the ranking of the options; a case built that way does not meet this specification.
2. The choice is *not* personally transformative: her core preferences, values, and self-conception come out of it as they went in, and at the moment of choice she can correctly predict that this will be so. No preference reversal, no new set of ends, no change of character.
3. Expected-utility reasoning nonetheless fails to determine a unique rational choice, and it fails specifically on the cardinal side. Her *ordinal* ranking of the outcomes is fixed and fully known to her in advance; the *intensities* are not, and the missing phenomenal information is exactly what would settle them.
4. The cardinality is doing real work rather than sitting inert. So the case must be one where magnitudes matter — a risky prospect, a probability threshold, or a third option — such that at some explicitly stated probability the rational verdict flips.

Constraints on the setting: mundane and realistic, not science-fictional. No parenthood, no religious conversion, no emigration, no career change, no marriage, and no medical or surgical procedure. No technology that does not already exist. The stakes must be of a kind a normal person could actually face and could actually decide about. The scenario itself is to be no longer than one paragraph.

Then do the demonstration, and do it in this order.

First, walk through properties 1 to 4 one at a time and show the case satisfies each, rather than asserting that it does. For property 2 in particular, say what her core preferences are and why the outcome leaves them untouched; it is easy to satisfy 2 only by vagueness about what the preferences are.

Second, state the decision problem explicitly: the acts, the states of the world, the outcomes, the ordinal ranking the agent has over them, and the precise point in that ranking at which the missing information bites. Then give the probability at which the verdict flips and show the arithmetic on both sides of it.

Third, the analysis. Name the single feature of the case that is doing the work in generating the failure, and say what happens to the verdict if you remove it. Two removals I specifically want: what happens if you add a third option that dominates on any plausible cardinal assignment, and what happens if you downgrade the missing information to something merely sensory. Then give me the strongest reply available to a defender of the two-legged view — not a strawman. I expect the serious reply to run roughly along the lines that a case with genuinely stable preferences cannot harbor the relevant gap, because a judgment about how much one will care about something is itself partly constituted by phenomenal knowledge of it, so any case that looks like mine is covertly a personal transformation. Reconstruct that reply as rigorously as you can and say whether it defeats the case.

Fourth, and connected to the third: say whether the residual difficulty in my case is a problem about transformative experience at all, or merely an instance of the familiar trouble of choosing under uncertainty about your own utility function — the kind of trouble that arises whenever anyone has to guess how much they will care about something. If it is merely the latter, say what, if anything, is left of my objection.

Finally, commit. I do not want a balanced survey of considerations on both sides with the weighing left to me. Give me a definite verdict on whether the two conditions are separable and whether the objection goes through as specified, with your reasons. If you conclude that the specification as written is unsatisfiable, or satisfiable only by a case that is trivial, say so plainly and show me where it breaks — do not quietly weaken one of properties 1 to 4 in order to make the construction go through. End with a brief note flagging any place where your reconstruction of Paul's argument, or of the objection I have attributed to myself, leans on a contested reading of it.
