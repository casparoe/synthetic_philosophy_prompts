I have been developing an argument about the standing to blame, and it now appears to establish considerably more than I am prepared to accept. I would like a diagnosis: which step actually fails, why it looked sound, and what each available repair costs.

Terminology. By blame I mean not merely a private negative evaluation, but holding a person's conduct up against a standard one endorses together with the address of a demand that they acknowledge the standard and conform to it. Call the following, as I will use the phrase, the Address Principle: one is entitled to address a moral demand to another only if one is oneself, at the time of the address, complying with demands of the same general kind.

The argument.

(1) Blame is essentially the address of a moral demand to its target, not merely the harboring of an evaluative attitude toward them.

(2) By the Address Principle, one is entitled to address such a demand only if one is oneself complying with demands of the same general kind.

(3) No one complies with all the moral demands they endorse, and any attempt to restrict "the same general kind" narrowly enough to leave someone compliant is arbitrary: there is no principled cutoff that does not simply encode the result one wanted.

(4) Therefore no one is entitled to address a moral demand to anyone.

(5) Since blame just is such an address, all blame is impermissible.

(6) It follows that hypocritical blame is not a distinctive wrong. The hypocrite does not fail where the sincere blamer succeeds; both fail alike, and hypocrisy is merely the visible symptom of a condition that is universal and therefore not diagnostic of anything.

(7) Therefore the moral community as ordinarily conceived — a network of mutual accountability in which members hold one another to shared standards — is impossible. What we have instead is a practice in which everyone addresses demands that no one is entitled to address.

I take (6) and (7) to be unacceptable. I would much rather retain (1) and (3) than (2), but I have no clear account of what I lose by giving (2) up: if compliance is not a condition on entitlement, it becomes hard to see why hypocrisy is a defect in the blaming at all rather than a defect in the hypocrite's character that leaves her blame untouched — and that alternative seems to let ordinary practice off the hook far too easily.

Two cases I want any diagnosis to handle.

Reform. Dana falsified expense reports for a decade and has genuinely stopped. She now reproaches Ellis for the same practice, and discloses her own history in the very act of reproaching him. On my argument her reproach is impermissible, and impermissible for the same reason as the reproach of someone still falsifying reports. That strikes me as exactly wrong: the disclosure appears to strengthen rather than weaken what she says.

Office. A court, an ombudsman, or an editor reproaches someone in a role the reproacher occupies, while being personally guilty of the same conduct. Does my argument reach institutional blame, or does holding an office supply a kind of compliance by proxy that escapes (4)? If the latter, I want to know what makes an office special, and whether that special feature could in principle be available to individuals.

Deliverable. Identify the step or steps at which the argument fails, and say whether the failure is a genuine equivocation or a valid inference resting on a premise I should simply refuse. For each of the obvious repairs — rejecting (1), rejecting (2), rejecting (3), or accepting (4) while denying that it yields impermissibility at (5) — explain what the repair preserves, what it forfeits, and in particular whether it can still account for the intuition that hypocrisy is a special way of going wrong in blaming rather than a general way of going wrong in living. Finally, say whether the Reform and Office cases are genuine counterexamples to the argument or artifacts of how I have stated it; if they are artifacts, tell me what I should have said instead so that they bite.
