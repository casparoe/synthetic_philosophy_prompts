I am a novelist, and a festival has asked me to set out in advance the moral-psychological assumptions my fiction runs on, so that a philosopher can take them apart in public. Before I commit myself to that, I should like the position audited privately. Here are the commitments, in roughly the order of how firmly I hold them.

(1) Shame and guilt differ in their objects, and the difference is not merely verbal. Shame concerns the whole self and its appearance — how one looks, and what one is, in the eyes of an observer; guilt concerns particular acts and the harm they have done to particular others. I hold that this distinction is real and does explanatory work in a life.

(2) I nevertheless reject the mid-century typology that divides whole peoples into shame-cultures and guilt-cultures. It is a relic, it flatters the classifier's own society, and I would not defend it for a moment.

(3) Shame requires an audience; guilt does not. The audience may be internalised — an imagined observer one has taken in — but there is one, and this is why shame is the heteronomous emotion and guilt the autonomous one. A person who behaves decently when unobserved and feels nothing either way is not, on that account, morally deficient; a person who feels guilt when no one will ever know is.

(4) I accept a familiar finding in moral psychology: shame-proneness tends to issue in concealment, withdrawal and outward-directed hostility, whereas guilt-proneness tends to issue in confession, apology and reparation. As a working assumption about how people actually behave, I treat shame as the emotion that makes a character worse and guilt as the one that makes a character better.

(5) At the same time I hold that guilt, precisely because it is tethered to identifiable acts and identifiable victims, cannot on its own reach what I most want my books to reach: complicity in arrangements from which one benefits without breaking any rule one acknowledges; obligations to the distant, the collective and the not-yet-born; the standing of one's whole way of life. For these, I think, a character needs shame, because shame concerns how one appears to a community, and communities are wider than one's intimates.

(6) A case from the novel I am writing, which I stand behind as both true and admirable. A man has grown comfortable inside an arrangement that profits from other people's misery; he has broken no rule he accepts, he feels no guilt, and I think he is right not to feel any, since guilt would be a confusion here. When the arrangement is exposed, he is undone by shame. Out of that shame — not out of any change of belief about what he owed — he dismantles the arrangement and makes restitution. I hold that he behaves well, and that the shame is the morally serious thing about him.

(7) A culture cannot choose its emotional repertoire by deliberation, because that repertoire is laid down by institutions, by child-rearing and by the stories a society tells about who may be ashamed of what, none of which is at the disposal of an argument. But I also hold that novelists do real work here: writing books is one of the few mechanisms by which a society changes what it is ashamed of, and I intend my own work to be such a mechanism.

Two things I should like from you. First, an audit rather than a lecture: identify which of these pull against one another, order the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost. Where a tension only looks like one, say so and say why it dissolves. Second, tell me which single revision would clean up the largest amount, and whether it is one I could make without dismantling the novels. Several of these commitments are load-bearing in the practical sense that scenes and characters depend on them, and a revision that left me unable to write the book described in (6) would not, for me, be a cheap one. Please do not assume I would surrender any particular item; I have no idea yet which of them I could live without.
