Six weeks ago I left an academic post to price contracts on a small desk that runs a market in questions with determinate answers that nobody currently knows: whether a specified four-hundred-line program halts within 10^20 steps, what the 10^40th decimal digit of π is, whether a named construction has a property that is decidable but not yet decided, whether a given formal system proves a given sentence within a stated proof-length budget. Each contract pays $1 if the sentence is true and $0 if it is false, and none of them carries an expiry date: they settle whenever the answer is established. My job is to quote prices, and the desk quotes both sides of the market at the same number.

Two weeks ago our outside auditor, who is a competent mathematician and enjoys this sort of thing, sent me the following argument, intended to show that our entire pricing policy is governed by a norm that nothing can satisfy. I cannot accept the conclusion. Neither I nor my colleagues have found the flaw. I set it out as precisely as I can.

Definitions. A price function P assigns to each sentence of the language of arithmetic a number in [0,1], the price at which the desk buys and sells the corresponding contract. A book against P is a finite set of such contracts, some bought from the desk and some sold to it, all at P's prices. A book is a sure loss for P if P is bound to come out behind once the sentences involved have their truth values. P is coherent if no book against it is a sure loss.

(1) Rational prices must be coherent. That is the content of the Dutch-book argument, and it is why the desk adopted coherence as its governing norm.

(2) If φ is true and P(φ) < 1, then anyone who buys the φ-contract from the desk at P(φ) is bound to come out ahead by 1 − P(φ). So coherence requires P(φ) = 1 whenever φ is true, and symmetrically P(φ) = 0 whenever φ is false.

(3) Therefore a coherent price function is arithmetically omniscient: it prices every sentence at its truth value.

(4) No finite agent is arithmetically omniscient. There are sentences we cannot settle at present, such as the halting question and the far digit above; and by the diagonal argument, for any agent whose verdicts can be listed, and which is sound, there is a sentence it cannot establish.

(5) Therefore no finite agent's price function is coherent, and by (1) every bounded agent is irrational: for each of us there is a finite book we would accept that guarantees our loss.

(6) Nothing in that turned on our being small. Given any agent whatsoever, however powerful, there is a sentence it fails to price at its truth value; so no possible agent is coherent.

(7) A norm that no possible agent can satisfy cannot be the norm in virtue of which prices are rational. So coherence is not a requirement of rationality.

(8) Yet the Dutch-book argument is the standard justification for every coherence norm we rely on elsewhere: for conditionalizing on what we learn, for not pricing two claims we know to be equivalent at different numbers, for respecting additivity over mutually exclusive claims. If the argument proves that rationality is impossible, it proves too much, and it supports none of those either.

(8) is what I cannot live with. I am quite willing to swallow (5): I would sooner concede that the desk is exploitable than concede that nothing is wrong with an incoherent quote, and I am reluctant to give up (1), since it is the only reason our numbers are more than hunches. One of our mathematicians suggests that the repair is to move to something like the logical induction criterion, where what is forbidden is not a loss at a single moment but sustained profit over time for a trader of limited computational power. I cannot tell whether that blocks the argument or merely relocates it.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look acceptable when I first read it? Where the argument turns on an expression that admits more than one reading, identify the readings and say what each does to the steps that depend on it. Then price the alternatives — what the desk gives up if it rejects (2), if it rejects (4), if it weakens (1), if it accepts (7) but resists (8). Say whether the bounded-trader move genuinely blocks (6) and (8) or only relabels the difficulty, and be explicit about what a resource-relativized norm can no longer claim. Finally, tell me whether the ordinary Dutch-book arguments for conditionalization and additivity, in the mundane case of contingent questions that get settled next week, survive your diagnosis or fall with it.
