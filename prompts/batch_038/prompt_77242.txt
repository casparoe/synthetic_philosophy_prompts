My friend from a political philosophy reading group has an argument that gains from trade justify moral compromise, and it's driving me nuts, because the conclusion is way too strong but I can't find the bad step. Here it is, roughly as he layed it out:

(1) People who care about different things can usually both get more of what they care about by each doing some of what the other wants, than by each doing only what their own values recommend.
(2) When an option leaves both parties with more of what they value, refusing it on principle isn't integrity, it's spite — so taking it is at least permissible, and often required.
(3) "More of what they value" can be put on one common scale of value-satisfaction, so the gains from a trade are comparable across the two people.
(4) So the way to handle moral disagreement is to hunt for trades: I give up some of what my values call for to the extent that you give up some of yours, until no remaining trade benefits both.
(5) And it doesn't matter how the opportunity arose. If a trade is only on the table because one of us has unusually extreme values, or has credibly threatened to do something the other finds appalling unless bought off, the trade still leaves both better off than not trading, so (2) still says take it.
(6) Therefore nothing is off limits — any moral disagreement should be settled by trading, and someone who refuses a trade is being irrational.

He thinks (6) is a feature, not a bug. I think it licenses blackmail, and it seems to mean nobody ever has a reason to hold fast to anything, which can't be right. I also have a vague hunch that (3) is doing quiet work: if there really is a single scale, aren't we just maximizing, and is there any pluralism left to compromise between? But I'd rather keep (1) and (4) if I possibly can.

Where does it break? After the table, a short paragraph naming the single step you'd cut and saying what survives of the gains-from-trade case for compromise once you cut it.
