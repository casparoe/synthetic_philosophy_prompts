I've spent a few years turning over the problem of self-deception on my own, with no philosophy training, and I've come to think the interesting question isn't whether people deceive themselves but what would have to be true of a mind for it to be possible. I want to build a diagnostic quiz for a reading group of equally untrained friends. Please write ten questions, each a short concrete case — the smoker who has genuinely decided to start quitting in January, the diary in which only good days are recorded, the partner who checks a phone while reporting, truthfully, complete trust, the man who says he isn't angry in a voice with no anger in it, the researcher who stops seeing a certain colleague so as never to hear an objection — with two or three answer options each. Sort people along two axes only: whether one mind can hold a belief and its negation at once, and whether anything like a choice figures in getting there. No question may mention knowledge, belief, or intention as such, since everyone should be able to answer without having heard the labels. Then give me a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the positions actually defended in the literature, including the one that dissolves the paradox into ordinary motivated bias, the one that partitions the mind, the one that denies any belief is present at all, and the one that says we are really looking at weakness of will. Name each leaf as its defenders name it, or mark it unnamed. Finally, tell me which pairs of leaves these ten cases cannot separate even in principle, and which cells of the two-axis space nobody occupies, with a guess at whether each empty cell is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
