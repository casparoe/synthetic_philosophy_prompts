I sit on an incident-investigation panel for a rail-signalling near miss whose causal story is almost entirely about things that did not happen: a maintenance check that was not performed, an interlocking that never dropped, a driver's intervention that never came. Our two most senior members have spent three months arguing past each other in a perfectly predictable way. One is a negative-causation realist who insists that absences are genuine causes and that a fault tree which refuses to enter "the check was not performed" as a basic event simply cannot represent the counterfactual dependencies that explain the outcome. The other is a process/connectionist theorist who insists that only physical processes cause anything, that omissions at most fail to prevent, and that the tree is sliding silently between caused and did not stop.

Draft the joint statement these two could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the claims both accept (about the formalism of the tree, about double prevention, about how ordinary causal talk assigns blame for omissions); the residual disagreements stated in wording each side would accept for its own position; and an honest flagging of which disagreements are merely verbal. Then give me the cruxes, the sub-questions where settling the matter would actually move someone. For each crux, name a case or argument both would agree bears on it and record each side's prediction in advance — my suspicion is that one crux is whether the profligate-omissions problem can be solved by relevance and contrastivity inside a negative-causation ontology, and another is whether causal-selection norms track ontology or only our interests.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table whose columns are the disagreement, each side's acceptable wording, and whether it is genuine or verbal; then the cruxes with predictions. End with a last line beginning "Verdict:" giving the single most compressed statement of what these two still disagree about.
