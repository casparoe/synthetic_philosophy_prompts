I am the handling editor of a small journal devoted to the foundations of quantum theory, and I sit on the four-member committee that screens manuscripts bearing on the status of the quantum state: on whether that state is itself part of the physical condition of a system (an ontic view) or a state of knowledge about some deeper physical condition (an epistemic view). Three years of decisions have given us a record, and each entry carries a minute in which the committee says why it decided as it did. I now have a submission whose place in that record I cannot determine, and I would like your judgement before we meet.

The record is this. We rejected a manuscript proving that no model can assign overlapping distributions of underlying physical states to two distinct pure states of a composite system, the pivotal step of the proof being that two systems prepared by causally isolated procedures in distant laboratories have factorizable joint distributions; the minute reads, "the conclusion is hostage to a premise about independent preparations that the interpretation under attack is free to deny." We accepted, after revision, a manuscript exhibiting an explicit model of a single qubit in which distinct pure states do have overlapping distributions of underlying states, and in which measurement outcomes are nevertheless determined by those states; the minute reads, "the model is contrived and does not extend to composite systems, but existence is the point, and the paper shows that further assumptions are always needed to rule such models out." We rejected a manuscript arguing that the epistemic reading is untenable because any model reproducing a system with a d-dimensional Hilbert space and Markovian dynamics requires at least 2d − 2 continuous underlying parameters; the minute reads, "a lower bound on the size of the state space says nothing about whether the distributions overlap, and the epistemic theorist can pay the baggage." We accepted a manuscript proving that no model can be maximally epistemic, in the sense that for every pair of pure states the overlap of their underlying distributions equals the quantum overlap, that proof running on the standard framework together with the premise that the underlying state is fixed at preparation independently of what is later measured on it; the minute praises the paper for confining its conclusion to that class and for stating explicitly that models with smaller overlap survive. We rejected a manuscript which argued, from a classical toy theory in which an observer may never know more than half of what could in principle be known about a system, and which reproduces no-cloning and measurement-update behaviour, that quantum states are states of knowledge; the minute reads, "the analogy cannot carry violations of Bell inequalities, and the inference from an analogy to an interpretation is not licensed." And we accepted a manuscript deriving a bound on the overlap for a single system from the same fixed-at-preparation premise, where that paper devoted a section to models that deny the premise and stated its conclusion as an explicit conditional; the minute reads, "the paper says exactly what it assumes and exactly what follows if we grant it."

The manuscript now before us constructs an explicit model of a single qubit in which every pure state is represented by a distribution over underlying states, and in which the distributions for any two pure states overlap as much as the quantum overlap does — that is, a model of precisely the kind our fourth decision proved impossible. It escapes that proof by denying its premise: in this model the underlying state assigned at preparation depends on which projective measurement will later be performed, the dependence being fixed by a time-symmetric constraint rather than by any signal, so that the no-signalling statistics are reproduced exactly. The abstract announces that the manuscript thereby refutes the claim that the quantum state must be part of reality, and that the only price is that the underlying state is not fixed at preparation. A section defends that price as no price at all by comparison with the block-universe reading of relativity. Of our fourth and sixth decisions, which rest on the premise it denies, the manuscript says only that the premise "is simply false in our model."

Tell me what the committee will do with this submission and what we ought to do if consistency with our own minutes is the governing consideration, giving your best guess and the reasons for preferring it. Set out the rules that the six minutes jointly support; I expect that more than one of them fits every decision we have taken and that at least two of them disagree about this manuscript, so say which fits best, and say plainly where our record underdeterminates the outcome rather than pretending it does not. Identify the closest analog among the six and the respect in which the new manuscript differs from it. Then address what seems to me the hinge: does a body that accepts a theorem whose premises include the fixed-at-preparation assumption thereby adopt that assumption, so that a model violating it is inadmissible, or does accepting a conditional result leave the condition open? Finally, say what principle we would be committing ourselves to by accepting and what by rejecting, whether either choice would oblige us to reopen one of the six decisions, and describe one further manuscript — its assumptions and its conclusion — that would settle which rule the committee is in fact running.

Please write to me in continuous prose, as one writes a letter: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. Put your predicted verdict and your recommendation in your first two or three sentences, and let the rest follow as argument.
