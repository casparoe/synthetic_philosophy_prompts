I am a novelist — five books, all of them concerned, to the exasperation of my reviewers, with how a person changes by imperceptible degrees — and this spring I have been invited, somewhat improbably, to give the keynote at a philosophy-of-language colloquium that happens to be co-hosted by a linguistics department and a small program in performance studies. My talk will defend a view I have been obliged to name myself, since it appears in no literature: I call it dramaturgical precisification. The claim is that for a restricted but philosophically central family of vague predicates — cruel, faithful, ruined, a heap, an affair, bald — the boundary is not fixed in advance by our conventions, our intentions, our perceptual apparatus, or the scorekeeping of the conversational context, but by the way the term is deployed over a sequence of occasions, such that the sequence itself settles where the cutoff falls without anyone ever settling it. My stock illustration is drawn from my own trade: a play in which a character's disloyalty accumulates across forty small scenes, none of which contains it, until at some performance the audience knows with certainty that it has crossed, and yet cannot point to the scene in which it did. On my view the crossing is not a concealed fact about the script awaiting discovery; it is a fact constituted by the enactment, and once constituted it constrains all later uses, which is why the paradox's intuition that each step is negligible can be honored without granting that no number of steps suffices. My argument sketch has three moves, and I would like your objections to target them rather than the illustration: first, that the three standard families of treatment — the epistemicist's unknown sharp boundary, the supervaluationist's precisifications, and the contextualist's shifting standards — each explain one of the two datum and orphan the other, since they must choose between the inevitability of the cutoff and its unlocatability; second, that performance cases demonstrate a coherent way to have both, because a boundary can be fixed by its own realization; third, that we already reason this way outside aesthetics, when we say of a marriage or a friendship that it ended and mean that nothing decided it.

I know in advance where I am thin. I have not shown that the sorites is dissolved rather than relocated: one can say that I have merely moved the unlocatable boundary from the term to the performance and left every puzzle intact. I also have no formal apparatus, and I suspect I will be asked to supply one on the spot. And the analogy to the theatre may be doing covert work — it may smuggle in an audience, a convention of attention, an interest in the outcome — that has no counterpart in ordinary baldness talk.

The room is specific, and the objections will be too. I expect, on the philosophy side, a pair of epistemicists thoroughly schooled in the line of argument that vagueness is ignorance of sharp boundaries and in the margins by which that ignorance is transmitted, and they will want to know what my constituted boundary is other than one I decline to locate. I expect a supervaluationist who will object that I have reinvented precisification while throwing away the machinery that makes it coherent, and who will press me on penumbral connections — my view had better say something about why the same man cannot be both bald and hairy. I expect at least one committed contextualist who thinks the whole debate is confused and that standards simply shift with the conversational score, and who will charge me with offering pragmatics dressed as metaphysics. On the linguistics side, two formal semanticists who work with degree-based accounts of gradable adjectives — the sort of framework in which an adjective denotes a measure function onto a scale and the standard of comparison is a free parameter set by context — will ask me what my proposal even means as a semantics, and whether I am committed to scales being closed or open in some novel sense. And there will be one scholar of theatre and performance studies, friendly in manner and ruthless in substance, who has written about presence and liveness and will object that I have treated performance as an ontological engine when it is really just a very slow context, and who will ask whether the view survives when a play is read alone, at a desk, by nobody.

What I want from you is not a defense of my position but a preparation sheet for the forty minutes after I sit down. Give me eight or nine objections in roughly the order they will actually arrive — which I suspect opens with the relocation charge and closes with whatever the performance scholar has been saving — each phrased in the vocabulary and with the temper of the person who will raise it, so that I recognize them when they come. For each: a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, an honest note on where that reply is softest, and a judgment as to whether the objection is merely irritating or genuinely fatal. Tell me which two or three things I should concede in the opening ten minutes so that they cannot be extracted from me later as admissions, and which single concession I must refuse at all costs. Identify the one reply that has to land for the talk to be judged a success rather than an interesting failure. And tell me which objection I am secretly hoping nobody asks — the one that exposes the crack I have been describing to you but have not yet been brave enough to name in these terms.
