I co-convene an MPhil aesthetics seminar, and a senior colleague of mine has a stock move that reliably ends our definitional debates. When a student proposes necessary and sufficient conditions for being a work of art, he replies that art is like a game: chess and tennis and ring-a-ring-o'-roses share no single feature, yet we deploy the concept competently enough by tracking overlapping resemblances, so the demand for a real definition of art rests on a category mistake about how the concept works. He presents this as Weitz's argument that art is an open rather than a closed concept, and he then presses it further: the historical and institutional proposals that followed — Danto's artworld, Dickie's conferral of status, Levinson's definition in terms of intended regard modelled on past art — are, on his telling, just the discredited project relaunched in a new costume. The analogy is carrying a great deal of weight in that room, and I want to know how much of that weight it can actually bear. Please write in British English throughout, and do not trouble yourself with references or a bibliography; I want the arguments themselves.

I would like the following:

1. The mapping, set out explicitly (a table is welcome): what corresponds to the paradigm games, to the rules that constitute a game, to the community of players, and to the invention of a wholly new game. Where a term in the artworld has no obvious counterpart in the source, say so rather than forcing a fit.

2. The disanalogies, sorted into those that are fatal to the use my colleague makes of the analogy and those that are merely untidy. Two I can see and want tested: games are activities whose constitutive conventions participants can adopt at will, whereas an artefact's candidacy is usually settled retrospectively by a history and an institution it does not control; and resemblance among games is supposed to be available on inspection, whereas the genuinely hard cases in modern art are precisely those perceptually indiscernible from ordinary commercial objects, where seeing whether something is art requires knowing a story rather than comparing appearances.

3. What the source domain quietly imports about the target: assumptions concerning a settled pre-theoretical extension, membership decided by observers rather than conferred by practitioners, and novelty as mere extension of usage rather than as something requiring justification.

4. Whether the conclusion survives. Does the games analogy establish that art has no essence at all, or only that any essence it has will not be a perceptual one? Is my colleague's dismissal of Danto, Dickie and Levinson supported by the analogy, or independent of it?

5. How the analogy fares against two rival diagnoses of the same data: Mandelbaum's complaint that the doctrine of family resemblances is asserted rather than analysed, and that apparent resemblances may rest on non-apparent properties; and Gallie's account of art as an essentially contested concept. Say which better explains our practice, and whether a different analogy — money, say, or citizenship, or a language — would license something more defensible than the one now doing the work.

Finish with a short verdict: what the seminar should and should not conclude from the comparison.
