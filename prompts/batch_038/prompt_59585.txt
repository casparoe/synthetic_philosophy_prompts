I chair a small working group that advises our national funding agency on the ethical review of animal research, and this year we are rewriting the schedule that determines which species receive which tier of protection. The draft currently on the table — my colleagues have taken to calling it the Cephalization Schedule — assigns every species a moral weight equal to the base-10 logarithm of its estimated number of pallial neurons, or, in animals that lack a pallium, of the neurons in whatever structure the drafter judges to be its functional analogue, and then caps the aggregate weighted harm of any approved protocol at a fixed budget. Two members of the group have been arguing about it for a month, mostly by memo, and I want to see the argument staged properly, in one sitting, before next week's hearing.

Please write a dialogue of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words between the schedule's principal defender, Ines Marchetti, a comparative neuroanatomist who holds that some measure of neural quantity is the only public and auditable proxy we have for the intensity and range of an animal's experience, and that a coarse metric applied even-handedly is morally preferable to a refined judgment that silently tracks the sympathies of whoever happens to be doing the judging; and its most persistent critic, Tobias Fenn, a philosopher of biology who holds that neural quantity is not a crude measure of moral status but a fairly precise measure of something else — how much tissue an animal needs to run a body of a given size and complexity — and that the schedule therefore mistakes plumbing for mind. Set the scene in a windowless committee room at the end of a long day, and have them work through a specific run of difficulties rather than trading generalities. Tobias should open on allometry, pressing the point that raw brain mass mostly tracks body mass, so a heavier brain may mean only a larger animal to control; Ines should concede the point and retreat from grams to neurons, at which Tobias should reply with the elephant — roughly three times the brain mass of ours and something like three times our total neuron count, but with well over ninety percent of those neurons in the cerebellum and a third of our cortical complement despite a cortex twice the mass — and note that the various "size" metrics do not even agree with each other, since the elephant's encephalization quotient is near the bottom of the mammalian range while ours is near the top and the great whales sit in between, so the choice among metrics is not forced by the data. Ines's answer should be to restrict the count to the pallium, and Tobias should press exactly there: once the schedule must say which structures count, it is no longer measuring the brain but presupposing a theory about which parts of it host experience, and that theory is doing all the normative work. Have them then get tangled in the distributed cases — the octopus, whose majority of neurons sit out in the arms that keep solving problems when severed, so the schedule must say whether weight is additive or unitary; and the insect mushroom bodies, where calling them pallial analogues collapses the tier structure across billions of farmed animals, while refusing to call them analogues makes the criterion behavioral and functional after all. Someone should ask Ines why the weight is logarithmic rather than linear, and she should be allowed a real answer about diminishing marginal intensity, but the exchange should not let her off the hook that any monotone transform fits the ranking equally well, so the transform is where the drafter's preferred aggregate results were smuggled in — whether a shrimp farm outweighs a single whale turns entirely on it. Finally, the schedule must be applied to our own species, ranking neonates and patients with advanced neurodegeneration below adult corvids and cetaceans, and Ines must either bite that bullet, restrict the schedule to non-human animals and explain why the restriction is not arbitrary, or move from actual neuron counts to species-typical or capacity-based counts and explain what is left of her thesis; Tobias, in turn, must be forced to say what he would put in the schedule's place and to face Ines's rejoinder that his substitute — affective circuitry, associative learning, evidence of suffering rather than mere nociception — is itself cashed out in neural terms, so that their disagreement may be about which neural facts matter rather than about whether neural facts do.

Both should be at the top of their form: I do not want the critic reduced to squeamishness about quantification, nor the neuroanatomist reduced to a Victorian brain-weigher, and each should score at least one point the other explicitly concedes. They should interrupt, answer the argument actually made rather than the one rehearsed, and stay concrete — the dialogue should turn on the cases above, not on competing definitions of sentience. Do not have either character cite studies, researchers, journals, or dates, and do not supply a bibliography; the empirical details you need are given here, and anything else should stay at the level of common knowledge. Keep stage direction minimal, avoid any summarizing coda or verdict on who won, and end in character with each of them naming the single crux they now take to be decisive and saying what would have to be true, in principle or in evidence, for the other to be right.
