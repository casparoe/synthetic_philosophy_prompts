ok so I need a piece of satire and I've got a pretty specific setup in mind.

I want a letter — like an actual "Dear Valued Member" notice a US health plan mails out — announcing that starting next quarter every decision about whether something is a treatment or an enhancement will be made by an automated adjudication system. Around 700 words. It's written by the plan's chief medical officer, who is sincere and proud of the thing: she genuinely believes she has finally made the distinction objective, consistent, and fair to everybody. No winking at the reader, no ironic asides, no raised eyebrow. The joke is that she means every single word.

The invented rule is the part I want you to actually run with, because it's mine and not something you'll have read anywhere: the plan calls it the Restorative Ceiling. An intervention is covered only if two conditions hold — first, that without it your expected functioning in the relevant domain would fall below the median of your reference class, and second, that the intervention brings you up to that median and no further. Restoring you is therapy. Carrying you past the middle of the pack is enhancement, and enhancement is a lifestyle choice, so it's yours to pay for. The reference class is whatever the actuarial team picks for the domain — age-matched, sometimes sex-matched, sometimes national, and for a few domains it's just you-at-twenty.

Then the letter has to walk members through some actual determinations from the pilot quarter, and every one of them has to follow correctly from the rule exactly as she stated it. That's the whole exercise, so no cheating with cases where the system simply malfunctions or somebody in claims drops the ball — the answers have to be the right answers under her criterion. The determinations should be the ones a person who knows this literature would instantly recognize as the pressure points: the preventive stuff, where nothing has gone wrong yet so nobody can show they were below a median; the cases where a treatment works too well and lifts someone clean past normal; the fact that a median is a statistical fact about a population and not obviously a fact about what anybody is owed; the members who don't want to be brought up to the median in the first place and are asking the plan to stop trying; and the ones where a narrowly drawn domain makes something plainly cosmetic look like a deficit. Don't name any of these as problems — she isn't reporting problems, she's reporting good news, and the word "objection" should never appear.

No philosophers, no citations, no bibliography, no references to real books or papers, nothing in the genre of "as Norman Daniels would have it." This is a letter somebody unfolded at their kitchen table, not a seminar paper.

For the ending: she reports that the system has also processed a request of her own, and she accepts the outcome warmly, in exactly the same register as the rest of the letter. Do not let her slip into regret or self-awareness there — if she feels anything it should be admiration for how even-handed the thing is.

Format: continuous prose only. No headings, no bullet points, no numbered criteria, no bold, no tables. If you need to state the rule, state it in a sentence. A salutation and a signoff are fine, thats letter furniture, but the body should run as flowing paragraphs the whole way through.

And it needs to work on two audiences at once: a bioethicist should be laughing at how precisely the rule generates the correct wrong answers, and a lay reader who has ever been on hold with an insurer should recognize the voice on the first line without knowing any of the philosophy at all.
