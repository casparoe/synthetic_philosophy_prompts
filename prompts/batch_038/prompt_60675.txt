Been arguing with a friend who works on classical Indian epistemology, and I think the argument I built backfires on me. Setup: Nyāya says there are four pramāṇas -- perception, inference, upamāna, and śabda/testimony. The upamāna case everyone uses is the guy from the city who's never seen a wild ox; a forester tells him a gavaya is like a cow, and later, when he's in the forest and sees one, he ties the animal to the name. Buddhists say there are only two, perception and inference. The materialists say one. Here's the reduction argument I put to my friend, in what I take to be its strongest form:

(1) X is a seperate pramāṇa only if X yields cognitions that no combination of already-accepted pramāṇas can yield.
(2) In the gavaya case, the payoff cognition -- this animal is the one the word picks out -- comes from a combination: perceiving the beast in front of him, remembering what the forester said, and inferring from the similarity he now perceives.
(3) Memory isn't a pramāṇa on anybody's list, since it only re-presents what some pramāṇa already delivered; perception and inference are already on the list.
(4) So upamāna isn't a separate pramāṇa.
(5) The same move kills śabda: hearing a reliable speaker is perceiving sounds, remembering what the words mean, and inferring from past cases that this speaker's utterances track the facts.
(6) Hence exactly two pramāṇas, perception and inference.

Now the backfire. (7) Run (1) once more, this time on inference itself. An inferential cognition is produced by perceiving the mark -- smoke on the hill -- plus remembering a pervasion I built up over a bunch of past observations, plus whatever habit-formed association carries me from there to the conclusion. Every ingredient is a perception, a memory, or something nobody counts as a pramāṇa. So by (1) inference isn't separate either, and (6) collapses to perception alone: the materialist's list, which is the one thing my Buddhist cannot accept. The argument seems to prove the Cārvāka conclusion out of premises the two-pramāṇa theorist is supposed to share.

I can't tell whether that's a real reductio on (1) or whether (7) just isn't a fair application of it. My friend says the whole thing rests on a category mistake about how pramāṇas get counted and won't say more than that, which is maddening. So: which step do you reject, why did it look innocent from the inside, and what does each candidate rejection cost? I'd much rather keep (1) than give it up, because without a criterion like it the counting debate looks like a fight over labels -- but I also don't want to keep (5) if it's doing damage. And if the two-pramāṇa side does have to swap (1) for something else, say what the replacement criterion has to be able to do that (1) was doing.
