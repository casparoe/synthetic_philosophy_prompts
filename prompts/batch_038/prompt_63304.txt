I sit on a tiny advisory committee for a family foundation that spends about $400k a year on farmed animal welfare, and this year we've gotten ourselves stuck on aquaculture. Four of us, four slogans, two months of going around in circles. One wants to fund a campaign for mandatory stunning at slaughter for farmed fish. Another says every welfare reform is a trap because it buys the industry legitimacy and lets it expand, so the money should go to getting people to stop eating seafood at all. A third won't touch anything involving shrimp or prawns on the grounds that the evidence for crustacean sentience is a lot thinner than for fish, and thinks the rest of us are papering over that. And the fourth — me, sort of — keeps pointing out that a big chunk of what a farmed salmon eats is fishmeal and fish oil rendered from wild-caught forage fish, so even a perfect welfare reform on the farm side still runs on a pile of wild suffering nobody in the room is counting. Last week a group pitched us on subsidising electric stunners on shrimp harvest lines, arguing the individual numbers are so enormous that a tiny per-shrimp improvement beats anything you could do for fish, and half the committee loved it for reasons I couldn't quite pin down.

So: build me a decision tree / taxonomy of the positions on this, one I can put on a table in front of four people. Please build it out of the questions rather than out of a list of named camps — lay out the branch points first, each phrased as a concrete question someone could answer without knowing which camp their answer lands them in, then locate the actual positions in the space you've made. I want nodes covering at least: whether fish and crustacean sentience evidence get treated as one block or split, and what you do with whatever uncertainty is left over (precaution, an evidence threshold, weighting the uncertainty by how many animals it covers); what the unit of accounting even is — individuals, tonnes, whole lives, discrete bad moments — including the awkward question hiding underneath that one, whether bringing a fish into a life worth living is a benefit to that fish, which seems to flip the whole debate if you answer yes; where the moral boundary sits, i.e. the farm only, or also the feed supply chain, bycatch, and the fish suffocating on decks; what you think a welfare reform causally does to the industry's growth; and whether any of this is verifiable in practice, given the killing happens at four in the morning on a harvest line with nobody watching.

Then the leaves: six to nine of them, each with the position's name if it has one ("unnamed" if nobody actually defends it), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one sentence on what our money would fund under it. The tree has to be able to hold all four committee views plus the shrimp-numbers view, and I want you to tell me which pairs of leaves it can't really separate — I suspect my abolitionist and the shrimp-numbers person end up prescribing nearly identical spending for opposite reasons, and I'd rather see that flagged than hidden. Point out any combination of answers that comes out empty and say whether its empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's thought of it yet. Collapse any branch point that never actually changes the funding decision, and tell me which ones you collapsed and why. Last thing: give me five short questions I can put to each person over lunch that would place them on the tree without ever asking them to pick a label. No bibliography, no named papers or thinkers — I just want the map and the questions.
