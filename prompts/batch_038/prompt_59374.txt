**3. Skolem's paradox and the context-sensitivity of "uncountable"**

3.1 *The apparent contradiction.* Skolem's 1922 address drew attention to an odd consequence of the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem. Because ZFC is formulated in a countable first-order language, if it is consistent at all it has a countable model; call one such model M. ZFC proves Cantor's theorem, so M satisfies the sentence saying that no function carries the natural numbers onto the power set of the natural numbers. Yet M has only countably many members altogether, so the object M takes to be the power set of ω contains only countably many of M's elements. Apparently M asserts that there are uncountably many reals while, viewed from outside, everything it quantifies over could be listed.

3.2 *Two points worth stressing.* First, the passage from consistency to a countable model is not an informal maneuver. ZFC proves the completeness theorem and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, so ZFC itself proves that it has a countable model. Second, M is a universe in which everything really is countable. Since M is countable, all of its elements are countable, and ω is absolute, so the natural numbers of M are the genuine natural numbers and a bijection between them and M's power set of ω exists in the background universe. M simply does not contain that bijection among its own sets; what M lacks is not sets but functions.

3.3 *The proposal.* My suggestion is that "countable" behaves like an indexical such as "here" or "now," with the model playing the role of the context of utterance. In something like the Kaplan framework, the character of "countable" is the rule that assigns to a context — a structure with its own membership relation and its own ω — the property of being in bijection with the natural numbers as that context determines them, while the content varies from context to context even though the sentence is held fixed. This would explain why the paradox presents itself as a contradiction: we fix the content of the sentence using the character supplied by our own context and then evaluate it against the deliverances of a different one, and the mismatch looks like inconsistency only because we have silently changed parameters. The disanalogy that worries me is transparency. A speaker who says that it is raining here understands perfectly well what it would be for the same words, uttered elsewhere, to be false, and can shift contexts deliberately. The inhabitants of M cannot form the thought that their ω₁ is countable, since they cannot express the requisite bijection and their language provides no device for pointing outside their own context; the relativity is visible only from a further context, which is itself just another model, so the analysis threatens to generate a regress of contexts with no privileged member.

3.4 *Putnam's stronger conclusion.* Putnam deploys this machinery in his paper Models and Reality to argue against metaphysical realism. An ideal theory — complete, consistent, and satisfying every operational constraint we could articulate — still admits models we would regard as unintended, and nothing about the world together with our total use of the language singles out one of them as the intended interpretation. When the realist replies that we simply add the requirement that the model be standard, Putnam's response is that this amounts to appending further theoretical constraints to a theory that was already supposed to be complete and so cannot do the work demanded of it. My objection is that the move from a theorem about first-order axiomatizations to a conclusion about language and world requires a premise the paradox does not supply. The theorem tells us that a formalism we chose underdetermines its models; it does not tell us that our ordinary use of set-theoretic vocabulary underdetermines its reference, and bridging the gap needs an account of what reference-fixing could consist in that is independent of the formalism.

3.5 *The second-order escape.* A natural way to block the paradox is to leave first-order logic. By Zermelo's quasi-categoricity result, models of second-order ZF are, up to isomorphism, the hierarchies V_κ built to a strongly inaccessible κ, and any two such models are comparable, one being isomorphic to an initial segment of the other; since Löwenheim–Skolem fails for second-order logic, there is no countable model and the paradox disappears. I think this relocates the problem rather than solving it. Second-order semantics is specified set-theoretically, in the metalanguage, so the question whether the metalanguage's second-order quantifiers range over a determinate totality is the same question one level up. And quasi-categoricity fixes the shape of the hierarchy given its height without fixing the height, so a residue of relativity survives even in the best case.
