Right, I need help with a speech and I've got nine days.

I came here at 24 on a work visa and I'm 35 now. Four months ago I finally got made a citizen, and the council has asked me to say a few words at the next citizenship ceremony as one of the new people. Town hall, roughly sixty new citizens with their families, so maybe 150 in the room: the mayor, three councillors, the local MP, the registrar who runs it, and a lot of people whose English is newer than mine. Three minutes, five at a stretch.

The reason I'm asking instead of just writing it is that I know what I want to say but I cant get it to come out as anything other than either a rant or a greeting card.

Here's the thing. For eleven years I had what anyone would call a free life. I could work, rent, open a bank account, drive, go abroad and come back, start a business if I'd wanted to. Nobody was stopping me doing anything. What I couldn't do was stop caring what the Home Office thought of me. Every form was a live wire. A fee went up, the rules changed under me twice, my visa had a line of small print on it saying I wasn't allowed to claim anything from the state even if I was drowning, and there was a fourteen-month wait for a decision during which I couldn't leave the country and couldn't plan a single thing. Then I got the certificate, and — this is the part I keep coming back to — nothing that was forbidden last year is allowed this year. No door has opened. What changed is that nobody can take it away from me because they feel like it, and if the state does something to me I get to argue about it somewhere that has to listen.

My mate who teaches politics told me this has a name, and that it isn't freedom-from-interference and isn't freedom-to-rule-yourself either, it's a third thing. Which was no use to me at all, because I still don't know what to say in a town hall on the day.

So I want the speech to be about that distinction, and to be exact about it — because otherwise it slides into what the mayor's office clearly wants, which is gratitude for the freedoms we enjoy in this country, plus the flag. But no philosopher's name in it, no abstract nouns like domination or arbitrariness, no jargon of any kind. My mother's in the front row and she has never read anything. The MP has said he'll quote bits of it in his newsletter, which means whatever I say gets heard by people who are not in the room.

And here's the knot I actually can't untie, so please think about this bit rather than writing something warm and vague: what I'm celebrating was given to me. Somebody decided. Which is precisely the thing I've just spent a paragraph saying was the problem. How do I stand up and say this status is different in kind from eleven years of being tolerated, without either pretending the giving wasn't discretionary and political, or turning the ceremony into a complaint about the giving — in front of the people who did it? I think there's a real answer, something about the difference between being handed a favour and being given standing, or about when a power over you stops being a power you have to please. I just can't get it into three minutes that a nineteen-year-old from Lusaka and a retired dentist from Slough can both follow.

Constraints: no 'journey' as a noun, no 'we are all', no parties or ministers named, nothing that implies your old country doesn't matter now (half the room is going home for Eid in the spring), and it has to work read aloud, so short sentences. Also — if you think the framing is a mistake, that the honest version of this speech simply can't be delivered at a citizenship ceremony, tell me that first and then write the best version you can anyway.
