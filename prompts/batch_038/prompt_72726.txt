I sit on the grants panel of a mid-sized city's community foundation, and our program has a weird legacy clause. The original donor was a Rousseau reader, and the trust deed says the money is for spectacles in which the citizens themselves are the performers, and explicitly not for entertainment that turns them into an audience. Everyone on the panel has been treating that as a mood rather than a rule, and we loose more time arguing about what the donor meant than actually reading applications. We get about 40 a cycle, two cycles a year, each read by two panelists with twenty minutes apiece, and only one of us has any arts training. The executive director signs off and applicants can appeal, so whatever we write down has to survive being read back to us later.

What I want is a procedure my panel can actually run on an application, built out of what Rousseau argued in the Letter to d'Alembert — his long reply to d'Alembert's Encyclopédie entry on Geneva, where d'Alembert floated the idea of a theater there and Rousseau said Geneva should have open-air public festivals instead. Not a summary of him; the working rule set. So: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which questions can end the review with a no, and where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to a person because it's a question of values rather than anything we can look up in the file.

A few things it specifically needs to get right. Rousseau's charge isn't that plays are badly made, it's that they can't change anybody — they take the dispositions an audience already has and give them a pleasant workout, and a playwright who wants to sell tickets has to flatter the room, so the satire always lands on someone the audience was already happy to see mocked. He works that out at length on Molière's Misanthrope, and the argument generalizes. If that's really the argument, then a big chunk of what our applicants promise (this show will change how people here think about X) fails for a reason that has nothing to do with the quality of the show, and the procedure needs a separate track for that case rather than letting it sneak in as artistic merit. Also: what he objected to was the asymmetry between the few who perform and the many who sit in the dark, plus the money and hours that costs and who is priced out of both — not participation as a warm feeling. Define that asymmetry in terms two tired volunteers can check against an application form, and say what evidence in a file would settle it either way.

Then run it on three of ours: an acclaimed fringe company touring a satire of our own city council to six neighborhood halls; an annual street dance and cook-off, free, no stage, anyone can enter, which our arts-trained panelist thinks is amateur rubbish; and a community musical with an all-local amateur cast, a professional director, and thirty-dollar tickets in a proper theater. If the first one comes out a no, say so plainly — it's the best work we fund and I'd rather find that out now than in an appeal.

Last thing, be straight about what doesn't survive the trip. Some of his reasons are unusable for us, the stuff about the acting profession as a trade and about women on the stage, and I want the procedure to say it's quarantining those rather than quietly dropping them. Tell me which of his underlying commitments the rest of the procedure is leaning on without admitting it, which distinction would break if we simplified it, where it's going to misfire in practice, and what it simply cannot decide.
