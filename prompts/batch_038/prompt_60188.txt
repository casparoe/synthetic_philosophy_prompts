Write a dialogue between two researchers who disagree about whether inverse reinforcement learning can recover human preferences from observed behavior. Both are sharp, both are well-read in the other's work, and neither is permitted to be a strawman.

**The interlocutors.** One is an applied IRL researcher who has shipped assistive systems and believes that reward inference from behavior is a hard but genuinely empirical problem with real, checkable answers. The other is a philosopher of social science who is technically fluent — comfortable with the math, not merely with the slogans — and believes that any inference from behavior to preference must pass through normative assumptions that the data cannot arbitrate, so that what the discipline actually recovers is a projection of the modeler's own choices about what counts as a reasonable thing to want.

**The setting.** They are jointly reviewing one concrete case, which you should describe in your two-sentence scene-setting and then keep returning to: an assistive system trained on several months of logged trajectories from a single user, with a reward that is linear in a set of hand-engineered features, fitted by a maximum-entropy method, and deployed with the system maintaining a distribution over rewards and treating user corrections as evidence. On one particular occasion the system did something the user afterward disavowed — she insisted she had never wanted it, even though her own logged behavior had pointed that way. Give the case enough texture (what the features were, what the behavior looked like, what the system did) that the two of them have something specific to argue about rather than a placeholder.

**What the dialogue must contain.**

1. The dispute has to be conducted at the level of the machinery, not above it. At some point the exchange must engage with: the fact that in the maximum-entropy framework the gradient of the log-likelihood with respect to the reward parameters is the gap between expected feature counts under the fitted policy and observed feature counts in the data, and what that identification result does and does not buy you; the invariance of the optimal policy under potential-based shaping and the resulting sense in which reward is recoverable only up to a class; the confounding of the reward scale with the assumed noise or temperature in the human's decision rule; and the restriction of any conclusion to the region of state space the demonstrations actually visited.

2. The skeptic must press at least two distinct worries and must not collapse them into one: a worry about the unobservable (time-inconsistent or non-stationary behavior, and whether fitting a single stationary reward to it manufactures a preference the person would repudiate), and a worry about the observable but unendorsed (the choice of feature basis as the place where the modeler's own normative commitments enter, silently and before any data is fitted).

3. The engineer must be allowed the strongest available reply, and the skeptic must answer it rather than dodge it: that the system never claims to read off true preferences, that it maintains and narrows a distribution over candidate rewards, that ongoing correction by the human is part of the loop, and that adequacy for safe coordination — not metaphysical fidelity — is the target the system is actually built to hit.

4. Neither side may score by appeal to what their respective literatures have concluded. No name-dropping of papers or authorities as a substitute for argument.

5. There must be real movement. At least twice, one of them concedes a specific point, and the argument relocates in consequence — the reader should be able to say, by the end, which of the original disagreements has been narrowed, which has been dissolved, and which is untouched.

**Form.** Roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words. Open with no more than two sentences of scene-setting and no more thereafter: speaker labels and the words they say, with no stage directions, no narration of gestures or expressions, and no summarizing interludes. Keep the register argumentative but collegial; these two want to be persuaded, not to win.

**Last line.** Do not resolve the argument and do not declare a victor. End with a single line in exactly this form:

Crux: [one sentence naming the one disagreement the dialogue leaves standing, and stating whether further data of the kind the system logs could in principle settle it]
