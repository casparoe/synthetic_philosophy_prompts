Just spent three hours going back and forth with a friend about when you're actually allowed to flat-out say something, and I think we're each half right, so I want a third party.

Her line is the simple one: you may assert p only if you know p. My problem is it keeps landing wrong, so I started writing down cases where I'm pretty confident about the verdict, and there's a shape to them I can't name.

1. My sister texts that her flight's delayed; I tell my mum "her flight's delayed." Fine, obviously.
2. Friend has one of about ten million lottery tickets, draw hasn't happened yet, I say "you didn't win." Not fine. I'd take it back. And I'm no more confident about lots of things I say happily all day.
3. Doctor after a biopsy: "it's benign, you don't have cancer." Test is something like 99.7% accurate and she's upfront about that. Totally fine — she should say it straight.
Case 2 vs case 3 is the pair that broke us. Roughly the same ballpark of odds, opposite verdicts, and I'm not moving on either.
4. A cousin who never reads anything announces at dinner "that bridge is going to come down, I just have a feeling." Bridge is in fact condemned and closes two months later. Still not fine, I think. Right answer, bad assertion.
5. A friend teaches high school biology, very religious, tells her class we share a common ancestor with chimpanzees. Privately she can't get herself to believe it, but she teaches it straight and says the evidence points there. I say that was a fine assertion. My friend says it's improper automatically, because she doesn't even believe the thing.
6. A committee chair reads out "we found the methodology unreliable" after voting the other way herself, unanimously outvoted by everyone else on the panel. Fine to say, I think, even though she doesn't know it and doesn't believe it.
7. I told a friend "your flatmate is taking your money" on the word of the other flatmate, and it turned out false. Improper, and "his evidence looked good at the time" doesn't make it proper — it just makes him less blameworthy. Blamelessness and propriety are two different things, which she agrees with.
8. I know a friend is pregnant, she hasn't told anyone, I say so at a party. Improper, but not because of anything about assertion as such — it's the telling. This is the only case we fully agree on.

My own guess at a principle, which I want you to attack: you may flat-out assert p only if you could produce, on demand, a reason for p that is about this particular case rather than about how things of that type usually go, and that would satisfy a reasonable person who doubted it. That handles 2 against 3 — all I've got for the lottery is the odds, whereas she's got a slide with your cells on it. It handles 4, since a feeling is nothing. It probably handles 1 and 7.

But it wrecks 5 and 6, which I'm not giving up, and here's the one I think kills it: I tell my nine year old the earth is about 4.5 billion years old. I could not begin to back that up on demand. I have no case-specific grounds at all, I'm just downstream of a lot of people I'll never meet. And it is obviously a completely fine thing to say. So either my principle is wrong or "produce it on demand" isn't the right test.

So, what's the principle? Give me your best one or two, clause by clause, and say which case each clause is there to handle. Then be honest about which of my verdicts it can't keep and which one I should drop — I suspect it's one of 2 and 3 and I want to know which, and what the argument is. Finally, what does it say about three we never got to: a climate scientist saying this heatwave was made about three times more likely by warming, which sounds statistical but feels perfectly assertable; a jury foreman saying "guilty" where the real evidence was a DNA match with a one-in-a-billion random match probability; and a mechanic saying "your alternator's shot" after a five minute test that's right about 95% of the time. And if your principle says the DNA case is fine but the lottery isn't, spell out exactly what's doing the work in that gap, because I'm confident there is a gap and I can't say what it is.
