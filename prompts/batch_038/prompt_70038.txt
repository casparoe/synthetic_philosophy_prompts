I am preparing a graduate seminar on explanation in the historical sciences, and while assembling the reading I came across a review essay in a general-interest magazine which characterises the new environmental history as follows:

"The environmental historians have finished off the great-man theory. On their account, climate, pathogens and volcanoes are not the scenery against which history happens but its principal actors: the eruption that dimmed the skies ruined the harvests, the ruined harvests made the revolution, and the revolution made the modern state. Human beings are the medium through which such forces work rather than their authors, so agency, strictly speaking, resides in the non-human world. The thesis is also unfalsifiable — whatever happens can be laid at the door of some environmental factor after the event — and it carries an awkward corollary: if the drought caused the famine, nobody caused the famine, and the vocabulary of responsibility simply does not apply."

My own inclination is to read this as a caricature dressed up as a précis, but I do not work in this literature and cannot say where the distortions lie. Would you take the passage sentence by sentence, and tell me which claims a practising environmental historian would endorse, which are harmless simplifications, and which misdescribe the field? I am particularly interested in whether 'actor' and 'agency' are being used in the senses the field gives them, and in how the determinism attributed to these historians relates to the anti-determinist commitments they generally insist upon. Finally, does the corollary about responsibility follow from anything true in the passage, or does it depend on an equivocation you can identify?
