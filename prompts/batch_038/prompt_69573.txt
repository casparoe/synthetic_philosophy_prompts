I am in the fourth year of a novel one of whose principals is a researcher in the science of consciousness — a woman whose clinical work is with patients who have lost the power of speech but not, she is convinced, the capacity for it to be like something to be them. I want her to sound like someone who does this work rather than like my paraphrase of an encyclopedia entry, so I have been reading the trade literature, and in one well-reviewed popular book on the mind I came across a passage I have been planning to put, nearly word for word, into her mouth: a scene in which she explains her method to a graduate student who has been reading her critics. Before I write it, I need to know whether the passage is a fair account of what it describes, or the sort of thing that would make a specialist set my novel down.

The author is no crank and appears to have no axe to grind. He is summarizing heterophenomenology, the method Daniel Dennett proposed for the science of consciousness, and this is roughly what he says:

"Dennett's proposal is disarmingly modest. Treat a person's talk about their inner life the way you would treat anyone's testimony about something you cannot check: as data, not as truth. Assemble from the subject's utterances and behaviour a text — a description of the world as it appears to that subject — and study that world instead of trying to peer behind it. What makes this possible is that the method takes no stand on whether the things described actually exist. When a subject reports a throbbing red afterimage, you record that the world according to this subject contains a throbbing red afterimage, exactly as you would record that the world according to a devout person contains an answering God. Hence the method's great virtue: it is unobjectionable. Because it suspends judgment on the reality of experience, it cannot be turned into an argument that there is no such thing as qualia; any such argument must be doing something else, smuggled in from outside. Its sharpest critics can therefore accept the method while rejecting Dennett's conclusions. The one real cost is that the method depends on speech, and so has nothing to say about animals, infants, or patients who cannot talk — a limitation its proponent concedes. What remains is a domain of items that are genuinely there, though not physically so: the things experiences are of, which exist as contents, in the manner of the objects of dreams."

My request is that you take that passage apart and tell me where each part of it stands. Which claims would a philosopher who actually works on this method accept as they are put? Which are honest simplifications, costing precision but misstating nothing? And which are wrong in a way that matters — where I would want to know what the view really holds instead, so that I know what I am cutting? Where you think a claim is wrong, say wrong; do not soften it into "somewhat overstated" for my benefit, since I have to decide what stays in the scene.

Three places interest me most. First, the inference from the method's neutrality to its harmlessness: does bracketing the question of whether the reported items exist really leave the method unable to support any conclusion about qualia, and does the claim that all its critics can sign up to it follow? Second, the remark about speech. Third, whether the closing sentence — that the contents are genuinely there but not physically — reports a commitment anyone in this debate holds, or invents a realm the view was designed to dissolve. And once you have corrected the passage, tell me whether the author's headline conclusion survives the corrections, or was resting on them.

For what it is worth, I am inclined to believe him, both because he is careful elsewhere and because his account flatters something I want for the book: that my character and the phenomenologists who accuse her of simply declining to look share a method, and differ only about what it shows. If the passage cannot carry that hope, I would rather hear so now than learn it from a reviewer.

Finally, and plainly: if my request is itself ill-conceived — if a paragraph lifted out of the chapter that surrounds it cannot honestly be audited line by line, or if the answer turns so heavily on a contested reading of one man's intentions that you would only be guessing — say that, and say what could be established instead. I do not want a courteous audit I cannot rely on.
