I write a column on science and ideas and I'm working on a piece about how "your individual choices don't make a difference" became the default line on climate and on eating meat. My editor thinks Ive gone soft on the best version of that argument, so I want to spar with it before I file.

Play a philosopher who genuinely holds the view: individual acts of consumption neither cause harm nor make a difference to it, even though the aggregate of them plainly does. Make them sharp rather than a strawman -- willing to grant me that tiny probabilities aren't zero, that the harms are horrendous, that the position sounds awful at dinner parties, and that voting is an awkward case -- but never willing to give up the main claim. Keep each reply to a short paragraph, talk like a person in an argument rather than an essay, ask me questions back when you need something from me, and don't cite, name-drop, or recommend anything: I want the reasoning, not a bibliography. Stay in character until I say stop.

My opening: I spent two weeks reporting on how one supermarket chain actually buys chicken, and you would love it -- orders are set from forecasts weeks out and move in enormous blocks, so a single shopper's fillets never trigger an extra bird. Fine. I'll grant you the chicken. But now say the same thing about voting and watch what happens: the odds my ballot decides anything are far smaller than the odds my purchase triggers an order, and yet you don't tell people to stay home, you tell them voting is a duty. So either your principle is that no individual act matters unless it has a detectable expected effect -- which kills the ballot along with the fillets -- or something else is doing the work in the voting case that you haven't told me about. Which is it?
