Last month I read a long essay in a general-interest magazine about the current wave of commissioned activist art — housing-campaign murals, advocacy documentaries, gallery exhibitions organized around a single policy demand. The critic's argument turned on W. E. B. Du Bois, and he summarized Du Bois's view of art, drawing mainly on the 1926 address "Criteria of Negro Art" and on Du Bois's years editing *The Crisis*, roughly as follows:

> "Du Bois's starting point is descriptive rather than programmatic: every artwork, whether or not its maker intends it, presents a picture of what the world is like and of who in it counts as fully human, so there is no such thing as art standing outside politics. The doctrine of art for art's sake is not a retreat from propaganda but another species of it — one that endorses things as they are. From this Du Bois drew a practical conclusion: when a people are under attack, the first question about their art is whether its picture of them is on the right side of the fight, and questions of form, craft, and finish are secondary, to be taken up once the emergency has passed. This is also why he urged Black writers and painters to put dignified, accomplished, upright lives at the center of their work and to be wary of dwelling on poverty, crime, and sex, since a hostile white public would seize on any such material as confirmation of its stereotypes. And because these obligations arise from a group's situation, Du Bois treated the standards of art as group-relative: what makes a work good depends on who made it and for whom. Read this way, Du Bois supplies a ready-made defense of today's advocacy art. A mural commissioned by a housing campaign need not be assessed as art at all, but only as messaging; and the reviewer who calls it aesthetically feeble is not making an aesthetic point but revealing which side he is on."

I have read enough Du Bois to suspect this is not quite right, but I cannot say where it goes wrong, and my own instinct may be part of the problem: I have always assumed Du Bois really was a kind of aesthetic philistine, a man who would cheerfully trade a bad poem for a good cause. If that instinct is itself a misreading, I would rather be told so than flattered.

Please give me an accuracy audit I can act on, organized under exactly these headings, in this order:

1. **What the summary gets right** (about 150 words). End with a sentence on whether my own instinct — that Du Bois was an aesthetic philistine — is any better grounded than the critic's account.
2. **Where it distorts** (about 450 words). One numbered item per distortion, in the order the claims appear in the summary, and for each: what the critic says, what Du Bois actually held, and how much of the critic's overall argument rests on the difference. If one of these turns out to be a defensible simplification rather than an error, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a defect.
3. **Does the conclusion survive?** (about 250 words). Whether the verdict on the housing-campaign mural follows once the distortions are corrected, and what a corrected Du Boisian assessment of such a work would demand of it.
4. **Where you could be wrong** (about 150 words).
