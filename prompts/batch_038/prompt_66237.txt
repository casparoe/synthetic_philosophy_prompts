So, background: I'm a backend engineer, never taken a philosophy class in my life, and last week our #random channel turned into a 3am argument about determinism because someone posted that "chaos engineering" (terrible name for what we actually do — we just kill nodes on purpose and see what falls over) proves systems are unpredictable. I noticed partway through that I have a big pile of extremely confident opinions about when something counts as *determined* versus when it counts as *predictable*, and they clearly don't add up to any rule I could actually state. I'd like someone to tell me what rule they do add up to — or, if there isn't one, which of them I have to throw out. Here they are, roughly in the order they came up.

1. **The lab double pendulum.** Same rig, released from what looks like the same angle, differs by a fraction of a degree, and after twenty seconds the two arms are doing completely unrelated things. My verdict: the motion is fully determined by the release, AND it's genuinely unpredictable past a short horizon for anything that can only measure to finite precision. I'm completely comfortable holding both.

2. **Idealized billiards.** Frictionless ball among circular bumpers, exact real-number initial state. My verdict: determined and predictable in principle, by something with exact access to the state. But then someone said these setups can simulate a Turing machine, so "where is the ball after N bounces" might be as hard as the halting problem. If that's true I don't know why I still want to say "predictable in principle" — but I really don't want to give this one up, because the ball *is* just following a law.

3. **A program.** Same binary, same input, same seed, single-threaded: same output, every time. Deterministic and perfectly predictable — just run it. And I think "just run it" is a totally legitimate prediction method, not a dodge. Separately: whether an arbitrary program halts is settled but not decidable, and I'd happily call a specific long-running job "unpredictable" in that sense while agreeing it's determined.

4. **A Life-style grid automaton.** No randomness anywhere, completely deterministic. But to know the state at step a million you have to actually grind through a million steps, there's no shortcut. My verdict: unpredictable in practice, predictable in principle. Except — and this is the part I can't get past — grinding a million steps of the automaton costs about the same as grinding a million steps of a very high precision simulation of the pendulum over the same wall-clock span, and for the pendulum I said simulation *doesn't* count. I want to say the difference is that the automaton never amplifies my input error and the pendulum does. Is "error amplification" the principle? Then case 2 breaks, since billiards amplify too.

5. **Coin flip vs. radioactive atom.** A machine flipping a coin with fixed launch parameters: determined, predictable if you knew the launch well enough, but random for every practical purpose — and I'd say "random" here just *means* that. A hardware RNG based on decay events: not determined, there is no hidden fact to know. I feel this is a real difference in the world and not just me being humble about my ignorance. But operationally I treat them identically (same entropy pool, same reseeding), and I can't point to any difference in how I'd ever use them. So is my distinction earning its keep?

6. **Weather.** Determined-ish, and a three-day forecast is a real prediction while a thirty-day forecast of the actual weather is not — but a thirty-year forecast of the *statistics* is a real prediction. I feel strongly that this isn't hedging, that "the trajectory dies, the distribution survives" is a genuine distinction. Except in case 4 I refused to let statistics count: knowing the distribution over grid configurations at step a million would NOT count for me as predicting the configuration at step a million. Same move allowed in one place, banned in the other, no reason given.

7. **Outages and blame.** We deliberately kill a node; sometimes nothing happens, sometimes it cascades region-wide. Verdict: the cascade was determined by the state of the fleet, it was not predictable by us, and it is unfair to say the on-call engineer should have seen it coming. But when the same team ships a change that takes down a service and a linter would have caught it, I absolutely do think "should have seen it coming" is fair. Same deterministic system, same finite humans, opposite verdict about blame. And case 8 below makes it worse.

8. **My own decisions.** Determined, I think — I'm made of the same stuff as the pendulum. Unpredictable in advance by anyone including me. And I don't think that buys me free will in any impressive sense. But I want to say there's an *openness* there that the grid automaton doesn't have, even though the automaton is also unpredictable in exactly the no-shortcut sense. If no-shortcut is all the openness there is, then my choices are no more open than a Life grid, and I flatly refuse to accept that. I just can't say what the extra ingredient is supposed to be.

Two things I picked up and don't know how to weigh. First, someone mentioned a theorem that a numerically simulated chaotic orbit is always close to *some* genuine orbit with slightly different starting conditions — shadowing, I think? If that's right, does my simulated pendulum trajectory count as a prediction of a pendulum but not of *that* pendulum, and is that a difference anyone should care about? Second, I read that there's a version of the wave equation with computable initial data whose later solution isn't computable at all. That looks like a third flavor of unpredictability that isn't error amplification and isn't no-shortcut, and I don't know where it fits in my list. And a physicist whose name I didn't catch argued in a talk that the real numbers in "initial conditions" are a fiction, that a region of space can only hold finitely many bits, so classical mechanics was never deterministic to begin with — which if true would collapse case 2 and a chunk of case 1.

What I want back: a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated tightly enough that I could argue with it — ideally something like "predictability is a relation between the system, a predictor class, a precision budget, and a time horizon," but with the clauses actually pinned down rather than as a slogan. For each clause, tell me which case above forced it in. Then run it on two cases I did *not* list (and definitely include what it says about an LLM's next token, since that's roughly what started the argument), where I have no idea what my own verdict is. Then be straight about which of my eight verdicts it cannot accommodate — I'd rather hear "case 4 has to go, here's why" than a baroque principle with nine exceptions that saves everything. Also, since I know almost nothing about this area: please clearly mark which parts of your answer are settled and which are live arguments among people who work on this, and don't hand me a contested position dressed up as the consensus.

Last line, exactly: `Verdict:` followed by the number of the single case you think I most need to give up, or `none` if you can genuinely save all eight.
