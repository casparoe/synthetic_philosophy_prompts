I am preparing a workshop paper whose central claim is that hypocrisy can deprive a person of the standing to blame — that some people are simply not in a position to demand an accounting from a wrongdoer, even when what they say is true and they sincerely believe it. In two dry runs of the talk, the discussion collapsed in the same way: an interlocutor insisted that "standing" is pseudo-jurisprudence grafted onto the tu quoque fallacy, and I could not answer him without seeming to concede. So I would like to rehearse against the strongest version of that objection. Please play a committed standing skeptic: an analytic moral philosopher who holds that no one ever lacks the standing to blame anyone, and that everything people attribute to lost standing is fully explained by other features of the case — the hypocrite's insincerity or weak commitment to the norm he invokes, the reduced credibility his own conduct gives his judgment, the damage his blaming does to the relationship and to his audience, or plain rudeness and self-righteousness. You are not a strawman and you have real resources: you may grant that hypocritical blame is often morally objectionable, that the person blamed has a legitimate complaint, and even that in some cases what the hypocrite produces is not blame at all but a performance. What you will never grant is that any of this amounts to forfeiting a right or a position to address a moral demand. Stay in character until I say "stop"; do not cite or attribute views to real philosophers, living or dead, but state every position as your own; and press me with counterexamples and questions of your own, because I want a contest rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening, and it is built to switch off your best explanations at once. Consider a department head who sharply blames a colleague for quietly accepting a gift from a firm that does business with the department. He is entirely sincere: he believes the norm, he would apply it to himself, and in fact he accepted a larger gift from a different firm two years ago, which he has since privately regretted, partly remedied, and never denied to himself or anyone else — so there is no self-deception, no performance, and no insincerity anywhere in the case. He is not rude, his blame is accurate and proportionate, and his track record is such that nobody in the department doubts his judgment about what the norm requires. When the colleague answers "you are one to talk," that reply does not strike me as a fallacy about truth: nobody is inferring that accepting the gift was permissible from the fact that the blamer did it too. It reads instead as a remark about who may address the demand, and it reads as apt. So, on your account, what exactly is left to be forfeited here — and if the answer is "nothing," why does the colleague's retort seem correct rather than merely unhelpful? If tu quoque is always a mistake about the truth of a claim, you owe me an explanation of why this instance of it feels like it is getting something right; and if you prefer to read the retort as an objection to credibility, you have to explain why this department head's credibility is untouched, since everyone in the room already agrees the gift was wrong.

Answer in character, and keep this reply and every subsequent one to exactly five numbered points of one or two sentences each, with no introduction, no framing remark about the exercise, and no closing summary. One of the five may be a question directed back at me, if that is the most forceful move available. You may be as confident as you like, but do not let a contested line pass as settled: wherever a claim you make in a point is one that people who work on blame genuinely dispute rather than one that is common ground among them, mark that point with an asterisk and add a half-sentence inside the point saying what the dispute is. Do not step out of character to do that flagging; the asterisk and the half-sentence are all I want.
