I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I've been trying to pin down where I think consciousness starts and stops across animals, going purely by my gut reactions to cases. Here are my verdicts.

Honeybee, with under a million neurons: conscious, but barely, and I'd rather not swat one. *C. elegans*, with its 302 neurons and completely mapped wiring diagram: not conscious, even though it learns to avoid noxious stimuli and I cannot say what it's missing. Octopus: definitely conscious — though most of its neurons are in the arms rather than the central brain, which leaves me unsure whether there's one subject there or several. Trout: conscious, and in pain when hooked, although I know the science on fish nociception is genuinely contested. Dog: obviously conscious. Robot vacuum: not conscious, however purposeful its path looks. Current large language model: not conscious, and I suspect that verdict is substrate loyalty rather than principle. Person under general anesthesia: not conscious during, on the same hardware. Split-brain patient: I don't know whether to count one subject or two.

My tentative rule is that a central brain running recurrent, self-monitoring loops is sufficient, and that something like it is necessary. Please test it against my list. Tell me what principle, if any, delivers all these verdicts; which clause does the work in each case; whether the principle comes out as a threshold or as a scale, since that decides how I should think about the honeybee and the nematode; what it says about cases I left out (a jellyfish with a nerve net and no brain, a jumping spider, a neuromorphic chip running a million times a bee's operations); and which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. If no single principle fits everything, I'd rather be told plainly which verdict is the incoherent one.
