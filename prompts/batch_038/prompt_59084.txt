I'm a third-year PhD student in normative ethics and I've been asked to give the remarks at a memorial dedication in June for a small passenger ferry that went down off our coast eleven years ago. Two rafts, eleven people in them. The lifeboat crew steered toward noise and shape in the dark and reached the raft that turned out to hold seven; when they got back to the other one, three of its four were gone and they pulled one out alive. The room will be the families from both rafts, the crew (two of whom have said publicly they'd do exactly the same again), coast guard brass, and about a dozen people from my department who know precisely what I think about aggregation.

Here's my problem. I think Taurek is right that the numbers shouldn't count, and I can't write the speech everyone is expecting — the one where brave men faced an awful sum and did the arithmetic well. I don't believe that arithmetic, and worse, that version quietly files the three under 'remainder.' But I equally cannot stand in front of those families and tell them their loss didn't add. So write me the remarks, call it six minutes: honor the crew and the three without asserting or denying that saving the greater number was required, and land as love rather than as evasion for a room where almost nobody has ever heard of Taurek.

The thing I want to build it on is a wrinkle the standard setup hides. The 'should the numbers count' question as it's normally posed assumes you can see both groups and tally them. Nobody on that boat tallied anything — the seven and the four only became numbers afterwards, on a manifest and then in an inquest file. I want that to carry the speech, not as a point about probability or uncertainty, but as what lets me say something true about each of them being, from where they were treading water, the entire case.

Constraints: no 'trolley problem,' no thought-experiment vocabulary at all, no coin flips mentioned out loud, and nothing that could be quoted back as a verdict on the inquest. Then after the speech, give me a short note — where you took liberties with Taurek, and separate what's actually settled in this debate from what's still live among people who work on it, because I don't want to walk up to that podium having been handed a contested view dressed up as the consensus.
