I spent thirty-eight years as a field physiologist and have taken up philosophy seriously only since retiring; my current preoccupation is the question of which animals are conscious. I attended a public symposium last autumn on the welfare status of cephalopods and decapod crustaceans. An audience member observed that the behavioural tests we apply to these animals — trade-offs against competing motivations, conditioned place avoidance, responsiveness to analgesics — have produced results that different laboratories read in opposite directions, and that no criterion commands general assent. One panellist replied that "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," and the room plainly took that to close the matter in favour of a presumption of sentience. I confess that in an earlier career I deployed the same line myself, against colleagues who inferred from an unsuccessful survey that a population was not present at a site. So I am not asking you to tell me the slogan is worthless; I want to know exactly what it can and cannot bear.

Please separate the distinct precise claims that one sentence might be expressing, and arrange them as a menu running from the near-trivial through the substantive to the flatly false. For each reading, state what it asserts, what would have to hold for it to be true, and whether it supports the use the panellist put it to. Three points especially need disentangling, and I would like each addressed explicitly. First, the difference between the situation in which no adequate investigation has been undertaken and the situation in which a well-designed investigation returned a null result: does the slogan apply equally to both, or only to one? Second, whether the slogan, even on its strongest true reading, can license anything beyond suspension of judgment — in particular whether it can generate a positive presumption that these animals are sentient, rather than merely blocking the inference that they are not. Third, whether it can carry the burden-shifting claim that was made alongside it, that the onus falls on those who deny sentience.

I am also curious whether the slogan has a purely practical rather than evidential employment — a reason to act protectively under uncertainty that does not depend on how probable the hypothesis is — and whether the panellist was in fact relying on that while appearing to rely on evidence.

Begin with one sentence giving your bottom line: which reading was doing the work, and whether it survives scrutiny. Then give the numbered menu of readings with a verdict on each, followed by your answer on the three points above. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
