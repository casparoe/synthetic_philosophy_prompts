I did a summer in a life and health insurer's underwriting unit and I'm working through the early exam material, so this vocabulary is genuinely the one I think in.

So here's the ask. Take the naturalism/normativism fight about disease -- the naturalist line where disease is an internal state that statistically depresses some functional ability below the typical level for your age-and-sex reference class, with functions fixed by what parts typically contribute to survival and reproduction, and illness treated as a seperate, frankly value-laden notion layered on top; the normativist reply that you can't pick out anything as a dysfunction until you've already decided which capacities are worth having, so the whole thing collapses into our interests; and the hybrid attempts to keep a biological core plus a normative filter -- and restate all three positions in my vocabulary. I mean literally: preferred/standard/substandard/declined classes, impairment ratings and how a manual stacks debits and credits into a number, extra premium, exclusions, postponements, material facts and what has to be disclosed, morbidity and mortality tables, adverse selection, the gap between a condition and an impairment and a claim, and the fact that we rate heavy things that aren't diseases at all (smoking, BMI, family history, some occupations) while never rating plenty of things that are. Assume I'm fluent there and only half-fluent in the philosophy. And please be disciplined about not smuggling: the easy move is to quietly turn "dysfunction" into "expected loss" or "disease" into "rateable impairment," and then report that the naturalists and normativists are arguing about pricing assumptions. That would make the whole exercise worthless. Wherever you have to import a notion that underwriting doesn't natively have, flag it as an import rather than dressing it up as a translation.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want -- don't make it a closing paragraph. Which of their claims carry over cleanly? My hunch is that the naturalist's age-and-sex reference classes are basically our rating classes with the money taken out, and that their need to put a hard disease/non-disease boundary on a graded distribution is structurally the same problem as having to draw an accept/decline line through a smooth risk gradient. Which claims only fit if you bend them? And which ones can't be said in my vocabulary at all? Two candidates I want you to test: the normativist's "we call it a disease because it's bad for the person," since in my framework bad only ever means costly, and the naturalist's insistence that the boundary is value-free, since we'd never claim our boundaries are found rather than chosen -- our manuals differ company to company for the same applicant and nobody thinks one of them is factually wrong. Tell me whether that residue means my framework is missing something real about health, or whether the philosophers' disagreement is thinner than it reads once you notice both sides agree the underlying facts are continuous and the disagreement is only about where the authority for the cut-off comes from. Finish with two or three concrete cases where the vocabularies would sort things differently -- something we'd rate hard that no naturalist would call a disease, and something that's plainly a disease on their account but that underwriting would leave at standard rates.
