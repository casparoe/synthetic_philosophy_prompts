**Draft chapter: "Aspiration and Evidence: On the Modal Commitments of the Kalai–Smorodinsky Solution"**

**¶1.** A two-person bargaining problem is a pair: a closed, convex and comprehensive feasible set S of utility pairs, together with a disagreement point d lying in S. A solution concept assigns to each such problem a point on the Pareto frontier of S. Nash's rule maximises the product of the two players' gains over d. The rule Kalai and Smorodinsky proposed in 1975 instead takes the ray from d through the ideal point a(S) — the pair formed by each player's best feasible payoff, taken separately — and selects where that ray meets the frontier. Equivalently, it selects the frontier point at which the two players realise equal proportions of their maximum available gains. Both rules satisfy Pareto efficiency, symmetry, and invariance under separate positive affine rescalings of each player's utility function. They diverge over a fourth condition. Nash imposes independence of irrelevant alternatives. Kalai and Smorodinsky drop it in favour of monotonicity: if the best payoff one player can attain at every level of the other player's payoff weakly improves, while the other player's best attainable payoff is left unchanged, then the first player's award must not fall.

**¶2.** The received reading of this divergence treats it as a quarrel about fairness. Nash's axiom is said to make the outcome indifferent to the shape of the menu beyond the chosen point; Kalai and Smorodinsky's is said to let the whole menu speak, so that a player whose prospects have genuinely improved cannot be told that nothing has changed because the selected agreement happens to survive the expansion. On this reading the two camps disagree about how much of a person's situation is relevant to a fair division, and the choice between them is a choice of moral temperament.

**¶3.** My claim is that the received reading understates the stakes, because it obscures a difference in the modal status of the inputs each rule consults. Everything the Nash rule looks at — the disagreement point, and the local geometry of the frontier — consists of jointly feasible agreements, or facts about jointly feasible agreements. The decisive input to the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule is not of that kind. Except in degenerate cases the ideal point lies outside S: it is built by pairing what player 1 can have at her best with what player 2 can have at his best, and there is, by construction, no agreement in S at which both obtain those figures. The rule therefore assigns decisive weight to a description of the situation that no available agreement satisfies. Its conception of fairness is aspiration-relative in the strict sense: what each party is owed is fixed by the proportion of an unrealisable maximum that they receive.

**¶4.** This is where the philosophy of utility enters. Suppose one holds a revealed-preference view in the Samuelson–Houthakker tradition: a utility function is a bookkeeping device whose entire content is exhausted by the choice data it rationalises, and whose numerical form is not itself an object of empirical knowledge. Afriat's theorem is the culmination of that programme, in that it shows a finite body of price-and-quantity observations to be consistent with utility maximisation exactly when it satisfies a system of inequalities, and permits a representing function to be constructed when it does. On such a view, a magnitude that never appears in any choice situation an agent actually faces is idle machinery. And it is tempting to press the worry against Kalai–Smorodinsky in just this way: the rule's proportionality condition compares the size of one player's gain with the size of the other's, and so requires an interpersonal comparison of utility increments, which the revealed-preference programme refuses to license. On that formulation, Kalai–Smorodinsky demands strictly more cardinal structure than Nash does, and the demand is precisely the one a behaviourally disciplined economics cannot meet.

**¶5.** A complication: both rules are invariant under separate positive affine transformations of the two utility functions, each player's function being rescaled independently of the other's. Whatever the two rules disagree about, therefore, it is not the amount of cardinal structure required, and neither can be accused of importing a common unit of measurement across persons. The difference between them must lie elsewhere.

**¶6.** Before saying where, let me press a technical point about how little work monotonicity actually does. Take d = (0,0). Let S₁ be the set of pairs (x,y) with x, y ≥ 0 and y ≤ 1 − x², so that player 2's best payoff at level x of player 1's is 1 − x². The ideal point is (1,1). The Nash rule maximises x(1 − x²), giving x = 1/√3 and y = 2/3, that is approximately (0.577, 0.667); the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule runs the diagonal into the frontier y = 1 − x², giving x = y = (√5 − 1)/2, approximately (0.618, 0.618). Now let S₂ be the set of pairs with x, y ≥ 0 and y ≤ φ(x), where φ is the concave piecewise-linear function through (0, 6/5), (7/10, 3/5) and (1, 0); so φ(x) = 6/5 − (6/7)x on [0, 7/10] and φ(x) = 2 − 2x on [7/10, 1]. The ideal point is now (1, 6/5): player 1's best payoff is unchanged, player 2's has risen, and player 2's best payoff at every level of player 1's is strictly higher than before. In S₂ the Nash rule maximises xφ(x) and selects exactly the kink, (7/10, 3/5) = (0.7, 0.6); the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule runs y = (6/5)x into the frontier and selects (7/12, 7/10), approximately (0.583, 0.7). So the Nash rule responds to an unambiguous improvement in player 2's opportunities by cutting her payoff from 2/3 to 3/5, while Kalai–Smorodinsky raises it from about 0.618 to 0.7. Monotonicity bites.

**¶7.** But notice how narrowly it bites, and this is the observation I most want the chapter to rest on. At any interior Nash point on a concave frontier, the tangent-line inequality together with the first-order condition gives the bound that player 2's award is at least half her ideal payoff: the tangent at the Nash point, extrapolated back to the axis, meets it at no more than twice the Nash payoff. In the example, player 2's award in S₂ is exactly 6/10 = half of 6/5, so the construction sits precisely on that bound. It follows that a violation of monotonicity by the Nash rule is impossible whenever the original frontier is linear, since there the Nash award is exactly half the ideal and cannot fall below it. The celebrated counterexample requires strict concavity: it requires diminishing marginal rates of substitution between the players' utilities, which in a bargaining setting usually means the object being divided is not a homogeneous pie at all, but a bundle of heterogeneous goods whose joint production exhibits curvature. The rhetorical force of monotonicity is drawn from the image of a growing pie; its logical force is available only where that image fails.

**¶8.** The mirror image is equally instructive. S₁ is strictly contained in S₂: every agreement feasible before remains feasible, and player 2 has strictly more. Yet Kalai–Smorodinsky leaves player 1 worse off, at about 0.583 rather than about 0.618. Monotonicity does not say that nobody loses when the feasible set expands; it says something conditional and asymmetric, protecting a player only when the other's ideal payoff is held fixed. Neither rule delivers the property the lay name suggests.

**¶9.** That leaves me with two conclusions and no way to choose between them, which is where I need help. The first is that the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule purchases its responsiveness to aspiration with an input that no behaviourally disciplined account of utility can supply, and that the axiom protecting that input does less work than its reputation implies. The second is that this line of argument proves too much. Expected utility theory is saturated with unactualised alternatives: the von Neumann–Morgenstern numbers are read off choices among lotteries, and almost none of the lotteries in the eliciting set is ever realised. If never being exhibited in choice disqualifies the ideal point, it disqualifies the cardinal scale itself, and Nash's rule with it. So the objection must be narrowed from "unactualised" to "realisable separately but never jointly", and I am not confident that this narrower category carries any epistemic weight at all. A player's best payoff is, after all, exhibited in choice: it is what she gets in some agreement she might actually sign.
