Thirty-eight years I spent in a lab measuring things, and roughly half of that was spent arguing with colleagues about whether a number actually meant anything — what scale it sat on, whether its zero was real or just convenient, whether my units were traceable to a standard somebody else could check. Retired four years back, started reading philosophy to fill the hours, and I've wandered into welfare economics and ethics, where one camp says you can't compare one person's utility with another's and the other camp says of course we can and do, and I cannot tell what either side is actually claiming. The measurement part of my brain says there are at least five separate questions tangled together here and everybody is answering different ones and calling it a disagreement.

So: build me a decision tree for it. The kind where every branch is a question you could put to someone who's never heard of the literature, and every leaf is a position with a name (or marked "unnamed" if nobody holds it). Seven or eight branch points, ten or so leaves. I want the branches to turn on the things I think actually matter, roughly: what sort of quantity welfare is in the first place (a ranking of alternatives? a felt state? a list of what people can do and be?); what scale structure the comparison needs — do I only need to know who's better off, or also by how much, and does the scale have a genuine zero or only differences; whether the units are pinned down across people or whether I'm allowed to rescale each person separately without changing any verdict; whether the comparison is impossible in principle, merely impossible in practice, or actually easy if you look in the right place (the brain, behaviour, what people pay); and finally what the comparison licenses once you've got it — rank, add, maximize, redistribute.

Please build it out of the logical space first rather than out of a list of famous people: lay out the dimensions and the cells they generate, then drop the real positions into the cells, then tell me about the cells nobody's standing in and why — incoherent, just unappealing, or simply unexplored. A few leaves I'd insist on seeing: the older view that interpersonal comparisons aren't merely difficult but not even the sort of thing that could be true or false; the aggregation result that gets you a weighted sum out of the Pareto condition plus expected-utility axioms; whatever position says comparing levels and comparing units come apart and that the second is the hard one; prioritarianism, which as far as I can make out wants cardinal levels but not comparisons between people and which I suspect my tree will struggle to seat anywhere; the view that there's a common neural currency so just go measure it; and at least one position holding that the whole question is malformed because welfare isn't the kind of thing that comes in units. Don't let any leaf just be labelled utilitarianism.

Phrase the branch questions as concrete cases wherever you can. The ones I keep chewing on: same noxious heat applied to two people's forearms, one reports 8 out of 10 and the other reports 3, and the scanner lights up in near identical places in both — which of those three readings is the welfare? Is a year of mild headaches for each of a million people worse than one hour of torture for one person, and does your answer depend on which branch you took earlier? Two people on identical salaries, one delighted with her life and one miserable, and the miserable one scores herself as happy as she was before an accident that cost her her legs — does the adaptation change the comparison or only the report?

Ground rules. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations; I've no way to chase them and I'd much rather you not manufacture any. If you're unsure whether a position or a name is genuinely out there, say so at the leaf instead of stating it brightly. And at the end, flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really pull apart — I'd sooner know where the instrument bottoms out than have it look tidier than it is.

Audience is my Saturday reading group, about a dozen retirees, half ex-engineers and half former schoolteachers, none of us has read any of this. So plain wording at the branch points, and every leaf gets one sentence on the most awkward thing it has to swallow. Oh — and one small thing, I keep mistyping "comparision" in my own notes, don't let that put you off.
