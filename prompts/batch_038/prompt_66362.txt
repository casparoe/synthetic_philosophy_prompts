Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about the handicap principle and ended up further apart than we started. Her position was that a signal earns its credibility by being wasteful: if an advertisement costs the advertiser nothing, it is mere cheap talk, and the extravagance of the peacock's tail is precisely what buys its reliability. Mine was that waste is beside the point. What matters, I claimed, is that the cost falls differentially — that the same expenditure is cheaper for the sender who genuinely has the quality than for the sender who does not — so that a signal can be perfectly honest while being trivially cheap for some senders, and ruinously expensive for all of them without separating anyone at all. We each produced cases and neither of us moved. She pointed to the depth of a toad's croak, which tracks body size, is free to produce, and cannot be faked. I pointed to plumage badges that are arbitrary and cheap but are kept honest because receivers who catch a bluffer punish it. We agreed only that both of us were happy to call the graduate degree an honest signal and unhappy about why, and that the dispute is really about whether costs and benefits trade off in one shared currency at all.

I would rather stage that argument than repeat it. I teach a 75-minute section of an upper-division philosophy of biology course: eighteen students, no biology background, comfortable with simple arithmetic and with the idea of a payoff table, and none of them has met signalling theory before. I want them to run into the problem rather than hear my version of it.

Design a game, with payoffs but no real money, that puts them on both sides of this dispute and forces the distinction to surface from play. Specifically:

- Roles, materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the whole 75 minutes, including a scoring sheet that students can complete without a calculator.
- A first round in which a genuinely expensive but uniformly expensive signal is available to everyone and separation fails. They should feel that costliness alone bought them nothing.
- A second round identical except that the cost of the same expenditure now differs by privately assigned type, so that separation emerges. Make the numbers concrete and check that the intended equilibrium is what a self-interested player would actually choose, rather than an outcome I have to talk them into.
- A third round introducing an index: a feature whose value is fixed by the role card rather than chosen, free to display, and impossible to misrepresent. Then a fourth round with a conventional badge: free to adopt, but honest only because receivers can pay to audit it and punish detected bluffers.
- An optional fifth round, if time allows, in which the accounting is multiplicative rather than additive — signalling lowers a player's chance of surviving to collect any payoff at all, instead of subtracting points — so they can see whether their ranking of the mechanisms changes when costs and benefits stop sharing a currency.

The words handicap, costly signal, index, and cheap talk must not appear in anything they are handed before the debrief. They should be arguing about why the second round worked before anyone has a name for it.

For the debrief: map each round onto the mechanism it was built to exhibit, then adjudicate the two slogans from my argument — waste versus differential cost — and say plainly which rounds vindicate which, and what each of us was half-right about. I also want you to flag the result that an honest signal need not be costly at equilibrium, and tell me how to raise it in fifteen minutes when my game, as designed, never actually produces such a case.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: where the pooling round will tempt someone into an accidental bluff that pays, whether receivers will simply stop attending to signals by round three, how quickly a small group of friends will collude out of band, and what the fix is for each. Finish with an honest accounting of which features of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply verbally — including whatever damage is done by the fact that my students choose their signals deliberately and know the payoff table, whereas the stability of a signalling system in nature comes from selection and statistical reliability rather than from anyone reasoning about an equilibrium.
