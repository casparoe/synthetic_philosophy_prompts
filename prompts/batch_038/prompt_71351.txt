I am a junior, and this spring I am taking AP United States Government while also serving as co-captain of our school's debate team, which means I have been drafting an op-ed for the student paper about whether Article V should be made easier to satisfy and whether the Senate's equal representation of states should be abolished. Over Thanksgiving my older brother, who is a sophomore majoring in political science at a state university and who enjoys this sort of thing far more than is reasonable, told me that my whole framing was naive. His argument was that the Constitution is what he called a Ulysses contract: the framers, foreseeing that future majorities would be tempted by short-term passions, tied us to the mast, and the entrenched features I want to loosen are precisely the ropes. He said the political theorist Jon Elster wrote a book called Ulysses and the Sirens developing the idea, and that once you see it this way, hard-to-amend provisions stop looking like rule by the dead and start looking like rational self-government extended across time. Our government teacher had earlier raised what I think is the Jeffersonian objection, that each generation holds the earth only for its own lifetime, as a kind of life-lease, and therefore should not be shackled by fundamental law written by people long buried; my brother waved that away as failing to understand who the "we" in the story is. I am not prepared to grant him that, but I also cannot articulate exactly where the picture stops carrying weight, and I would rather not publish an op-ed whose central metaphor I have not audited.

So I would like you to audit the analogy as my brother is using it. First, please give me an explicit table of correspondences: which feature of the constitutional order plays the role of Ulysses, the mast, the ropes, the sailors, the wax in their ears, the sirens, the song, and the stretch of water the ship must cross regardless. Then please work through the disanalogies I have half-formed and any I have missed. (1) The wax strikes me as an entirely different device from the ropes: the sailors are not constrained in what they may do, they are deprived of the ability to hear the temptation at all, which is a mechanism aimed at preferences and access to information rather than at action — so which constitutional arrangement is supposed to be the wax, and does the fact that the myth uses two incompatible devices at once mean the analogy is doing double work that hides an argument? (2) Only Ulysses is tied, and the crew retains the physical capacity to ignore his screaming and cut him loose, which raises the question of who actually holds the knife; in the constitutional case, is Article V the release clause, in which case the analogy is not about ropes at all but about how many hands must agree to reach them? (3) Ulysses knew in detail what the sirens would offer and knew his own weakness in advance; the framers could not have known what a modern emergency, a mass medium, or a two-hundred-and-forty-year-old party system would look like, and I suspect the analogy only licenses precommitment when the future temptation is foreseeable. (4) Ulysses binds himself against a craving he judges, both beforehand and afterward, to be a mistake rather than a considered judgment; if the later self regards its wish to be untied as a reasoned change of view rather than an urge, what could possibly distinguish a siren song from a changed mind, and who is competent to make that call? (5) The story borrows a single continuous person with a body, memories, and an identity that persists across the interval, whereas a polity has none of those and gains new members who were not alive at the binding and were never asked; my brother's reply about who the "we" is seems to assume the answer rather than give it, and I would like to know whether that is a genuine disanalogy or only a rhetorical one.

What I want out of your answer is a judgment about the conclusion, not just a list. Please sort the disanalogies into those that actually undercut the inference my brother draws — namely, that because entrenchment can be a rational form of self-binding, the specific hard-to-change features I proposed to loosen should be left in place — and those that merely make the picture less pretty without touching the inference. Please say plainly what the analogy smuggles in that the target case lacks: a prior account of which desires are authentic and which are sirens, an assumption that the future self is epistemically worse off than the past one, and the presumption that the party being bound is the same party that did the binding and consented to it. Then tell me whether the conclusion survives once those are on the table, and whether there is a weaker claim the analogy can still support, such as that some precommitment is defensible in principle even if these particular ropes were tied by people who are not us. Finally, I would like a better analogy if one exists. My brother mentioned in passing that the same phrase is used, I believe, for advance directives in which a person with a recurring psychiatric condition authorizes treatment in advance that they may refuse during an episode, and it occurs to me that this may be doing more of the intuitive work than Homer is, since in that case a clinician rather than the patient decides whether the ropes hold; alternatives I have been turning over include a trust fund, a lease with a penalty for early termination, a savings account with a withdrawal delay, a two-key system for launching something dangerous, and a do-not-resuscitate order. Please say which, if any, of these maps better onto constitutional entrenchment, what each would imply about who is entitled to object, and which one I should use in the op-ed if I want the metaphor to survive a reader as suspicious as my brother.
