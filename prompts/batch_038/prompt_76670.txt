I'm a third-year philosophy of science PhD student and I've been roped into drafting the first pass at a joint statement for an adverserial collaboration that two people in my department have agreed to, semi-reluctantly. They've been circling the same fight for years and my advisor thinks making them co-sign something will help. My job is to write the document they'd both put their names to, and I'd like your version of it rather than mine, because I'm too close to it.

Camp A — call them the universalists — holds that Solomonoff induction isn't one model of inference among competitors but the normative answer to the problem of induction. Their line: the universal prior dominates every computable measure up to a multiplicative constant, so no computable learner beats it in the limit; Solomonoff's convergence results then say the posterior concentrates on the truth; and so Occam's razor stops being a heuristic we keep because it has worked and becomes a theorem about the shape of the right prior. The incomputability of Kolmogorov complexity is, on this view, beside the point — what's being justified is a norm, not a procedure.

Camp B — call them the relativists — grants all the mathematics and says it's normatively idle. Their complaints: K is only defined up to an additive constant fixed by the reference machine, and for any pair of machines you could actually write down, that constant dwarfs the complexity gaps between rival scientific hypotheses; the domination and convergence results are asymptotic, and an asymptotic guarantee with an uncomputable constant is silent at n = 10^3; the choice of reference machine is doing all the substantive work and is either arbitrary or a smuggled empirical hypothesis; and "simplicity means short in the right notation" just relocates the problem of induction into the choice of notation. They also lean on no-free-lunch style results — averaged over all logically possible data-generating processes, anti-inductive priors do as well as inductive ones.

The thing I need the statement to actually engage with, rather than wave at, is a toy study I ran. I built two tiny languages that are universal only in the technical sense and barely even then: a stripped-down Brainfuck-style tape language (6 commands, binary cells) and a tag-system-style string rewriter that needed a much fatter alphabet than I expected. Bounded search for shortest programs on 200 binary strings of length ≤ 48, cutoff at 20 symbols, so everything is an upper bound — I know that's a caveat and I want it stated. Results: K_U(x) − K_V(x) was nowhere near constant across strings, it spread over roughly 17 symbols; and on 14% of the strings where I had two rival candidate generators, the ranking of which generator was simpler flipped between the two languages. Camp A tells me the invariance theorem only ever bounded the supremum of that difference, that machines this tiny are the pathological regime, that real theory pairs differ by far more than any real machine-pair constant, and that my 14% is an artifact of the cutoff. Camp B tells me the flip rate is exactly the point: the theorem permits ordering inversions up to the constant, and nobody has ever measured that constant for a pair of machines big enough to be interesting.

What I want back is the drafted statement itself, with these parts:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign — I'd expect somewhere between 8 and 12. Don't make them vacuous ("both sides agree simplicity has some role in theory choice" is worthless to me), but don't include anything either side would need a footnote to swallow.
2. A disagreements table with columns: issue | Camp A's formulation, in wording A would accept | Camp B's formulation, in wording B would accept | genuine crux or verbal.
3. For each genuine crux, a specific test, argument, or case that both would accept as bearing on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance so the crux is at least quasi-falsifiable. My toy study should show up in here somewhere — including what additional data would settle it and what each camp predicts about that data.
4. A short closing section where each camp states what would actually move them.

My own guess is that the two real cruxes are (i) whether fixing a reference machine is a conventional choice or a substantive empirical posit about how compressible the world is, and (ii) whether an asymptotic guarantee carries any normative weight at finite n — but I'd like you to check whether those are the right two, and specifically to rule on whether the "does 2^−K justify simplicity or merely restate it" dispute is substantive or just two ways of saying the same thing.

Two things I care about most. First, commit. I don't want every row of that table marked "genuine but unresolved" — actually rule on which disputes are verbal and which are substantive, and say why in a sentence. Where you have to make a judgment call about what a camp would accept, make the call and defend it in one line rather than hedging both directions; and if you think I've characterized either camp unfairly, rewrite that camp's position into something they'd genuinely sign and tell me you did it. A draft that both sides would call slanted is worse than useless to me, but so is a draft so diplomatically bland that nobody learns anything.

Second, flag your own fudges. If a clause only survives because it's ambiguous enough for both camps to read it their way, keep the clause but mark it with a bracketed note saying so — I'd much rather see the diplomatic blur labelled than have it invisible.

No bibliography and please don't invent citations; I can't check them and neither of these two would let me get away with it. 1200–1800 words is about right.
