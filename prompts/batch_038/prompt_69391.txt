I write a monthly column for a regional magazine's ideas section, and this month's piece is about what we owe one another when we say things out loud — in politics, in journalism, in the ordinary business of telling people what you think is true. While researching it, I read a long essay in a general-circulation magazine by a journalist who has clearly done some reading and who interviewed a philosopher along the way. Two of its paragraphs are about the philosophical debate over what licenses a flat-out assertion, and I want to lean on them, partly because I found them persuasive and partly because I don't have the training to reconstruct that debate for myself. My worry is that I would be laundering someone else's simplifications into my own column. A friend who took an epistemology seminar in college told me, unhelpfully and without elaborating, that the essay gets the shape of the debate right and the details wrong. Here is the passage, reconstructed from my notes — the wording is mine, but the claims and their order are hers:

"There is a rule for assertion, and the rule is knowledge: you may say that p only if you know that p. This is not moral advice, and it is not a report of what people actually do; it is more like the rule that a bishop moves diagonally. Chess is the game it is because of its rules, and assertion is the speech act it is because of its rule, which is why, on this picture, assertion is the only speech act with a norm built into it — and why a flat-out assertion of something you do not know is less a bad assertion than a failure to assert at all. What makes the view attractive is that it settles several puzzles with a single move. It settles the lottery: you may be as confident as you like that your ticket will lose, and you will very probably be right, but you still should not say so, because probability, however high, is not knowledge. It settles the oddity of saying that it is raining and that you do not know whether it is raining; that sentence is no contradiction, but its second half undoes what its first half does. And because knowledge is at bottom just true belief resting on adequate support, the rule amounts to something familiar — say only what is true, and only what you have good enough reasons for. The cost is that the rule cannot be lived. You can never be sure that you know, so you can never be sure your assertion is in order, and whether you kept the rule is something you discover only afterwards, when it turns out that what you said was true or false. If this really were the rule, no doctor could report a diagnosis, no historian could tell you what happened, and ordinary conversation, which runs on hunches and half-memories, would be one uninterrupted sequence of violations. Defenders have a reply, but it amounts to a retreat: they concede that the rule cannot govern everything we call assertion and fall back on the weaker claim that you may say whatever you have good enough evidence for, which is not the knowledge rule at all. My conclusion is that the philosophers are right about the aspiration and wrong about the details, and that what we owe one another in speech is not knowledge but diligence — say what you have done the work to check, and own it when the work turns out to have been bad."

What I need is an audit I can act on rather than a general impression, so please go through it claim by claim and tell me, for each one, whether a defender of the knowledge norm would accept it, accept it as a fair simplification, or object — and if they would object, what precisely they would say. Three places especially confuse me. First, the chess comparison, and the claim that assertion is the only speech act with a norm built into it: is the analogy doing legitimate work, and where does it start to mislead? Second, the sentence saying that knowledge is at bottom true belief with adequate support. That made the rule feel trivial to me, in a way the rest of the passage insists it is not, and I suspect the two halves of the essay are describing different views without noticing. Third, the claim that you find out only afterwards whether you kept the rule. I cannot square that with the lottery paragraph, where the whole point seems to be that you can tell in advance that you should keep quiet. Beyond those, I would like to know whether either of the two objections the essay presses — the doctor and the historian, and the supposed retreat to evidence — survives once the summary is corrected, and whether the essay has fairly characterized what its opponents actually hold (I gather the knowledge rule is mainly associated with Timothy Williamson, though she never names him, and I also gather there are rival norms in play that she does not distinguish from one another). Finally, and most important for my purposes: her conclusion is that we owe each other diligence rather than knowledge. Does that conclusion depend on any of the errors you find, is it even the right sort of thing to compete with a norm of assertion, and how does it handle the lottery case she herself raises? Please do not send me a reading list; I would rather have your assessment of the passage than a set of titles I have no way to check.
