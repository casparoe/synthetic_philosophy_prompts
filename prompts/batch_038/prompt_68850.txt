I sit on the board of trustees of the Ashcombe Institute, whose special collections hold the papers of the late novelist Ilse Marchetti. Under the terms of her will, the correspondence was deposited in two tranches: the first was opened to qualified readers in the reading room five years ago, the second has remained sealed and falls due for review at our meeting on the fourteenth. The question before the board is what to do with roughly nine hundred letters in the sealed tranche written to Marchetti by private individuals — friends, lovers, a sister, two former students, a physician — most of whom are still living and none of whom signed anything. The institute's access policy commits us only to weigh "the legitimate expectations of living third parties" against "the scholarly value of the collection," and neither phrase has ever been given content.

Three positions have hardened. The appointed biographer argues that the sealed tranche covers precisely the decade in which Marchetti's work turned, that redaction would leave the record unintelligible, and that anyone who chose to write intimately to a famous author assumed the risk of eventual disclosure. The estate, through Marchetti's daughter, argues that her mother's wishes are dispositive and that she plainly intended the letters to survive her. A solicitor acting for four of the correspondents argues that copyright in a letter belongs to its writer but the physical object belongs to the recipient's estate, so these people have no property claim left and privacy is the only door still open to them; he asks for a further fifty-year seal.

I must declare an interest that the memo has to confront rather than tiptoe around. Before the first tranche was restricted, I read it under the institute's standard undertaking, and a transposed episode from one correspondent's account of her marriage now appears in my own novel, published last spring. Nobody outside this board knows which letter it came from, and the correspondent has not, so far as I know, recognised herself. I am not asking the board to pretend that this makes me disqualified; I am asking the memo to say what turns on it — whether a novelist's absorption of such material into fiction is a different kind of use from a biographer's documentary quotation, and whether the difference lies in identifiability, in purpose, in the fact that fiction cannot be checked against the record, or in nothing that ought to comfort me.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the trustees in advance of the fourteenth. It should recommend one concrete disposition — open, open with redaction, open under a restricted quotation regime, continued seal, or some tiered combination — together with a stated term and the practical machinery for it, and it should supply a test the reading-room staff could actually apply letter by letter without consulting us again. Reason from what privacy is protecting here and who gets to claim it, rather than from jurisdictional technicalities or from authorities the trustees would have to look up; I want the argument to stand on its own. And give the biographer's risk-assumption argument its full strength, since I expect it to carry the room, before explaining why it does or does not prevail.
