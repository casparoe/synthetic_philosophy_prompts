I do time series analysis for a living — state-space models, unobserved components (local level, local linear trend, stochastic cycle), change-point and structural break detection, unit-root versus trend-stationary tests, spectral estimation, forecast-error variance decompositions. I'm completely at home in all of that. What I'm not at home in is French historiography, which I've read mostly at the level of summaries and secondhand accounts.

I've been trying to make sense of Braudel's scheme of historical time — the near-motionless history of people and their environment, the slowly rhythmic history of economies and societies, and the short, event-sized history of individuals and politics — by translating it into the machinery I actually use. Concretely: I've got roughly 400 years of anual series for one region (grain prices, real wages, crude death rates, tonnage through the main port, tax receipts, counts of notarial contracts), and I want to fit a common unobserved trend plus a stochastic cycle plus a fat-tailed high-frequency residual, and then say that the trend is the longue durée, the cycle is the conjoncture, and the residual is the event. I'd also want to say that what Braudel means by geography shows up in my world as the reason the trend is common across all the series from the same basin, i.e. as a cointegrating relation — and that his contempt for event-history is just the claim that the residual carries a negligible variance share.

Your job is to do the translation as faithfully as you can, working through those four identifications one at a time (trend, cycle, residual, cointegration-as-geography), and for each of them tell me whether it carries over clean, whether it carries over only with distortion — and if so exactly what I'd have to bend — or whether he's saying something my vocabulary simply can't say. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actualy care about: gather up the leftovers and tell me where they belong.

A few things I'd rather you be blunt about than paper over:

- My framework allocates explanatory importance by share of variance, and I suspect Braudel's claim is closer to the opposite — that the slow layer matters most precisely because it barely moves. If that's right, say it loudly, because it would mean my variance decomposition is answering a question he wasn't asking.
- A stochastic cycle presupposes something like stationarity in the deviations. I think his conjonctures don't have a characteristic frequency at all and aren't mean-reverting in any strong sense. Is that a real problem for my translation, or am I reading in a technical notion of cycle he never claimed?
- He also seems to be making modal claims — that structures permit some events and rule others out, that they constrain what could have happened rather than forming a component of what did. I have no way to express that. Is the modal content something my vocabulary is missing, or is it a grand way of dressing up an ordinary low-variance claim?
- And there's a polemical layer: his hostility to event-history is partly an argument about which history deserves writing, and by whom. Does any of that survive as a claim about a model, or is it strictly a claim about the profession?

Please don't hedge at me. I want a committed answer: give a verdict on each of the four identifications (holds, bends, fails), and then take a definite stand on the overall question — whether the residue shows my vocabulary is genuinely inexpressive about something real, or whether his scheme, once translated, turns out to be mostly a persuasive way of saying things that were either obvious or unfalsifiable. Give me the reasons for the verdict, flag the one or two spots where you're least sure, but still land somewhere instead of laying out the options and leaving me to pick.
