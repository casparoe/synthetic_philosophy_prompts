I write historical novels — long, several-strands ones. The one I'm stuck in at the moment has strands in Seville, Mexico City, Istanbul, Nanjing and Goa, all between roughly 1450 and 1700, and the thing eating me alive is that everytime I cut a chapter I've secretly decided who in that room is still medieval and who has gone modern. None of the characters get a vote on it.

A friend runs a residential writing retreat and has handed me a slot: two hours, fourteen people, about half novelists and half history PhD students, one long farmhouse table, a whiteboard, a printer that works, no projector. Most of them have never touched historiography and I refuse to stand up and lecture, partly because its boring and partly because they'd just agree with me politely and learn nothing.

Design a tabletop game they can play in that room that walks them straight into the problem — that 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance', 'the Scientific Revolution' are boxes somebody assembled after the fact, and that assembling them isn't automatically a fraud — before I'm allowed to say any of that out loud. I want them arguing about the boxes, not about me.

Here's the skeleton I already have. Keep what's good, cut what isn't, and tell me where you cut.

- The board is a grid. Columns are decades, 1400 to 1700. Rows are regions: Iberia, the Ottoman lands, Ming-then-Qing China, Mesoamerica and New Spain, Mughal north India, Japan, Songhay and the West African coast, Poland-Lithuania, the Andes. Teams of three or four.
- Event cards get pinned into the grid. I've got some anchors already: Constantinople 1453, the Columbus landfall 1492, Luther's theses 1517, Copernicus's book appearing 1543, Galileo and the telescope 1609, the Principia 1687, the first Zheng He fleet sailing 1405 and the last one coming home 1433, Panipat 1526, Japan closing up in the 1630s. Write me about thirty-five more, spread across all the rows, decade-level dating is fine, and just mark the ones you're not sure about rather than inventing a confident year.
- The nasty rule, which is the whole game: each team gets three walls, and a wall has to be one straight vertical line through every row at once. You may not wall off a single region. Then they have to name the period on each side of each wall, and the names get scored.
- Patron cards. Before play each player draws one and never shows it: a private agenda their wall placement has to satisfy, which they can only work for through table talk. Give me fifteen or so, varied enough that no two teams play the same game, and make them recognisable motives rather than arbitrary handicaps — the sort of thing a person would want out of a periodization if they had something to prove.
- Scoring: no money, beans or wall-pins or discarded cards are fine. But I want a challenge round in there — an opposing team picks one of your walls, picks one row, and tries to break it with a card off the deck; if they break it you lose the points and the name.
- Round two: change exactly one rule, that walls may now be slanted, or partial, or per-row. Tell me what that does to play, what teams discover they've lost, and how you score a periodization nobody else can use as a shared vocabulary. That loss is the point of round two, so make it hurt.
- Last fifteen minutes: a writing task, not a discussion. Everyone writes 150 words from inside their own period, from inside a single life, with every period word banned — no renaissance, no modern, no medieval, no enlightenment, no revolution. Then the room guesses which side of which wall the character is standing on. If they can't tell, the wall was never lived, and I want that landing on them before I open my mouth.

Then the debrief. Map specific moments from play onto specific claims — the vertical-wall rule, the naming, the patron cards, the challenge round, the round-two collapse, the writing task — and say plainly which of those claims is the strong one and which is just a nice party trick. Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, including what I do if there's a real early-modernist at the table steamrolling everyone, and if a team simply stops caring and parks all three walls in one decade to game the scoring. And be honest about which feature of the actual debate the game cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself — I suspect it's that a periodization is supposed to answer to evidence and not to a vote, but tell me if you think there's something worse.

One last thing: keep real historians' names and who-coined-what off the cards and out of the debrief. I can't check any of that and neither can they, and I don't want to be the guy handing invented attributions to a room full of grad students.
