Retired three years ago after 41 years in sedimentary geology — cores, grain-size curves, error bars, that sort of thing. Since then I've been reading philosophy like a man making up for lost time, which means I read fast and understand maybe half of it.

I've just finished von Neumann's little book on the computer and the brain, the one that was supposed to be his Yale lectures and came out after he died. What I expected was a mathematician showing off. What I got was a hardware engineer thinking aloud, and the part that grabbed me is the noise business. He points out that the nerve impulse is all-or-none, so whatever the nervous system is doing is basically digital even though it looks analog at first pass, and then he deals with the obvious objection — neurons are sloppy, far less reliable component by component than the tubes he was working with — by leaning on redundancy and error margins, the way you'd paper over a flaky relay.

Fine. But here's where I've got myself stuck and can't get out again, so I want you to make the arguement properly.

The redundancy trick only works if a system can tell a message that arrived wrong from a message that arrived right, and a computer can do that because the stuff that holds information isn't the stuff that works on it. Memory sits over there; it gets written by the processor and read back by the processor, so there's a version that was intended and a version that showed up, and the two can be compared. In a brain the synapse that carries the memory is itself one of the components doing the computing. A change in synaptic weight can't get sorted into corruption-versus-learning by the machinery, because the machinery is the thing that changed.

So here's my claim, and it's the one I want argued: take his digital conclusion at face value and you don't end up with a noisy system plus corrected memory. You end up with a system where there is no stored content at all in the sense the book assumes — nothing with an address, nothing that sits there as a determinate state while the rest of the system churns. Which means his whole treatment of memory depth, the couple of seconds here and the lifetime over there, and his big estimate of total capacity in bits (something like ten to the twentieth, arrived at by counting up a lifetime of sensory input, if I remeber right) isn't wrong exactly. It's measuring a quantity that has no bearer.

I'd also want to press his remark about the nervous system's code being less logically deep than ours but more arithmetically deep, because that looks to me like a concession that helps my side: comparing depths of memory across two systems already assumes they keep time and keep bits in the same currency.

Give it to me as a numbered chain, premise to conclusion, with a line after each step saying what that step actually depends on, and tell me plainly where it's soft — I'd rather have an honest skeleton than a smooth essay. Then give me the best reply von Neumann could make from inside the book, not a straw man. And no reading list, please; I can't chase references, and there's more mud in my past than bibliography in my future.
