Long-time lurker on LW and the SSC comment section, and I'm stuck in a recurring argument with a friend who does transit planning for a mid-sized city. He's a Deweyan, I've been reading around Carnap's takes on politics and planning, so help me settle whcih of us actually has the better framework.

The Carnap-side position as I've reconstructed it (tell me if I'm mangling it): value statements aren't the kind of thing that can be true or false, they're expressions of a stance, so neither science nor logic can hand you your ends. But that doesn't dump planning into arbitrary will, because there's still a lot of rational work left: working out what your ends actually commit you to, showing that two ends you're simultaneously holding are mutually incompatible, and ranking means by their expected consequences. And Carnap was anything but a quietist here — he was a socialist who thought a rationally planned social and personal life was an urgent project, which is precisely the bit I have trouble squaring with the non-cognitivism about ends.

The Dewey side as I understand it: ends aren't outside inquiry at all. They're ends-in-view, proposals formed inside a problematic situation and tested by acting on them, so keeping the fact/value line Carnap keeps is a mistake that hands live questions over to "well, that's just a preference" prematurely.

The LW-ish way I'd put it: Carnap says the utility function is exogenous and only the search over means is rational; Dewey says the utility function is itself something you should be updating on, and that treating it as frozen is how people end up defending terminal values they'd drop if they bothered to simulate the consequences.

So compare the two on the question of what a planner is allowed to treat as settled, and then commit. Specifically I want: the class of planning question where the two frameworks route the answer differently — one sends it to evidence and argument, the other sends it to a vote or a decision of will — and a call on who's right about that class. And tell me whether the Carnapian move (we can still show your ends are inconsistent, and we can show what they commit you to) already gets you most of what Dewey wants, meaning the disagreement is just about where to draw a line inside shared territory rather than a real fork.

Roughly — (1) the load-bearing disagreement, stated so it can't be dissolved by redefining terms, (2) what the Carnapian view tells my friend to do with such a question, (3) what the Deweyan view tells him, (4) the single thing that decides between them, (5) your verdict. On (5), pick a side and give the reason, plus the strongest comeback the loser has; don't both-sides it, I'd rather be wrong in a definite direction than get a survey.
