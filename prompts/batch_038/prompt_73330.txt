I'm prepping a journal-club talk for my lab and I built a little argument as a warm-up, the kind of thing you throw at people to get them talking, and now two weeks later I can't take it apart. It's about the units of selection. Here's roughly what's on the slide:

(1) Take any population of things that make more things resembling themselves. Then the change in frequency of any trait over one generation can be written as a covariance term plus a transmission term. That's just algebra; it doesn't need any biology, and it holds whether or not the things in question are alive.
(2) Plenty of stuff besides organisms makes more stuff resembling itself: autocatalytic molecules in a beaker, prion conformations templating each other inside a cell, surnames in a parish register, ideas in a population of heads, even cracks in a sidewalk that spread every winter when the water in them freezes.
(3) In each of those cases you can compute a nonzero covariance between having the trait and leaving more of whatever counts as the next generation.
(4) A nonzero covariance between a trait and differential reproduction just is what "there is selection on that trait" means.
(5) So there is selection, always, at every level of every hierarchy — genes, organelles, cells, organisms, colonies, species, and also beakers and sidewalks.
(6) So the answer to "what is the unit of selection?" is "everything, simultaneously," and the gene-versus-organism-versus-group debate is a quarrel about which covarience term you write down first. It's notational.
(7) But that can't be right. People have argued about this for decades as if something real were at stake — for instance whether sterile worker castes in insects need selection acting among colonies rather than among genes inside colonies, which turned into a genuinely public and genuinely bitter fight not that long ago, with a big pile of signatures on one side of it.
(8) Therefore (4) is false.

The trouble is I don't know how to reject (4) without cheating. It looks like it just spells out the definition, and every fix I can think of either smuggles the biology back in by fiat ("only living things count," which is precisely what was supposed to be explained) or is so weak it fails to exclude the sidewalk cracks. I asked about it in our reading group and someone said "you're equivocating, obviously" and then refused to say between what and what, which was maddening.

So: which step is actually the culprit, and can you say exactly what the equivocation is, if there is one? Why did that step look innocent when I wrote it down? And what do I pay for rejecting it — if (4) goes, what replaces it, and does the replacement give a principled line or just a redescription of what biologists happen to care about? I'd much rather keep (1), which I'm confident is arithmetic, than keep (6), but I also want to know what happens to (2) and (3), because I suspect at least one of my examples is doing sneaky work. Run the diagnosis on at least two of them: I'd like to know whether the prions and the sidewalk cracks come out the same way or differently, and why. And if you can, say what the fix implies for the real question — what does a bee colony have that a beaker of autocatalysts lacks? Or does the honest answer turn out to be that it has nothing, and the difference is only in our interests?
