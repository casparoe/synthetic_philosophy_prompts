The question, as it was given to them: "Lewis and Kripke disagree about whether an ordinary object can exist in more than one possible world. Set out the disagreement, explain Kripke's objection to Lewis's alternative and Lewis's response to it, and say which side you think is better off and why."

Here's the student's answer:

---

Lewis and Kripke disagree about whether one and the same ordinary object can be located in more than one possible world. Both of them think of possible worlds as real, full-blooded worlds of the same kind as the actual one; what they disagree about is whether an inhabitant of one of those worlds can also be an inhabitant of another. For Lewis, worlds are spatiotemporally and causally cut off from each other, so nothing is part of two worlds, and there is no such thing as an object being wholly present in two worlds at once. That is the real difficulty with the Kripkean picture on his view, and counterpart theory is meant to dissolve it rather than to accept it. So 'Humphrey might have won the election' is not made true by Humphrey winning somewhere else. It is made true by there being, in some other world, a counterpart of Humphrey — something similar enough to him in the respects that matter to the question — who wins. Lewis is careful that the counterpart relation need not be one-one: an object can have several counterparts in a single world, or none.

Kripke, by contrast, allows objects to be identical across worlds, so the same sentence is true in virtue of Humphrey himself winning in another world. His objection to Lewis — the Humphrey objection — is that Lewis has changed the subject. Humphrey does not care whether some other man, however similar, wins an election in another world; he cares whether he himself might have won. If the truth of the modal claim is fixed by the doings of something that is not Humphrey, then Lewis has given us an account of a different claim. Kripke also thinks the so-called problem of transworld identity is not a problem: we do not have to peer into other worlds and work out which of the people there is Humphrey, because we stipulate which worlds we are talking about in the first place, so there is no identification question left over. Lewis's response, as I read it, is that the counterpart relation just is a relation of similarity, and similarity is reflexive, symmetric and transitive, so the counterpart relation is near enough to identity that Kripke's objection loses its force; besides, the objection assumes what is at issue, since it simply takes it for granted that Humphrey cannot be his own counterpart. Lewis also stresses that which similarities count is a matter of context, so there is no single context-free fact about what Humphrey might have done. That means his modal claims have no truth value except relative to a context, which is why Lewis is best read as anticipating the fictionalism about modality that Rosen went on to defend.

On balance I think Lewis is better off. The counterpart theorist has a principled reason to deny that one thing can be wholly located in two worlds, while the transworld identity theorist has no answer to this: if Humphrey exists in w1 and in w2, and he wins in w1 and loses in w2, then he both wins and loses, which violates Leibniz's Law, so Humphrey-in-w1 and Humphrey-in-w2 cannot after all be the same object. Lewis also gives us something to check modal claims against, instead of leaving them primitive.
