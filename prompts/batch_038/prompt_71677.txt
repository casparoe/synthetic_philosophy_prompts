so context: I'm in a philosophy-of-war seminar and we've been asked to hand in a one-page statement of where we actually stand before the peer-review session next week, and I want to know whether mine hangs together or whether I've just stacked positions that sound good next to each other. My friend in the seminar is a pretty orthodox Walzerian and she's already smirking, which makes me nervous. Here's the list:

1. I reject the moral equality of combatants. Whether a soldier is liable to be killed depends on whether his side's cause is just — the just side's soldiers don't become liable merely by fighting, the unjust side's do.
2. But I don't think any of that belongs in the law of armed conflict. Combatant privilege and POW status should stay symmetrical, because a legally asymmetric regime would just be ignored by whoever's losing and would strip out the incentive to stop fighting at all. Morality asymmetric, law symmetric.
3. Liability doesn't require culpability. A soldier can be fully liable to defensive killing while being morally innocent — no bad intent, no blameworthy choice — and I think that's a feature, not a bug.
4. Conscription cases though: an 18-year-old who got drafted, was fed propaganda his whole life, and whose individual contribution to the war effort is basically a rounding error is not liable. The excuse does real work for me here, I'm not pretending otherwise.
5. I reject contingent pacifism. I don't think the right conclusion is "in practice almost nobody on either side clears the bar, so almost all killing in war is impermissible." If a theory of the ethics of war spits that out, the theory is broken and you should go fix it.
6. Noncombatant immunity is near-absolute in the sense that you may never intend a civilian's death, but I'm fine with the ordinary double-effect business about foreseen-but-unintended civilian deaths when the military advantage is big enough.
7. Supreme emergency exemptions are bogus. No quantity of "we might lose" licenses terror-bombing cities.

Go through it as an audit rather than a lecture: which pairs genuinely pull against each other, ranked by how bad they are, and for each one what has to give and what the cheapest revision costs me. Two things I specifically want pressed on: whether I'm entitled to (4) after (3) — I have a feeling something's wrong with the reason I give there even if the verdict is one I want to keep — and whether (5) is a stance I'm allowed to take given the rest of the list, or whether I'm treating a consequence of my own premises as a refutation of them. Also flag the pairs that only look bad so I don't waste half the one-pager defending them, and say why they dissolve. And be upfront about the limits of your audit: where are you most likely to be wrong, which of your severity rankings would a Walzerian most plausibly overturn, and for each tension you find, what argument or consideration would make you reverse your verdict on it?
