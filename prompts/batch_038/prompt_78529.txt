Last night I argued with a friend until nearly two in the morning — she is finishing a public-policy degree, I audit a political-philosophy reading group on Wednesdays — and we never resolved the thing we actually started out fighting about. The trigger was embarrassingly local: our city is proposing to cut a crosstown bus route in order to fund a network of protected bike lanes, and she said the proposal was obviously egalitarian while I said it was obviously not, and within twenty minutes we were four layers down into the currency-of-justice debate with no way back up. We both know the standard repertoire: Sen's question about what the equalisandum should be; the expensive-tastes objection to taking welfare as the metric; Dworkin's envy test and his hypothetical insurance market for resourcism; Sen's conversion argument, the point that the same bicycle or the same income does different work for differently situated people; Nussbaum's list of central capabilities; and the adaptive-preferences charge against anyone who reads satisfaction off a person's current psychology. The problem is that every case I tried to build in which the three metrics genuinely rank two people differently was demolished by her, usually rightly: I was smuggling in a disability, inventing a cultivated luxury preference, or describing someone whose wants had been shaped by the very deprivation I was trying to measure. So I want the case built to specification instead of fished out of the usual stock examples.

Here is what I need. Construct a scenario about two named individuals in one ordinary city, with one paragraph for the initial situation and a second paragraph for a single policy change — call it the pivot — that follows it. Constraints on the initial situation: no disabilities, illness, or impairments of any kind; no pregnancy; no climatic differences; no discrimination motivated by prejudice; no luxury, exotic, or deliberately cultivated expensive tastes, so nothing in the family of rare eggs or fine champagne; and no adaptive preferences, in the strong sense that the worse-off party's desires must be well informed, reflectively stable, and not a reconciliation to her circumstances. Under those constraints, all three metrics — welfare as the equalisandum, resources in Dworkin's sense, and capabilities in Sen's and Nussbaum's sense — must nevertheless agree that person A is worse off than person B and that A has a claim for compensation, and the agreement must rest on three non-overlapping grounds, which you should state. Then the pivot: one realistic municipal decision of your choosing — a route cancellation, a zoning change, a voucher, a licensing rule, a public facility — that flips exactly one of the three comparative verdicts while leaving the other two precisely where they were. The pivot may alter one variable only; it must not quietly change A's preferences, her health, her beliefs, or her tastes, and it must not smuggle in a conversion problem through the back door of a new impairment. Let me be explicit about what the case is for, since that determines what counts as cheating: my friend's position is that capability is not an independent metric at all, but a redescription that tracks resources and welfare whenever those two are held fixed, and the scenario is meant to test that claim. A case that fails is therefore one in which the flip is stipulated rather than generated — where you simply declare that A's capability set has expanded, or where the pivot works by moving something I told you not to move. The flip has to fall out of each theory's own machinery, and you have to show me the machinery turning.

After the two paragraphs, deliver the demonstration in this order. First, for each of the three views, the verdict before the pivot and after it, with the reasoning spelled out; for the resourcist in particular, actually run the hypothetical insurance market rather than invoking it — say what people behind the veil know about themselves, what they would insure against, at what premium, and whether A's situation is covered. Second, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, then delete it and report what each of the three verdicts becomes, so I can see whether the case survives without it. Third, give the strongest available attack from whichever view ends up as the odd one out — its own best argument that the case is rigged or misdescribed — and tell me honestly whether you think the attack succeeds. Fourth, close with a short judgment on my friend's claim: if the construction works, does it show that capability is doing independent normative work, or only that the three metrics can be made to diverge by an artifact of how the case is set up? Please write in American English, keep the whole thing tight enough that I could hand it to the reading group as a discussion sheet, and do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations — I have the literature, and what I am after is the construction and the proof that it meets the specification.
