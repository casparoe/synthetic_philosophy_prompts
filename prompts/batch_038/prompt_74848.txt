I work in the economics of organizations — Coase, transaction-cost economics, the Grossman–Hart–Moore property-rights approach, mechanism design, and the fiscal-federalism literature on the assignment problem. I have essentially no training in political philosophy or constitutional law; I know subsidiarity only as the word federalists use, and I can read a treaty article well enough to paraphrase it but not well enough to adjudicate interpretive disputes about it.

At a seminar last month a colleague defended the following analogy: a federation is a holding company whose constituent units are wholly owned operating subsidiaries; subsidiarity is simply the principle that determines where residual control rights over a given activity should sit; and the entire constitutional apparatus built up around subsidiarity — the treaty language, the encyclical that supplied the doctrine, the protocol giving national parliaments a monitoring role — is the public-sector equivalent of a decentralization policy memo. He then drew two conclusions from it.

First, subsidiarity is purely an efficiency principle: allocate decision rights to the level that minimizes the sum of coordination costs, agency costs, and preference-mismatch costs, exactly as Oates's decentralization theorem trades scale economies and spillovers against heterogeneity of tastes. On this reading nothing normative survives. The moral vocabulary — Pius XI's Quadragesimo anno of 1931, which treated assigning to a higher association what lesser and subordinate bodies can accomplish as an injustice and a disturbance of right order rather than as a miscalculation — is decoration, and the EU's Article 5(3) test (act only if and insofar as the objectives cannot be sufficiently achieved by the member states and can, by reason of the scale or effects of the action, be better achieved at Union level) is a cost–benefit test wearing a robe.

Second, subsidiarity therefore supplies no brake on centralization. If the center can credibly satisfy the scale-and-effects test, the principle itself requires the transfer upward; it is self-consuming, and federalist resistance to that transfer is interest-group politics dressed as constitutional argument.

I want the analogy audited, not the conclusions evaluated in the abstract. Concretely:

Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the parent, the subsidiaries, the residual control rights, the ownership of assets that grounds those rights, the objective function, the board, and exit. Where my colleague's mapping is silent or ambiguous, flag the silence rather than filling it in for him.

Then list the disanalogies and rank them by whether they bear on the two conclusions he wants to draw. The ranking is what I care about: some disanalogies are real but harmless to his argument (a firm has residual claimants, a federation has none), and I suspect others are load-bearing. Candidates I would want you to assess rather than accept from me: in the American and Swiss cases the constituent units pre-exist the union and cannot be abolished, renamed, or sold by the center; the anti-commandeering line of Supreme Court decisions, New York v. United States and Printz v. United States, forbids the center from directing the personnel of the units in a way no parent company would tolerate for a moment; German federalism inverts the corporate structure by having the Länder execute centrally legislated law, which is where Scharpf located his joint-decision pathology; and Protocol No 2 of the EU treaties gives each national parliament two votes and, if reasoned objections reach one third of those votes within eight weeks, requires the draft act to be reviewed — a monitoring right held by the units against the center that, so far as I can see, has no counterpart in a corporate group, where the analogous right sits with the board and runs to shareholders rather than to subsidiaries.

Tell me which features of the source the analogy quietly imports into the target and then reports as discovered. I have three in mind and want to know whether you agree: a single cardinal objective commensurable across units; residual rights that are transferable at the discretion of whoever owns them; and exit by divestiture as the discipline that keeps the assignment honest over time.

Then give me the verdict. Does the first conclusion survive the disanalogies, or was it carried entirely by the analogy's assumption that there is one measurable objective being maximized? Does the second survive — in particular, is the self-consuming reading of Article 5(3) a result, or an artifact of treating a rule that limits competence as though it were a rule that allocates tasks within an existing competence? Bear in mind that Swiss constitutional practice pairs the subsidiarity requirement with the provision that the cantons are sovereign except insofar as their sovereignty is limited by the federal constitution and that they exercise all rights not vested in the Confederation, a pairing for which I cannot find a firm-theoretic reading.

Be strict about translation hygiene. Do not smuggle firm-theoretic primitives in under the label of translation, and do not flatter the analogy by selecting whichever federal system happens to fit it best. Where a claim of my colleague's has no expression at all in the holding-company vocabulary, say so plainly and tell me whether the silence is a defect of the vocabulary — a missing primitive, something like original and non-transferable authority as distinct from delegated authority — or evidence that the claim was less clear than it sounded when he made it.

Finally, if the analogy fails where it matters, propose a better one that carries the same explanatory work without the smuggling. And if the honest answer is that the phenomenon resists analogical treatment from my side of the fence, say that, name the concept in the economics of organizations that comes closest, and state what it leaves out.
