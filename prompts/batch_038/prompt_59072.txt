I run service delivery for a mid-sized firm — about 260 people, mostly remote — that does clinical coding, transcription and query handling for a dozen hospital trusts up here. Roughly twice a quarter somebody puts an automation proposal in front of me: sometimes it's a vendor's sales engineer, sometimes it's our own internal automation lead, who is an enthusiast and doesn't hide it. Each proposal touches between eight and forty people. I get about a fortnight to make a recommendation, and the managing director plus our staff forum have to sign off on anything that changes someone's job. At the moment we have no framework whatsoever — we just argue about headcount and error rates and whoever is loudest wins, which is usually the person with the demo.

What I want is a written decision procedure my team leads can actually run against a proposal: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it stops being a procedure and hands the decision up to a human being because the remaining question is about values rather than facts. I want it grounded in whatever the philosophy of work genuinely has to offer here, but translated — no slogans about dignity of labour, and please no reading list or bibliography, we won't have time to chase any of it up and neither will you need to convince me you've read the right things. The distinctions I specifically care about, because I keep running into them in practice: the economists' point that automation eats tasks rather than whole jobs; the difference between a role disappearing and a role being hollowed out, where the title survives and the person is still on the payroll but everything that made the work worth doing has been siphoned off; the way the entry-level tasks that used to be how people learned the craft are exactly the ones that get automated first, so the ladder up quietly vanishes; recognition, i.e. whether the person still gets credit for the output once the system is producing it; and something a friend of mine who does philosophy called 'counterfactual contribution' — whether the work would be noticeably worse if this particular person wasn't doing it — which seems to me to be a different thing from both being busy and merely having a job. Tell me which of those the procedure can actually test with information we'd have in a fortnight, and which it can only flag for a human to worry about.

Then run the procedure on three cases we're genuinely facing. One: automated first-pass coding of routine outpatient letters, with a coder checking a ten per cent sample and nothing else. Two: a system that drafts the query letters our coders send to clinicians, leaving the coder to read, amend and send. Three: scrapping the junior transcription QA role outright because measured error rates fell after the new software went in, with the two post-holders redeployed to 'exception handling' across the whole floor. I expect at least one of these comes back with an answer I won't like — tell me which one and don't soften it, that's the whole point of having a procedure instead of a meeting. And finish with the limits: what the procedure can't settle, where it will misfire systematically (I suspect it's going to be over-optimistic about anything where the benefits arrive fast and the harms arrive slowly, but you tell me), and which of the steps are really judgement calls dressed up as procedure so I don't fool myself about how much of this is mechanical. Please write the whole thing in British English — spellings, but also the idiom, since it's going to be circulated to people in Newcastle and Leeds and I don't want it reading like it was written in California.
