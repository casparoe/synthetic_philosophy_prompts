Our department's decision-theory reading group has been working through superrationality, and to keep the session from collapsing into a retelling of Hofstadter's original column I constructed a variant. Take the Platonia dilemma as already known to everyone in the room; I am not after a summary of it.

The setup, which I have been calling the Chorus Dilemma. Twenty-five people are each asked, in isolation and with no possibility of communication before or after, whether to press a button. Payoffs: if exactly one person presses, that person receives one million dollars; in every other case — nobody presses, or two or more press — each of the twenty-five receives fifty dollars. All of this is common knowledge, as is the fact that all twenty-five are competent reasoners, have been given the same description of the situation, and know that the others are reasoning about it in the same way. Pressing weakly dominates refraining for each individual, so the unique symmetric equilibrium is for all twenty-five to press and collect fifty dollars each. Note also that the game has twenty-five asymmetric pure equilibria, in each of which exactly one specified person presses and takes the million; what rules those out as superrational outcomes is precisely the symmetry requirement, absent some salient way to single one player out.

The proposition I want a number on is this:

P: Superrationality — the view that reasoners alike in information and in their reasoning will arrive at the same choice, and who should therefore deliberate as though their choice were replicated in all the others — recommends that a participant press with probability one twenty-fifth, by means of an independently randomized device, rather than pressing or refraining with certainty.

For reference, one twenty-fifth is the pressing probability that maximizes the chance of exactly one press, about 0.375 under independent randomization, which would give an expected payoff near three hundred seventy-five thousand dollars against the fifty dollars that every correlated pure outcome delivers. Treat P as made true or false by whether the most coherent elaboration of that idea licenses the mixed strategy here, not by what any particular author happened to say.

Give me your credence in P, and give it to me decomposed. At a minimum I want numbers, with a line of justification each, for the following:

(a) whether the replication premise can survive randomization at all — whether reasoners whose choices issue from independent coin flips still count as making the same choice, given that replication is what generated the superrational conclusion in the first place;

(b) whether, if the random devices are treated as correlated along with the reasoning, so that everyone flips the same way, the mixed strategy retains any advantage over the pure ones, and what follows for P from the answer;

(c) whether the fifty-dollar floor changes the verdict relative to a version in which failed coordination yields nothing to anybody;

(d) whether there is a determinate fact about what an under-specified normative idea like this recommends in a case it was never built to handle — and if there is not, whether P should be scored as false, as indeterminate, or by some third route, and how that affects the number you report.

Add any further terms you think belong. Show how the terms combine into your bottom line, and say which one the result is most sensitive to: which single term, pushed from your estimate to its extreme, moves the final credence furthest.

Two of us are far apart on this. I sit at roughly 0.75; a colleague sits at roughly 0.2. Once the decomposition is on the table, tell me which term or terms most plausibly account for that gap, and what we would have to settle first before our exchange could amount to anything more than trading intuitions.

Finally, and distinct from the sensitivity question: what would change your mind? Name the argument or consideration that would push you across the halfway mark in either direction, and say plainly where you think your own reasoning about this case is most likely to break down. No bibliography, no list of sources, no suggested further cases to consult — I want the derivation itself.
