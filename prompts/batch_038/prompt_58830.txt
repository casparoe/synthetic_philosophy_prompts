So I cover science and ideas for a mid-size magazine, and for the last two years I've basically been stalking a philosopher who runs a weekly Q&A where readers mail in cases and she rules on whether some observation counts as evidence for some general claim. I'm writing a profile-ish piece and I want to get her method right before I put words in her mouth, and there's one submission sitting in her inbox that I genuinely can't predict.

Her rulings so far, roughly in order:

- Birdwatcher out specifically looking for ravens logs a black raven. Confirms the claim that every raven is black. Her note: obviously, that's the whole exercise.
- Reader pushes back: what about the white sneaker on my porch? Her ruling: yes, it confirms, by a hair. And she's explicit that the hair-thinness comes from how many sneakers there are compared to ravens, not from any rule about what you're allowed to go looking at.
- Reader: I went outside with the stated aim of finding something that isn't a raven and came back with a red fire hydrant. Her ruling: still confirms, same hair, and she spends a paragraph saying your intentions don't get a vote. An observation either shifts things or it doesn't.
- Museum volunteer sorting a crate of bird skins turns up a white heron. Confirms, same hair.
- Reader: a black crow. Her ruling: neutral. Not nothing-nothing, but don't log it in either column.
- Island ecology team, day one of a survey, catalogues 400 non-black things, none of them ravens. Day two, they catalogue 400 more, in the same format, same observers. Her ruling: the day-two batch confirms *less* than day one did, even though the reports are of exactly the same logical form. Her reasoning, as far as I can reconstruct it: the first batch told you something about how the island's fauna is distributed, and by the second batch you already knew the place was full of non-black stuff.
- Different hypothesis entirely: every beetle in a certain genus has six legs. Someone points at a centipede. Confirms, hair-thin. She says the raven business generalizes and people who think it doesn't are usually smuggling in a preference for one way of phrasing the hypothesis over an equivalent one.

Now the new submission, and this is the one I can't call. Same island team. Over six years they've established that the island's non-black biomass is enormous and mostly uncounted — lichen, pale insects, bleached driftwood, quartz, salt-scoured everything. They also work with an auxiliary assumption about island birds that she has mentioned approvingly in print twice: a species here either has no pale individuals at all, or pale individuals turn up at roughly a fixed rate relative to how much pale stuff there is generally on the island, so a paler island is an island where any given species is likelier to have a pale member. The submission: a grad student sieving leaf litter pulls out something like ten thousand pale springtails and beetle larvae, none of which are ravens, obviously. Does that batch confirm the claim that every raven on the island is black?

My problem is that I can see two completely different answers both being consistent with everything she's ever ruled, and I don't want to write a piece that attributes her a view her own record contradicts. So:

1. Predict her ruling, and commit to one. Yes-hair-thin, no-neutral, or actually-negative — and give me the reasoning she'd most likely write.
2. Name the rule she's running. And then name the other rules that fit all seven of the verdicts above just as well, and say what each of them predicts about the leaf-litter batch. I care more about this list than about the label you put on her.
3. Point out which of her past rulings already knock out which candidate accounts, so I don't credit her with something she's explicitly rejected — the intentions one and the day-two one feel like they're doing a lot of work here and I can't tell exactly what.
4. There's something I keep tripping over: the feature that makes each pale springtail individually worthless as evidence seems to be the same feature that might make the batch as a whole do damage. Is that a real tension, or am I confusing two different quantities? Explain it in a way I could put in a magazine without a formula.
5. Give me one follow-up case I could mail her next week that would force the two readings apart — something where they can't both be right.

Casual register is fine, I'm not writing a term paper, but don't just hand me a name for the paradox and call it a day. I've read the wikipedia page. What I need is the carve-up of her actual decision procedure.
