I am the research-integrity officer at a mid-sized research university. My office investigates allegations of fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism in published work. Roughly once a month a respondent's written answer does not contest the facts we have assembled but contests the standing of the methods by which we assembled them: the image-analysis suite used to conclude that a panel was manipulated has never been validated on images from the instrument that produced it; the finding that records are missing depends on a laboratory logbook that the allegation itself says was written up after the fact; the statistical test showing an excluded outlier was material was selected after the outlier was seen; and, at the outer limit, the claim that an office whose remit is to find misconduct cannot be structurally capable of finding none. Two of my findings in the past three years were set aside on internal appeal on the ground of circularity — the panel held that I had used the contested method to establish the reliability of the contested method. I do not think that criticism was empty, but I also cannot suspend an inquiry every time a respondent names a presupposition, and neither of my two investigators has any training in epistemology.

What I want is a decision procedure my office can run, and I want it built from whatever is usable in the philosophical discussion of Moore's proof of an external world — the argument that proceeds from a pair of demonstrated hands to the existence of the external world, and the subsequent debate over whether an argument can confer warrant on a conclusion when the warrant for its premises already depends on that conclusion being true. The parts I suspect are portable are: the distinction between an argument's being epistemically in good standing and its being useless for persuading someone who doubts the premises; the idea that some warrant is not evidential at all but is something an inquirer is entitled to proceed on absent specific grounds for doubt; and the opposing insistence that a doubt must have a determinate object before it obliges anyone to answer it. I am not after a survey of that debate, and I do not want a bibliography or a reading list — if this ends up in a policy document I will supply the references myself. I want to know what survives translation into a form two non-specialists can apply under deadline.

Please deliver the following.

First, a classification test that can be run on a challenge letter in under two minutes, sorting it into kinds — a challenge that the method fails by its own internal standards; a challenge that the warrant for the method depends on the very conclusion in dispute; a challenge to something no evidence in the proceeding could reach; a challenge that is really an objection to persuasiveness rather than to correctness; and a challenge filed to consume time. Give me the discriminating question for each kind and the tell-tale sentences that signal it.

Second, an ordered procedure that follows from that classification: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the default action at each exit — proceed on the record, commission independent validation from a party with no stake in the outcome, suspend, or refer upward. Our constraints are hard: a challenge must be classified within two working days of receipt, a finding must be producible within thirty working days, and any suspension longer than twenty working days requires the provost's signature. We also maintain a standing register of methods whose presuppositions have already been litigated, so that a challenge to a registered method is answered by citing the prior ruling; the procedure must say when a register entry should be reopened and when the register itself becomes a way of freezing a bad ruling in place.

Third, mark every point at which the procedure must stop and hand the matter to a person because what remains is a question of institutional values rather than fact. Say what those questions are and who should answer them. My adjudication panel is two scientists and one lay member, none of whom will read philosophy.

Fourth, run the procedure on three cases: the unvalidated image-analysis suite; the logbook that is both the evidence for missing records and the object of the allegation; and the challenge that the office's funding by the institution disqualifies any procedure it runs. For at least one of them, tell me plainly that the procedure yields an outcome I will dislike — obliging me to treat a challenge I consider frivolous as legitimate, or permitting me to dismiss a serious one on grounds that will read as circular in the written findings. Say which case that is and why.

Fifth, state the failure modes honestly: which of the classification steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, how the test can be gamed by a well-advised respondent, and what the procedure cannot decide at all. One further constraint on the drafting: every exit must be justifiable in a single paragraph that a non-specialist panel, and on appeal a lawyer with no patience for philosophy, can follow — which means the written justification may not contain the phrase "begs the question" or any synonym of it, since on appeal those read as admissions. Give me the plain-language substitutes you would have my investigators use instead.
