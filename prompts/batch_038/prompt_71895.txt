I hold a chair in practical philosophy and sit as one of three external members of the grants committee of the Trethewey Foundation, a British charitable foundation with an endowment of some £500 million. The committee makes one large award each round; this round's award is £4.5 million over five years, and we meet on 12 March. The committee is evenly divided, and the chair has asked each member to circulate a short written opinion in advance. I should like you to draft mine. Please write it in British English, in a register suited to intelligent trustees who are not philosophers, and keep it to between 1,000 and 1,300 words under clear headings. No bibliography, no named authorities: the memo must stand entirely on its own reasoning.

The deed of trust (1978) directs the trustees to secure "the greatest good for the greatest number of persons, whether those persons live now or in the remotest future", and adds that the trustees "shall not discount the interests of any person merely on account of the distance in time at which that person lives". One trustee maintains that the first clause settles this round's decision outright; another maintains that the second clause forbids pure time preference and nothing else. A third has argued that the discounting rule proposed below would itself breach the deed, since it discounts the interests of future people on account of the remoteness of our estimates about them. I should like the memo to disentangle these readings rather than assume one.

The two proposals.

*Long Horizon* (request: £4.5m). A consortium of engineers, archivists and political scientists proposing a hardened, distributed repository of technical and agricultural knowledge in three countries, together with a standing cadre of some 200 people trained and retained to restore essential industrial processes. The consortium's evaluation, which our analyst has checked for arithmetic but not for assumptions, puts the programme's effect at a reduction of one part in 50,000 in the probability of permanently unrecoverable civilisational collapse within the next two centuries. On the foundation's standard working assumption that such a collapse forfeits a future containing of the order of 10^15 human lives, that is roughly twenty billion lives in expectation. The analyst's sensitivity work has one striking feature: she can find no plausible range of assumptions under which the competing proposal comes out ahead — even cutting the probability estimate by a factor of 200,000 leaves the expected benefit some forty times larger. What she does say is that the one-in-50,000 figure is not the output of any calibrated process. It is, in her phrase, "a number the consortium's economist would be willing to defend in print", and it rests on a chain of five conditional estimates, each with wide and unquantified error bars.

*Lead Remediation* (request: £3.9m). Removal of lead service pipes and lead-bearing paint in four post-industrial towns, with blood-lead screening for some 40,000 children. The evidence base is a randomised evaluation of a comparable programme elsewhere plus an established dose–response relation; the expected effect is about 2,600 premature deaths averted and 1.4 million years of healthy life lost averted over thirty years, and the lower bound of its uncertainty band exceeds the measured benefit of most programmes we fund.

Procedural facts. Long Horizon's site leases and partner agreements lapse on 31 October, and the consortium tells us it will dissolve if we defer, so deferral amounts to rejection; it also says it cannot begin below £2.8m. Lead Remediation is scalable: at £1.7m it would cover two towns plus the screening, for roughly 45 per cent of the benefit; deferral to our spring round in eighteen months would cost, on the analyst's estimate, about 400 further years of healthy life lost and would put one of its two co-funders at risk of withdrawing. The committee may make a single award, divide the sum into two, or defer.

Two further matters I want the memo to address.

First, a trustee proposes a standing rule for this and future rounds: no claim resting on a probability smaller than one in 10,000 is to be given any weight. Assess it. Note what it does here (it eliminates Long Horizon, whose figure sits below the threshold), what it costs us elsewhere (last round we approved £250,000 for seismic works on a property in a low-risk region on a one-in-25,000 chance of severe damage over fifty years), and what it does to applicants' incentives, since a proposal can be rescued by moving a claimed probability from one in 10,001 to one in 9,999. If you reject it, substitute a rule for handling claims whose magnitude is enormous and whose probability is unverifiable — a rule a non-specialist trustee could actually apply to next year's applications.

Second, two objections are in the air and should be taken seriously. One member argues that if we may credit Long Horizon's speculative chain of consequences, we may equally credit Lead Remediation's — healthier children, better schooling, more stable towns, sounder institutions — which is no shorter and no less conjectural; on this view the long-run considerations cancel and we should decide on what we know. Another argues that a rule which always favours the largest claimed number, however wild, would oblige us to hand the endowment to any confident claimant who produced a big enough figure.

The memo must give a definite bottom line: which course you recommend (single award, a stated division with amounts, or deferral), with the sums and the dates attached. It must state the strongest consideration in favour of the course you reject in its strongest form and in no fewer than 150 words, and explain why it nonetheless fails to carry the decision. It must flag every point at which your recommendation depends on a contested premise — about how the deed is to be read, about whether an uncalibrated estimate can function as a probability at all, about whether the value of a future turns on how many people it contains — and say what the committee should do on the day if a majority rejects that premise. Do not simply split the difference to be agreeable: if you recommend a division, justify the particular amounts.
