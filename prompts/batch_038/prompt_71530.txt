Our university's "future of humanity" society ran a panel last week on whether the student investment fund should put money into a Mars-settlement startup, and one of the panelists kept closing every exchange with some version of "we have to make life multiplanetary, it's a moral imperative." The annoying part is that he used it for two different jobs inside the same hour. When someone said a self-sustaining colony is centuries out, so it does nothing about the risks that could actually kill us in our lifetimes, he said the imperative is about the long run and near-term risk isn't what the slogan is about. When someone else said okay, but then why not fund biosecurity, which cuts extinction risk sooner and way cheaper, he said no, the whole point is an independent backup, redundency, not just slightly lower risk on the same rock.

So I want to work out what that sentence can even be claiming. Can you lay out the distinct theses hiding in "humanity ought to become multiplanetary" as a menu? For each one: what sort of claim it is (about risk reduction? about total value? about a duty? about human nature? a mere permission dressed up as an obligation?), what would have to be true for it to hold, and whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or just false. Specifically I want to know:

- which readings, if any, actually get you from "it would be good" to "you should fund this now, ahead of the other things on the table";
- whether any reading survives the correlation objection — that for a long time a Mars colony shares basically all its failure modes with Earth (the same launch infrastructure and supply chain, a pathogen that arrives on the same ships, an impactor big enough for both, plus whoever runs it making the same bad calls in both places), so it isn't the independent backup the slogan implies, it's a very expensive correlated one;
- whether one of these readings is a population-ethics claim in disguise — spreading out creates a lot more lives rather than saving the ones we'd otherwise have — and if so, whether the panelist would want to say that out loud, given what it commits him to about adding happy people versus preventing bad outcomes;
- and whether "imperative" in any of the readings is a duty owed to someone (future people? the species? the fact of intelligent life?) or just "it'd be a real shame not to."

And if some of these are positions people genuinely hold while others are just what the slogan gets stretched to cover mid-argument, say which is which. Then tell me what the strongest version of his case would have looked like — the one reading I should have been arguing against instead of the one he was saying.
