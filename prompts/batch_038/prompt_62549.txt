Been going down a rabbit hole on Sidgwick and the Society for Psychical Research for a few weeks — the memoir, some of the old SPR journal material — and I've run into a configuration I don't have a name for. I suspect it isn't really about psychical research at all, that's just where I noticed it.

Here's the thing. His ethics needs survival of death to be true. The Methods of Ethics ends with the clash between egoism and utilitarianism left unresolved unless the universe is morally ordered in a way that lines duty and happiness back up, and in 1880 he wrote that "I do not know whether I believe or merely hope that there is a moral order in this universe that we know … I cannot accept life on any other terms, or construct a rational system of my own conduct except on the basis of this faith." So the belief is load-bearing for his entire practical outlook, not a hobby. And yet the project he sets up to test it is the most merciless, self-defeating evidence hunt imaginable: co-founding the SPR, twenty years of sitting in dark rooms gripping the hands and feet of mediums, personally exposing Palladino's tricks at the Cambridge sittings in 1895, then writing to the British Medical Journal to point out that the society had never once published a favorable report on a professional medium. He holds the one proposition he most needs to a stricter standard than anything else in his life. And when the evidence keeps coming up empty, nothing moves — the belief stays parked in that "I don't know whether I believe or merely hope" state and the investigation resumes. The failures get logged openly, they're never explained away, and they never add up to a refutation for anyone, least of all him.

Two features I think any name for this has to capture, because they're what seem to separate it from the obvious candidates. First, the belief is open to confirmation but closed to disconfirmation, and not because he's rationalizing — because it was never held on evidence in the first place. It's held as something his conduct presupposes, and he can tell you exactly why, and the reason isn't "the data says so." Second, he's completely aware of this and scrupulous about it, so from the outside the whole thing looks like the opposite of wishful thinking. He isn't fooling himself. He's running a genuine audit on a belief the audit cannot dissolve.

I know this lives somewhere near unfalsifiability, but that word makes it sound like a failure, and here the audits are real and the negative results are published. Is there an established concept — in philosophy of science, philosophy of religion, or the history of psychical research — for the specific shape of this, or is it nameless? If it's nameless, coin one, define it, and tell me what falls outside it; I'd want to know how it differs from a plain double standard, from a hypothesis that manufactures its own confirmation, and from someone just being unwilling to give up a cherished view.
