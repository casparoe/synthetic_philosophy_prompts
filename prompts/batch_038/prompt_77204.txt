Act like a sci-fi writer who moonlights as a philosopher of science — someone comfortable building an arguement out of plot and setting instead of exposition. Before drafting, spend three or four lines brainstorming your angle and where the ending lands, then write the story, around 900 words.

Seed: In a port city, the harbor board licenses "advisors" who are graded on one thing only — how well their forecasts come out. The top man, Oren, builds his forecasts on a picture of the city that everyone agrees is nonsense: the harbor has exactly two wharves, no merchant ever surprises another, grain never moves unless it's already been paid for, and the cranes on the quay reach for cargo as if they knew precisely where the light falls best. He has never missed on any question the board has actually put to him, so the board wants to make him a Fellow — on the reasoning that the wilder the nonsense and the better the forecasts, the more the man must genuinely be onto something, since accuracy that absurd can't be luck.

A junior clerk objects, and the objection should be the engine of the story: by the board's own stated rule, what a man believes is beside the point, only his predictions count — so how can the outrageousness of his beliefs suddenly get promoted into evidence on their behalf? He can't be credited both for being unfalsifiable and for being right.

(For you, not for the story: this is basically the F-twist complaint against Friedman's as-if instrumentalism, plus the worry that useful fictions only carry weight inside the range of questions they've been graded on. Don't name-drop him or anyone, no citations, no footnotes — keep it fully self-contained.)

Dramatize the clerk's point instead of stating it: put it in a hearing, in a column of a ledger, in the worst possible moment. And end with the board asking Oren to forecast something genuinely new — outside every question he's ever been scored on — so the reader feels the fictions come loose from the world. Don't let anyone resolve it with a speech, and leave a typo or two in the board's minutes if it suits the texture.
