I am applying for a twelve-month research fellowship in the history of ideas, and I need a proposal of about 1,500 words on a question that has been nagging me about Henry Sidgwick. Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a fundable project.

In the first book of The Methods of Ethics, Sidgwick sets out conditions that a proposition must satisfy before it may be accepted as self-evident: the terms must be clear and definite, the self-evidence must be confirmed by careful reflection rather than assumed, the propositions accepted must be mutually consistent, and there must be general consensus among competent judges, with any disagreement shown to be explicable by identifiable non-evidential causes. Roughly twenty years later, as first president of the Society for Psychical Research, he oversaw and reported on the Census of Hallucinations, a mass survey of some seventeen thousand people about apparitional and similar experiences, whose central inferential move was to compare the reported coincidences between an experience and a distant death against the number expected by chance alone, and to treat the residual agreement of independent witnesses as evidentially significant. My suspicion is that the Census is not a lapse from Sidgwick's epistemology but an unacknowledged operationalisation of it: the consensus condition becomes survey statistics, and the requirement that disagreement be explained becomes the search for a chance baseline and for identifiable sources of misdescription. The puzzle is that in ethics Sidgwick is acutely alive to the ways in which widespread agreement can be manufactured by association, custom, and authority rather than by evidence, and he allows that possibility to deflate the authority of common sense; in the Census he seems to treat the same structure of agreement as positive evidence for a fact about the world.

The question I want the proposal to ask is therefore whether Sidgwick owes, and can supply, a principled account of when aggregate agreement among reporters has evidential weight and when it is merely a symptom of shared causal history — and whether the account he needs in ethics is compatible with the one he deploys in psychical research. The question must be framed so that it has a determinate answer, not so that both readings come out victorious.

Please give me: a precise statement of that question; an explanation of why the two standard readings leave it open, namely the compartmentalisation reading on which the philosopher and the psychical researcher never meet, and the continuity reading on which everything is driven by his hope for empirical evidence of survival — treat both as serious positions rather than as foils; the method, in stages, with a month-by-month plan, saying what I would actually be doing with the texts at each stage; a defence of that method against the obvious alternatives, which I take to be a contextualist intellectual biography, an internalist ethics scholarship that ignores the SPR material altogether, and a modern statistical re-analysis of the Census data; what a successful result would look like, including any by-product of general interest for the epistemology of testimony and expert consensus; and what would count as failure, in particular the objection that Sidgwick's conditions were designed only for first principles and never for matters of fact, which might make the whole comparison a category mistake.

Two constraints. Do not supply a bibliography, a reference list, or page citations — I will add those myself, and a committee will read invented ones as a fatal flaw. And keep the project neutral on whether telepathy or survival is real: the question is about the structure of Sidgwick's reasoning, and a proposal that reads as either a debunking or an advocacy piece will not be funded. Close with an honest assessment of scope, saying what about this could balloon beyond twelve months.
