I need help writing a speech and I'm kind of panicking. On the 14th my city is dedicating a small plaza and naming it after Dolores Reyes-Arriaga — everybody called her Do — who ran a tenant organizing shop and then a legal aid clinic on the near east side for about forty years. She died in February. The city asked me to give the main address, which is nuts, because I'm 29, I was her intern for one summer when I was 19, I'm not from that neighborhood, and I've never spoken in public to anything bigger than a seminar. The audience will be her kids and grandkids, a couple hundred former tenants (mostly older, some of whom will need it interpreted for them), two city councilmembers, a county judge, and two professors from the state university who actually know political theory and wrote a piece about her organizing years ago. A local paper is covering it. Nine or ten minutes, outdoors, off paper, with traffic noise — so it has to be speakable: short lines, nothing that depends on a subtle turn of phrase landing.

Here's the problem, and the reason I'm asking you rather than just writing something heartfelt. I've been slogging through Berlin's two concepts of liberty and some Pettit for my quals, and the more I read the clearer it's become that Do would have hated two of the three things people are going to say about her on the 14th. She hated the leave-us-alone framing, because for her tenants the total absence of interference was exactly the situation they were in when a landlord could put them on the street at the end of a lease and did not need a reason. And she hated the freedom-to-build-a-better-life-for-yourself framing even more, because in her experience it was always the opening move in somebody explaining that the block was bad because the people on it were bad. The city's own press release about the plaza used that second framing almost word for word. What she actually fought for was the third thing — not being at anybody's mercy — and she had her own field test for it that she applied to everything: when someone offers you a protection, ask whether the person giving it can take it back without giving a reason. She used to distinguish freedom you own from freedom you're loaned, and she counted nearly all of the second kind as no freedom at all, however pleasant the lender happened to be. One story you can use, since I dont have many: she kept a card file of every address where she'd lost — evictions that went through, buildings that got converted — and when people asked why she kept it, she said she needed to know how much of the map was still at somebody's say-so.

So the speech has to be built entirely out of that idea, and the hard part is that I can't say the idea out loud. No "non-domination," no "republican liberty," no "negative" or "positive" freedom, no naming Berlin or Pettit or any book or essay, no quotations from anything printed. The two professors should recognize the spine and enjoy it silently; everybody else should just hear a warm, concrete, occasionally funny speech about a specific woman. The idea has to do structural work — it should decide what I'm allowed to praise, what I can't praise, what counts as a victory in her life story and what counts as a defeat. There are three knots I don't know how to get through:

1. Ray Vitullo owned most of her tenants' buildings from roughly 1979 to 2006. He never raised a rent above the cap, fixed heat same-day, once paid for a family's flight to a funeral out of his own pocket. He's 88 and will be sitting in the plaza, and Do's daughter specifically asked that he be thanked from the podium. By Do's own test everything he gave those families was loaned and could have been taken back on a Tuesday for no reason, and she told him so to his face once — it made them enemies for eleven years and then, bizarrely, friends. I need to thank him without retracting her point and without making an 88-year-old man sit there being told he was the villain.

2. The plaza sits on land the city took by eminent domain in 2009 from a hardware store that had been on that corner for forty years, after the block was declared blighted. Do testified in favor of the taking because the plan included sixty permanently affordable units. The hardware store owner's son is coming, and he's already told the paper that the dedication is a monument to a woman who signed off on his father's ruin. I have maybe ninety seconds of the speech to spend on this. I can't dodge it, and I also can't apologize for her in a way that turns the other eight minutes into a lie. This is the one that has me stuck.

3. Both councilmembers have asked, through the city's events office, that I thank the council for the new Housing Access Pilot: four hundred slots, a lottery, and a caseworker who decides case by case who qualifies as extreme hardship. Do would have called this her exact enemy wearing a smile — a benefit you can lose without a reason, delivered by an office that turns every recipient into a supplicant. I've been told fairly clearly that a thank-you is expected. Find me a way to acknowledge the pilot that's honest, that doesn't require me to endorse discretion itself, and that a councilmember could plausibly hear as praise — or tell me I have to eat the awkwardness, and write it so I eat it gracefully.

Some constraints on the draft: about 1,200 words; exactly one light joke, not at anybody's expense; don't make her a saint — she was abrupt, she held grudges, she lost more than she won, and the room knows it; don't put any new words in her mouth beyond the ones I gave you above; don't have me claim a closeness I didn't have (I can say what I saw from a desk in the back of her office in one summer, and that's the honest register); and it has to survive being read aloud by a nervous person, so no long subordinate clauses. Before the speech, give me two or three sentences on where you think this no-jargon constraint is costing me and what you'd say to that room if you were allowed to use the vocabulary. Then the speech. Then, as the very last line of your whole reply, a line beginning "Spine:" followed by one sentence naming the claim the speech is built on.
