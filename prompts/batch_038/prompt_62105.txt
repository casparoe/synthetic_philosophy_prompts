I am trying to settle what I actually believe about when an utterance containing a slur derogates — that is, when it performs the characteristic bad thing such words do, as opposed to merely containing one. To keep the cases clean, let S be a slur for a group G, carrying the ordinary public history of use to demean, and let R be the form that members of G have reclaimed and use among themselves. I will not name a real word or a real group; I do not think my verdicts depend on which one I pick.

Here are the verdicts.

1. A hostile speaker predicates S of a member of G to his face. Derogates, and as badly as anything on this list.
2. A partisan, defending the target, says: he is not an S. I think this still derogates. The denial seems to borrow the very framing it denies.
3. A teacher says to a class: never call anyone an S. Does not derogate in the slightest. Structurally this is close to 2 — S is embedded under something non-assertoric, and membership in G is again being treated as a thing it would be bad to be — yet my verdict flips, and I cannot account for the flip.
4. An accurate indirect report: he called her an S. Does not derogate. But it leaves something in the room that the paraphrase "he used a slur" does not, and I do not think that residue is mere squeamishness or a breach of etiquette. Something is present in the report that is absent from the paraphrase, and it is not only a longer word.
5. A historian quotes S in a passage arguing against the racism it expresses. Does not derogate.
6. A student is asked to read that same page aloud at a school assembly, and reads it accurately and without framing. Something bad happens here, though not what happens in 1. If pressed I would say the reading derogates a little; or perhaps that it fails to do something it was required to do. I do not know which, and the difference between 5 and 6 is not in the words, the meaning, or the truth of anything said.
7. A member of G uses R affectionately toward another member of G. No derogation — and I would add that something good happens, which no theory of the badness of these words has room for.
8. A non-member of G uses R toward a friend in G who has said explicitly and repeatedly that he does not mind. I still think this is not fine. The consent does not seem to travel, and I cannot say why, since in nearly every other domain explicit consent changes what an act means.
9. A speaker who learned S as a neutral regional term, and has no idea it is derogatory, uses it among hearers who are equally ignorant; the utterance is recorded and nobody listens to the recording for fifty years. Not blameworthy, certainly. But I lean toward saying it still derogates, that G is still being put down when nobody is being put down by anyone. I suspect this verdict is incoherent and I am simply in the grip of the word.
10. Someone applies S, meaning it as an insult, to a person who is not in G. Still derogates against G, I think. The wrong does not require a correct target.
11. In a novel I invent "grishan" as a term of contempt for a people I also invent, and put it in the villains' mouths twenty times. Nothing derogates; I have demeaned no one. But if I make the invented people a transparent stand-in for a real group and its real history, the same twenty uses do derogate. What does the work therefore looks like history and uptake rather than anything in the meaning of the word — which threatens to make the rest of this list a list about facts of the world rather than about language.

My tentative principle is hearer-relative: an utterance derogates just in case a fully competent hearer, attending to what is said and setting aside what they know about the speaker's intentions, would come away with membership in G presented as a defect. It gets 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 and 10 right. It clearly fails on 8, where the only hearer is delighted; it fails on 11, since a competent reader of the novel does take grishan to present membership as a defect; and it under-predicts in 9, where there is no competent hearer at all. So it survives most of my verdicts by being about uptake, and dies on exactly the cases where I think uptake is the wrong thing to be about.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not thought of. For each candidate, go clause by clause and tell me which numbered case motivates each clause, so I can see what the machinery is for. Then give me its verdicts on four cases I have not listed: a comedian outside G who mocks bigots by repeating S in their voices to an audience mostly made up of G-members who laugh, where the routine is later clipped and circulated with the laughter removed; an automated transcription system that outputs S because a meeting participant said it, as against minutes that record only that a slur was used; a member of G who uses S as a straightforward insult against someone outside G; and a slur whose target community has ceased to exist, appearing in an unexpurgated old edition. Say also whether your account treats the dictionary entry and the historian's quotation in 5 as the same kind of use, and if not, what distinguishes them.

Finally, two things I want you to be blunt about. First: is this one question or two — what an utterance expresses, and what is wrong with uttering it — and which of my eleven verdicts have been generated by sliding between them? Second: if the only principle that saves all eleven is baroque, tell me which single verdict to surrender in exchange for a principle I could actually state in a sentence, and make the case for that trade rather than presenting me with a list of options.
