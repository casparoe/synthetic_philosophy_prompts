Our intake desk handles roughly forty proposals a month at a mid-sized, corpus-driven general dictionary — descriptive in principle, 1.4 million citations, about 300,000 numbered senses — and the house style guide gives my two junior editors exactly one word to work with when they decide whether a usage is a new numbered sense, a sub-case of an existing one, or a metaphor that has not yet hardened into meaning: the usage must be *established*. In practice they settle almost every hard case the same way, by drafting a candidate definition and then testing the usage against it, which is precisely the habit I have been asked to break, because the words that generate nearly all of our disputes are words for which no such definition can be written — and not because we are lazy. Wittgenstein's discussion of games gives the diagnosis: the applications of such a word hang together by a network of overlapping and criss-crossing similarities, no one of which runs through all of them, like a thread whose strength comes from many short fibres overlapping rather than from a single fibre running its whole length. That is a perfectly good account of why our current method fails and no help at all on a Thursday afternoon. I also know how this goes wrong when people try to operationalize it: either the checklist turns out to be a classical definition in disguise — a fixed feature list applied jointly to cases that conspicuously lack one of the features — or it collapses into "count the resemblances," which is not a test, since anything can be made to resemble anything in some respect.

So please write me the triage protocol the desk can actually run. Write as a lexicographer who has spent twenty years on an intake desk and reads the *Investigations* sideways; before you draft the steps, spend a short paragraph identifying the failure modes a protocol of this kind is exposed to, and let that drive the design. I want an ordered sequence of steps, and for each step: the question asked, the evidence that counts as a sufficient answer, and where that answer routes the proposal. The protocol has to say what a candidate is compared *against* — which paradigm entries, kept how, and revised under what conditions — and how a resemblance claim gets recorded as named respects of similarity and difference rather than as a general air of kinship. It has to tell my editors how to say which respects are load-bearing, and to do so without pretending that this is a computation. It needs an explicit branch for the case where our corpus holds no close paradigm on either side, and an explicit treatment of non-transitivity: we already accept two senses that overlap with each other, and a candidate overlaps with the second but with neither the first nor the original core, and a chain of that kind can walk a concept anywhere it likes, so I want to know what the protocol says stops it. It should distinguish a usage that is frequent from one that is established, and live figurative use from an acquired sense. And it should mark, visibly and in its own typographic register, the steps that are judgment wearing procedural clothing, plus the class of case where the question ceases to be lexicographic and comes to me as editorial policy — organized pressure from a trade body, foreseeable legal consequences, register politics. Every step's output must be phrased so that it could appear verbatim in the public note explaining a decision to someone who lost. Assume the users have no philosophy training and twenty minutes a case; assume I sign off only if the trace ends in a stop or in an escalation to me. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list, and if you invoke Wittgenstein, paraphrase rather than quote and do not attach section numbers you are not certain of.

Then run the protocol on three live proposals, showing the step-by-step trace rather than a verdict, and let at least one of them come out against my wishes:

1. **Oat, almond, and soy "milk."** Used predicatively and in recipes by people who do not drink dairy, attested across registers, and the object of sustained pressure from a dairy trade association asking us to restrict the word. New numbered sense, sub-case of the existing one, or unresolved? Note that one of the three inputs here is not lexicographic evidence at all, and I want the protocol to show that.
2. **"Algorithm" as a fixed routine in ordinary non-technical speech** — the algorithm of the morning commute, the algorithm of an election campaign. This is my own case; I have argued for a separate sense at two panel meetings, and my editors are polite about it. I expect the trace to reject it as figurative-and-not-yet-established, and I want the reason written so plainly that two junior editors can hand it to me.
3. **Whether "e-sport" falls under our existing sense of "sport."** We already admit recreational and blood-sport senses that overlap with *game* rather than with *athletics*, and an international governing body's recognition of the activity has been cited at us. Use this one to expose both the chain-walking problem and, if it does in fact arise here, a step at which the protocol cannot deliver and the case escalates to me.

Close with a short section on what the protocol cannot do. I specifically want you to say, if you think it is true, that family resemblance underdetermines the extension — that the comparisons only fix a ranking of candidates and never a boundary — and that what actually settles the marginal cases is our ledger of past decisions and the practice built on it, not the reasoning in any individual trace. If that is right, say it plainly and tell me what safeguards a desk like mine needs so the ledger does not simply drift, rather than papering over the gap with a final step called "editorial judgment."
