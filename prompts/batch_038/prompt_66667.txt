I write a science-and-ideas column and I'm doing a piece on why economists can't agree about how much to discount the future, and somewhere in the reporting I ended up in Sidgwick — Methods of Ethics, in the chapter near the end of Book III where he sets prudence against benevolence. There's a paragraph in there I've read maybe fifteen times and I still can't tell what he's actually claiming. I don't have the book with me so this is my paraphrase, not his words: roughly, the mere fact that one moment of consciousness comes before another is no reasonable ground for caring more about the earlier one; a later pleasure isn't less real for being later; what does count is that we're less certain about the future and imagine it less vividly, but that's a different thing from simple earliness. Then in the same breath he slides from time to persons and concludes that from the point of view of the universe the good of any one individual is no more important then the good of any other — and then, which is what throws me, he admits he can't actually reconcile egoism with universal benevolence unless something like a moral ordering of the world gets assumed.

Help me get staight on a few things: is the time move supposed to do independent work, or is it just a runway for the person move? He grants uncertainty and vividness as legitimate reasons to weight the present more — does that concession gut the anti-discounting point, so that a defender of pure time preference can say Sidgwick has handed over everything that matters? Where exactly is the line between a rational discount and a merely psychological one? And is the point-of-view-of-the-universe bit doing argumentative work or is it decoration — has he earned that standpoint at that point in the book? Finally, how much of the later stuff (Pigou's telescopic faculty, Ramsey calling pure discounting a failure of imagination, Parfit picking Sidgwick up) is already sitting in this paragraph versus added on top? No reading list, please — I mostly want to know whether I can responsibly say in print that Sidgwick thought pure time preference was irrational, and tell me plainly if my paraphrase has mangled something.
