Our health system's Resource Planning Committee votes next month on how emergency-department demand will be forecast for the coming fiscal year, and I need a memo to circulate to the other eight members beforehand.

For eleven years the number has come from a standing committee — the ED medical director, the nursing leads from our four hospitals, the admissions analyst, and me — meeting three times over the autumn and converging on a figure for average daily arrivals during the peak winter weeks. Last year we piloted an alternative: the same question posed to an internal prediction market, play money, a prize for the three most accurate participants.

On the surface the pilot was a success. The market's closing price landed within 3 percent of the realized figure; the committee's number came in 14 percent low. Three things have emerged since, and they are what the vote will actually turn on.

First, both prize-winners who did real work — pulling shift-level data, calling dispatchers, following the regional respiratory-surveillance feed — have said they will not take part again. Their reasoning is that once the price began drifting toward their estimate, everyone watching could see it converging, and further analysis stopped being worth their evenings. One of them asked why she should spend her nights discovering what the price already knew.

Second, one nursing lead traded deliberately to move the price toward a figure that would justify additional float staffing at her hospital. She moved it substantially, and she has not pretended otherwise.

Third, our CFO proposes that we simply adopt the market's closing price as the operational target the EDs are then managed against. Several members object that a forecast we manage to becomes a forecast we produce.

The committee is split down the middle. One faction says the price is a better epistemic instrument than any individual among us, because it combines information nobody holds on their own, and that reverting to the committee squanders exactly the advantage the pilot demonstrated. The other says the price was accurate only because some participants refused to treat it as authoritative, and that formalizing it will destroy the conditions that made it work. They add that the case for the market quietly assumes traders who begin from a shared view of the world and who have no motive to move the number except getting it right — and that in our pilot neither assumption held.

Draft the memo. It must recommend one of three outcomes — adopt the market for the operational forecast, revert to the committee, or a hybrid — and if you recommend a hybrid, specify its machinery concretely enough that we could hand it to operations: who trades, under what incentives, what the resulting price is used for and what it is explicitly not used for, and what happens in the first quarter if the two instruments diverge sharply.

Engage the strongest case for the outcome you reject, in its best form rather than as a caricature; the members who hold it are not confused, and a memo that treats them as such will move nobody. Argue from the structure of the case rather than from authority: no citations, no reading lists, and if you invoke a formal result, state it in ordinary language rather than leaning on its name.

Begin with your recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the situation above. Close with two or three sentences saying how confident you are in the recommendation overall, naming the single claim in the memo you are least sure of, and saying what evidence would change your mind about it. Keep the whole thing under 900 words.
