Six months ago I moved from flight-controls work into a group that writes functional requirements for an automated infusion pump, and I have since become the person who argues about the wording of individual clauses. That has made a pattern visible to me that I do not know how to name.

Three things I watched happen in review, all within one quarter:

1. A clause that would have required the pump to halt on detecting a particular incompatible drug combination was replaced by a clause requiring the pump to display an advisory and log the event. The hazard scenario is unchanged; what changed is that the last step now belongs to a nurse who has eleven seconds in an alarm-saturated ward. Nobody argued during the debate that the revised clause was safer. The argument was that it was less prescriptive.

2. A maximum-rate limiter carried over from the previous generation was dropped because clinicians complained that it interrupted legitimate workflows. In the hazard analysis, the overdose it would have prevented is now filed under clinician error.

3. Rather than specify what the pump should do when a partial occlusion later becomes total, the draft simply does not enumerate partial occlusions, so the generic pressure-monitoring behaviour governs. The safety argument consequently contains no requirement about that failure mode, and there is nothing in the document that could ever be shown to have been violated.

What troubles me is that none of these is an oversight. In each case the outcome was foreseen, the protective step was available and cheap, and the decision was taken with that knowledge in hand. Yet the resulting record is one in which the harm arrives through a process that the design merely declined to interrupt, so that on paper there is no act to point at, only an absence. And the reviews consistently treat these as a different and far less objectionable category than the corresponding positive requirement would have been, even though the foreknowledge and the degree of control are identical.

I have been told this is just the familiar distinction between doing something and letting something happen. That does not seem right to me. That distinction, as I understand it, concerns someone standing by at the moment of the event; what I am describing happens months earlier, in a document, by a party who authored the situation in which the standing-by will later occur — and who authored it partly in order that standing by should be what happens. A colleague who reads law remarked that American courts sometimes hold a public body answerable for a danger it helped bring about even where it did not itself inflict the injury, and I would like to know whether that idea is the same thing, a cousin, or unrelated.

So: is there an established concept — in ethics, in the philosophy of causation, in legal theory, or in the literature on automated systems — that names the deliberate arrangement of a situation so that one's own contribution to a foreseen harm takes the form of an absent safeguard rather than a performed act? If there is one, I want considerably more than the label: how it is defined, what it excludes, how it relates to the two ideas I raised above, and whether it survives contact with the standard reasoning about harm that is intended versus harm that is merely foreseen, or whether that framework is simply blind to it. If there is none, please say so plainly and propose a term with a definition that has edges — a case that clearly falls inside it, a case that clearly falls outside, and the hard intermediate case that decides whether the concept is doing genuine work or merely restating an older distinction in engineering vocabulary.

I am not after a reading list; I can find sources once I know what to search for. What I need is the carving.
