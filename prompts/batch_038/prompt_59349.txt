"This is all settled. A lie is when you assert something you believe is false *and* you intend to deceive the listener; misleading is when everything you assert is true but you lead them to a false conclusion by implication. Grice explained the mechanism back in the seventies — the listener infers an implicature, and an implicature isn't part of what the speaker said, it's basically the listener's own guess, so the misleading speaker never actually communicated anything false. Kant is the one who put the distinction on the map, that's the whole point of his murderer-at-the-door case: you can't lie, but you can mislead. And the moral gap is enormous, because lying corrupts your words themselves, which is why it's uniquely bad, whereas misleading leaves you an honest person who just didn't volunteer information. So the two categories don't overlap — a given statement is either a lie or a misleading one, never both — and if you keep every sentence true you're in the clear. Anything, person or program, that never asserts a falsehood can't lie, full stop."
