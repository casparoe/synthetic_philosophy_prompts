For eleven years I have facilitated a monthly bereavement group at a community center. I have no academic training; the language I actually work in is the group's — stages and tasks of grieving, processing, triggers and anniversary reactions, closure, holding space, complicated grief, continuing bonds. I have also been reading Zhuangzi in translation, especially the passages on death: beating time on a pot and singing after his wife dies, the dream conversation with the skull, his instructions to his disciples about being left to the crows.

Restate what he is claiming in those passages in my vocabulary, claim by claim, and for each one say whether it translates cleanly, whether it survives only if I stretch my words, or whether my language has no slot for it at all. Then give me your verdict on the residue: is the leftover material a genuine gap in how bereavement work talks, and what would have to be added to close it, or is Zhuangzi merely sounding profound while saying nothing my group could not already say? Confront one case in particular. In my group, a member who is steady and even cheerful two months after a death is someone we worry about, and I have come to think Zhuangzi would call that worry itself the mistake. Translate the worry into his terms and report what is left of it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating my question.
