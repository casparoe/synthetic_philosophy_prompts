Quick background: I'm 71 and spent about thirty-eight years in a materials lab — oxide crystals first, then in the last decade of it combinatorial thin-film work, which if you think about it is just an industrial-scale knob-twidling operation with a fancy name on the grant. Since I retired I've been reading way outside my field with no discipline to it at all, mostly philosophy of mind and this creativity literature, and there's a little reading group at the library that meets twice a month. Right now I'm stuck between two accounts that I suspect are closer to each other than they look, and I can't work out whether they genuinely disagree or are just carving the same thing at different joints.

First one is Hofstadter, the Metamagical Themas chapter with a title something like "variations on a theme as the crux of creativity" — I dont have the book in front of me so don't hold me to the wording, just to the idea. As I took it, the claim is roughly this: a creative act is a variation on some pre-existing theme, where the theme is a structure with parameters hanging off it. Some of those parameters are obvious knobs, and some are things nobody thinks of as knobs at all — you only realize you were turning one afterwards. Turning a visible knob gets you something ordinary; turning several at once, or turning one you didn't know was a knob, gets you something that feels like a leap. But the theme has to stay recoverable, or what you have isn't a variation, it's noise. The second account is Margaret Boden's three-way split from her book on the creative mind: combinatorial creativity (familiar ideas in unfamiliar combinations), exploratory creativity (searching out a structured space of possibilities that some style or rule set defines), and transformational creativity (altering the rules of the space itself, so that ideas which were not merely unvisited but literally impossible become possible, and some things that used to be generatable stop being so).

My instinct — and this is what I want you to push on — is that Boden's exploratory creativity is just Hofstadter's knob-turning with the knobs pre-listed, and that everything interesting happens at the transformational end. So:

1. Can Hofstadter absorb the transformational case by treating the frame itself as a higher-order knob, a knob about knobs? Show me what that move looks like concretely on some real example, then say whether it's a legitimate generalization or whether it drains content out of "knob" — i.e. whether the model stops being falsifiable once any parameter of anything can be promoted to a knob.
2. Is there any residue, anything in Boden's transformational category that resists being redescribed as a variation on a theme? Look especially at the parts of her account that aren't about generation: the claim that transforming a space changes what counts as valuable inside it, and the fact that such work is usually rejected for a while. Does the variations-on-a-theme model have a counterpart to that resistance, and if so is it the same notion or just a lookalike?
3. Machines: both are friendly to computational accounts of mind, but I'd bet they put the line in different places about whether a program could be genuinely creative rather than merely turn out creative-seeming products. Say where each would put it and why, and then tell me whether that difference follows from their two accounts of creativity or gets imported from somewhere else in their thinking.
4. Here's my test case, and please actually run it instead of using it as decoration. In 1994 we were depositing composition libraries, a few hundred spots on a wafer, with substrate temperature and oxygen pressure as the two axes we cared about. On one run a shutter on a source we were not studying stuck open for maybe ten minutes overnight, so the whole library got doped with something that wasn't on the experiment sheet. One band of spots came out with a structure nobody in the group had seen, and that turned out to be the interesting phase — it was a couple of years before we understood it well enough to publish. Note that nothing changed in anybody's head, and the frame that changed was the apparatus's, by accident. Which account describes this better? Was it exploratory, transformational, or neither — and under Hofstadter, what exactly is the theme, and is the result a variation on it? Also: does either account require an agent who intends and values the outcome in order for "creative" to apply, and if so what does that predict about a case like mine, where the credit eventually went to people who had nothing to do with the generating event?
5. Finish with a straight verdict: which of the two is the better account of what's central to creativity, or are they not really competitors (one a taxonomy of creative outcomes, one a story about the mechanism)? And since I can't help asking — what kind of observation or experiment would actually count as evidence for one over the other?
