In the autumn I took up a post as assistant textual editor on a scholarly edition of a novelist who died in 1971: three novels, plus companion volumes of her letters and journals. Until last month my work was collation, and I never had to ask why the house rules are what they are. I have now been asked to draft the general editorial introduction to the first volume, in which I am expected to state and defend the project's principles to an advisory board containing both literary historians and philosophers of language. Reading the style manual in order to defend it rather than merely apply it, I have found that I cannot state it without asserting things I am not sure I believe, and that several of its provisions seem to rest on incompatible claims. I would like to think the matter through before I write.

The policy, in substance, is this.

1. The reading text of each novel is to represent the author's final intentions. Where a novel survives in manuscript, in a first printed edition, and in a revised edition she supervised and signed off before her death, the last supervised version is the basis of the edition and the earlier states go into the historical collation in the apparatus.

2. Exception: where correspondence or publisher's records establish that a change in the final version was made at the publisher's insistence — for example the excision of a chapter he threatened to sue over — the editors restore the earlier reading into the main text, on the stated ground that a coerced alteration does not express the author's intention. Changes she made freely in revision are never restored, however much the editors think them a deterioration.

3. Spelling, punctuation, capitalisation and paragraphing are silently conformed to house style. The manual justifies this by saying that such accidentals are not meaning-bearing, that most were in any case introduced by compositors, and that the reader should not be distracted by them. In at least two cases the manuscript shows the punctuation is hers.

4. The introductions to each volume argue, at length and with textual evidence, that her punctuation and her refusal to break paragraphs at certain points are interpretively decisive for particular passages.

5. The letters volumes are edited on the opposite principle. Documents are transcribed exactly as they stand — misspellings, false starts, unfinished sentences, her amanuensis's hand — with no emendation and no attempt to produce a text she would have approved. The general editor's stated reason is that a letter is a document and a novel is a work.

6. In the interpretive introductions, the author's later remarks in correspondence about what she meant by a passage are treated as authoritative evidence of its meaning; in the editorial introduction, the same correspondence is treated as evidence only of what happened in the production of the document, never of what the text means.

What I want is an analysis, not a defence of me and not a survey of the field. Please do not build your answer on citations or a reading list; I cannot check them, and the board is not moved by names. I want the reasoning.

(a) Reconstruct the theory of meaning and of intention that provisions 1 and 2 jointly presuppose. Does the practice require the view that what a text means is fixed by what its maker willed to convey by it? Or could the whole workflow be licensed by something weaker — that the author's final decisions are simply the best available evidence of which public-language artifact she authorized, with meaning settled by the language and conventions that artifact engages whether or not she willed it? Separate sharply what the practice strictly cannot do without from what merely fits it comfortably, and mark the places where it is underdetermined, so that rival theories would license the very same page.

(b) Provision 2 assumes that some of an author's own deliberate, signed-off decisions are not her intentions while others are. What account of the will does that require? Is the coerced cut genuinely analogous to a compositor's error — an intervention by someone other than the author — or is the analogy strained, given that she chose to cut rather than withdraw the book, in a sense in which the compositor's meddling was not her choice at all? Can the line be drawn without a theory of which of an author's motivations are authentically hers? If it cannot, say what the project is thereby committed to about persons and their wills.

(c) Provisions 3 and 4 look to me like a flat contradiction. Are they? What would have to be true of the relation between marks on a page and what is said for punctuation to be normalizable without loss of meaning? And if the introductions are right that her punctuation bears on interpretation, does that show the reading text is not the text whose meaning the introductions expound — and if so, which part of the edition is about the work?

(d) Provision 5 runs on an apparently contrary principle inside the same project. Reconstruct the theory that licenses it. Is document versus work a philosophically substantive distinction, or a convenience that smuggles back in exactly the intentionalism the letters volumes claim to dispense with? Can the project keep both principles without inconsistency, and at what cost — that is, what must it then say about the identity of the thing being edited in each case?

(e) Provision 6 lets the correspondence testify to meaning in one place and bars it from doing so in another. Is a principled line available — say, between using an author's testimony to identify which text she made and using it to say what that text means — or does the distinction collapse?

(f) Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change — in the shape of the published volumes, the apparatus, the introductions, the workflow — if the project replaced its implicit theory with each of two rivals: the view that the work exists in several equally real versions and that a single ideal reading text is a fiction the edition imposes; and the view that meaning is determined by the uses readers and interpreters make of a text rather than by its maker's design. For each, say whether the change would be cosmetic or would yield a recognizably different object, and what the board would lose that it now has.

Please be explicit about where you think the practice is philosophically innocent — where I am manufacturing a problem out of ordinary craft convention — and where you think the difficulty is real and I should say so in print.
