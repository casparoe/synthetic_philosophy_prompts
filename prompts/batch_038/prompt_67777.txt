I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student working on mathematical evidence, and last year I sat in on our analytic number theory reading group so I could write about how unproved conjectures get justified in that field. I've drafted an argument for a seminar next month that started out as a way of making precise something the number theorists kept gesturing at in discussion, and it now looks like it proves far too much. Two people in the seminar do analytic number theory for real, so I'd rather get torn apart by you first. One thing I keep coming back to: the field knows perfectly well that heuristics plus computation can lie — the Mertens conjecture had close to a century of numerical checks behind it before Odlyzko and te Riele killed it in 1985, and to this day nobody has actually located an n where it fails. My advisor read the draft and said only that I'm sliding between two senses of something in the middle, which isn't enough to go on. Here it is:

(1) When number theorists say a counting conjecture is supported heuristically, the procedure is roughly: pretend the integers are sampled at random, assign each n a probability of having the property in question (1/log n for primality), assume independence across different n and across different primes, compute the expected count, and note that it matches the data to several digits. That match is then cited as evidence, and the conjecture gets described as morally certain despite having no proof.

(2) The match can't be evidence that the integers really are random, since they plainly aren't — primality is deterministic and every pair (n, n+2) has a settled answer. So what the match is evidence for is the weaker claim that the primes behave, for counting purposes, as if they were independent random variables with the right marginal densities. Call this the presumption of independence.

(3) But the presumption, taken by itself, systematically gets the answers wrong. For twin primes, multiplying out the naive densities gives x/(log x)², whereas the conjecture everyone believes has a singular series in front of it — a product over the odd primes of local correction factors, there because the events "n is not divisible by p" and "n+2 is not divisible by p" are not independent. Cramér's model says the largest prime gap below x should be about (log x)², and Granville's refinement says the constant ought to be 2e^(-γ), roughly 1.12 rather than 1, again because of divisibility by the small primes. Maier's theorem says the model fails outright for intervals of length (log x)^A: the ratio of the actual prime count to the predicted one doesn't tend to 1 at all, its lim sup is above 1 and its lim inf below. And the model has no room for Chebyshev's bias, where primes congruent to 3 mod 4 outnumber those congruent to 1 mod 4 the overwhelming majority of the time, even though Littlewood showed the lead changes hands infinitely often.

(4) In every one of those cases the correction is not extracted from the presumption of independence. The presumption says the correction factor is 1. The correction comes from separate arithmetic reasoning about the primes one at a time, and then gets multiplied in afterwards.

(5) So "the heuristic method" is not one method with a track record. It is a schema plus an open-ended supply of correction factors, and which corrections get included is settled by which version of the schema reproduces the data the field already has.

(6) A procedure whose free adjustments are chosen to fit the data cannot fail to fit the data, and a procedure that cannot fail confirms nothing.

(7) Therefore heuristic arguments in analytic number theory provide no evidence for the conjectures they are cited in support of.

(8) But (7) can't be the whole story. The Hardy–Littlewood constants have been checked numerically over enormous ranges and nobody in the field doubts them; the field uses heuristic agreement to rank which unproved conjectures are worth attacking and that ranking has a decent hit rate; and when a heuristic fails, as with Maier, people treat it as a signal to refine the model rather than as a refutation of the whole approach — which only looks rational if they think the approach is tracking something real.

(9) And the same reasoning, applied to anything with fitted parameters — every regression, every effective field theory with its counterterms, every virial expansion — would show that no fitted model ever confirms anything, which is obviously false.

(10) So something went wrong between (1) and (6), and I can't find it.

Which step do you reject? Name the premise or the transition, explain why it looked unobjectionable to me when I wrote it, and then price the alternatives — for each of the steps I could give up instead, what would have to be true of the actual practice for giving it up to be tolerable. I'd rather keep (1), since I'm fairly confident it's a fair description of what people do and I don't want to say the singular series is a fudge, and I'd rather keep (8) too, because the alternative is claiming the field has no epistemology at all. If you think the argument is valid and I just have to eat (7), say so — but then tell me what number theorists are actually doing when they call a conjecture morally certain. Give me your bottom line in the first sentence, just the step number and a clause, and then the reasoning; no preamble and don't restate the question back at me.
