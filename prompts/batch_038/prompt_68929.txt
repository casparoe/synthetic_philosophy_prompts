Been having a running argument with a mate who reckons "Moloch" is just the prisoner's dilemma with better graphics. His line is that any trap you can't escape by getting the players in a room and letting them sign something isn't really a trap at all, it's a contracting failure with extra steps, and that everyone who talks about multipolar traps is really just complaining that lawyers are expensive. I want to build him a counterexample, and I want it built to order rather than found in the wild, because every real case I've tried — fisheries, antibiotics, arms races, AI labs — comes with an obvious Coasean reply already stuck to it. So: construct a thought experiment in which a multipolar trap closes around a group of people even though they all like and trust each other and talk openly about everything; they each know the others' costs and benefits exactly; any subset of them can make a binding, enforceable side-deal with any other subset at any time; none of them gives a jot about relative standing, beating a rival, or getting there first; and nothing external shifts — no new technology, no shock, no bad year. The trap has to come out of the structure of a sequence of deals they all freely and cheerfully choose.

The spec I really need you to hit: every deal in the sequence is a genuine Pareto improvement at the moment it's signed — better for the signatories, and not yet worse for anybody who didn't sign — and yet the endpoint is worse for every single one of them than the place they started. So the harm must not exist at any individual step; it only exists once enough of the deals are stacked up. On top of that I want the case escape-proof at every moment in time: at no point in the sequence is there a subset of players who could actually have met and signed something then, and who would all have been better off blocking what's about to happen — even though at the very beginning, before any deal was on the table, all of them together would have done better never to start. Keep it mundane and put it in an invented valley so nobody has to fact check me: say six family farms sharing one watercourse, and the deals are pairs of neighbours agreeing to do something to their own land that helps them both — drainage, ditch work, whatever you think holds up agronomically — with the nasty consequences only arriving once a threshold is crossed. One paragraph for the scenario itself, no sci-fi, no corporations with legal teams, and please no climate change, because I don't want the answer sliding into a climate policy essay.

Then do the demonstration, since a case that just asserts its verdicts is no use to me. Walk the sequence step by step and show for each deal why it improves things for the signatories and why the non-signatories aren't harmed yet — this is where the threshold structure has to actually do work rather than be waved at. Then show the endpoint and why each of the six is worse off than at the start. Then go back through each moment and name who could in principle have blocked and why they wouldn't have, and I want that failure to come from their own interests, not from anyone being thick or badly informed. After that, the honest bits: tell me which single feature of the construction is doing the work, and what the case collapses into if I take it out — if the effects were smoothly additive and continuously visible instead of thresholded, does this just turn back into a standard commons or an arms race? Give me the strongest thing my mate could say to attack it, which I suspect is something about the deals not really being Pareto improvements once you count the options they foreclose, and tell me whether he's actually right. And finally settle whether this thing deserves the Moloch label at all or whether its a different animal — nobody is racing anybody for the first few steps, so "race to the bottom" feels like the wrong words, and I'd like to know what family it does belong to.
