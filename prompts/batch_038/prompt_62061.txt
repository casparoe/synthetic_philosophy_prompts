You are an expert in early modern political philosophy who has taught Spinoza's political writings to mixed undergraduate audiences many times, and you have a well-developed sense of where non-specialists reliably go wrong with him. Please think carefully before answering: I would like you to begin with a short diagnostic of the idea itself — what has to be in place for Spinoza's account of democracy to make sense at all, and what depends on what — and only then produce the teaching plan. I want a plan for how to explain it, not an explanation of it.

I am a PhD candidate co-teaching a second-half survey on the history of political thought, and next Thursday I have eighty minutes alone with the section. The students have read the chapters of Leviathan on the commonwealth and the relevant part of Locke's Second Treatise, and they have been assigned the chapter of the Theological-Political Treatise on natural right and the foundations of the state together with its closing chapter on the freedom to philosophize, plus the chapter of the Political Treatise on democracy. There is a whiteboard, no slides, and no handout. The room is uneven: about half are political science majors who have absorbed social contract theory as the default grammar of early modern politics; two are coming from a seminar on deliberative democracy and will want to drag in Rawls and Habermas; a couple have never read anything from the period and are nervous about it. One deliberate constraint I want to hold myself to is that for the first thirty minutes I do not use the words contract, consent, or agreement in my own voice, because I think that vocabulary is what allows them to file Spinoza under Hobbes before they have seen what is different about him. Please tell me if you think that constraint is worth keeping or whether it costs me something important.

What I need from you, in detail: (1) the order the components must go in, with an explicit account of the dependencies — I suspect that natural right as coextensive with power has to be settled before anything about the transfer of right can be intelligible, that the account of freedom as being determined from within rather than from without has to precede the claim that democracy is the most natural form of state, and that the limits on what a sovereign can do to minds has to come after rather than before the discussion of right and power, but I want you to argue the sequence rather than assume mine; (2) the one case or scenario that should carry the whole thing, and what it must have and must lack — I have been toying with something about a law that commands outward conformity and a law that tries to command belief, but I am not sure that single case can also carry the point about remaining a part of the sovereign; (3) a map of the misconceptions they will arrive with or form on the spot, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. My own list is incomplete and I want you to correct and extend it: that identifying right with power is a crude might-makes-right position; that Spinoza is a contractarian who simply likes the outcome better; that his democracy is just majority voting; that freedom in the state means fewer laws, so that the more law the less free; that the closing chapter of the Theological-Political Treatise makes him a modern liberal on free speech; and — the one I dread most, because it will swallow the last twenty minutes if I let it — that the exclusion of women, servants, children, wards, and foreigners from the citizen body in the Political Treatise is either a mere period prejudice to be noted and left behind, or a flat inconsistency, rather than something generated structurally by his criterion of who is independent under the law alone, including his argument from experience and the Amazons. I am not asking you to adjudicate whether he was right about that; I am asking you to help me plan how to handle it so that it becomes a place where they finally understand the account rather than a place where the class stalls; (4) one check for understanding I can pose in the last five minutes — a scenario or a short argument to repair, not a request for a summary, and not something a student could get right merely by having been persuaded that Spinoza is interesting; and (5) what I should deliberately leave out at this level — the metaphysics of the Ethics, the conatus doctrine's technical apparatus, the details of his institutional design for monarchy and aristocracy — and what I should say out loud about leaving it out. Please do not give me a bibliography, a list of further reading, or any citations; I know what is on the syllabus and I want the conceptual architecture, not the scholarship.
