So I co-host a tiny student podcast — call it 400 downloads an episode, recorded over Zoom, edited badly by me — and next month's guest is a philosopher of science whose new book tries to do something I've never seen anyone else seriously attempt: make Lakatos's progressive-versus-degenerating distinction actually scoreable. Her proposal, roughly as I understand it, is that for each research programme and each year of its life you tally the confirmed predictions that were novel in Zahar's sense (the fact wasn't part of the problem situation that governed the theory's construction), subtract the auxiliary patches that restore fit without buying any new testable consequence, weight each term by how close to the hard core the predicting mechanism or the patched assumption sits, and then look at the sign of a ten-year moving average of that quantity — she calls it net heuristic yield. A programme is degenerating, on her account, iff that moving average stays negative for two consecutive decades. To her credit she eats the results instead of hiding them: her score apparently comes out negative for Mendelian genetics in the decade or so after the rediscovery, because of all the linkage-and-exception patching, and positive for a field most of my friends consider unfalsifiable storytelling, on the grounds that cheap-to-confirm predictions are easy to rack up in volume.

The reason I care is that I came to Lakatos the way a lot of people on the rationalist blogs did: through arguments about whether this or that programme — alignment research, big chunks of psychology, the replication-reform stuff — was progressive or degenerating, where "degenerating research programme" mostly functions as an insult with a citation attached. So I'm sympathetic. But I also tried scoring a couple of real cases myself with a crude version of her metric and bounced of exactly the places I'd expect the literature to press: I couldn't individuate the thing I was supposed to be scoring, I had no principled way to say whether a given auxiliary was near-core or peripheral, and one dramatic success seemed to launder a decade of quiet patching. Write me the question list for the interview, in the order I'd actually ask them — ten to twelve questions, each with a one-line note on the answer she's most likely to give and the follow-up keyed to that answer.

Make sure the sequence covers, at minimum: individuation, including whether the Copernicus-versus-Ptolemy comparison that Lakatos and Zahar themselves used to argue one programme's objective superiority even survives being fed into her machinery, given that people have argued they were scoring a rational reconstruction rather than the actual history; the near-core weighting, and whether there's any non-circular criterion for it or whether it's expert intuition with a subscript on it; hindsight novelty versus the novelty of a prediction designed in advance, and whether her coding of Zahar's counterfactual comes out of the primary papers or out of what historians say afterwards; the exchange rate problem — how many patches does one confirmed novel prediction offset, and where does that rate come from; the overfitting worry put the way I'd put it, which is whether the weights were fixed before she looked at the historical cases or tuned on them, and if tuned, whether her own methodology flunks her own degeneration test; the objection that this is just a measure of average corroboration with a Lakatosian paint job, and what she'd actually loose if we dropped the vocabulary entirely; and what a negative score licenses anyone to do, including whether a programme that scores negative for twenty-five years and then comes roaring back is a counterexample to her or a vindication of the moving average. I also want one question that hands her the graceful exit — that the metric is a tool for reconstructing the past, not a decision rule for working scientists, so the hindsight complaint just misses — and asks straight out whether she'll take it, and what the book gives up if she does.

End on a question about what would count as evidence against her operationalization specifically, not against Lakatos: what pattern of historical verdicts would make her revise the weights, and what pattern would make her abandon the project. Then, after the list, add a short paragraph telling me which two questions she's best equipped to wiggle out of and what the wiggle sounds like, so I'm not ambushed on tape. No "what drew you to philosophy of science" filler — she's generous and fun, but she has a habit of answering structural questions with historical anecdotes, so build in at least two spots where I can politely cut off a story and drag her back to the machinery.
