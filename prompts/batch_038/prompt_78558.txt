My sister is the reviews editor at a monthly history magazine. She's the one who decides whether a submitted review runs as-is, goes back for a rewrite, or gets killed. She's on a ten hour flight right now and the issue closes at 5pm tommorow, so I've been left holding a review she assigned and I want to predict what she'd say before I do something she'll hate.

Her calls that I know about:

1. Review of a book on the early modern English witch prosecutions. The reviewer called the judges and jurors murderers who should have known better, and never once described what the accused were supposed to have done or what the demonology looked like from the inside. Killed, presentist.

2. Review of a biography of a Roman senator who owned several hundred slaves. The reviewer said flat out that owning people was a grave wrong, then spent most of the piece showing that the senator's own tradition had told him so — jurists writing that slavery was contrary to nature even while the law enforced it, contemporaries who freed their slaves and wrote about the shared humanity of slave and master. Ran unchanged; she called it a model.

3. Review of a book about British district officers in colonial East Africa. The reviewer's whole argument was that we shouldn't judge these men at all, that they acted in good faith inside their own moral world, and that anyone who says otherwise is being arrogant. Also killed. Her note in the margin: same disease, symptoms reversed.

4. Review of a book on medieval peasant diet that used modern nutritional analysis to argue the church's fasting calendar produced real protein deficiency in some regions. Barely any moralizing about the clergy. Ran.

5. Review of a book about the famine bureaucracy in Ireland. Impeccably sourced, and the reviewer was careful to reconstruct the Treasury officials' own reasoning before condemning them, so it wasn't case 1. But the last third turned into an op-ed comparing those officials to the current government's benefit cap and ended by telling readers who to vote for. Killed. Her note: using the dead as ammunition.

The one I have to decide on is a review of a book about the superintendents of the big American public asylums, roughly 1850 to 1890. What the reviewer does:

- She reconstructs moral treatment theory seriously and from the inside, quotes the superintendents at length, and shows that several of them were privately uneasy — one wrote in a letter that he sometimes felt he was running a prison and not a hospital.
- Then she argues that the full wrong of what they were doing, the way those institutions worked as storage for the poor, the troublesome, and the daughters families wanted out of the way, was not available to anyone at the time, not even to the dissenters, because it needed a vocabulary of institutional power that nobody then had. Her condemnation is stated in exactly those terms and she says so explicitly.
- The book never mentions the present. No comparisons, no lessons, no politics. But the author says in her preface that she wrote it because she spent two years working in a detention facility and wanted to understand how the people who run such places live with themselves, and that she hopes readers come away uncomfortable.
- The reviewer quotes that preface and spends a paragraph on it, approvingly.

So what does she say? I can genuinely see it both ways and I don't know which rule she's running. Give me the candidate rules that fit cases 1 through 5, what each one predicts here, your best guess with reasons, and which of the earlier cases is the closest analog. Then tell me what a sixth case would look like that would settle which rule she's on. And if her precedents really don't determine this one, what is the magazine committing to by running it?

Separate question: is case 3 actually consistent with case 2? Every time I try to explain her rule to someone I end up saying something that makes case 3 look like a flat contradiction of case 2, and I can't tell whether she's inconsistent or I'm just stating the rule wrong.
