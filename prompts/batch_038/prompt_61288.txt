I convene a small MA seminar on divine omniscience and human freedom, twelve students, and I need one essay question for the ninety-minute, twenty-five-mark paper that sits at the end of Michaelmas term. We have spent four weeks on Molina's *Concordia* in translation and the standard three-moment schema of natural, middle, and free knowledge; two weeks on Plantinga's free-will defence and the notion of transworld depravity; and two on the grounding objection as pressed by Adams and Hasker, with Flint's replies. My problem is that every question I have drafted so far is answerable by a student who has simply memorised the labels: they write out the three moments, name the grounding objection, mention that counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are supposed to be true prevolitionally, and stop. I want a question whose crux is a proposal they have not seen in print, so that the only way to answer it is to reason about the proposal on the page in front of them.

The proposal comes out of last year's cohort. One student, frustrated by the objection that nothing could make a counterfactual of creaturely freedom true before any divine decree, suggested that what grounds such a counterfactual is not a disposition — dispositions, she said, are causal-explanatory, and a causal ground would destroy the freedom Molinism is trying to protect — but what she called the creature's *counterfactual character*: a property the creature would have were it created, which fixes what it would freely choose in any fully specified circumstance, and which relates to those choices the way a person's sense of humour relates to her laughing at a joke, i.e. explanatorily but not productively. So "were Smith offered the bribe, he would freely refuse it" is made true, prior to God's will, by the counterfactual character Smith would have, conceived as a property of a merely possible person. I want the question to present this proposal in the students' own terms, self-contained enough that someone who has never opened Flint or Freddoso can engage with it, and then to ask two things of candidates: first, whether counterfactual character is anything more than a redescription of the very counterfactuals it was introduced to ground, and in particular whether the sense-of-humour analogy can carry the weight placed on it, given that humour seems to explain laughter precisely by disposing the person and given that a character one can revise oneself invites a regress; second, whether a successful answer to the grounding objection would even matter, since the objector's second prong — that a God who knows exactly which circumstances each creature would sin in, and actualises those circumstances, is the author of the sin regardless of who grounds the counterfactual — is untouched by truth-makers. A first-class answer should get clear on the direction of explanation the Molinist needs, and should say something about whether stable counterfactual characters make transworld depravity look less like an open contingency and more like a fact about the creatures God is choosing among.

Please give me, in this order: the exam question as it will appear on the paper, with the proposal stated fully enough to be argued with in isolation; a model answer of roughly eight hundred words that argues a definite thesis rather than surveying the options; and the marking scheme as a Markdown table with exactly these columns — **Component**, **Marks** (the components must total twenty-five), **What a first-class answer does here**, **What a solid but unexceptional answer does instead**, and **The specific error that caps the mark at this component**. Do not append a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I want the deliverable to be usable by a candidate sitting the paper with nothing but the question in front of them.
