I am assembling readings for a graduate seminar on skepticism, and one candidate is a chapter from a recent companion volume in which the author summarizes Nozick's tracking account as follows:

"Nozick keeps the traditional analysis of knowledge as justified true belief and adds a fourth condition: the belief must track the truth, meaning that had the proposition been false, the believer would not have believed it. Applied to the skeptic, the account delivers something surprising. Were I a handless brain in a vat, I would still believe that I am not one; that belief therefore fails the test, and I do not know that I am not a handless brain in a vat. I do, however, know that I have hands, for had I lacked hands I would not have believed that I had them. Since knowing the first of these would follow from knowing the second together with the obvious entailment between them, the principle that knowledge is closed under known entailment must go. Nozick gives it up without hesitation, on the ground that the principle was never more than a piece of philosophical housekeeping. The damage is contained. Closure fails only where the skeptical hypotheses are concerned, so the skeptic is conceded a single item of ignorance and ordinary knowledge is left exactly as it was."

Before I assign the chapter, I want an accuracy audit. Take the passage claim by claim and sort its contents into three bins: what a careful reader of Nozick would accept as stated, what is a tolerable simplification needing only a footnote, and what misdescribes the view. I am most unsure about how the analysis is structured — what the subjunctive conditions are conditions on, and how many of them the account actually contains — and about whether the rejection of closure is as localized and as cost-free as the passage suggests. Conclude by telling me whether the final two sentences survive once the rest has been corrected, since those are precisely the claims I would least want my students to carry away.
