I'm a staff engineer on a platform team at a mid-sized infrastructure company. My degree is in computer science and I have no formal philosophy background; what I know about causation I've picked up from twenty years of debugging. Two of my senior colleagues got into an argument during an incident review last month that continued for two hours afterward at a bar, and I sat there taking notes because I found the argument more interesting than the review. I'd like your assessment of the argument itself. I'm reporting it as neutrally as I can and I genuinely don't know which of them was right.

The incident, briefly. One node running a Java service had a stop-the-world garbage collection pause of roughly 900 milliseconds. Its lease with the coordinator had a 500-millisecond TTL, so the coordinator marked the shard dead and reassigned it. Clients retried. The retry path used a fixed 250-millisecond delay with no exponential backoff, no jitter, and no attempt cap, so the retries arrived as a synchronized wave against the remaining nodes. Those nodes slowed down under the extra load, their pauses lengthened, more leases expired, and the failure propagated across the fleet in about four minutes. We had seen the same pattern twice before in two other services — one written in Go, one in Python — with completely different codebases, different triggers (a slow disk flush in one case, a DNS resolution timeout in the other), different kernels, and different cloud providers. In all three cases the postmortem named the same primary cause: unthrottled, unsynchronized retries.

The argument was about whether a sentence like that can state a cause at all. Dana has a PhD in particle physics and moved into engineering six years ago. Marcus has been writing distributed systems for twenty years. Neither has any philosophy training, so I may be reporting terms they were using loosely.

1. Dana: The postmortem's headline cause isn't a cause. Every event in that outage is fixed by what the code and the machines did. The architectural sentence is a compression of those facts — useful for communicating to forty engineers, but there is exactly one causal story and it lives at the level of processes, packets, and hardware. The physical level is causally closed, which isn't a philosophical thesis, it's how physics works, and if it's closed there's no room left over for an architectural fact to do anything.

2. Marcus: The same defect has now taken down three services that share no code, two languages, and three providers. If the only causal story is at the code-and-machine level, then the four incidents have nothing in common except a sentence we happen to write afterward. But there is something in common, and it's the thing that lets us predict the next one. Also, the garbage collection pause isn't doing the interesting work: pauses of that length happen several times a day across the fleet and nothing happens. Remove the retry policy and that same pause is a non-event. So the pause didn't cause the outage; the policy did.

3. Dana: Two problems. First, you're trading on a distinction between the trigger and the standing condition, and both of those are just descriptions of low-level arrangements. Promoting one of them to "the real cause" is a statement about what we care about, not a fact about the world. Second, your commonality across services is a type, and types don't cause anything; the outage was a particular event, and only particulars cause particulars. Separately — "no jitter" is an absence. Absences aren't events. If absences were causes, then the absence of a firebreak caused every city that ever burned down.

4. Marcus: If absences can't be causes, then almost nothing we call a defect is a cause, including yours. You spent last quarter on a data race. The fix was to add a lock. On your account, adding the lock caused the correct behavior, but the lock's absence didn't cause the corruption — which is operationally insane and I think philosophically insane too. And "types don't cause" cuts against you: the generalization that unthrottled retries amplify load and can cascade supports counterfactuals and interventions across implementations. If that isn't lawlike, nothing in this field is, and every postmortem ever written is fiction.

5. Dana: Fine, grant absences. But notice what you have to do with them: you cash "the missing lock" out as a positive fact about the ordering of memory accesses in the running processes. Do the same with "no jitter" — it's a positive fact about the shape of a function in each of those three binaries. Once you've cashed it out, the architectural vocabulary has vanished from the causal story, and what remains is a correlation between a pattern present in several codebases and an outcome. A correlation across a type is exactly what a compression is. It doesn't buy you a second causal layer.

6. Marcus: Then explain the compiler with your compression view. Our binaries are generated from source. You cannot explain why the emitted code contains a particular unrolled loop or a redundant bounds check without reference to the source construct that produced it — the binary does not explain itself. In engineered artifacts the high level isn't a lossy summary of the low level; it's what the low level was derived from. Your picture has the direction backwards for everything humans build. And it isn't only compilers: the retry policy in the architecture document is why a human wrote the code that way six months before the incident. The architectural fact is upstream, literally.

7. Dana: That's a pipeline, not downward causation. Source precedes binary in time; a document precedes a commit in time. Earlier in the causal chain is not higher in the level structure. Once the binary is running, the source is inert — nothing in the system consults it. And your upstream argument proves too much: by that reasoning the founder's business plan caused the outage, and so did the invention of TCP. You've collapsed causation into "anything you can trace backwards." A notion of cause that can't be wrong isn't a notion of cause.

8. Marcus: Locality and contemporaneity are physics notions, and you're importing them into a system that isn't physical in the relevant sense. The components are on three continents. There's no global clock, so the ordering of the very events we're trying to explain is only partially defined — that's why we maintain vector clocks. If you insist on a local, bottom-level causal account of a distributed system, you owe me an answer to what the bottom level of such a system even is. It can't be silicon, because the structure we're explaining is about message orderings and retry policy, which are not properties of any single machine. So your closure premise has no level to attach to.

Dana's summary at the end was that Marcus had opened with a metaphysical question and closed with an engineering one. Marcus said that was exactly the point: she had no account of which level the system is at, so her premise was empty. Neither budged.
