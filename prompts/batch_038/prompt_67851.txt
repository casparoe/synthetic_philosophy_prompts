Retired three years ago from a solid-state lab — thirty-eight years of it, mostly reading diffraction patterns and spectra, first off strip-chart recorders and later off screens. Since I stopped going in I've been working through philosophy on my own, badly, with a reading group of four of us at the library, and we've just come to the end of a long stretch on perception. I'm stuck. The argument from illusion is supposed to knock over naive realism: the stick looks bent in the water, and a hallucination can be, from the inside, exactly like the matching perception, so whatever the two have in common can't be the external object, so experience must be some inner item — and off we go, either to sense data, or to representational contents, or to the disjunctivists who say there is no common factor at all and the whole argument was a trick. I can follow each of those moves separately, but I've realised I have no idea what would count as a theory of perception succeeding. Before I read another page I want the specification.

So write me the requirements list. What does any adequate account of perceptual experience have to deliver — the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse, the results it can't flatly contradict — with the case or the consideration behind each entry rather than just the label. Some things I'd expect on there and want handled properly: the perfect-match hallucination; the difference between an illusion (the stick genuinely looks bent), a hallucination (no stick), and plain misperception (I take the coiled hose for a snake), which every book I've read slops together into one; the fact that some illusions stay stubborn once you know exactly what's producing them and others you can unlearn with practice, and what an account is supposed to do with that asymmetry; the fact that as a graduate student and as a professor I looked at the same trace and, honestly, saw different things in it; blindsight, change blindness, the split-brain work; and the constraint that whatever it says about ordinary seeing, it must not turn my whole working life into a mystery — because almost nothing I ever observed, I observed with the naked eye. What is a reading off an instrument? Perception, judgment, or a third thing? An account with nothing to say about mediated observation isn't a theory of how scientists see, and that's the only seeing I know much about. Add one more that I haven't seen anybody raise, and tell me if it's bogus: in the lab we had reliability numbers for observers. We knew whose peak-height estimates to trust, we ran duplicates, we did blind analysis precisely because we knew that what we expected to see would leak into what we did see. So a theory has to be able to say why one person's experience of the very same thing is worth more, evidentially, than another's, and what it could even mean to put error bars on an episode of seeing. If it makes every veridical perception an equally direct acquaintance with a fact out there, I don't see how it can say that, and I don't see how it can be right about science either.

Then do the sorting, which is the part I actually care about. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as something only one camp insists on, and be blunt about the second category, because I suspect a good number of the things presented as constraints are premises in disguise. Start with this one: is the demand that a hallucination and its matching perception be the same fundamental kind of state a requirement any theory has to meet, or is it the conclusion of the argument from illusion wearing a requirement's clothes? Seperate the genuine desiderata from that sort of thing, and if you can show me a subset of the requirements that no theory can satisfy all at once, do — that's the result I'm after, and it would explain why I've been going round in circles for a month. Also tell me which single requirement does the most to knock candidates out. I'll settle the scorecard myself, but if you must, end with two sentences each on the sense-datum view, the representational view, and the disjunctivist one (McDowell and Martin, I'm told) saying only whether they survive the unsatisfiable subset. As for purpose, since adequacy is relative to it: I want a theory good enough to tell a working scientist when a trained observer's report is evidence and when it isn't.
