I teach a small grad seminar in formal epistemology — 14 students, one 75-minute block. They've all seen the Ellsberg setup and can recite what an interval-valued credence is, but theyve never actually had to *do* anything with one. I want an in-class activity that puts two things in their gut instead of their notes:

(a) that getting more evidence can leave you strictly *less* informed — the dilation phenomenon — and
(b) that an imprecise credence can be perfectly coherent, no Dutch book available against it, and still leave you unable to choose, so that the "just maximize expected utility" advice simply runs out.

Design the whole thing: rules, materials, a timeline with minute markers, and a payoff scheme using no real money — something 14 grad students will actually care about that costs me nothing and doesn't depend on me being generous in a way that feels like pressure.

Some constraints, because every generic version of this I've tried has fallen apart at the same places:

- The physical engine should be one opaque bag of 100 chips where the only thing students know is that the number of red chips falls in some range. You pick the range. The dilation moment has to be triggered by a true announcement I read off a card, about a second bag that the students will take to be irrelevant to theirs. If the whole trick depends on their prior admitting a correlation they'd have no reason to posit, then build that correlation into the rules *earlier* in the session without explaining why — that's fine, that's actually the point, but it has to be planted, not sprung.

- Give me the actual numbers and show the arithmetic for the interval before and after the announcement, so I can check it on paper before I glue anything together. If your numbers only deliver dilation under a particular update rule for imprecise credences, say which one and say what happens under the others.

- Students keep a ledger where they have to write intervals, never points. I need a real rule for the student who writes [0.5, 0.5] or who quietly collapses to a midpoint halfway through. The rule can't just be "the facilitator tells them not to" — it has to be built into the scoring or the game so that collapsing costs them something they can see.

- There has to be a betting-desk segment where at least one student, following a decision rule that's perfectly defensible given their own ledger, turns down two bets they'd each take on their own. That's the paralysis. They should hit it cold, not walk into it warned.

- Nobody hears the word dilation, and none of the standard decision rules for imprecise credences get named, until the debrief. There's more than one reasonable rule and they don't agree with each other, and I want them to feel that disagreement as a practical problem first and get the labels after.

Then the debrief: map specific moments of the run onto the formal structure, in order, so I can point at what just happened in the room. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what I do about it in the moment — including the kid who's already read ahead and starts narrating the paradox out loud. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce. I already suspect that me withholding information about a real finite bag isn't the same thing as the imprecision people think is built into the world, but tell me where else it leaks and what I have to supply from my own mouth.

American English please.
