**The question as set:**

John M. Taurek, "Should the Numbers Count?" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 1977), asks us to consider a case in which a single dose of a drug can be used either to save David or to save five strangers, each of whom needs the entire dose in order to survive.

(a) State Taurek's central argument against the claim that we are morally required to save the five. Identify precisely what the argument denies and what it leaves standing.

(b) What does Taurek think we may do in the David case, and on what grounds? In particular: does the permissibility of favouring David depend, on Taurek's account, on David's having a stronger claim on us than the five strangers have?

(c) Taurek considers the suggestion that, where we cannot save everyone, each person might be given an equal chance — for instance by tossing a coin. What rationale could Taurek offer for such a procedure, and how does that rationale differ from the rationale a consequentialist might offer for a lottery?

(d) Is Taurek committed to the view that the deaths of five people are not worse than the death of one? Explain.

**The student's answer:**

Taurek's case is this. You hold the only dose. David will die without it, and so will each of the five strangers. The consequentialist says you must save the five, since five deaths make for a worse state of affairs than one. Taurek's objection is that a harm has to be a harm to somebody. When one person dies, that person loses everything. When five die, nobody loses five times that much: each of the five loses only his own life, and there is no further subject over and above the individuals, no group entity, that experiences the total. So the claim that the losses of the five add up to something greater than David's involves a kind of double counting; it smuggles in a collective patient who does not exist. Once that is seen, the inference from "more people will die" to "you are required to prevent those deaths" is left without support, because the numbers as such do not strengthen anyone's claim.

Taurek therefore concludes that we may save David, and the reason is that David's claim on us is stronger than the strangers' claims: he is a friend, and friendship generates a weightier duty. In the stranger case, where no such tie exists, Taurek says we ought to toss a coin. The coin toss is the fair procedure because it maximizes the expected number of lives saved subject to scarcity, while treating everyone impartially: each of the six has a one-in-six chance of getting the drug, and that is the most anyone could ask for. Taurek's position is that morality requires impartiality, so what singles out David is only that our partiality towards those we love is permitted by the rules.

Finally, Taurek is not committed to saying that five deaths are not worse than one. He holds that each death is a loss of the greatest magnitude for the person who dies, and five such losses are clearly worse in total. What he denies is only that this fact about the total generates a duty for the bystander, since the bystander is not responsible for the total and did not create the situation.
