You're a philosopher of mind who's patient with non-philosophers -- play that role. Think carefully, and feel free to brainstorm out loud first, but end with actual numbers, not hedging.

I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, so excuse the framing. I run software for a fleet of ~30k devices: some are learning thermostats that revise their own schedule, some are little warehouse robots that rebuild their map of the floor every night, and some are LLM-based agents with persistent memory that rewrite their own goals/instructions when they decide they've been doing badly. Our team has a half-joking metric we call revisability: how many of a system's own setpoints -- maps, schedules, goals, values -- it can change by itself in response to evidence about how things are going for it. Someone on the team proposed using it as a proxy for moral status, on the theory that a system that can notice and correct its own bad states is likelier to have bad states in the first place.

Two things:

1) Score each of these 0-10 for how much its experience should count morally: the learning thermostat, the warehouse robot, one of our self-editing memory agents, a honeybee, a jumping spider, an octopus, a goldfish, a potted tomato plant that shuts its stomata and sends chemical distress signals when dry, and a person in a persistent vegetative state. Numbers first, then one to three sentences each, and flag the ones you're least sure about.

2) Then be blunt with me: does revisablity actually track anything morally relevant, or would it rank a spreadsheet with an auto-tuning knob above a dog? Name the two or three items where your score and the metric come apart hardest, and say what you'd measure instead if you had to build something like this for real.
