I co-teach a graduate seminar cross-listed between philosophy and anthropology on how you're supposed to get from objects in the ground to claims about what people believed and how they were organized — the whole evidential chain, and every place it can snap. Weeks one through three are the familiar material: the processual/post-processual fight, the middle-range and actualistic research tradition, the arguments over ethnographic analogy, equifinality, and the general complaint that material culture isn't a text and shouldn't be read like one. By week four my students can recite all of it flawlessly and not one of them has ever felt the weight of it, because they've never been in the position of having to write a sentence that commits them. So this year I want to hand out an original piece of short fiction instead of yet another article — something they can fight about for an hour — and everything I can find is either a museum thriller or a time-slip where the modern excavator meets the ancient potter, which teaches exactly the wrong lesson.

Write me that story, call it 1,800 to 2,500 words, give it a title. Here's the situation I want dramatized, and feel free to rearrange the furniture as long as the epistemic shape stays: fourth season at a small mound somewhere on the Anatolian plateau (name it whatever you like, just don't make it findable). In a burned two-room structure under a collapsed roof they've found, along the wall of one room, a plastered bench carrying 37 unfired clay cones, each about the size of a date, each with a single fingernail impression near the base — and the impressions vary a lot in size, some of them very small. The fire partially baked the cones nearest the center of the bench, while the ones at the ends are still raw and are slumping into mud. Under the floor of the second room, an infant burial, and one of the cones is in the mouth. No texts, no sealings, no iconography, no comparanda from anywhere nearby in the period. The team splits four ways, and I want each of the four to be a real inference with real support rather than a cartoon: one says it's a household cult deposit and the cones are votive, with the mouth-placement as funerary protection; one says they're counters, the nail marks are tallies or ownership marks, and the bench is where somebody kept the household's accounts — which, if it's right, means the season has found an economic institution and not a shrine; one says unfired clay is just cheap and they're practice pieces or game pieces or a child's things, and the tiny nail impressions are the evidence for that, so the whole ritual-versus-economy argument is a projection of our categories onto the cheapest objects imaginable; and the fourth says none of these is decidable in principle and the only honest report is a description. Its important to me that the story not decide between them by fiat.

Then the pressure. The season report is due in three weeks. A journalist has already got hold of something and the ministry's press office has leaked a version of it, so there's a public sentence floating around out there that the excavation didn't authorize and can't retract. The conservator says the raw cones at the ends of the bench won't survive lifting, so somebody has to choose which to take and which to leave, and the choice will destroy part of the record — whatever the answer turns out to be, the surviving documentation will be photographs and notes that were taken before anyone understood what mattered. And I want one genuine reversal in there: a feature everybody treated as decisive — say, the fact that the cones are in a neat single row — gets re-read late in the story, because the work foreman who has dug thirty seasons points out something about the direction of the roof collapse and the scorching on the bench plaster that means the row they photographed is partly the pattern the falling debris made. Give me at least one character whose competence is in their hands and eyes rather than in their citations, and don't turn them into a mystical peasant for that; let them be wrong about one thing too. Tell it from inside the dig house, and I'd lean toward the foreman or the person who takes the finds photographs rather than the director, but that's your call.

Craft constraints, and these are where I'll be picky. All the philosophy has to live in what the characters say to each other and what they decide to type — no narrator essayizing, no paragraphs of gloss, no character conveniently explaining equifinality to another character who already knows it. The ending has to be a decision with a cost attached, somebody signs something, and it can't resolve the interpretation either way; but I also don't want the shrug version, the story whose moral is that we can't really know anything about the past, because that's just as lazy as the confident one and considerably more common. Some readings should come out of it looking better supported than others even though the question stays open, and the students should be able to argue about which. Please avoid: the museum-plaque epilogue, the dream sequence in which the woman who made the cones appears, italicized invented chanting, and any variant on the past-is-a-foreign-country line. And don't seperate the science and the politics into different characters so cleanly that the politics is obviously the "human" side — the person pushing the institutional reading should have the better taphonomy.
