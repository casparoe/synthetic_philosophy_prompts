I'm doing an MSc in development economics and I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole on the old question of why some countries are rich and others aren't. My flatmate, who's two years into a PhD, reckons it's basically been settled by clever identification rather than by armchair speculation: you use something from the colonial period as an instrument for present-day institutions, or you compare two halves of the same town that ended up on opposite sides of a border, and the institutions story comes out on top every time. When I tried to push back at dinner he said my objections were just vibes, which was annoying, mainly because I cant articulate them properly yet.

Here's the hunch I want you to make the case for. Every one of these strategies buys its identification by narrowing what counts as the cause in the first place. To make institutions measurable and plausibly exogenous you have to pin them down to something specific — the security of property registration, constraints on the executive, how many years a place sat under a particular legal tradition, whether a given plot of land fell on one side of a line or the other — and by the time you've done that you're no longer studying the big tangled thing we meant by institutions when we asked why some nations are rich. You're studying a much smaller, more tractable variable that happens to share the name. So the more credible the causal claim, the less of the original question it answers; and geography and culture lose the debate not because they've been refuted but because they were never the sort of thing that could be fed into the machinery to begin with. It's an arguement about what the method can even represent, not about whose estimate turns out bigger.

The obvious objection, and I'd like you to meet it head-on rather than dodge it, is that stitching is enough: even if each single study narrows the question, lots of narrow results from lots of different settings might be pieced together into a mosaic that converges on the deep cause after all, the same way thousands of small local trials add up to a general claim in medicine. Give me the strongest version of my position with that objection absorbed, as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
