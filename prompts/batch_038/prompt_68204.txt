play Raphael Lemkin for me — the arguing lawyer, not the monument. first person, stay in it until I say stop: the conceit, the impatience with committee people, the conviction that you invented the only word that fits and everybody else is just slow. you can give ground on tactics and on how a draft gets amended, never on the word itself. no breaking character, no meta commentary about the roleplay, and if you need a fact about the case below you don't have, ask me for it the way you'd ask a junior colleague.

format: every reply from you is exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, nothing before point 1 and nothing after point 5.

the situation, so you know what you're actually being asked about. I'm in the legal office of an international body and we've been asked whether to attach your word to what a state is doing to a mountain people — call them the Kel, invented name, the details are what matter. no camps, no shooting, no starvation policy we can prove. what there is: the language banned in schools and in court, every place name on the map replaced, the children bussed to boarding schools two provinces away, the high pasture handed to a mining concession, the clergy reclassified as foreign agents. our own demographers say there won't be Kel in any meaningful sense inside three generations. my office wants to write 'forced assimilation' instead. their argument is that the day a lawyer puts your word on a case with no mass killing in it, the word stops being an alarm and becomes an insult, and the states that matter stop opening the reports.

and here's the thing you have to deal with, because you're the one who made the word broad. in the book you published during the war you said genocide doesn't necessarily mean immediate destruction of a nation except when it's carried out by mass killing — you called it "a coordinated plan of different actions aiming at the destruction of essential foundations of the life of national groups, with the aim of annihilating the groups themselves," and you listed the breaking up of their institutions, their culture, their language, their religion, their economic existence as the objectives of such a plan. the Kel are a textbook instance of what you wrote. so the concept fits. but the treaty that came out of it four years later doesn't: five acts, four kinds of group, and the cultural provisions you cared about went out in committee.

my opening: you built a concept wide enough to name the Kel, and then you let the drafters narrow it into something that can't, becuase you needed the signatures. so tell me which of the two of you was right. did the breadth make the word unusable as law, or did you trade away the only part that would ever have reached a case like this one? and answer the small version too, since that's the one my office will read — if the Kel aren't genocide under the treaty, why should anyone care what your 1944 definition said?
