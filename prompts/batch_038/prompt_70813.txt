I hold a version of the epistemic case for large-scale democracy that runs through the Condorcet jury theorem: on questions that have a right answer independently of how they are decided, a large body of individually fallible voters is more likely to be right than any of its members, and more likely than a small committee of specialists, with the advantage compounding as the body grows. I intend to use this to argue, in a seminar on institutional design, for replacing a standing expert committee with a large randomly selected citizen body. Before I commit that argument to paper, I want a rigorous map of what would count against the position.

Give me that map, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. In building it, address at least the following.

First, which of the theorem's assumptions my use of it silently depends on — independence, competence above one-half, sincere voting, a binary choice, a shared question — and, for each, what the world would have to look like for the assumption to fail in a way that destroys the conclusion rather than merely weakening it. On independence, work through the design-effect calculation: with average pairwise correlation ρ among votes, the effective sample size is roughly n / (1 + (n − 1)ρ), so any correlation bounded away from zero sets a ceiling on reliability that no additional membership can lift. How large must ρ be for that ceiling to bind in practice, and do party discipline, common media environments, or deliberation itself plausibly supply it? On competence, note the inversion: below one-half accuracy, larger groups become more confidently wrong, not less. Is there any procedure-independent way of estimating competence on a live political question that does not beg the question by reading it off the outcomes the theorem is meant to explain?

Second, whether the position can be attacked without attacking the premises — cases in which the assumptions are granted and the conclusion still fails to support what I want. I have in mind the theorem's indifference to how errors are distributed and what they cost, so that a procedure right most of the time with catastrophic exceptions may lose to one right less often with bounded ones; and the many-alternatives case, since my proposal concerns bodies choosing among policies rather than answering yes or no. On the latter, I have seen it claimed that under plurality rule with m options, if each voter chooses correctly with probability p and spreads the remainder evenly across the wrong options, the correct option wins with probability tending to one whenever p exceeds 1/m, so that the threshold falls rather than rises as options multiply. Check that claim, and tell me whether it helps or hurts me once I drop the assumption that errors scatter evenly. Where do cyclical majorities and agenda control enter?

Third, whether the theorem cuts against something else I want. Discussion correlates votes, so a consistent defender seems pushed toward unstructured aggregation and away from the deliberation that ordinarily makes citizen bodies attractive. Say what has to be given up, and whether the loss is survivable.

Fourth, an honest assessment of insulation. Is competence above one-half ever independently established here, or is epistemic democracy self-confirming? Has the view been qualified — ideal conditions, idealized voters, the theorem as model rather than prediction — to the point where nothing could count against it? If so, is that a genuine defect in a thesis of this kind, or is it doing ordinary work?

Throughout, distinguish what a committed defender of the view would concede as counting against it from what they would dismiss as a misreading, and say whether each dismissal is actually available or whether it quietly surrenders the conclusion. Tell me also what my position predicts that its rivals do not: a discriminating signature, for instance, in whether aggregate public opinion turns out stable and unbiased or stable and systematically skewed, or in how accuracy scales with group size under controlled conditions.

One note on what would move me: not another objection to independence, which I already take seriously. What would move me is an argument that the theorem, with every assumption granted, still does not license the institutional conclusion — or a demonstration that it licenses that conclusion only under further conditions I have not stated. Reasoning and verdicts, please, not references.
