I keep hitting a wall on the famines-and-responsibility stuff and I want you to defend a side I'm not sure I actually hold.

Setup first. Call it a **culprit famine** when you can point at identifiable people making identifiable choices that broke norms those same people accepted — hoarding, blocking relief, shipping grain out of a starving district. Call it a **lawful famine** when nobody broke any rule at all: the entitlement map (what people can command through production, trade, employment, state transfers) shifts underneath them, wages fall behind food prices, and the same laws, markets and administrative routines that worked fine a decade earlier now deliver starvation. My read is that Sen's entitlements move, taken seriously, pushes most major famines toward the lawful end of that spectrum — probably including chunks of Bengal 1943 and the Great Leap Forward, where the villainous-looking decisions were downstream of a mapping problem rather than the mapping problem itself.

So: argue that framing famine as a political *crime* is a net loss for actually preventing famines and for holding anybody to account, and that this follows from Sen's analysis rather than contradicting it. I want the positive case built out — why the crime frame mislocates agency, why it only becomes available after the bodies exist so it can't guide anyone ex ante, why it makes famines look like aberrations commited by monsters instead of equilibria produced by rules, and why a remediation program organized around finding the guilty tends to fix the wrong thing while leaving the entitlement map intact.

Then face the counter I think is strongest, and don't strawman it: the criminal language is what generated the political will in the first place. De-agentifying famine risks functioning as absolution, and telling people who starved that no one wronged them is its own kind of insult. And in the Great Leap case there really were people, and they really did have information. Meet that head on and either absorb it into the position or say which piece of the position you'd surrender.

Last thing: pick whichever of the three — Bengal, Ireland, Great Leap — best stresses the distinction, and show me concretely what each frame would have told a contemporary actor (district officer, relief committee, newspaper editor) to do differently, plus where the two frames end up giving the same advice anyway.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the position, stated flat, so I know immediately where you're landing — and then give me the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back to me. Argue from the concepts, not from quotes or citations; I don't want a bibliography.
