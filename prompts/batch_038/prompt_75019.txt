I'm the returning secretary of a mid-sized academic society — about 1,400 members — and we run three elections a year (president-elect, a slate of council seats, and two best-paper prizes) plus occasional member referenda on bylaw changes. Everything is plurality or, for the prizes, a hand-rolled ranked thing nobody has ever audited. Every year or two some member emails saying the process is gameable, and there right: people float near-identical nominees to split a rival bloc, committees propose three options knowing one is a decoy, someone occasionally threatens to just not vote if their favorite makes the ballot. I've read enough to know the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says I can't fix this in general, which is not a usable answer for me — I still have to pick rules, defend them at the annual meeting, and answer specific accusations after the fact.

So: write me a working procedure, the kind I could hand to a three-person elections subcommittee with no social choice background, for two things — (a) evaluating a proposed voting rule for a given election before we adopt it, and (b) triaging a complaint after the fact that the outcome was manipulated. I want it grounded honestly in what the theorem and its escape hatches actually license (restricted domains, cardinal or scoring rules, randomization, controlling the option set, giving up determinism, accepting a tie-breaker that is basically a small dictator), but the deliverable is the procedure, not a lecture. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which question terminates in "no, don't use that rule," and where the answer turns on values or on facts about our members that a subcommittee can't derive from the armchair and has to go ask somebody.

Constraints, since they change what's usable: no budget for a mechanism designer; ballots are run by a volunteer on a platform that can't do anything fancier than ranked or scored; results have to be certified within a week; and anything we adopt has to survive a plain-English explanation at a member meeting without someone saying "so you're admitting the chair decides."

Then run the procedure on three real cases, with the steps actually filled in rather than waved at:

1. The president-elect race, where a well-organized bloc is expected to nominate two near-identical candidates to crowd out a third.
2. A best-paper prize with four finalists and a committee of nine that both nominates and votes.
3. A bylaw referendum where the council sets the wording of the options and one of the three is widely regarded as a decoy put there to make another look moderate.

One of these should come out badly for me. I'd much rather the procedure tell me to abandon a rule I like than quietly invent a way to keep it — so if a case forces something ugly, say so plainly and say what we lose by accepting it.

Sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

- **What the theorem does and doesn't buy you** — ~200 words, enough that the subcommittee understands why the later steps exist and doesn't think this is all procedural fuss.
- **Pre-adoption procedure** — numbered steps, ~600 words.
- **Complaint-triage procedure** — ~400 words.
- **The three cases** — ~250 words each.
- **Where the procedure breaks** — ~200 words: the questions it can't settle, the ways it will misfire in practice, the cases where it gives confident-sounding answers it hasn't earned.

Close with a short note on how confident you actually are in what you've claimed — particularly any assertion about what a given rule can or can't be made to do — and single out the one step or claim you're least sure about and why.
