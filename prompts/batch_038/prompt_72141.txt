I'm in a joint seminar between the philosophy department and the law school on what contracts actually are — mostly people arguing about offer and acceptance and whether consideration is a moral idea or a formality — and for my term paper I tried to show that the whole "contract is a promise" story is incoherent at the root. My advisor read the draft and wrote in the margin "this proves far too much; fix it or scrap it," and I can't tell whether it's fixable. Here's the core argument, cleaned up a bit:

(1) An utterance has its conventional effect only if the felicity conditions for the kind of act it is are satisfied. (2) One of the conditions on promising — Austin lists it right alongside the others, not off to the side as a moral afterthought — is that the speaker actually intends to do the thing promised, and goes on to do it. (3) So if I don't intend to Φ at the moment I say "I promise to Φ," that condition fails, and by (1) no promise comes into existence. (4) And the intention can't be manufactured by the promise itself: if the utterance is what creates the intention, then at the moment of utterance the condition in (2) wasn't yet met, so by (1) the utterance had no effect, so there was nothing there to create the intention. (5) So the intention either precedes the promise or the promise never happens. If it precedes it, the promise is just a report on a state of mind that was already in place, and whatever normative hold Φ-ing had on me was already there before I opened my mouth — the words added nothing. (6) Either way promising doesn't generate obligations: it either describes them, redundantly, or fails to occur. (7) And none of (1)–(6) was specific to promising — run the same thing on "I accept your offer," "I hereby resign," wedding vows, or "I sentence you to ten years," and every status-changing or self-binding utterance comes out either redundant or empty. (8) Therefore there's no such thing as doing something normative by saying something.

Obviously I don't believe (8), I've signed leases. Step (7) feels shakiest to me on its own, since "I hereby resign" doesn't seem to require any forward-looking intention at all, but my advisor said the damage is done well before I get there. There's also a wrinkle that I suspect survives whatever you do to (2)–(3): if the person you're promising already knows you're lying, they don't take you to be promising at all, and if uptake is itself among the conditions then the promise fails even on a friendlier reading of (3) — so it looks like you can't simultaneously save promising from the liar and from the gullible listener. (This is vaguely the Hume neighborhood, the claim that there's no act of mind a promise could consist in, though I don't want to lean on him, since my argument is supposed to be about the utterance and not the psychology.) So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want the price spelled out — specifically, what a theory of commisives has to give up in order to keep an insincere promise binding, whether the redundancy horn in (5) can be blocked without making the obligation depend on exactly the mental act that's supposed to be missing, and whether (7) really does fall out once you've fixed the earlier steps or whether it's an independent slide. If it turns out the cheapest exit is to deny (1) outright, say so, but I'd rather keep (1) than (5), since (1) is what makes speech acts interesting in the first place. Sorry this ran long.
