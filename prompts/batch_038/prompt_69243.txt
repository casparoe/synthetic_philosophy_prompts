Been going back and fourth on the Mencius/Xunzi fight about human nature and I want to stress-test a fairly deflationary line. Here's the thesis I'd like you to argue for, as hard as you honestly can:

The dispute is mostly verbal, because the two are using 性 to pick out different things. Xunzi means the unlearned, inborn raw material — senses, appetites, what you have without study or effort — and he's right that following it unchecked gets you greed and strife. Mencius means something closer to the characteristic trajectory of the species under decent conditions: the way sown barley reliably comes to maturity by the solstice, with any differences owed to soil, rain, and how much work the farmer put in, not to the seeds differing in kind. Read that way they can grant each other nearly everything — Xunzi can allow the sprouts are real tendencies, Mencius can allow that uncultivated people turn out badly — and the "good vs. bad" labels are mainly marking where each one draws the boundary between the organism and its growing conditions. Make the analogies carry the weight here as evidence: the kid about to tumble into the well and the water you can dam uphill but which always seeks the low ground, against the crooked timber in the straightening frame, the blunt blade on the whetstone, the potter working clay. Argue that those analogy choices are doing the real definitional work, not decorating it.

But "it's all a misunderstanding" is a cheap win, so don't stop there. Argue that exactly one substantive disagreement survives the dissolution, and use this framing for it: treat ritual, roles, teachers, and codified norms as a constructed niche — an environment the species builds and then gets shaped by. The live question is whether a normative capacity that only manifests inside a self-built niche counts as natural or as artifact, and they have to answer differently, becuase their political projects hang on the answer. Mencius needs the tendency robust enough that a ruler can largely count on it if he stops getting in the way — though his own story about the man who pulled up his seedlings to help them grow cuts against the pure hands-off version, so deal with that. Xunzi needs the institution to be load-bearing, since otherwise why insist so hard on sages, teachers, and ritual. Say which of these you think the surviving disagreement actually is, and handle the bootstrapping objection head on: if the raw material really is everything Xunzi says it is, where does the first ritual-maker's sense of 义 come from? Does his own appeal to accumulated deliberation and the heart/mind's weighing quietly concede a sprout, or is there a route out?

If you think the survivor is something other than what I sketched, argue for yours — but flag that you're overriding me rather than quietly substituting.

No chapter-and-verse apparatus and no reading list, I just want the argument. A page or two is plenty.

Last thing, the epistemic ask: give me separate confidence levels for the two halves — the verbal-dissolution claim and the surviving-disagreement claim — and then name the single point in the whole thing you're least sure of, the spot where someone who reads these texts in the original could most plausibly say you've got it wrong.
