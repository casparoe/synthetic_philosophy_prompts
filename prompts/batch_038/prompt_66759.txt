I work at a small tenants'-and-workers' rights outfit and I do the first pass on incoming complaints, deciding which ones we call freedom cases and which ones we hand off to a benefits advisor or a counsellor. Over a few years I've built up a set of gut calls, and a new staffer asked me what my rule was, and I couldn't state one. So here are the calls, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to — negative liberty, positive liberty, the republican domination thing, or some hybrid I've invented by accident.

1. Woman whose partner controls all the money and pre-approves every purchase, but has never once raised his voice or said no outright. I call that unfreedom. Her sister, who has a chaotic bank account and gets declined for things constantly, I call free.
2. Tenant on a month-to-month lease the landlord can end for no reason, twenty years without incident: unfree. Same tenant under a just-cause lease with a landlord who is a pain about noise complaints: more free, even though she gets hassled more.
3. A fixed traffic camera that stops everyone equally: not a freedom loss in my book. A checkpoint where an officer waves some cars through and pulls others as he feels like it: a freedom loss, even when the same number of cars get stopped.
4. A delivery driver whose app can deactivate him with no appeal, though it never has: unfree.
5. A monk who has given up nearly everything and could walk out today: free, arguably freer than most of our clients, despite having almost no options.
6. Citizen under a tax system backed by real force: not unfree. Same citizen whose spending is monitored for fraud, data never touched, never misused: I want to say that IS a freedom loss, which I realize makes no sense next to the tax one.
7. Guy who says he wants to quit heroin and can't: unfree. Person who is deeply depressed and stays in bed: I used to say unfree, and now I don't want to, because the moment I say "not what you really want" I've handed somebody else the pen, and I've seen where that goes.
8. Someone raised inside a very controlling community who is happy, would refuse an exit offer, and says so sincerely: I call her free. Prisoner who could walk out the open door but won't, from shame: I call him unfree. Same shape, opposite verdict, and I can't fix it.

My tentative rule is something like: you're unfree when another particular person gets to decide, at their discretion and without owing you an account, whether you can do the thing. Test it against all eight. Tell me which verdict has to go — I suspect it's one of 6, 7, or 8, but I'd rather you argue it than agree with me — and then run whatever principle survives on a case I haven't listed: a national digital ID required to open a bank account, in a country where the ID data has never once been misused.

Be direct about where you think my reasoning is weakest and what would change your mind: what argument or consideration would push you off the principle you land on, and which clause of it you'd expect to break first under pressure.

After the prose, give me a table with these columns exactly: `case` (1–8 plus the ID case), `my_verdict`, `principle_verdict`, `match` (y/n), `deciding_clause` (the bit of the principle that settles it), `note`.
