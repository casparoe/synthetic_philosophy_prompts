I am the technical lead for autonomy at a company that retro-fits autonomous mobile robots into operating client sites — distribution warehouses and, more recently, two hospital trusts. Our stack is hybrid: an LLM-based task interpreter converts a natural-language instruction from site staff into a candidate plan, and a classical planner with hand-written action schemata validates and executes it. Those schemata were written by four different engineers over three years.

Our worst incidents have not been perception failures. They have been cases in which the world changed in a way no schema anticipated, or in which a schema implied that nothing else had changed when something plainly had. Our audit team logs these under three internal bug classes of its own invention: silent-delta (the action had an effect nobody wrote down), ghost-precondition (the action's executability quietly depended on a fact nobody checked), and stale-world (the planner's belief about a fluent outlived the change in the environment that invalidated it). Last quarter a silent-delta in a charging-dock schema left forty robots standing idle in an aisle for nine hours.

What I want is a review procedure my team can actually run, not an essay on the frame problem. It will be used at our weekly design review, on one action schema or one interpreter-to-planner hand-off at a time, by engineers with no philosophy background, in about thirty minutes, and it must leave behind a written record that a hospital client's risk committee can audit and that our safety lead countersigns.

Please provide the following.

1. The questions to be asked, in the order they should be asked, each with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as a failure to answer. Where the procedure terminates in a do-not-ship, say so. Where it hands the decision to a human because the question turns on judgment, engineering risk appetite, or values rather than fact, say to whom and why.

2. A treatment of the distinction the procedure depends upon between the narrow problem of default persistence — which has genuine engineering solutions, including the discipline of writing one effect axiom per fluent and deriving non-change from them rather than stating it — and the wider problem of knowing which facts are relevant at all, which no checklist can close. For each question in the procedure, indicate which side of that line it sits on. The procedure must not market the first as a cure for the second.

3. A routing rule that lets a reviewer decide in under two minutes whether the defect in front of them is a persistence problem, a problem about the indirect consequences of an action, or a problem about whether an action's enabling conditions can ever be fully stated. We currently log all three as silent-delta, and I suspect our taxonomy has no slot for two of them. Tell me plainly whether my three bug classes are the wrong shape for this distinction and, if they are, what to replace them with — bearing in mind that renaming a bug class across four engineering teams costs me a quarter.

4. A one-page record format for the reviewer to complete, together with a note on which fields are objective, which are estimates, and which are judgment calls dressed in procedural clothing.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show me the completed record, or its equivalent, for each.

(a) An open-dock-door schema in a fixed-layout warehouse where inventory positions are surveyed nightly and the door state is polled by a sensor every two seconds. I expect this to pass.

(b) The interpreter handling a request to take a specimen to lab three through an occupied hospital corridor, where the route passes a fire door, a lift lobby and a ward entrance, and where the robot's presence changes what the people near it do. This is the case our commercial team is demonstrating to a third trust next quarter and I would very much like it to pass. If the procedure fails it, I want it failed clearly, with an account of what would have to change for it to pass — not passed with caveats I already know will be ignored by the demo team.

(c) A relocate-pallet-to-overflow-bay schema in which the pallet's new position narrows a marked escape route below its lawful width. Nothing among the schema's stated effects is incorrect; the trouble lies in what the new position entails. I want to see whether the procedure catches this, or whether this is precisely the kind of case our current review waves through.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do, where it will generate false confidence, and which single step is most likely to be skipped under schedule pressure — and what to require in the record so that skipping it is visible afterwards.

Please write the answer in British English. Do not append a bibliography or a reading list; if you name a technique or a known result, name it and say in a clause what it does, and go no further.
