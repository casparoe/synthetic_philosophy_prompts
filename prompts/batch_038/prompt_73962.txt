Retired analytical chemist here — 33 years doing QC and method validation for a generics maker, and since I stopped working I've been chewing through philosophy of science, mostly library books and podcasts on my walks. I've hit something I can't find a word for, and I'd rather not invent a term for something that already has one I just haven't stumbled across.

Set-up, because I think the concrete version is the only way I can describe it. For years we ran a sulfate test on material coming out of one particular vessel, and the working rule was that every sample from that vessel would come back positive. Alongside the real samples we always ran a reagent blank — clean water plus everything except the sample — and of course it came back negative every single time. The younger techs used to joke "one more for the rule" whenever a blank came back clean, and here's the part that still nags me: the joke was half right. If a blank *had* come back positive, we'd have concluded the assay was picking up sulfate from the glassware or the reagents, which would have thrown doubt on all the vessel results too. So the clean blank was doing some work, not zero work — but a tiny amount compared to one more real sample from the vessel. And how tiny changed depending on the set-up. With our old method, which needed sulfate specifically to throw a precipitate, there were hardly any ways for a blank to go positive, so a clean blank was worth nearly nothing; when we moved to a sloppier colorimetric procedure that also lit up for a couple of other anions, a clean blank became genuinely informative becuase it ruled out a big chunk of possible interference. Same observation, same rule, different dose. There was a population effect on top of that: for a contaminant we expected in about half the incoming lots, one clean lot moved the needle a lot, and for one we expected in maybe a lot out of ten thousand, a clean lot barely registered.

I know this is raven-shaped. I've read the white shoe business, and I know the Bayesian crowd says the shoe really does confirm the claim about ravens but by a negligble amount, and as far as it goes I buy that. What I'm after is whether the general thing underneath it has a name of its own: the fact that how much an observation is worth isn't fixed by the observation and the hypothesis alone, but depends on background — how the two populations compare in size, and how many different ways the test could have come out. Is that just what a statistician means by a likelihood ratio or "weight of evidence," or does philosophy of science keep a separate concept for it? I keep seeing "reference class problem," but that looks like it's about *picking* which class an observation goes into, whereas I'm asking what it's worth once you've picked, so I think that's a different question. And I don't think it's the grue thing, since nothing odd is happening with the predicates here. So: does my thing have an established name; what are the two or three nearest concepts, and exactly where does each stop overlapping with it; and does pinning it down actually dissolve the raven problem or just relocate it — say, into where the background proportions came from in the first place? One more edge I'd like nailed down: are there cases where the dose is genuinely zero rather than merely small, or is it always just asymptotically small? If it turns out nobody has a term for it, please coin one, with a definition tight enough that I could tell whether a given case falls inside or outside — I'm not after a label to wave around at the book group, I want the boundaries.
