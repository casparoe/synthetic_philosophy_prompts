I'm working on a chapter that's basically a friendly-but-firm attack on care ethics and I want to know if the argument can actually be carried, or whether it dies halfway. Write it out as a real argument, not a survey.

The claim: care ethics makes moral perception basic. Everything in the view is organized around a need that got noticed — you attend, you take responsibility, you do the work competently, you check at the end whether the care actually landed for the person receiving it. That last stage is supposed to be the built-in corrective. But there's a whole class of cases where the need-signal itself is corrupted: someone shaped by long subordination who has stopped wanting, stopped asking, and stopped being legible as needy. She says she's fine, she wants nothing, don't trouble yourself. The carer who is genuinely good at attending, genuinely engrossed, gets "no need here" as the honest deliverance of her attention. Adaptive preference formation isn't a fringe case, it's a normal output of the social conditions care ethics says it takes seriously.

So argue the following, and argue it hard:

- attentive perception can't self-correct in this case, because the evidence available to the attentive carer *is* the well-formed absence of a claim;
- the internal fixes each fail — the "did the care get received?" stage still takes the receiver's uptake as the criterion and so inherits the corruption; appealing to the virtue of the good carer makes it worse, since the better the carer the more finely tuned she is to a distorted signal; and adding a critical analysis of oppression smuggles in an outside standpoint, i.e. a theory of which desires are deformed and why, which is exactly the impartialist machinery the view was built to displace;
- therefore this isn't a gap in application that better training fixes, it's a structural limit: care ethics can be a good account of how we come to notice needs and a bad account of what to do when noticing fails, and its defenders have to pick.

Then do two more things. First, argue that the view's own stock examples hide the problem — parent and infant, nurse and post-op patient, friend in crisis are all cases where need is legible and where the receiver can register satisfaction or not, so the theory keeps getting confirmed by data selected to confirm it. Second, give the strongest reply a care ethicist actually has, which I think goes something like: care is a public practice with standards, not a private sentiment, so a carer who keeps "attending" to someone who is visibly wasting away is failing the practice by its own lights and needs no outside theory to be told so. Argue against that reply. If you think it wins, say which of my steps it defeats and what's left of the thesis — I'd rather have the loss located precisely than a hedge.

No citations, no reading list, no name-dropping, I know the literature already. ~1300 words. Also: tell me what a case would look like that would show I'm wrong, where care ethics's own resources do reach a corrupted signal without borrowing from impartialism. And one small thing — keep the tone like a person arguing with a colleague, not a journal article.
