Geologists formally voted the Anthropocene down in 2024: the working group had proposed a golden spike in Crawford Lake sediment, start date 1950, marked by fallout plutonium from bomb tests, and the Quaternary subcommission rejected it mostly because the sedimentary record is too shallow and the start date too recent. The same bodies then said the term will keep being used anyway, just not as a formal unit. What theory of periodization does that procedure presuppose?

Two bits I cant reconcile. The Holocene only got its own formal spike in 2008, in a Greenland ice core, and was only subdivided into stages in 2018 — so there the boundary existed for eleven thousand-plus years before anyone certified it. Yet for the Anthropocene, being too young for the record to show clearly apparently counted as a reason it isnt an epoch. And duration doing that work is weird to me: why should how long a stretch of time is bear on whether a real transition happened inside it?

Sort out what the golden-spike procedure strictly commits you to versus what it's merely compatible with — mind-independent joints in Earth history that we discover, conventions licensed by what's currently resolvable in the record, or some inconsistent mix of both — and flag where the practice is underdetermined by several rival pictures of natural kinds. Then say what it rules out, including on the question of who gets to name a period whose defining cause is human activity when the deciding vote is taken by a few dozen stratigraphers, and what would have to change in how time units get defined if you instead held that a transition is epoch-making just in case it changes the Earth system, spiked or not.
