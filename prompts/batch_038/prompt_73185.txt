Acting as someone who's spent a career in philosophy of social science but has actually read the economic history — you're the person they'd put on a panel called "can culture cause anything?" — think hard about this one. Before the analysis proper, give me a short mapping sketch (five to ten lines, no more) so I can see how you're lining the pieces up, then do the real thing.

The episode: South Korea, roughly 1960 to 2000. One of the poorest places in Asia at the start of the period, war-destroyed and aid-dependent; Park Chung-hee takes power in 1961, the state launches five-year plans from 1962, directs credit, sets export targets, disciplines and rewards firms, builds on an earlier land reform, with the chaebol conglomerates as the vehicles; OECD membership by the end of 1996; then the 1997 crisis and the huge IMF-led rescue, followed by restructuring. Somewhere in the 1980s a whole debate sprang up claiming that what made this work was the Confucian heritage: education fever, thrift, discipline, deference, loyalty to the group, family-run firms. And after 1997 a good number of people turned around and blamed the very same cultural habits — crony ties, incestuous bank-firm relations, obedience instead of transparency — for the collapse.

The theory: Weber's Protestant-ethic explanatory template, plus his own comparative study of Confucianism and Taoism (1915), where he argued that Confucian rationalism amounted to a rational adjustment to the world rather than a rational remaking of it, that there was no prophetic tension between a transcendent demand and the world to generate an inner-worldly ascetic life-conduct, and that clan and piety obligations worked against the impersonal, methodical conduct capitalism needed. He also insisted he wasn't swapping a one-sided materialist explanation for a one-sided spiritualist one.

Now run the Korean arc through that template, in both directions:

1. What plays the role of the "spirit" here, exactly? Which dispositions, held by whom — bureaucrats? firm managers? workers? households? — and what's the chain from disposition to conduct to macro outcome? For each link, say what would count as evidence for it and against it.
2. What does a values explanation actually handle well, and what does it miss or misdescribe? I'm thinking the state's role, directed credit, the war and the aid that followed, access to foreign markets, and the fact that the same cultural inventory gets recruited for both the boom and the bust.
3. The sign-flip problem. Is "same culture, opposite causal verdict before and after the outcome" fatal for cultural explanation in general, or only for this application? Is there a formulation that isn't hostage to how things turned out — something like Weber's elective affinity rather than straight causation — and does that formulation still earn its keep or does it just become unfalsifiable?
4. Does the episode falsify Weber's claim about Confucianism, or does his claim survive because it was about something narrower — say, the absence of an autonomous religious force that would generate capitalism endogenously, in a country that instead industrialized by borrowing a capitalism that already existed elsewhere? Be clear about which version of the thesis the Korean case actually bears on.
