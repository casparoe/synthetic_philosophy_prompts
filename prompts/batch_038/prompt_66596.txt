Write an essay of roughly 1,500–2,000 words on the following controversy in normative ethics, and commit to a verdict.

The controversy: whether the problem of cluelessness — the systematic inability of agents to know the long-run effects of their acts — is a specifically fatal objection to consequentialism, or merely one instance of a general epistemic predicament that leaves the theory's action-guiding ambitions intact.

Before you begin, here is a case and an argument that the essay must engage with. Treat both as stipulated; you need not, and should not, hunt for sources for them.

The case. In a river town in 1911, a municipal engineer must decide whether to authorize a tenth evening ferry crossing. Her evidence about the intended effects is good: the extra crossing will save commuters some four hundred hours a year and will, on the best available statistics, prevent roughly one drowning per decade. What she cannot know, and could not know even in principle by further investigation, is that the crossing will bring two particular strangers onto the same boat; that their great-grandchild will, ninety years later, hold a patent on a cheap refrigerant; that the refrigerant will be installed in something on the order of a hundred million households; and that its slow leakage will produce health and climate effects many orders of magnitude larger than the entire intended benefit of the ferry schedule. Two features of the stipulation matter. First, the disproportion is not merely large but unassignable: nothing in the engineer's evidence fixes its magnitude or even its sign. Second — and this is what separates the case from ordinary hard choices — her ignorance is not a matter of lacking probabilities she might, with effort or expense, have obtained. There is no probability distribution over the far effects to be had, because those effects depend on the chaotic amplification of details that were, at the moment of decision, physically indistinguishable from noise. Her epistemic position is not imprecision but indeterminacy.

The argument. Call this the Stopping-Point Argument.

1. If the rightness of an act depends on all of its consequences, then in cases like the ferry the right-making facts outrun anything the agent could come to know, and the theory delivers no action-guiding content.
2. If the rightness of an act depends only on the consequences the agent could foresee, the theory has quietly relocated the object of evaluation from outcomes to epistemic states, and it ceases to be the kind of view that motivated consequentialism in the first place.
3. Any intermediate cutoff — consequences knowable at reasonable cost, consequences within some temporal horizon, consequences above some probability threshold — is either vague in a way that admits of no principled sharpening, or it is defeated at the margin, since chaotic amplification operates across every temporal and probabilistic boundary one might draw.
4. Therefore consequentialism must accept one of the two horns: emptiness or self-abandonment.

Your task. Assess the Stopping-Point Argument and reach a definite conclusion about whether cluelessness is a fatal objection. Weigh, at minimum, these three lines of reply: (a) that the argument overstates the damage, because a theory can be correct about what makes acts right while being only partially usable as a decision procedure, and conflating the two is the real error; (b) that the indeterminacy in the ferry case is not peculiar to consequentialism, since any normative theory whose verdicts depend on facts about the world inherits the same ignorance, so the objection proves too much; and (c) that the engineer's position is not genuinely indeterminate, because rational credence under complete ignorance is still constrained in ways that make some options choosable and others not. Say which of these, if any, defeats the argument, and say which premises of the argument you accept and which you reject. Then state the strongest objection to your own verdict and answer it.

Requirements. End on a committed position rather than a balanced shrug. Conditional verdicts are welcome — "the objection defeats consequentialism as a decision procedure but not as a criterion of rightness" is a paradigm of the precision I am after — but "both sides have something to be said for them" is not a conclusion. Define your terms as you go. Let the argument turn on reasons rather than on authorities: no bibliography, no reading list, no appeals to what particular philosophers have written, no citations of any kind. What I want is your own reconstruction of the dialectic and your judgment about where it lands. Finally, be explicit about what, on your view, survives of consequentialism's claim to guide action once the ferry case is granted.
