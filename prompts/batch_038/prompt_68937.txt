I serve on the award committee of a learned society: seventeen voting members elected on staggered three-year terms, so that roughly a third of the panel turns over each year, and a chair who has no additional vote but does control the order in which nominations are discussed and whether a screening round happens at all. Each spring we select one winner from a field that runs between three and six finalists; the shortlist is circulated to the whole committee before any ballot, and although the vote itself is secret, everyone's tastes are well known from years of shared service, so in practice nobody is voting under much uncertainty about the others. The bylaws are silent on the decision rule, which means the committee effectively re-adopts one every few years, usually by muddle. Before I draft a proposal, I want to know what I should be demanding of a rule. Please write the desiderata: the specification any candidate aggregation procedure for this purpose must meet in order to be defensible. For each item give the requirement itself; the concrete situation that motivates it, with small-committee examples preferred to national-election ones, since the small committee is my setting; a label saying whether you regard it as non-negotiable, as negotiable given my purpose, or as a prejudice of a particular camp — the ordinalists, the partisans of pairwise majority comparison, the intensity-and-utility people; and a note on which other items on the list it competes with, since I expect several of them cannot all be had at once. Be candid in particular about which of the things habitually insisted upon are really aesthetic: neutrality among candidates, anonymity among voters, monotonicity, agreement with majority preference in head-to-head contests, explainability to a layperson in a single sentence.

The center of gravity should be the incentive conditions, and there I want the distinctions drawn finely rather than the familiar slogan repeated. Results of this kind are standardly stated for a deterministic rule whose range contains at least three alternatives and which is defined on every possible profile of preferences; tell me which of those hypotheses is doing the real work in my setting, and what survives once one of them is dropped — a range deliberately restricted to two finalists after a screening round, randomization, cardinal ballots that let voters express intensity, domain restrictions of the single-peaked variety, or weaker notions of incentive compatibility defined relative to what a voter knows about the others, or relative to how obvious a successful manipulation would have to be for a non-specialist to detect. I also want to know whether resistance to manipulation is even the right target for a body like mine, where misrepresentation is costless, ballots are secret, and a member who shades her ranking pays no penalty whatever; and whether the repeated structure of our work — the same faces applying the same rule every spring — should change what is demanded, weakening conditions that bite in a one-shot election and strengthening others. Beyond the ballot, please separate at least four kinds of strategic conduct and tell me whether a single desideratum can govern them or whether the list needs four items: manipulating one's own ballot; manipulating the candidate set by nominating or withdrawing a finalist; manipulating the agenda through the ordering of discussion and of screening rounds; and manipulating the choice of rule itself in an earlier year with a later contest in view. Include whatever further conditions you judge genuinely load-bearing but habitually omitted — how much of her preference a voter must be able to express; how the procedure behaves when members are indifferent or abstain; whether a member can be made worse off by participating honestly; whether two near-identical finalists should be able to split support between them; whether the outcome may depend on the sequence in which ballots are counted.

Finally, state which subset of your desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and if the list is itself an impossibility result, say so plainly and identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidates. Where the standard theorems are silent about my setting — because they presuppose a large anonymous electorate, or a fixed candidate set, or no chair with agenda power, or one-shot play — mark the silence instead of filling it with a confident guess. Close with a compact table scoring a half-dozen plausible rules for a committee of this size against your list. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, publication dates, or the history of who proved what, and please do not quote from any source; what I want is the reasoning about what ought to be required and why, stated as claims about the structure of the problem itself.
