**The student's answer.** I have bracketed and numbered its principal claims (S1–S9) so that the marking can be anchored to specific loci; the numbering is mine, not the student's.

> (S1) The paradigm coherence argument is the Dutch book theorem. De Finetti defines a set of credences as *coherent* just in case it conforms to the axioms of the probability calculus, and then proves that coherent credences are exactly those against which no book of bets can be made that guarantees a net loss.
>
> (S2) The constructive direction of the theorem runs by cases. Given any violation of the axioms — a credence below zero or above one, a failure to assign credence one to a tautology, a failure of additivity over mutually exclusive propositions — one can specify a finite set of bets, each of which the agent finds acceptable taken singly at the price fixed by their own credence, but which together lose money in every possible world.
>
> (S3) That construction depends on auxiliary premises: that the agent accepts any bet whose expected value, computed at their own credences, is positive; that they evaluate a package of bets by summing its components; and that nothing limits stake size or the number of bets they can be induced to take. Remove any of these and the sure-loss result fails. The theorem is therefore best read as a conditional result about betting dispositions rather than a direct result about belief as such.
>
> (S4) The corresponding argument on the preference side is the money pump. An agent with a cycle of strict preferences, A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A, can be handed C, then offered the trade up to B for a cent, then up to A for a cent, then back to C for a cent, and so on around the cycle. At each step they take what they prefer, and after one circuit they hold precisely what they began with, minus three cents. Since the terminal outcome is dominated by an outcome available to them for free, the underlying preference pattern is defective.
>
> (S5) The money pump is simply the Dutch book theorem transposed from the doxastic to the conative side: beliefs are pumped by bets, preferences are pumped by trades, and in each case the defect amounts to the agent's being guaranteed to end up worse off than they need have been.
>
> (S6) It follows that every departure from expected utility maximisation is exploitable in this way. An agent whose preferences display the Allais pattern violates the independence axiom, and can therefore be pumped around a cycle of choices exactly as an intransitive agent can; the two arguments differ only in notation.
>
> (S7) Foresight does not rescue such an agent either. When each trade is offered, the agent prefers it to what they then hold, so a fully sophisticated agent accepts it all the same. The only way to escape the cycle is to decline a swap one prefers, which is a graver failure of rationality than the intransitivity itself. The pump accordingly requires no assumption of myopia, only the dominance premise that a rational agent never knowingly accepts a package dominated by an available alternative.
>
> (S8) What the arguments do not show is that incoherence is intrinsically bad. Exploitability requires an exploiter: a bookie prepared to offer bets at exactly the agent's own prices, or a trading partner able to arrange the cycle. Offers of that kind are rare, transaction costs absorb much of the sure loss, and an incoherent agent may go an entire life unexploited. Since the only defect the arguments identify is a liability to loss in a betting situation, and since that liability may never be triggered, the coherence requirements turn out to be norms of market-savviness: they instruct the agent in how not to be beaten at a bookmaker's counter, not in how to think.
>
> (S9) Finally, even where a representation exists it does not pick the agent's attitudes out uniquely. Distinct pairs of probability and utility functions can represent one and the same preference ordering, so the representation theorems license only the claim that the agent behaves *as if* coherent; they do not license the claim that the agent has beliefs and desires of the kind the representing functions display.
