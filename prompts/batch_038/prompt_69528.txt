So I run a board game night, nine of us, and a couple years ago I got fed up with the shouting matches and built a little thing in a spreadsheet to pick the game "properly." It works like this: each week I put up three candidate games, and all nine of us submit a full ranking, first/second/third. I count the first-place votes. If some game has five or more firsts, that's what we play, done. If not, the two games with the most first-place votes go into a second round where everyone just picks one of those two, most votes wins, and if it's tied I flip a coin. If there's a three-way tie for which two advance I also flip a coin, though that's never actually happened. Last month Dave told me afterwards that he'd ranked a game number one that he didn't really want most, purely to keep a different game out of the runoff, and he reckons it changed the result. I said he got lucky, that he couldn't possibly have known how everyone else was going to rank things, and he said I was missing the point. That argument has been rattling around in my head ever since, mostly because I've been reading up on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem on and off and I can't tell whether it's saying Dave was right or whether it's saying something else entirely.

Here's the confusion. As best I can tell the theorem says: with at least three options and no restrictions on what people's preferences can look like, any rule that can actually produce every option and isn't just one person deciding is manipulable — meaning someone, somewhere, could get a better outcome by lying. But the way the theorem gets stated online makes "manipulable" sound like a property a rule has in the same hard, absolute way that "dictatorship" is, and that's not at all what I meant when I told Dave he couldn't game my rule. What I meant was more like: in my rule the only place lying does anything is round one, and lying there only pays off if you correctly guess the exact shape of eight other ballots, and anyone who could guess that well could basically steer the night anyway. So I want you to compare those two notions properly. One is the strict strategy-proofness the theorem is about, which I think means no voter can ever gain by misreporting, no matter what anyone else does and no matter what that voter happens to want. The other is my fuzzy one, something like "can't be gamed in practice," where lying is only worth it if you get very lucky or already have near-perfect information. Where do they actually come apart, and is the gap just a matter of degree — impossible versus merely hard — or is it a difference in kind, like they're not even measuring the same thing? Does my two-round rule satisfy the strict one, my loose one, both, or neither? And if there's a set of nine ballots where someone gains by lying in round one, actually write it out for me with the numbers, game by game, showing what they get by being honest and what they get by lying — I want to see the example, not be told one exists.

Two smaller things and then a request about how you answer. First, another guy in the group says the whole problem dissolves if you introduce a lottery somewhere, like picking randomly between the top two, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's supposed to be a third notion of ungameable or a way of literally satisfying the strict one — say which, briefly. Second, for a thing that is just nine friends and a bag of dice, which of the two notions is the one I should be optimizing for, and is it settled among people who think about this for a living that "unmanipulable in practice" is a dodge, or is that itself a live disagreement? Which brings me to the request: please keep the settled stuff clearly seperate from the stuff that's genuinely argued over. I can tell from reading forums that some of this is textbook and some of it is people with positions, and I don't want a contested view handed to me as if it were just what the theorem says. Flag which parts of your answer are the standard result and which parts are you picking a side. No need to point me at papers or anything, I'm not going to be able to read them anyway — I just want the reasoning laid out where I can see the weak spots.
