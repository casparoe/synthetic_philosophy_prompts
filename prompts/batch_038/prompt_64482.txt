I've been circling this fight for years via rationalist-adjacent summaries and never actually pinned it down, so help me pin it down. Compare the etiological / proper-function account of biological function — the Millikan-style line where a trait's function is whatever it was historically selected for — with the causal-role or dispositional account, the Cummins-style line where a trait's function is just its contribution to the capacities of whatever bigger system it sits inside. Skip the textbook intro. I want the actual disagreement: the cases where the two give different verdicts, and which one comes out right more often on those cases.

Here's the test case I care about, and please treat it as the main event rather then a side quest. A synthetic-biology group builds a free-living cell top-down: every component's job is written into the design spec, but there's zero selection history, no ancestors, nothing that ever replicated before. Then the lab propagates it for ten thousand generations under a stable regime, and one of the designed parts — say a chaperone-ish protein — slowly gets co-opted into doing something nothing like its spec, and that new job is what's actually keeping the lineage alive. For each of the two theories tell me (a) whether the cell has any functions the moment it comes off the bioprinter, and if so where they come from given that no selection touched it; (b) what that protein's function is at generation ten thousand; (c) whether there's a specific moment where the answer flips, and if so what makes that moment special rather than arbitrary. Bonus points if you're willing to say the theories give different answers to (a) than most people assume.

Second thing. I keep wanting to reach for a hybrid, so tell me whether it's worth having or whether I should drop it. Call it the load-bearing account: a trait's function is the causal role whose removal would cost the organism the most fitness in the environment it actually lives in right now, with selection history demoted to a prior or a tiebreaker for when the costs come out close. Is that a genuine third position or just causal-role with decoration on it? And does it dissolve the duplication problem and the novelty problem for etiological accounts, or does it only relocate them and then pretend the relocation was progress?

Last bit, which is why I got here in the first place. People on my side of the fence say things like: a drive's function is what selection shaped it to do, not what it happens to be doing in a modern environment, and they use that to argue about mismatch and about what our values are really for. Does the etiological account actually license that inference, or is it smuggling in a claim about current utility that the theory doesn't own? And does the causal-role camp have any respectable comeback, something better than just insisting that functions are interest-relative?

Bottom line in the very first sentence — which account you'd bet on, and the single reason, in one line — then the reasoning after it. No preamble, don't restate my question back at me, no bibliography. American English, please.
