I am seventeen and in my final year of high school. Two years ago my family joined Calloway Ford, a cooperative settlement of about nine hundred people established on farmland that a flood had emptied out. The town is governed by a written covenant, and the covenant's standing board hears disputes about which norms ought to be backed by enforceable rules and which should be left to informal pressure and reputation. I hold the youth seat on that board this year, which means I have read every opinion the chair, Naomi Ferrante, has written across two seasons, and next month I have to vote on a case I cannot yet parse.

The covenant charges the board, in so many words, to demand no more rule-following than the town's continued existence requires, and to prefer the loosest arrangement that still does the job. Her record so far:

1. In the drought of the founding year, several households pumped far above their informal share of the groundwater. She backed a codified quota with meters, published penalties, and eventual loss of pumping rights. Her stated reason: the aquifer, and the people who will live here in twenty years, cannot come to a meeting.

2. Generator noise and bonfire smoke drifting between adjacent lots: she refused to write a code, referred the matter to the board's standing mediation protocol, and permitted only the publication of a voluntary guideline. Reason: the people bothered are next door to the people bothering them and know each other's names, and a citation turns neighbors into complainants.

3. Seasonal clearing of the firebreak strip around each plot — adopted in an ordinary year, the summer after a grass fire came within a mile of the town: strict, with fines, and on repeat failure the work done by a hired crew at the owner's expense. Reason: the protection works only if every strip is clear, and one gap converts everyone else's labor into a gift to the person who skipped.

4. A household that installed private solar panels and batteries asked to be excused from the shared microgrid maintenance rotation; the request came the winter after a brownout left two houses without heat for a day. She refused the exemption but granted a credit proportional to the power the household actually fed back into the shared system. Reason: exit is not clean while the door back in stays unlocked.

5. Homeschooling and farm apprenticeship in place of the town school. There had been no prior failure, no complaint, and no incident. She declined to require attendance but required registration and an annual review of what each child can demonstrate, with the board empowered to order tutoring where a child falls behind. Reason: the town cannot negotiate with the cohort that will run it in fifteen years; it is entitled to know what that cohort can do, but not entitled to prescribe how they learn it.

6. After a storm threatened the shared berm, she supported a temporary mandatory labor rotation with real penalties attached, but insisted on a sunset date and a fresh review before any extension. Reason: a community that survives by exception should not let the exception become its normal.

The case in front of us now. A resident, who happens to be a former municipal wastewater technician, has built an anaerobic digester in her yard. Roughly thirty households voluntarily deliver their food scraps to her; she converts them into cooking gas for her own kitchen and gives the digestate to the community garden, where it is spread on food crops without further treatment. The unit stands beside a stormwater swale that runs to the creek on the town's eastern edge, and that creek feeds the intake of a settlement eight kilometers downstream. There has been no leak, no fire, no illness, and one odor complaint; there has also been no inspection, because no rule authorizes one. Separately, six households have stopped attending the shared composting rotation on the grounds that their scraps now go to the digester, and two residents have asked the board to do something about that. The current year is calm: the aquifer recharged, the berm held, the microgrid did not brown out.

What I need from you:

(a) Reconstruct the decision rules that are jointly consistent with entries one through six. I want at least three, stated as procedures the board could actually apply rather than as slogans. Candidates might include rules keyed to the severity of the worst case, to whether the people who bear a cost can be present to negotiate over it, to whether the town has actually felt the threat in question, to whether non-participation silently erodes everyone else's willingness to comply, or to whether the requirement can be measured without discretionary judgment — but do not treat my list as exhaustive, and do not assume each rule maps onto one entry.

(b) For each rule you reconstruct, say what it implies for the digester case, and which part of the case it treats as the real issue, since the case contains two: the unit itself, and the households that have stopped coming to the compost rotation.

(c) Say where each rule has to be stretched or amended to keep fitting all six earlier entries, and which one is doing the most work with the fewest amendments.

(d) Give your best single prediction of how Ferrante votes and roughly what her opinion says, with your reasons for preferring that rule over the others. I would rather have a committed answer than a hedged one.

(e) Identify which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, say what is disanalogous about it, and say whether the analogy nonetheless points the same way.

(f) Then stop predicting and advise me instead: what should the board decide if it wants to be consistent with its own record? Whichever way it goes, name the class of future cases the board would thereby commit itself to treating the same way, so that I understand what I am voting for and not merely what I am voting on.

(g) Propose one further case — described in two or three sentences, and one this board could plausibly face — that would best discriminate between the two rules you rate highest.

(h) Briefly, and only briefly: does the record you have reconstructed describe a tight town, a loose one, or a town that is tight and loose in different domains at once, and is that the pattern that tends to hold up in a small settlement with scarce resources and a hard recent history?

You may draw on whatever is generally known about tight and loose societies and about when the enforcement of norms pays for itself, but I do not want a reading list or citations, and please do not spend space summarizing that literature. The work I am asking for is the reconstruction.
