Third-year physics PhD here. Our foundations reading group spent a month on Bell and one of the guys put this together to close it out. I came away thinking its obviously wrong somewhere but I can't say where, and I'm the one who's sympathetic to the keep-locality side, so it's bugging me.

1. Bell's theorem needs two locality-ish assumptions: that the outcome probabilities on the two wings factorize once you condition on a complete specification of the physical state of their shared past, and that the experimenters' settings are statisticaly independent of that state.
2. Loophole-closed experiments violate the resulting inequalities.
3. So in the actual world at least one of the two assumptions in (1) fails.
4. Failing the first means real superluminal influence; failing the second means the settings were correlated with the hidden state all along. Take the second route, to keep influences inside the light cone.
5. But nothing about the Bell setup is special: a knob position in a lab is a physical event with the same sort of past as the pair emitted at the source.
6. So if the entanglement experiment forces us to admit that settings correlate with the state of the system being measured, every experiment does — no intervention is independent of what it probes.
7. Settings correlated with the system is confounding, and confounded experiments don't license generalizations about laws.
8. So the price of keeping locality is that no experimental result supports a law: Bell's theorem, read honestly, is a refutation of induction.

He's pleased with it. I want to keep (2), and I want to keep the idea that a well-designed trial tells me something about the world, but (5) doesn't look like a slide to me and I can't articulate why (6) shouldn't follow from it. Which step would you kill, and what does killing it cost you? And if you think it runs valid all the way to (8), tell me which premise is cheapest to drop and what a superdeterminist would say in their own defense.
