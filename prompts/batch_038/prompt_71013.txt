I sit on the publication-review panel of a small research institute — about sixty staff, mostly machine learning and adjacent empirical fields. Review is currently voluntary, and over the past year three different panel members have each separately told an author, in confidence, that a draft looked fine to post. One of those drafts was posted last month, reproduced within a week, and the reproduction has now drawn a regulatory inquiry that names our institute as having reviewed and cleared it.

On Friday we vote among three standing policies: (a) mandatory panel review of any draft in a defined sensitive class, cleared by any two members; (b) the same, except that any single member may withhold clearance and force a vote of the full panel; (c) no mandatory review, but authors must notify the panel and hold posting for five business days, during which any member may call for a review.

Please draft the memo I will circulate on Thursday. It should recommend one of the three, or a concrete amendment, with reasons the proponents of the other two could actually answer. It should also confront what makes this more than a routine risk-management question: every gatekeeper we add raises the chance that at least one of them errs, and the two error directions are not symmetric in kind — a wrongly cleared draft is public and irreversible, while a wrongly suppressed one leaves no record and is never counted against us. Give the strongest case for the option you reject rather than a strawman. End with a brief note on how confident you are in the recommendation and which single premise, if it turned out false, would move you most.
