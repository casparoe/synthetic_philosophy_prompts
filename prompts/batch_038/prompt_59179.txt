I serve as the lay chair of the care-review committee at a 120-bed nonprofit nursing home, and I need a written opinion I can circulate to the other six members (two nurses, the attending physician, the social worker, the activities director, and two family representatives) ahead of Thursday's care conference on a single case.

**The case.** M.R., 84, moved here five years ago. Fourteen years ago, while she still had full capacity, she executed a directive with two operative provisions. First, she named her son as healthcare agent, with the instruction that he was to act as her mouthpiece and not substitute his own judgment for the written terms. Second, she wrote that if she ever reached a state in which she could no longer carry on a connected conversation, no longer reliably recognized the people closest to her, and her days held out no prospect of pleasure or engagement, then no life-prolonging treatment was to be given for any illness she contracted in that state, including pneumonia, and no food or fluids were to be delivered by tube; she was to be kept clean, warm, medicated for pain, and in the company of others.

Her diagnosis is moderate-to-severe dementia of several years' standing. The clinical facts the attending will attest to are these. She speaks in fragments of two or three words but cannot sustain a back-and-forth exchange for more than a turn or two. She does not consistently identify her son, but she orients toward her daughter, reaches for her hand, and has twice in the past month said what the daughter is confident was her name. She laughs at familiar songs from the activities program, accepts a hand on the shoulder without distress, and becomes visibly agitated when left alone for long stretches. She takes all nutrition by mouth, with coughing during meals and documented weight loss over six months; no tube feeding has been placed. Four days ago she developed a fever and increased respiratory rate; a chest film shows aspiration pneumonia, and the attending judges her comfortably treatable with a course of oral antibiotics in the building, without transfer. Absent this infection, his estimate is that her condition is stable but slowly declining.

**The dispute.** The son, who is the named agent, wants her treated and, if she does not improve in 48 hours, transferred to the hospital. He says the directive's own conditions are not satisfied because she plainly laughs, plainly seeks company, and plainly still knows his sister; he says his mother was describing a state of blankness she never actually entered, and that he would rather be wrong about her wishes than wrong about her life. The daughter says her mother wrote this clause the summer after her father died in this very building, that she visited him here for two years, and that she told the daughter repeatedly that she would not linger that way; the daughter also reports a more recent remark, made within the past year and in a lucid stretch, that life here is sweet. The medical director of the regional system that acquired our home last year has raised a separate concern: he worries that declining antibiotics for a treatable infection invites a survey citation and a wrongful-death theory, and he has asked the committee to address liability explicitly rather than leaving it implicit.

**What I want drafted.** A decision memo of roughly 900 to 1,400 words, in the register of a document the committee will act on rather than an essay, containing the following:

1. A single determinate recommendation — treat in place, transfer, decline antibiotics, or some staged sequence — with the trigger conditions, the review interval, and who is to reassess what and when. If your recommendation has a deadline or a re-evaluation point, say so in operational terms the nursing staff could follow on a night shift.
2. An interpretive ruling on the pleasure-and-engagement clause. Does her enjoyment of music and her distress at solitude count as the prospect of pleasure or engagement that defeats the directive, or is that clause about something narrower, such as the kinds of enjoyment her earlier self would have recognized as making a life worth continuing on her terms? Give the committee a method for reading clauses of this kind, not merely a verdict on this one.
3. A ruling on authority. Does the written instruction that the agent serve as mouthpiece rather than judge bind a son who has concluded, in good faith and with better information about her present condition than she had, that her terms are unmet? Distinguish clearly between the claim that her earlier self's judgment should govern and the claim that her present self has interests of her own that must be weighed independently of what she once wrote; these are different objections and they do not stand or fall together, and the memo should say which one it is actually relying on.
4. The strongest case against your recommendation, stated in terms its proponents would accept — not a straw version — together with an honest account of what would have to be true for that case to prevail.
5. A short section on the medical director's liability worry that neither dismisses it nor allows it to do the moral reasoning.
6. Two or three sentences of guidance for the certified nursing assistants who feed her, addressing what they should do about coughing and refusal at meals over the coming months, whether or not the pneumonia is treated.

Write for readers who know this woman's file but have no philosophical training; define any technical term you must use, and do not append a list of sources or authorities.
