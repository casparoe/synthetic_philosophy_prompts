I am a retired hardware-store owner with no degrees in any of this, and for eleven years I have served as a volunteer trustee of the Kettle River Museum of Natural History, a small county museum with two paid staff. I chair its Exhibits Committee. We meet on the second Tuesday of next month, and the committee has to settle one question then, because the painters are booked for the spring and the wall cannot be opened up twice.

The question concerns the Deep Time Wall: a forty-foot painted timeline in our entrance hall, installed in 1998, running from the formation of the Earth to a final brass marker reading Holocene, the last 11,700 years, and then "you are here." The renovation will have it repainted, and a bequest is restricted to that wall specifically. Three things now have to be decided at once, and everyone on the committee keeps sliding between them: whether the wall should continue past the Holocene into something labelled the Anthropocene at all; if so, what date the boundary tick should carry; and whether the wall should carry any note about the standing of that word.

On the standing, here is what I understand, though I cannot check sources and am not asking you for any. My reading is that the working group of geologists who spent years on the proposal eventually pointed to the middle of the twentieth century, with fallout in the sediments of a lake in Ontario as the reference marker; that in 2024 the stratigraphic bodies voted the formal proposal down, largely because the record is too thin and the start date too recent; and that the same bodies nonetheless said the word would go on being used by scientists and by the public as a description of human impact. Competing start dates I have seen argued for include the spread of farming several thousand years ago, the coal and steam era around 1800, and the aftermath of European contact in the Americas around 1600, on the grounds that the collapse of indigenous populations changed the air itself. One of our trustees, a retired schoolteacher, wants that last one, and wants the wall to say so plainly.

Two complications are ours rather than the world's, and I think they matter more than people on the committee admit. First, our bylaws contain a clause I helped write in 2003 without any idea it would be tested: no unit of geological time may be named on the Deep Time Wall unless it appears in the current published geologic time scale. Read literally, that clause now forbids the word altogether. Second, the wall is to scale. At our scale, seventy-five years is narrower than the painter's smallest brush, so any mark we make in that last stretch is not a representation of a duration but a rhetorical gesture, and there is no version of the wall on which the Anthropocene is visible as a period.

My own idea, which nobody else on the committee proposed and which I want examined rather than flattered, is that we should date the boundary locally. In 1919 the last of the old-growth timber in this valley was cut, the mill began running, and the river silted for a generation; you can still see the change in the cut banks and in the county historical society's account of the lake beds. A visitor here can walk out the door and stand on the boundary. My worry, which I have not solved, is that if the boundary can be local then there is no single boundary, and also that a date of 1919 in this county names a company and a handful of families, several of whom still live here and one of whom sits on our board.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the committee a week before the meeting. Argue from reasons, not from authority, and address it to intelligent people who have never read a stratigraphy paper. Use exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Bottom line — roughly 150 words. What we should vote on each of the three questions, and what we should do instead if the bylaw clause cannot be amended.
2. What is actually being decided — roughly 200 words, separating the questions the committee runs together and saying which of them is genuinely in our hands.
3. The bylaw — roughly 250 words. Whether the clause, given what it was written for, permits or forbids the word, and what we should do about it.
4. The date question — roughly 400 words. What each candidate boundary does to the idea that naming an epoch assigns responsibility to someone, and why the choice cannot be made on evidence alone.
5. My local proposal, assessed — roughly 300 words. The strongest version of it, then where it fails, including the problem that a local boundary may not be a boundary at all.
6. The strongest case against your recommendation — roughly 250 words, made by someone who actually believes it.
7. Wording — roughly 150 words. Draft text for the wall itself, and a one-line note on the status of the term.

If one of my ideas cannot be saved, say so directly and tell me which of the three questions I should stop raising.
