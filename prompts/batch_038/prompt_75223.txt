**Draft: "Displacement, or Why the Fink/Mask Taxonomy Does Not Carve at the Joints"**

1. The simple conditional analysis of dispositions — x is disposed to manifest M when stimulated S just in case x would M if S — is now universally rejected, and it is rejected because of two families of counterexample. Finks are cases, following Martin and Lewis, in which the stimulus removes the disposition or its basis before the manifestation could occur: the sorcerer who makes the glass non-fragile at the instant it is struck, or the electro-fink that renders a dead wire live only while it is touched. Masks are cases in which the object retains the disposition and the stimulus occurs, but an interfering condition prevents the manifestation: the glass packed in styrofoam, the soluble tablet behind an enteric coating, the irritable man who has taken a sedative. Lewis's repair was to add a clause about the object's own intrinsic character at the time of the stimulus; later repairs have weakened the conditional into a claim about what happens in a suitable proportion of the relevant cases. The standard gloss on the difference between the two families is temporal and locational: a fink is a case in which the object stops being disposed, a mask is a case in which it stays disposed and fails to manifest anyway.

2. I want to argue that there is a third family, and that its existence is more damaging than the first two. Call it displacement. In a displacement case, at the moment of the stimulus the object retains the disposition, the causal process that constitutes its manifestation runs to completion, and what results is a real change in the object — but the change lands in a different observable channel from the one that individuates the disposition ascribed. Nothing was removed, as in a fink. Nothing was blocked, as in a mask. The disposition was exercised, only not where we were looking.

3. Three cases, which I take to be of a kind:

   (a) A man who is irritable in the ordinary sense: insulted, he shows the characteristic arousal, the characteristic impulse, the characteristic attention to the slight. But he has been trained, or the circumstances make a display ruinous, and instead of snapping he flushes, becomes hyper-formal, and is short with his family that evening. Compare the sedated man, in whom no such process runs at all. The difference is not merely phenomenological: the displaced case leaves the after-effects that exercising the disposition characteristically leaves, while the masked case does not.

   (b) A brittle piezoelectric crystal, struck. In the fixture I have in mind the impact is discharged as a charge across its faces rather than as a crack. But the crystal is afterwards measurably fatigued in the way struck brittle things are fatigued, not in the way shielded ones are. The process ran; it registered elsewhere.

   (c) The canonical "mask" of the soluble tablet dropped into a solution already saturated with the same solute. On my criterion this is not a mask but a displacement: solute leaves the tablet and returns to it, and the exchange is detectable by isotopic labelling. Contrast the enteric coating, where nothing of the sort occurs. My draft therefore reclassifies a case the literature treats as a mask, and I think that is a feature rather than a bug.

4. The general criterion I propose: a fink removes the disposition; a mask arrests the manifestation-process; a displacement lets the process run but diverts its registration into a channel outside the manifestation type that was ascribed. The diagnostic is an after-effect test — ask not whether M occurred, but whether what occurred bears the signature of D's having been exercised.

5. Here is the damage. Whether a case counts as a displacement depends on the grain at which we specify M. Make M coarse enough — "the crystal undergoes a stress response" — and case (b) is not a counterexample at all, since the disposition manifests, just not observably in the expected channel. Make M fine enough — "the crystal cracks at this point at this time" — and the case becomes a fink, since that hyper-specific disposition was never there or was lost. Displacement exists only at intermediate grains, and intermediate grains are selected by what we want the ascription to do: to predict, to classify, to assign credit or blame. So the manifest taxon M is not fixed by the object alone.

6. If that is right, the fink/mask project has been misdescribed. The literature treats the sorting of counterexamples as the discovery of a metaphysical structure — kinds of way in which the conditional can fail — when in fact the sorting is an artifact of a descriptive choice that the literature has never explicitly made. Two further consequences I want to develop. First, Lewis's intrinsic-character clause does not help: displacement cases satisfy it, so the reformed analysis predicts manifestation in the ascribed channel, and repairing that requires relativizing the manifestation clause to a channel, which requires fixing the grain, which is precisely the choice that was supposed to be given. Second, the proportion-based analyses face a counting problem rather than a mere vagueness problem: whether a displaced manifestation counts toward the proportion for D has no interest-independent answer, and if it does count, the analysis stops discriminating between the disposed and the merely similar.

7. I end with a conjecture rather than an argument: the long-running dispute over whether a fink can be intrinsic may have stalled for the same reason. What counts as a change in the object rather than a change in its circumstances is fixed by the same descriptive choice that fixes M, so the question is not well posed until the grain is.

Some things I already know are weak: I have not said what fixes the grain in a non-question-begging way, and my after-effect test may simply be a restatement of "the disposition was exercised," which would make it useless as an independent criterion.
