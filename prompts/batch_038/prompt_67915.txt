hi — I'm writing a chapter on comparative and artificial minds and I want a consistency check on my working position before I circulate a draft, because these views have accreted over a couple of years and I'm fairly sure some of them can't all stand together. The backbone is the Darwinian/Skinnerian/Popperian/Gregorian hierarchy of kinds of minds.

Here's what I currently hold:

1. The hierarchy is cumulative and nested in the strict sense: a Popperian creature is a Skinnerian one that has gained something, and going up never costs you a mechanism. So nothing can sit at stage 4 without sitting at stage 3.
2. What fixes a stage is the source of the design information — genes; an individual's own history of reinforcement; internally generated trials that get eliminated before the body does; and the cultural inheritance of external, mind-making tools — not how much computation is happening or how impressive the animal looks doing it.
3. So 'higher' must not be read as 'better at solving problems', and I deny that stage membership tracks behavioural flexibility in any simple way.
4. I set a high evidential bar for stage 3: detour tasks, apparent insight, tool use in corvids and the like have to be shown not to be explicable by a Skinnerian history before I'll credit internal simulation.
5. A creature counts as Popperian only if a merely simulated failure can actually cost it something — only if it can be harmed by a hypothesis dying in its head instead of in the world.
6. On my view octopuses get to stage 3 but are permanently locked out of stage 4, becuase they're short-lived, essentially semelparous and don't overlap with their own offspring, so there's no vehicle for the intergenerational transmission the Gregorian step needs. I want to use this as evidence that the fourth step depends on life history and not only on cognition.
7. Large language models are Gregorian in the sense that matters — they are assembled out of, and operate entirely upon, the external artefacts of a Gregorian species — but they are not Popperian. Training hands them the sediment of other creatures' discarded hypotheses rather than a simulation loop of their own that they can be harmed by.
8. New Caledonian crows I'd call Popperian, though I also think a lot of their hook-making is developmentally canalised and merely fine-tuned by watching older birds.
9. Stage membership is graded, not a switch flipped: I'm perfectly happy asking how Popperian something is.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? For every real tension tell me how bad it is, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list — and rank them by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. Don't just reach for (7) as the thing to drop; if the LLM verdict is what has to go, say which of the others forces it and whether some re-description of what Gregorian-hood consists in would let me keep the verdict while keeping the nesting in (1). Flag the merely apparent problems too and say why they dissolve, since I don't want to 'fix' those. And separately: can my own principles in (2), (4) and (5) actually deliver the verdict I want in (8)? I suspect not, and I'd like to be told exactly where they run out.
