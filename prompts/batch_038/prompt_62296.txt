I am a fourth-year doctoral student in economic history, and this fall I have been handed a single seventy-five-minute session in our department's first-year methods sequence — a slot that used to be filled by a now-retired colleague's lecture on causes, conditions, and conjunctures. The cohort is roughly twenty students, almost all incoming economics and political science admits who have completed a year of causal inference at the level of potential outcomes and instrumental variables, and who have essentially no philosophy of causation and no historical training. There is a whiteboard, no projector, and I have been told explicitly that the aim is not to turn them into skeptics about quantification but to give them a grip on one idea: what a historian is actually doing when she says an outcome was overdetermined, and why that is not the trivial claim that many things contributed. I do not want the idea explained to me; I want a plan for explaining it, organized around the dependency structure — which pieces must be in place before which others can do any work, and why. My provisional ordering runs like this: begin with the single-cause contrast they already trust, where the counterfactual "absent the treatment, not the outcome" is well-defined and estimable; then the firing-squad structure in which two independently sufficient causes are jointly present, so that the counterfactual comes out false for each even though each intuitively caused the death; then the mirror-image structure in which every factor in a set is necessary and none sufficient, so that no factor survives the counterfactual test in the other direction either; and only then the question of what historians are up to when they press both structures onto the same event at once. I do not trust this ordering and would like you to argue with it — say where it is wrong, what actually depends on what, and how I keep the two structures visibly distinct on a whiteboard over seventy-five minutes, since they collapse into one another constantly.

Second, I need one carrying case and I want you to be decisive about it. I had been assuming the July Crisis of 1914, but I suspect it fails precisely because it is narratively seductive: students leave believing the lesson was that the war was inevitable, which is a different and worse claim, and the telegrams swamp the causal architecture I am trying to isolate. Tell me what the case must have and must lack for this job — how much background it can afford to consume before it eats the session, whether the counterfactual in it should be cleanly specifiable or genuinely hard to specify, whether it should be political or economic given who is in the room, and whether it should be a case where professional historians still disagree about the structure or one where they do not — and then name the case you would actually use.

Third, the misconception map, which I think is where this session lives or dies. Give me both the errors they arrive with and the ones my own sequence will manufacture, each tagged with the point at which it must be headed off rather than merely corrected later. My candidate list: that overdetermined means complicated or multicausal; that if an outcome was overdetermined, inference about any single cause is impossible and historians are therefore licensed to ignore it; that overdetermination is a property of the world rather than a consequence of which contrast one asks about; that an enumerated list of contributing factors is an explanation; that the difference between a cause and a mere condition is discovered rather than chosen; and — the one I dread, because it will come from the economists — that all of this is the omitted-variable problem in a bad mood, dissolved by a sufficiently rich specification. Some of these I suspect should be absorbed rather than refuted; tell me which, and how. Close with one question I can put to them in the final five minutes whose answers would separate having grasped the idea from having enjoyed a good story, and a short list of what I should deliberately leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving out — I am thinking of whatever machinery would take thirty minutes to earn and buy nothing here, and of the quarrels over whether multi-causal explanation is a substantive position or a way of evading causal selection. Please do not send me readings; the syllabus is fixed and outside my control. I want the plan, and I want you willing to tell me that my ordering or my case is simply wrong.
