so i've been reading about climate policy on and off for a couple of years — newsletters, a few books, way too many comment threads — and i've finally written something down for the community paper in our town. it's about 550 words, pasted below. before i send it in i want a reality check, because i know exactly enough to be confident and i'm pretty sure that's the dangerous amount.

context: everyone reading this already accepts the climate is changing, so i'm not arguing for that. the split here is between the people who want the city to ban things (gas hookups in new buildings, leaf blowers, parking minimums) and the people who say put a price on carbon and get out of the way. i'm in the second camp and i suspect i'm in it too hard.

here's the draft:

Every climate fight in this town is really the same argument wearing different clothes: do we tell people what to do, or do we make the wrong thing cost more?

A carbon tax is a thermostat. You set the number once and the house takes care of itself. A regulation is someone walking room to room telling everybody to put on a sweater — it might work, but it takes a lot of staff and it never quite covers the whole house.

Rules have to guess at the cheapest way to cut emissions and then force everyone down that path. A price doesn't have to guess. Tax carbon, let it rise every year, let it flow through to gas, electricity, airfare, cement, beef, and every household and business quietly rearranges itself around the new number. The steel mill that can cut emissions for less than the tax cuts them. The one that can't pays. Nobody at city hall has to know which is which.

The level is the whole game. If the tax equals the actual damage a ton of carbon does, the market stops lying to us — things only looked cheap because somebody else was paying for the smoke. Get the number roughly right and a thousand seperate decisions no planner could ever coordinate start pointing the same direction. Get it badly wrong and you've just added a surcharge to the power bill.

Prices are also more honest than rules. Every rule creates a hidden subsidy for whoever lobbied hard enough to be exempt from it, and the exemptions always show up eventually. A tax sits out in the open where you can see what it costs and argue about it.

And it can pay for itself politically. Send most of the revenue straight back as an equal check to every household, use some of it to cut payroll taxes, and a lot of working families come out ahead while still facing the higher price at the pump — the higher price is the part that actually changes behaviour. A regulation gives you the higher price and no check.

One concession to the other side: efficiency doesn't always deliver. When something gets cheaper to use we use more of it, which has a name (Jevons paradox) and a long history. But that's an argument for keeping the price rising, not for going back to writing rules.

Where I stop listening is the claim that pricing is a delay tactic and that what we really need is to stop growing. Shrinking an economy is what a recession is, and a recession is the one climate policy guarenteed to be regressive, because the people who lose their jobs first are the ones with the least cushion. Britain has grown its economy by something like three quarters since 1990 while cutting its emissions by nearly half, which suggests growth and emissions come apart if you work at it. And if the answer is that we only managed that by shipping the dirty part of what we buy to other countries, then the honest version of that argument is about trade, not about growth.

Price the damage, send the money back, let people decide how to live. That's a policy you can pass and a policy you can measure.

[end of draft]
