At a reading group last week I pressed ring species, and extensively hybridising plant populations, against a lineage-based account of the species category, and the reply I received was simply that species are individuals, not classes, so that my objection presupposes precisely what the individuality thesis denies. I cannot yet tell whether that is a genuine response or a conversational trump card. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct and precise theses that slogan might be carrying: an ontological claim that species taxa are concrete, spatiotemporally located particulars with parts rather than members; a claim that their properties are wholly relational rather than intrinsic, so that they admit of description but not definition; a semantic claim that species names behave like proper names, their reference fixed at a type specimen and passed down a causal chain; a mereological claim that 'belongs to' should be read as 'is part of'; and, behind all of these, some determinate sense of 'individual' itself — an integrated, cohesive whole of the sort a biological species concept demands, or merely anything with a beginning and an end in time. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something I ought to resist; indicate where the readings come apart, since the weakest seems compatible with almost any species concept while the strongest plainly is not; and, most importantly, say whether any of them actually disposes of an objection about indeterminate boundaries and hybridisation, or whether parthood turns out to be every bit as vulnerable to vagueness as membership was. Finally, how much of the slogan can a pluralist about species concepts retain, if the same stretch of lineage may be carved up differently by different concepts — which individual, then, is the species?
