Working on a short essay for a workshop and I want a real read, not encouragement. Rough draft:

Most attacks on Arendt's Eichmann book go one of two ways. Either Eichmann wasn't banal at all — the Argentina transcripts that Stangneth worked through show a boastful, convinced, ideologically driven antisemite, not a vacant functionary — or banality is a dangerous idea because it turns atrocity into a cognitive accident and quietly lets the perpetrator off. I want to argue both attacks assume something Arendt didnt claim: that banality is a portrait. Call that the portrait reading, the thesis as a description of what Eichmann was like on the inside.

The alternative I'm pushing is that banality is a claim about explanation rather than psychology. On this reading her discovery in Jerusalem was that the deeds and the available descriptions of the man refuse to line up the way we expect. Malice, sadism, fanaticism, weak character, careerism, devotion to a cause — these are the explanations we reach for, and each comes up short by roughly the same enormous margin no matter how much evidence you feed it. Banality names the shortfall, not a personality type. The cliché-saturated speech isn't a symptom she's diagnosing; it's data confirming there's nothing in there to explain with.

That buys me two things. It makes the archival objection misfire, since showing Eichmann had convictions just adds one more candidate to the pile that fails. And it moves the moral work somewhere more interesting: the scandal isn't that ordinary people can do this, it's that we habitually hand out what I'm calling depth credit — treating sincere conviction as proof that an agent had a self worth holding to account, and treating its absence as a kind of mitigation.

The comments I actually want:

1. Does any of this survive her explicit material on thinking — the two-in-one, the silent partner, the claim that the inability to think is what left him unable to tell right from wrong? That stuff reads like it's about psychology, which would undercut me, and I don't know how to absorb it.
2. Be straight with me about whether I've built an unfalsifiable theory. If every piece of counter-evidence just "adds another failing explanation," the thing is rigged, and I need to be able to say what would defeat it — or admit I can't.
3. Point out where the prose is inflating a modest observation into a Grand Claim.

Numbered and blunt, no need to be nice about it.
