I chair the program review panel of a mid-sized private foundation whose board was deliberately constituted, forty years ago, out of two blocs with genuinely incompatible moral outlooks: a faith-based bloc for whom certain acts are wrong in themselves regardless of consequences and who treat the foundation's endowment as held in trust for a particular community of beneficiaries, and a secular bloc that reasons almost entirely about aggregate outcomes and is willing to fund anything that demonstrably reduces suffering. Every allocation decision needs a two-thirds vote, so neither bloc can govern alone and both have learned to negotiate. Last night I argued for two hours with a friend who teaches philosophy, and he was not impressed by the way I described my job. His line, roughly, was this: the whole talk of gains from trade between value systems quietly assumes there is some common currency in which each side's stakes can be measured and compared, and none of these outlooks grants that — the faith bloc denies that its prohibitions have a price at all, and the secular bloc denies that anything but outcomes can be traded — so what I call a compromise is really either one side's capitulation with a receipt attached, or the mechanical reproduction of whichever side has the stronger fallback position, since the disagreement point that any such bargain is measured against is fixed by the standing rules and the endowment, which is to say by power rather than by morality. I want to know whether he is right, and if he is partly right, whether a usable procedure can still be built inside the part he is wrong about.

So I am asking for a decision procedure my panel can actually run on a contested item, not an essay about whether moral compromise is coherent. The constraints are these: we meet quarterly and each meeting carries eight to fourteen contested allocations; the panelists are practitioners — a hospital administrator, two program directors, a retired judge — none of whom has any training in ethics and none of whom will read anything longer than two pages; I have about forty minutes per item, and I chair and must produce a recommendation that survives a twenty-one-member board vote. The procedure needs to state the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a flat no, and where it deliberately hands the question to a human being because the issue is one of value rather than fact. It has to handle the hard cases explicitly rather than wave at them: how to classify what is actually on the table, since a genuine trade looks identical from the outside to a truce, to a split-the-difference fudge, and to a surrender; whether trades may cross domains (a bloc abstaining on program A in exchange for money flowing to unrelated program B) or must stay within one domain, and what follows from each choice; how to treat claims about the intensity of a moral stake when intensity is unverifiable and obviously worth overstating; whether randomizing between two disputed policies — funding one this year and the other next, or drawing lots — is ever available, given that it presupposes the parties accept reasoning in expected terms and one of them may not; what happens when a party refuses to state its position in tradeable form at all; whether we should keep a running ledger of past concessions across items and years, and whether keeping such a ledger corrupts the thing it is meant to record; and what standing, if any, belongs to the people affected by a decision who are not at the table and cannot consent, including a bloc that claims to speak on their behalf. Please also be honest about which of your steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing.

Then run it on three items from this year's docket. First, a family-planning program that the secular bloc values highly on outcome grounds and that the faith bloc opposes as complicity in something it holds intrinsically wrong, with a palliative-care program on the same docket that the faith bloc cares about intensely and the secular bloc rates as merely moderately cost-effective — an obvious candidate for a trade, and I want to see the procedure either construct that trade or explain why it cannot. Second, an item where both blocs mildly favour the program but each has told me privately that its support is conditional on the other bloc dropping an unrelated proposal; tell me whether the procedure should let that condition count, and what it does to the rest of the docket if it does. Third, a proposal I personally want to make: funding two mutually exclusive interventions in alternate years by lot, so that each bloc gets its way half the time and neither has to concede anything in principle. I suspect the procedure should reject this and I would rather it told me so plainly and said why than accommodate me. Finally, close with a residue report: which parts of the gains-from-trade idea you had to discard to make the thing runnable, which distinctions you insisted on preserving, and a short statement of what this procedure cannot do — the class of disputes it will systematically mishandle or fail to resolve, so that I know when to stop chairing and simply let an item die.
