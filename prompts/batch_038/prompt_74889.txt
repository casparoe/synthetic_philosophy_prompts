I have no training in philosophy or art history. These are views I've worked out for myself over many years of galleries and arguments with friends, and I want to know whether they can all be held at once.

1. If a fake is genuinely indistinguishable from the original — no expert, no test, no amount of looking could tell them apart — then it is every bit as good a work of art. Once nothing could be perceived differently, there is nothing left for it to be worse at.

2. What makes a work great is what the person who made it did: the problem they set themselves, the thing they saw that nobody had seen, the break they made with what came before. Greatness is a fact about the making, not only about the looking.

3. Even so, I would far rather stand in front of the real one, and that preference would be exactly the same if provenance were never published and nobody could use it to impress anybody.

4. When the Van Meegeren Vermeers were exposed, critics who had been lavish in their praise began saying the pictures looked laboured, empty, faintly vulgar. I think they were fooling themselves. The paintings hadn't changed; only their story had.

5. Someone who sets out to make a convincing fake purely to prove it can be done, and labels it as such, has done nothing wrong, and what they make can be great art in its own right.

Tell me which of these genuinely conflict and which only appear to, and what the smallest sacrifice would be that leaves the rest intact. Rank the conflicts by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each say what giving up that commitment would cost me. I don't know where the trouble is, so please don't hedge by declaring everything in tension with everything else.
