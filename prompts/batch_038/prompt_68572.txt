I am drafting a paper on natural kinds and, wanting to avoid the usual run of chemistry-and-taxonomy examples, I have assembled a case base spread across several sciences and recorded my own verdict on each item. I want to know what principle, if any, these verdicts add up to, and what I have to give up to get one.

The verdicts are as follows.

Jade is not one kind: jadeite and nephrite share a name, an appearance, and a cultural history, and almost nothing else that supports inference. Gold is one kind, although no sample of it is pure and although iron pyrites shares the appearance that got both named. Water is one kind, and heavy water is water. Diamond and graphite are two kinds, not one. The two mirror-image forms of carvone are two kinds, smelling of spearmint and caraway respectively. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are two kinds. I can see that these last five do not sit well together — if isotopic substitution divides kinds in carbon, why not in water, and if mirror-image structure divides kinds in carvone, why not in a molecule whose isotopes are shuffled? — and I do not know which of them to abandon.

Fish is not a kind and birds are, though to a non-specialist the two cases look alike: birds form a clade, fish do not. Reptiles are not a kind unless birds are reptiles. Tree is not a kind at all, being a growth form arrived at repeatedly from unrelated starting points. Weed is not a kind.

C4 photosynthesis is a kind, even though it has arisen independently many times across grasses, sedges, and several eudicot lineages, so that a C4 plant is no closer to another C4 plant than to its own C3 relatives. Ectomycorrhizal fungi are a kind on the same footing. So is the TIM barrel as a protein fold. So are prion strains, which differ in nothing but conformation, involve no difference in sequence, and yet breed true on passage and give distinct disease phenotypes. I am therefore committed to the view that a category cutting across every genealogy can be a natural kind — which makes my rejection of fish and tree look like special pleading.

A bacterial operational taxonomic unit defined by a 97% sequence-similarity threshold is not a kind, whatever else is true of microbial population structure. "Super-Earth" is not a kind: it picks out a radius range, and objects in that range turn out to be rocky, oceanic, or enveloped in hydrogen and helium, with no shared mechanism behind the shared size. "Gas giant" is a kind.

"Vitamin C" is a kind, although the category is relational — ascorbate is a vitamin for me and not for a dog — and so is "essential amino acid." "Keystone species," "bioindicator," and "invasive species" are not kinds, and they are relational in what seems to me a very similar way. This is the second place where I cannot state my own criterion.

Low-density and high-density polyethylene are two kinds; polyethylene is not a kind but a repeat unit. Steel is not a kind; a specified stainless grade is. Manufactured categories are not disqualified as such.

Diabetes mellitus and diabetes insipidus were for a long time a single category united by thirst and copious urine; they are two kinds with nothing mechanistically in common, and the surviving shared name is a fossil of the old symptom-based grouping. Schizophrenia is not one kind. Alzheimer's disease is a kind, provisionally, despite being fixed by criteria rather than mechanism and despite the people who meet the criteria without the pathology and those with the pathology who never meet them. A ring complex of populations, each interbreeding with its neighbours, whose two ends overlap and do not interbreed, is one kind. The solitary and gregarious phases of a locust are two kinds, which I expect is wrong but cannot bring myself to retract.

On that basis, please do the following. State the principle — or the small number of competing candidates — precisely enough that a new case can be run through it, and say for each clause which of my cases forced it in. Then say which of my verdicts the best candidate cannot deliver, and rank the casualties: which one I should retract first, and what I lose by retracting it. Next, give me its verdicts on four cases I have not listed: a material defined by a size range, on the model of "super-Earth," but whose size-dependent behaviour is genuinely shared across unrelated chemistries; a lichen, a stable and reproducible body assembled from partners drawn from distant lineages; glass, defined by the absence of crystalline order; and the current astronomical definition of "planet," which turns on dynamical dominance rather than composition or origin. Finally, tell me honestly whether the principle makes kindhood a matter of degree and interest-relative, and if it does, what work the word "natural" is still doing — or whether my case base shows only that scientists maintain many useful classificatory schemes of different kinds, none of them privileged.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle you think my verdicts converge on, or your verdict that they do not converge — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of the question.
