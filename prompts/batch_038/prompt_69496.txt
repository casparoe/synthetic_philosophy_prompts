ok so I've been going around in circles on the covering-law versus narrative fight in the philosophy of history and I want a diagnosis, not a survey.

the setup as I understand it: Hempel says a genuine historical explanation is a sketch of a nomological derivation — you're leaning on some general law, you just haven't spelled it out, and if you couldn't spell it out in principle you haven't explained anything, you've told a story. Dray says that's wrong about a huge range of cases: often what the historian does is show that the action was the reasonable thing for the agent to have done given what he believed and wanted, and that fully answers the why-question with no law anywhere in it. Danto sharpens the point with narrative sentences, the kind of statement about an event that only someone who knows how it came out can make, which means it can't figure in a law and couldn't have been predicted at the time. Then add the later move where one side stops talking about explanation altogether and starts talking about emplottment and representation, and you've got fifty-plus years of this.

and yet nothing got settled. nobody converted. people emigrated. the analytic side went off to mechanisms and counterfactuals, the other side went off to text and narrative, working historians read neither. so what kind of disagreement was this, actually?

my hypothesis, which I want you to attack as much as develop: there were never one debate but two sharing a track, and the stall happened because each side kept answering the question it actually cared about. one question is about the warrant for singular causal claims — what has to be generally true for this-happened-because-of-that to be more than a hunch. the other is about the explanatory unit of the writing — what makes a stretch of history explanatory rather than a chronicle with good transitions. Hempelians talk past the narrativists every time they say sure there's a law, here, ceteris paribus, hungry crowds riot; narrativists talk past Hempelians every time they insist the content is in the shape of the telling.

second half of the hypothesis, the part I'm least sure about, which I'll call the indexicality worry: every candidate law in history fails one of two ways. stated loosely enough to apply to lots of cases, it's unfalsifiable mush doing no explanatory work. stated precisely enough to be testable, its predicates smuggle in a reference to the particular time, place and group you started from, and it stops being projectible anywhere else. if that's right, the Hempelian demand can always be met formally and never substantively, and the narrativist can always say the real content lives in the particular — which would mean the impasse isnt a failure of cleverness but something structural.

here's the case I keep turning over, because I don't think either framework handles it. a historian explains why a ferry service between two towns shut down for eleven days: the new crew-scheduling system put the only two certified engineers on overlapping shifts during the annual hull inspection window, and the harbour rulebook gave nobody on site the authority to break the assignment. that is a completely satisfying explanation. there's no law in it that I could state without either trivialising it or smuggling the ferry in. but it also isn't a Dray case — the whole point is that nobody chose anything, the shutdown fell out of two individually sensible rules colliding. so no reason-explanation and no covering law.

question one: is a case like that support for my two-questions diagnosis, or support for something worse — that both frameworks mis-describe what historical explanation is, and it's really about tracing how an outcome depends on one particular configuration of constraints, with law and reason both being the wrong categories?

question two: given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress? concretely. a distinction that dissolves it. a formal result — has anyone actually got as far as showing that rational explanation is just a covering-law schema with a decision-theoretic generalisation plus ceteris paribus clauses, and if so does that end the fight or only relocate it? a shared-corpus test, where historians and formalizers get handed the same paragraph and are asked seperately what would falsify it, and we check whether their answers agree. a change of question. or nothing at all, because the question is ill-posed and the honest move is to retire it — in which case I want the argument for that, not a shrug.

and if you think my two-questions story is too tidy, that the two sides really did disagree about one single thing and I'm papering over it with taxonomy — say so, and tell me what the one thing is.
