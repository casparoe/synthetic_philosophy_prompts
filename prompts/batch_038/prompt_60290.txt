I teach an upper-level seminar in social and political epistemology: 24 students, one 90-minute session, a room with three round tables and a whiteboard. They have had two weeks on majority cycles and Arrow-style impossibility results, but nothing on judgment aggregation, and they have not encountered the discursive dilemma or the doctrinal paradox. I want an activity in which they run into both by doing something, not by hearing about them: neither term should be spoken aloud until the debrief.

The frame I have in mind is a standing panel deciding a case governed by a multi-element rule, where a finding against the respondent requires that three separate propositions all be accepted. Please build the entire thing. What I need:

1. The case and its three propositions, with real normative weight so that the procedural choice feels consequential rather than recreational. Suggested allocation of bodies: three panels of seven, plus one student playing the respondent and two playing scribes who post the tallies on the board.

2. Role cards that differ not by whim but by evidence: each panelist has seen a different slice of the record, so that their beliefs about the three propositions are sincere responses to partial information. I want the three panels to be non-identical in a specific way — one in which the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures deliver opposite verdicts, one in which they agree, and one in which a two-thirds supermajority on the conclusion yields a third outcome again. Students should be able to compare a panel where the procedure decided the case against a panel where it did not, and see that the divergence is contingent rather than automatic.

3. A structured deliberation rule that keeps the engineered profile intact. If panelists may testify freely, they will simply pool everything and the paradox evaporates; design the constraint (turn order, time limits, which proposition each may speak to) and tell me why it holds.

4. Two or three cards carrying a secret stake — a member who wants a particular verdict independently of what the premises support — so that strategic voting on premises becomes something the room discovers rather than something I assert.

5. A run sheet: minute-by-minute timing, materials list, board layout, the exact wording of the ballot, and the wording in which the chair announces the voting procedure at the start. That announcement should read as a piece of administrative housekeeping, and the session should make the students retroactively furious about it.

6. A final round in which the chair is invited to switch procedures after the votes are in. I expect that fight to be the payoff of the hour; give me the questions that force it to the surface, including the regress point that the choice of aggregation rule cannot itself be made by the same rule.

7. A debrief in ordered sequence: the reveal of the two names, a mapping from moments in the room to the formal features (premise versus conclusion procedure, independence of judgments, and the trade-off among unanimity preservation, anonymity or neutrality, and collective consistency — described in plain terms, with no theorem names), and the epistemic point that the premise method pools information the conclusion method discards.

8. A frank failure analysis: what predictably goes wrong the first time this runs and how to handle each instance — students reverse-engineering the design after round one, members refusing to vote against their card, one panel finishing early and leaking, and the room settling prematurely on a single procedural fix without seeing what it costs.

9. What the activity structurally cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. My guess is that a class sees one profile and files it as a curiosity of the handout rather than as a general result, and that nobody in the room is answerable for the decision the way a real collegial body is over time. Tell me what else.

No bibliography, no citations, no reading list — I want the machinery, the cards, and the sentences I say out loud.
