I am a novelist, and in six weeks I deliver the annual lecture in a university's Literature and Moral Philosophy series. The room will be about sixty: literary critics, several of them trained in historicist and political modes, who tend to treat any appeal to the tragic as a way of evading ideology; two analytic moral philosophers, one of whom works on vagueness and comparability and has argued that incommensurability is largely an artefact of our tolerance for small improvements, the other of whom writes on duty and distrusts any ethics that ends by saying nothing settles the matter; one economist who does decision analysis and cost–benefit work for public agencies; and a contingent of MFA students who came for craft and will resent metaphysics.

My thesis, stated in the first ten minutes, is this. A novel earns its form only when the choice at its centre refuses to resolve into a ranking, and — the more controversial half — the incommensurability belongs to the telling rather than to the values. I call it teller's incommensurability. The claim is not that keeping a promise and protecting a person are incomparable in some Platonic register; it is that no single narrative intelligence can hold both descriptions in view at once without collapsing one into the vocabulary of the other, and that this failure is not a defect awaiting a better theory of value but the condition under which narrative attention works at all. I intend to defend three corollaries: (1) a novel in which the reader can conclude, without residue, that the protagonist should obviously have acted otherwise is mis-made whatever else it gets right; (2) the familiar devices for withholding verdict — free indirect style slipping between the vocabularies of the two options, a final chapter that refuses aftermath, imagery parcelled out one system per horn — are not stylistic preferences but the technical expression of a substantive claim about value; (3) cost–benefit reasoning and its literary cousin, the well-made moral fable, both presuppose a single commensurating narrator, which is why each can describe a decision but cannot enact one.

My running example will be my own third novel, in which a municipal archivist must decide whether to release a set of records that would expose the collaborators of a dead man and, in the same act, destroy the only surviving testimony of a community that no longer exists. I gave each horn its own section and I ended on the weather.

Prepare me for the question period. I do not want a defence of the thesis; I want the objections this particular room will actually raise, in roughly the order they will arrive, phrased as each of these people would phrase them, with what I should concede and what I should hold. Include at least two from the literary critics, one from the economist, one from each philosopher, and one from an MFA student pitched entirely at craft — the version that says I have only described my inability to end a book. Include also the question I am hoping nobody asks, and the single reply that has to land if the evening is to count as a success.

Deliver everything as one JSON object and nothing outside it, conforming to this schema:

- `opening_concession`: string — the one thing I should grant in the first minute of questions to buy credibility with this room.
- `objections`: array of 7 to 9 objects, ordered by expected damage to the thesis, each with:
  - `rank`: integer
  - `voice`: string — which member or faction of the audience raises it
  - `as_they_would_put_it`: string — one to three sentences in their own idiom, first person, with their technical vocabulary and their characteristic impatience
  - `targets`: string — one of `thesis`, `corollary_1`, `corollary_2`, `corollary_3`, `example`
  - `severity`: integer 1–5, where 5 means the thesis cannot survive it unmodified
  - `why_severe`: string
  - `concede`: string — what I should grant at once
  - `reply`: string — roughly two hundred words, what I would say aloud in ninety seconds
  - `soft_spot`: string — where that reply fails, and the follow-up question it invites
  - `exploitable_ally`: string or null — another person in the room whose own commitments cut against this objector, and the sentence that turns them into my ally
- `hope_nobody_asks`: object with `question`, `why_fatal`, and `emergency_answer` (all strings; the last may be a partial answer, honestly flagged as such).
- `must_land`: string — the one reply without which the lecture fails.
- `closing_line`: string — a final sentence I can use to end the questions.

You may name standard positions and standard arguments where you are confident of them, but no bibliography, no reading list, and no attributions to particular authors or works; if you are not sure a name is right, describe the view instead. Do not invent novels, essays, or lectures.
