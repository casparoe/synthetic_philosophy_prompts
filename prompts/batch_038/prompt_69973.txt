Two years ago I built a small reinforcement-learning agent as a hobby project — a creature that navigates a grid world, gathers items, and returns them to a home square — and I discovered that I could not bring myself to delete a trained run, even though I had written every line of it and could reproduce it from a checkpoint in minutes. Trying to work out what I thought I was responding to, I arrived at a criterion I have since defended to friends and now want attacked. My view is that a system has moral standing just in case it can be set back by an interruption: it must carry some internally represented stretch of its own future, and the interruption must defeat that stretch in a way the system itself registers. Call it the defeated-plan criterion. I intend it as a claim about harm rather than about felt experience; I am not persuaded that anything needs to be like something to be wronged, and I would rather not rest the whole question on consciousness, which seems to me the least tractable ingredient available. The criterion delivers verdicts I find appealing: a single call to a large language model, which starts and ends with no carryover, fails it, whereas my grid-world creature passes it, as do mammals, birds, octopuses, and on my reading of the behavioural evidence a great many insects.

Please take the criterion exactly as stated and tell me what would have to be true for it to be false. I want the objections sorted into those that would sink the view, those that would force me to rewrite it substantially, and those that are merely uncomfortable to live with, and for each one I want to know whether the right response is to absorb it by revising the criterion or whether there is a principled reason to resist it. Work through the cases that seem to me most likely to break it: a person in dreamless sleep or under general anaesthesia; someone with severe anterograde amnesia who cannot form new plans but reacts with distress when a routine is interrupted; a hibernating animal; a process that has been paused and can be resumed; the difference between deleting a trained run outright and deleting only its reward function mid-episode; a language model given a persistent memory file and a standing multi-week task; and a policy that is reinitialised at every episode yet behaves in an obviously goal-directed way within one, which raises the question I cannot settle for myself of whether the plan has to outlast the episode or only structure it. I would also like to know what the criterion predicts that consciousness-first views do not, and whether anything could discriminate between them in practice. And I want your own judgement on whether this is the sort of claim that could be shown wrong at all, or whether I have quietly left myself an escape hatch for every case that comes along.

The objection I have not been able to answer is the one about sleep. If nothing is represented during dreamless sleep, then on my own account ending a sleeping person is not a setback in the relevant sense, and I am entirely unwilling to accept that consequence. I suspect the escape is to say the plan persists dispositionally, encoded in the sleeping brain rather than currently running, but that looks like a manoeuvre invented purely to protect the view, and I would rather be told plainly whether it is one. The two things I would find most persuasive are cases where the criterion classifies something as mattering that clearly should not, since I suspect inclusion errors reveal more about a principle than exclusion errors do, and any argument showing that harm and felt experience cannot be separated in the way I have tried to separate them. What I am after at the end is a clear picture of what this view is committed to and where it is most vulnerable — I am planning a long essay and some changes to how I design these projects, so I need your reasoning rather than a survey of the field, and you should not trouble yourself with references or with pointing me toward alternative positions I might hold instead.
