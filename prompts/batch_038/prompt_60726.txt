You are a philosopher working in aesthetics and nineteenth-century German metaphysics. Think carefully, and before writing anything argumentative, spend one short paragraph brainstorming the candidate mappings between the episode and the theory, noting which ones strain.

**The episode.** On August 29, 1952, at the Maverick Concert Hall in Woodstock, New York, David Tudor premiered John Cage's 4′33″ as part of a recital of contemporary piano music. He sat at the keyboard and marked three timed movements — thirty seconds, two minutes twenty-three, one minute forty — by opening and closing the piano lid. He played nothing. What the audience heard was the hall, the weather outside, and eventually itself: coughing, muttering, a good deal of anger, some people walking out. Cage said for the rest of his life that this was his most important work, and that its point was that there is no such thing as silence, only sounds no one intended.

**The theory.** Schopenhauer's metaphysics of music. Music alone among the arts is not a copy of the Ideas but a copy of the will itself, so it gives us sorrow and joy as such rather than particular instances of them. And that content is carried by structure: the fundamental bass stands for the lowest grades of the will's objectification, inorganic nature and mass; the inner voices for the intermediate grades; the free, singing melody for the highest, the intellectual striving of an individual; dissonance demands resolution because willing demands satisfaction.

Analyze the premiere through this account. First, what does the theory say Tudor performed — is uncomposed ambient sound a copy of the will, or nothing at all, given that Schopenhauer ties that content to bass, inner voices, and melody, and given that nobody willed anything in that hall? Second, which features of the evening does he explain better than an expressionist or representational theory would (start with listeners finding shape and affect in sounds no one composed), and where does he fall silent or misdescribe — the piece's institutional point, say, or the absence of an agent whose willing is being objectified? Third, run it the other way: does the episode confirm Schopenhauer's premises while overturning his conclusions, or reveal that the premises were never doing the work attributed to them? And finally, what does Cage's aim — attention without an expressing ego — do to Schopenhauer's insistence that aesthetic experience is will-less contemplation, when music is supposed to hand us the will more directly than anything else can?
