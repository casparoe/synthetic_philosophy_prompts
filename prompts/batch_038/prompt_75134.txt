I am trying to work out what I actually believe about whether destroying habitat is bad for the wild animals living in it, and I would like help locating the principle behind my case-by-case verdicts rather than a survey of the literature.

Start from an empirical backdrop that I want you to hold fixed throughout, whether or not it is exactly right. The sites are temperate grassland and open woodland. For every thousand small mammals born in a season, roughly forty survive to breed. The median individual dies within a few weeks of birth, of cold, starvation, or predation. The adults who do survive lead lives that contain, on balance, somewhat more good than bad. Predation and parasitism account for most deaths. There is no management, no veterinary intervention, no supplementary feeding, and no rescue.

Here are the cases and my verdicts on them.

(1) A hundred hectares of this grassland are ploughed and planted with a cereal crop. The field supports perhaps a tenth of the small-mammal biomass the grassland did, and the ploughing itself kills many animals outright. I think this is bad for the animals, all things considered.

(2) The same hundred hectares are instead flooded to make a reservoir. Nearly everything that cannot swim or fly drowns in the first days; afterward the site supports some waterbirds and essentially no terrestrial small mammals. I think this is worse than (1), and not only because the transition is more violent — I think the end state is also worse for animals.

(3) The same hundred hectares are instead built over as low-density housing: gardens, hedges, bird feeders, cats, and rodenticide in the outbuildings. Wild biomass drops again, but the survivors are largely commensals — house sparrows, rats, mice — cushioned by human provision and cut short by human control. Here I waver. I catch myself thinking this might be roughly neutral for the animals, or even slightly better than the grassland, and then distrusting the thought because it makes paving things over look harmless.

(4) A grain warehouse adopts a rodenticide program. Over a decade the rat population is held at a few hundred instead of several thousand, and most of the rats that do exist die of anticoagulant poisoning, which is slow and wretched. I think this is bad, and worse than the alternative of sealing the building and making it rat-proof, even though that option also removes the rats — by preventing their births rather than by killing them.

(5) A wildlife trust proposes to immunocontracept the rabbit population on a reserve rather than cull it, holding numbers at a level the vegetation can carry. Same equilibrium, same number of rabbits never born. I think the contraceptive program is clearly permissible and the cull clearly not, and I cannot justify the difference in a way that does not sound like squeamishness about the mechanism.

(6) Wolves are extirpated from a valley. Deer numbers triple, the herd overshoots the browse, and a large fraction starves over two winters. Each surviving deer lives longer and less fearfully than its ancestors did. I think the extirpation is bad for the deer.

(7) A marsh is drained to eliminate mosquito-borne disease in a neighboring village. I think this is permissible, perhaps required. An identical marsh, with an identical mosquito, invertebrate, and amphibian population, is drained to make a golf course. I think this is wrong. The animals affected are the same in number and kind; the difference lies entirely in the human reasons.

(8) I will not kill the large spider in my bathtub. I catch it under a glass and put it outside at some risk to myself and twenty minutes of fuss. Yet I do not think the spider's continued existence is a good thing, and I would not object to learning that a wasp had eaten it an hour after I released it.

The two places I want the most pressure applied are (1) against (3), since I am unwilling to say that building over grassland is good for its animals but equally unwilling to say a garden suburb is worse for them than a monoculture; and (7) against (8), since in (7) I let human purposes override an identical animal cost while in (8) I absorb a cost myself to preserve an animal whose continued existence I do not value.

My tentative principle, which I suspect is broken: an act is bad for wild animals to the extent that it reduces the total wellbeing they would otherwise have had, where total wellbeing is the sum over lives of the goodness of each life, where lives never begun count for nothing, and where lives cut short count in full. Two things bother me about it. It seems to imply that a larger population living worse could be worse than a smaller one living better, which I am not sure I believe; and it seems to leave me unable to distinguish the winter that kills most of a vole population from the plough that does the same, which feels like a serious defect rather than a harmless one.

Tell me whether there is a principle, or a short list of competing candidates, that gets all or most of these verdicts right. State it precisely enough that I can check it against the cases clause by clause, and say which case motivates each clause. If some of my verdicts are really about how things go for the animals while others are about what is all-things-considered permissible, say so plainly and tell me which principle answers which question instead of forcing them into one. Then tell me what your preferred principle delivers on cases I have not listed: whether a rewilded former coal mine is better or worse for animals than the grassland it replaces; whether a hard winter that kills most of a vole population is bad for those voles, and if so whether it stands on the same footing as a controlled burn that kills the same number; and whether it lets me support fertility control for feral cats while opposing fertility control applied to a rodent population for the same reason. Add one further case of your own choosing that you think is genuinely diagnostic for the principle, and give the principle's verdict on it.

Finally, be explicit about which of my eight verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. Where a verdict can be saved only by adding a clause, tell me what the clause costs, and tell me which verdicts you think I should simply abandon rather than complicate the principle to preserve.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists of your own, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables. Referring to my cases by their numbers inside ordinary sentences, as in (4) or (7), is fine.
