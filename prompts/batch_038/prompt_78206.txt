I work as a type designer and have been reading the letter-spirit literature — Hofstadter's long-running project on what makes every 'a' an 'a', and the thesis associated with it: that a letterform's identity is not a checklist of features but a style or essence, apprehended holistically and by analogy, with graded membership and fluid boundaries rather than sharp ones. I would like that thesis run against a real episode in which the identity of letterforms briefly became a matter of public, quasi-legal argument.

In July 2017, during the Pakistani proceedings that grew out of the Panama Papers disclosures, the then-prime minister's daughter submitted to the Supreme Court's joint investigation team a set of family trust documents said to have been signed in February 2006. Observers noticed almost immediately that the documents were typeset in Calibri — a face Microsoft began designing around 2002, circulated in beta through 2005 and 2006, and released to the general public only at the end of January 2007. The inference that spread through the press and social media was blunt: a document produced in 2006 cannot have been set in a typeface that did not yet exist, therefore the paperwork was back-dated. The typeface's designer said publicly that the documents could not be genuine. Two counter-considerations were equally obvious — the face had existed in unreleased form well before its public launch, and a genuine 2006 agreement could have been retyped or reprinted years later, in which case the typeface dates the file rather than the transaction. The Court's eventual ruling turned on other grounds, and the affair became, in effect, a public argument about what a typeface is, conducted with no theory of the matter in hand.

Analyze the episode through the letter-spirit question. In particular:

1. What would have to be true about typeface identity for the claim that a given document is set in Calibri to count as a finding of fact? Is recognizing a face an act of essence-perception in the letter-spirit sense, or something else entirely? Note that almost nobody in the debate appeared to be perceiving a style: what moved opinion was a name attached to an image of text, combined with a release date.

2. The letter-spirit project asks about a category of letterforms — the 'a' as it survives across fonts. Fontgate asked about a category of faces: all the Calibris, against the look-alikes. Are these the same kind of category question? If not, what does the difference consist in, and does the framework have resources for the second kind, or is it silent there? As a complication, consider metric-compatible substitutes — faces issued later by other vendors that reproduce a given face's spacing and line breaks exactly while drawing recognizably different letterforms. What survives in such a case: the spirit, the letter, or neither?

3. What does the episode do to the claim that a letterform's essence can be apprehended but not enumerated? The whole affair depended on people confidently distinguishing a face from its near-neighbours by eye, and yet what finally carried weight was artifact history — design dates, beta distributions, licensing channels, file provenance — none of which is a matter of style. Does that refute the thesis, refine it, or fall outside its intended scope?

4. A court needs a binary, dated answer; the letter-spirit picture offers graded membership and blurred edges. Say what happens when a theory of essences built for perception is put to evidentiary work, and whether the mismatch is a problem for the theory, for the court, or for neither.

Where the framework has nothing to say, say so plainly. I would rather have the silence identified than have the theory stretched to cover it.
