Okay, so I write novels — three published, all quiet literary ones that shift maybe nine hundred copies each — and I have this recurring arguement with my brother-in-law Dev, who teaches philosophy at a state school and is a genuinely sweet guy with an infuriating habit of ending a conversation with a slogan. Last Thanksgiving I tried to explain something that's been eating at me for the whole of the book I'm drafting now. Readers write to me. They say they cried when a character died, that they grieved, that they had to put the book down and walk around their kitchen for a while. And I can't shake the sense that something in that report is crooked — not that they're lying, obviously, but that whatever's happening to them can't be the same kind of thing as grief for a real person, since they know perfectly well, at the very moment the tears are coming, that nobody died. The feeling seems aimed at something the person simultaneously believes isn't there. Dev listened to about forty seconds of this and said, "that's what suspension of disbelief is for — while they're reading they believe she's real, there's your answer," and then went off to check the turkey. The one time he'd mentioned it before he gave the problem a name, something about a paradox of fiction, which sounds like a thing people have written about, but he wouldn't say more and I don't want to lean on a name.

Here's what I want from you, and I want it as a menu rather than one tidy theory: take that phrase apart and lay out the genuinely different claims somebody could be making when they say a reader "suspends disbelief." I can feel there's more than one hiding in there and I suspect the differences are the whole ballgame. For a start — does suspending disbelief even amount to believing? I keep tripping over the fact that not-disbelieving something isn't the same state as believing it, and a phrase built out of a negative might be doing a lot of quiet work. Then: suspended how, by whom, for how long, and on purpose or not? Is it a decision you make before chapter one, a thing that happens to you when the prose gets good enough, a temporary bracketing that leaves your actual beliefs untouched underneath, a sort of game or pretence you knowingly join, or a claim about how you entertain a thought without assenting to it? And what is the object of it — the existence of the characters at all, or just the improbability of what happens to them? Because I have a half-memory that the phrase is much older than this use and came out of a poet explaining how he planned to handle ghosts and cursed sailors and supernatural material in a book of ballads with a friend, in which case it was never aimed at my problem in the first place. If that memory is right, tell me whether the original sense has any use here or whether it's been borrowed for a job it can't possibly do.

For each reading you land on, I want three verdicts: is it a platitude nobody would deny, a substantive and defensible claim about how minds work, or just false; is it actually true of readers like the ones writing me letters; and — the part I care about — does it deliver what Dev wanted, i.e. does it make the crying non-mysterious, or does it leave the puzzle exactly where it started, or maybe even make it worse. Tell me which reading you think he was reaching for when he said it four seconds before the turkey, and which one is the best on offer if you had to pick one to argue with him over. And there's a practical reason this isn't idle for me: if some reading is true it has consequences for how I write, for whether piling on sensory detail and interiority is supposed to tip a reader into a belief-state or whether that's a superstition I inherited from every craft book I ever read — so if any of the readings imply something about the sentences on the page, say that too. Just tell me what you think, plainly, and where you're unsure mark it as unsure instead of smoothing it over.
