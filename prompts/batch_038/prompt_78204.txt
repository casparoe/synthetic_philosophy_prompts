I serve as counsel to the board of a regional transit-and-housing authority. Roughly twenty-five times a year a proposal reaches my desk that would bind future boards: a dedicated revenue stream locked to debt service for thirty years, a supermajority requirement for amending a program, a clause providing that some policy may not be repealed before a stated date, or an interlocal agreement in which we promise a neighboring jurisdiction that our successors will not disturb a land-use commitment. The members who propose these have generally read nothing about precommitment, and my job is to tell them in one page whether the constraint is worth adopting and, if so, in what form. I have about forty-five minutes per proposal and no outside counsel; a junior attorney on my team, who has no background in the theory, must be able to run the procedure from the proposal documents and a short interview with its sponsor. The board votes on the final language. Separately, our state legislature can override much of what we do, and I want the procedure to treat that as an input rather than an embarrassment.

Please write that decision procedure. I want it grounded in what the precommitment literature actually establishes — Elster's Ulysses contracts and the surrounding argument about whether one legislature may bind the next, and about the objection that the dead should not govern the living — rather than in general good-governance intuition. Deliver an ordered list of questions, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer; the points at which the procedure terminates in do-not-entrench or in entrench-only-in-this-weaker-form; the points at which it hands the decision back to the board because what remains is a question of values, or of how much the present owes its successors, rather than of fact; and a short menu of the weaker instruments to consider before endorsing a lock — sunset with affirmative renewal, a funding floor, a delay or supermajority speed bump, shaping preferences instead of constraining them, and anything else you think belongs.

Several distinctions have to survive the simplification, and I want you to say explicitly which you kept, which you dropped, and why: whether the constraint binds the same decision-makers who adopted it or their successors; whether the threat being guarded against is foreseeable weakness of will, foreseeable change in what the board wants, or unforeseeable change in circumstances, since only the first is a threat that precommitment is well suited to answer; whether the clause is self-releasing or genuinely entrenched; and whether any enforcement mechanism exists that a future board cannot simply vote away. Above all, build in a question of this shape: what will the future board say when it wants out, and does the drafted language anticipate that particular argument? That is the feature of the Ulysses story practitioners reliably miss — he arranged in advance for his own later pleas to be ignored.

Then run the procedure on three live proposals:

1. Bond counsel proposes dedicating a half-cent sales tax increment to debt service on a light-rail extension for thirty years, amendable only by a two-thirds vote of the board plus a public referendum. The rating agencies have asked for it and the board expects routine approval.

2. After a well-organized fare-free campaign, a member proposes entrenching zero fares on all local bus routes for ten years, repealable only by a two-thirds vote and a referendum, expressly on the ground that she expects a future board to reverse the policy under fiscal pressure. The board supports it seven to two and expects my memo to bless it.

3. At the request of an adjacent suburb, we would covenant in an interlocal agreement not to approve dense housing or a maintenance facility on a forty-acre parcel we own along their border for twenty-five years. The current board is unanimous, foresees no downside, and wants the covenant as a bargaining chip.

Give the recommendation and the reasoning for each, not merely the outcome, and let at least one come out against what the board wants.

Finally, two paragraphs of self-criticism. Identify the two questions in the procedure on which a reasonable practitioner is most likely to reach the wrong answer, and say what would change your mind: which argument, if sound, would flip your recommendation on the second proposal, and which of my stated constraints — the forty-five minutes, the legislature's override power, the board's political exposure — you suspect is doing illegitimate work in shaping the procedure rather than merely limiting it. Then tell me what the procedure cannot do: the classes of proposal on which it offers no guidance or actively misleading guidance, and the point at which I should stop and seek outside judgment.
