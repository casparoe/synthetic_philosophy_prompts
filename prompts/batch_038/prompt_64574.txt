My brother-in-law Ravi teaches comparative constitutional law, and over the last year or so we've gotten into a routine of arguing about church and state over Sunday lunch while the kids run around. He's given me his call on a bunch of real situations and I've been keeping notes on my phone, partly so I can catch him out. Here's what's on the list:

- Crucifixes hanging in Italian state-school classrooms: fine, nothing is being asked of anybody.
- The French statute keeping conspicuous religious signs out of state school classrooms: goes too far. His line is that the state is behaving itself there and the kid is the one being told what she may wear.
- Norway's compulsory school subject on Christianity, religion and philosophy, where parents could get a kid excused from bits of it but not from the course: bad.
- Turkey's compulsory religious culture and ethics course, which only pupils whose parents declared they weren't Muslim could be exempted from: bad.
- A town council in the US opening its meetings with prayers from local clergy, overwhelmingly Christian with the odd exception: fine, they're adults, they can step out, nobody's being tested on belief.
- The American school football coach praying at midfield after games with the team around him: bad, totally different animal, those kids can't not be there.
- A big Latin cross war memorial on public land in Maryland that the parks authority maintains: fine, it's been standing since the 1920s and reads as a memorial now.
- Germany's church tax, collected by the state alongside income tax from anyone who hasn't formally left their church: fine.
- Turkey's state directorate of religious affairs, which pays the imams: the paying is fine, he says. What isn't fine is that it writes the Friday sermon and distributes it.
- The couple of dozen Anglican bishops with seats in the UK's upper house: fine, grudgingly, because they're a rounding error and can't swing anything.
- Denmark, where the constitution names a Lutheran folk church and the state supports it, but you're free to leave: fine.

Now here's why I'm asking. Our regional government — doesn't matter where, just assume somewhere with a historic national church, a mostly secular public culture, and a bunch of much newer immigrant congregations — has introduced a four-part faith-and-public-life bill, and Ravi has been asked to give evidence to the committee in a few weeks. The parts:

1. The parish offices stop keeping the civil register of births, marriages and deaths, which all moves to a secular state office. Any registered religious community can then solemnize a marriage with full civil effect.
2. Every pupil in a state school attends a twenty-minute weekly common assembly. The bill describes it as reflective and non-confessional and never mentions God, but in practice in most schools it's led by the local parish minister and includes a prayer. Parents can withdraw their child in writing; from age 15 the child can withdraw herself.
3. Any taxpayer can redirect 2 percent of their income tax to a registered religious body. If you tick nothing, your 2 percent goes by default to the national church.
4. A new heritage grant pays for upkeep of buildings used for worship. It's open to every registered community, but only if the building is at least a hundred years old.

What I want is a prediction: which parts he'll call acceptable, which he'll call over the line, and what he'll say about the bill as a package. But more than that I want the reasoning underneath. What rule is he actually running — and give me at least two other rules that fit that list of eleven verdicts just as well as the one you land on, because I'm pretty sure the list doesn't pin him down. For each candidate rule, say what it implies for each of the four parts and where they split. Then give me your best guess about what he'll actually say and tell me which entries in my list are carrying the weight for that guess. Two extra things: I suspect the age-15 self-withdrawal clause in part 2 matters more than it looks, so tell me whether it makes that part better or worse on each rule. And if I could put exactly one question to him at the hearing to force him to show which principle he's operating with — a question where the honest answers diverge depending on the rule — what should I ask? Flag too which of my notes is the shakiest bit of evidence, in case I misheard him. Keep it to the argument, no reading list, I'm not going to have time to chase anything down before Thursday.
