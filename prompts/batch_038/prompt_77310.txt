I am marking a decision theory midterm and would like an independent assessment of one submission. Please grade it and write the comments I would give the student.

**The question (20 marks).**
(a) [6 marks] State the two choice problems in the Allais paradox, giving the payoffs and probabilities, and identify the pattern of preferences that generates the puzzle.
(b) [8 marks] Explain precisely which axiom of expected utility theory this pattern violates, showing the structure of the violation rather than merely naming it.
(c) [6 marks] Say whether the paradox shows expected utility theory to be false, and address the objection that an agent with Allais preferences can be turned into a money pump.

**The student's answer.**

(a) In the first problem, option A pays $1,000,000 for certain, and option B pays $5,000,000 with probability 0.10, $1,000,000 with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01. In the second problem, option C pays $1,000,000 with probability 0.11 and nothing with probability 0.89, and option D pays $5,000,000 with probability 0.10 and nothing with probability 0.90. Most people prefer A to B and D to C. Expected utility theory says that is impossible: it requires either A and C, or B and D.

(b) Write A as a mixture: an 0.89 chance of $1,000,000 combined with an 0.11 branch that pays $1,000,000. Write B as the same 0.89 branch combined with an 0.11 branch that pays $5,000,000 with probability 10/11 and nothing with probability 1/11. Now write C as an 0.89 branch that pays nothing, combined with an 0.11 branch that pays $1,000,000, and D as the same empty 0.89 branch combined with the very same 10/11–1/11 branch that appears in B. So A versus B and C versus D are one and the same comparison — a certain million against a 10/11 chance of five million and a 1/11 chance of nothing — differing only in what is attached to the common 0.89 branch. Reversing your preference when you change only that common branch violates the independence axiom, also known as independence of irrelevant alternatives: if x is preferred to y, then any mixture of x with a third option z is preferred to the corresponding mixture of y with z. Violations of this kind are called common consequence effects. The standard psychological explanation is the certainty effect: people weight outcomes that are certain out of proportion to merely probable ones. Prospect theory models this by transforming probabilities rather than outcomes, with a weighting function that jumps disproportionately as probability approaches 1.

(c) The violation has consequences beyond these four options. Because the agent's ranking depends on what is attached to a branch she should be treating as common, her preferences cannot be transitive: she ranks x above y in one framing and y above x in another. Intransitive agents are money-pumpable — one can offer them a sequence of individually attractive trades that leaves them strictly worse off — so the Allais agent is exploitable, and the objection in the question is correct. It follows that no utility function whatsoever can represent these preferences. That is why this is a refutation rather than a curiosity: expected utility theory claims to describe how rational agents rank risky prospects, and here is a ranking that no assignment of utilities to outcomes can deliver.

The theory's normative standing, however, is untouched. What subjects actually pick has no bearing on what they ought to pick; the independence axiom is a claim about rationality, so a questionnaire result showing that most people depart from it establishes only that people are imperfectly rational, or confused by how the options are described. The paradox is therefore a serious descriptive failure and no kind of normative problem.
