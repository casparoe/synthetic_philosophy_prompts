I am a junior, and this spring I am debating on the affirmative side of my school's resolution: "Resolved: the public science curriculum should exclude any discipline that fails a test of falsifiability." Our theory-of-knowledge seminar spent two weeks on the philosophy of science as preparation, and the instructor assigned a transcript from a well-known popular science podcast as background reading. The passage that everyone on our team is now relying on summarizes Popper as follows:

> "Karl Popper solved the problem of what separates genuine science from everything that merely imitates it. His test is falsifiability: a theory belongs to science only if some possible observation would show it to be false. A claim that no conceivable observation could contradict is not just unscientific — it is meaningless, in the same category as poetry or religious faith. This is why Einstein passes and Freud fails. Relativity risked everything on the 1919 eclipse expedition: it predicted a precise amount of bending for starlight passing the sun, and had the measurements come out otherwise, the theory would have been finished. Psychoanalysis cannot be endangered in that way, because any behavior whatever can be redescribed as an expression of the same few unconscious forces; there is nothing a patient could do that would count against it. Marxism fares no better: it offered no predictions that could actually be checked, which is why the catastrophes of the twentieth century never registered as refutations. Popper was equally clear that science never proves anything. No number of observed white swans establishes that all swans are white, while a single black swan demolishes the claim, so knowledge grows purely by elimination, and a surviving theory is not a true one but only one that has not yet been refuted. The scientist's duty is to abandon a theory as soon as evidence conflicts with it; refusing to let go is precisely the pseudoscientific reflex. What follows is that the more a theory forbids, the better its science, and a discipline whose practitioners can always produce one more assumption to absorb an inconvenient result has ceased to be science altogether, however useful it may be."

My lab partner, who debates with me, has converted this passage into the spine of our case, and he draws two conclusions from it. First, he says evolutionary biology fails Popper's own test, because an awkward fossil can always be accommodated by revising the assumed dates or by adding a supplementary story about the local environment — which is exactly the "one more assumption" move the transcript says disqualifies a field. Second, he says that since unfalsifiable claims are meaningless, the resolution also permits us to dismiss ethics and metaphysics from the curriculum without any further argument. I am uneasy about both moves, and about the passage itself, but I cannot yet articulate where the trouble lies.

Could you take the passage apart assertion by assertion and tell me which statements a philosopher of science would endorse as they stand, which are harmless simplifications, which misdescribe Popper rather than merely oversimplifying him, and which are simply false? Two points especially. I would like to know whether "no conceivable observation could contradict it" and "it is meaningless" really amount to the same thing on Popper's account, or whether the transcript has borrowed something from a rival tradition he disagreed with. And I would like to understand how the black-swan line is supposed to coexist with the closing claim about extra assumptions, since they seem to pull in opposite directions — my own suspicion is that even identifying the bird as a black swan is an observation that could go wrong, and I do not know whether Popper had a response to that worry or whether it is an objection that defeats him. Once the passage is corrected, does either of my partner's two arguments survive? Please also say what the repaired criterion still leaves out that we would need in order to make the resolution workable in an actual debate, and be candid about whether it can bear that much weight at all.
