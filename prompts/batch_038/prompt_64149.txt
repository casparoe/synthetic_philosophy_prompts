I'm about 200k words into a novel set in a slightly-off version of the north of England where the removals trade got captured by metaphysicians in the nineties and never recovered. Chapter 4 is my protagonist's first shift at a family firm called Hogg & Daughter Removals, and she gets handed the new-starter pack. I need you to write the centrepiece of that pack: the Inventory Protocol — a deadpan staff handbook extract on how to fill in a Schedule of Goods. Write the handbook itself, not a description of it.

The firm's house metaphysics, which I want stated through procedure rather than argued for: parts only compose a further thing when they're caught up in a life. So the fern is one item and the pot isn't; the cat is one item and the cat carrier isn't; the customer is an organism but is not goods. Nothing inanimate composes. No tables, no wardrobes, no lorries, no houses, no earth under any of it. The firm calls this answer Life, internally, in capitals.

Beats I'd like in there — use the ones that work, you dont have to fit all of them:

- a real sample of the inventory form with the item counts, and why a fridge full of live yoghurt is the worst job on the books
- the cut Christmas tree / potted Christmas tree distinction, handled as a compliance matter rather than a philosophy one
- the escalation form for borderline lives (the slime mould in the shower, the dormant seed, the ant farm, the sourdough starter that's been going since 1974) and clerks being firmly told not to decide these themselves
- the scripted reply to complaint number one, you smashed my table — a sincere paraphrase, something along the lines of the simples arranged tablewise being arranged tablewise at collection and arranged otherwise on arrival, which reliably escalates things
- the two rival firms: one that lists every plurality whatever, including the one composed of the customer's left shoe and the depot roof truss, and bills accordingly; one that lists nothing and eventually noticed it had no vans, no staff and no customers either
- somewhere in the small print, the accusation that all three firms are merely using their words differently, answered the way a compliance officer would answer it

No philosopher names, no real companies, no dates. If you want a founding text, invent an in-world pamphlet. About 800 words; section headers, numbered clauses and small print are all welcome.

The jokes have to come from getting the metaphysics right, not from the customers being thick. Someone who works on composition should be able to spot the real objections — why a life is the cutoff when touching and gluing aren't, that any line you draw is either sharp-and-arbitrary or vague, that this is all a verbal dispute — staged as office procedure and never named as philosophy. And keep it deadpan throughout: no winking, no aside about how absurd the firm is. The handbook believes itself completely, and the straighter it stays the funnier it is. A reader who knows nothing about any of this should still laugh at a removals company that refuses to acknowledge the sofa.

Then, after the handbook text and on its own line, I want exactly one thing for my notes: Verdict: followed by one of universalism, nihilism or Life — whichever way you think the firm's own rules secretly wobble once a clerk actually tries to operate them. Don't justify it, just the label.
