I've been sitting in the same rationalist-adjacent forum arguments about whether history has a direction for something like fifteen years now — you know the genre exactly: somebody posts a chart of declining violence or a paper on AI timelines, and within forty comments someone has invoked Providence, or Hegel's cunning of reason, or "the moral arc of the universe," and everyone else rolls their eyes and says you're smuggling a designer back in through the window. Last month I decided to stop relying on the summary versions of these gestures and actually go read the people making them, which is how I ended up reading a battered public-domain translation of Schopenhauer on my phone in bed. I'm in the supplementary volume — the third one in the old Haldane and Kemp edition — where he's building the case that the intellect is a latecomer and the will is what's really doing everything. A few lines before the chunk I'm stuck on he says: "In general nature signifies that which operates, acts, performs without the assistance of the intellect." Then comes this:

"On this account, it may be remarked in passing, my direct antipode among philosophers is Anaxagoras; for he assumed arbitrarily as that which is first and original, from which everything proceeds, a νους, an intelligence, a subject of ideas, and he is regarded as the first who promulgated such a view. According to him the world existed earlier in the mere idea than in itself; while according to me it is the unconscious will which constitutes the reality of things, and its development must have advanced very far before it finally attains, in the animal consciousness, to the idea and intelligence; so that, according to me, thought appears as the very last. However, according to the testimony of Aristotle (Metaph., i. 4), Anaxagoras himself did not know how to begin much with his νους, but merely set it up, and then left it standing like a painted saint at the entrance, without making use of it in his development of nature, except in cases of need, when he did not know how else to help himself. All physico-theology is a carrying out of the error opposed to the truth expressed at the beginning of this chapter—the error that the most perfect form of the origin of things is that which is brought about by means of an intellect. Therefore it draws a bolt against all deep exploration of nature."

I can follow the polemic but not the argument, and there are three seperate things I can't resolve. (1) The Anaxagoras material reads to me like two complaints stapled together: a metaphysical one, that on his picture the world existed in the idea before it existed in itself, and a methodological one, that even Anaxagoras got no work out of his nous and left it parked at the entrance like decoration, only consulting it in emergencies. Which one is actually load-bearing for the "error" he names at the end? Because the thing that's been bugging me all week is that complaint two seems to rebound: if blind striving can't tell you why *this* trajectory of events rather than another, and the will only gets invoked to fill gaps in your account of nature, then Schopenhauer's will looks like exactly the same painted saint he's mocking. Is there a principled difference between "an intelligence that nobody can get any predictions out of" and "a will that nobody can get any predictions out of," or is his advantage purely that the will is known from the inside?

(2) I've been privately sorting teleological claims into three bins and I want to know if the passage forces a different sorting: top-down (there's a planner with an end — providence, cunning of reason, the "Nature has a secret plan" move), bottom-up (no planner at all, but the substrate has directedness baked in, so where things end up isn't accidental), and bookkeeping (nothing is directed, you just narrate a path and then retroactively describe it as if it had been aimed at). Which bin does the will go in? And if it's bottom-up, is he entitled to the "therefore it draws a bolt against all deep exploration of nature" jab, since that sounds like precisely what a bottom-up teleologist would say against a top-down one and I don't see why it doesn't cut both ways. (3) This is the reason I actually care: what does any of this leave for *history*? My hazy recollection from years ago, possibly garbled, is that Schopenhauer elsewhere complains that history isn't really a science at all, more like a running log of the species, and that it's the same story retold in different costumes with no real development — which would be the sharpest possible rejection of the arc-of-history crowd. Can you help me see whether that recollection is consistent with what he's doing in this passage, i.e. whether denying that intellect comes first in nature commits him to denying that history goes anywhere, or whether a bottom-up directedness could give him a direction of history without a providence.

Two smaller things, then I'll shut up. Please unpick the syntax of "All physico-theology is a carrying out of the error opposed to the truth expressed at the beginning of this chapter—the error that the most perfect form of the origin of things is that which is brought about by means of an intellect" — I've read it six times and I still can't tell whether "opposed to the truth" modifies error or is doing something weirder, or where the appositive after the dash attaches. And what's the painted-saint image concretely: I'm assuming a votive panel hung in a church porch, so the sense is that you install it reverently and then never actually consult it about anything? Don't send me a bibliography or a list of things to go read, I'm mostly offline and I want the reasoning rather than the pointers — a reconstruction of the argument in the passage, where you think it's strong, and where you think it's just Schopenhauer being pleased with himself.
