I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and for the last two months I've basically been embedded with one syntactician for a long piece on the innateness debate. She's a card-carrying nativist but a strangely disciplined one, and since I'm moderating a panel with her at a festival in the spring I've been keeping a list of her verdicts, mostly so I can catch her out. Here's the list as close to her own words as I can manage.

On forming yes/no questions in English — the fact that you front the auxiliary belonging to the main clause, so "is the man who is tall happy" rather than the first auxiliary you hit going left to right — she says that one is built in, full stop, because a kid who tried the linear rule would never be told its wrong, the input doesn't disconfirm it, and yet kids don't make the mistake. Same verdict for the ban on pulling a question word out of a relative clause ("what did you meet the man who bought"). On binding she splits in a way that surprised me: the ban on a sentence-initial pronoun picking up a name that comes later ("he thinks John is clever") is innate, because kids obey it as soon as they can string two clauses together at all; but the ban on a pronoun taking a nearby antecedent — the one that makes "Mama Bear is touching her" mean somebody other than Mama Bear — she insists is *not* innate, that it falls out of the other principle plus some processing or pragmatic limitation that clears around six, which on her account is why kids fail the standard picture-matching experiment for years. Word order, which auxiliaries a language happens to have, irregular past tenses and plurals: all learned from the input, no complaint, she's happy to give those away. And the bit I keep coming back to: when I asked how a child ever gives up "goed" if nobody corrects them, she said statistics — the kid hears "went" often enough in exactly the contexts where they'd have said "goed," and the overgeneralization gets knocked out by entrenchment. She was pretty firm about that, and she added that the overregularization error itself still shows there's an innate default to add the regular ending, so the default is built in but its range of application is learned. That distinction has been rattling around in my head ever since.

The question I've got loaded for the panel is about dropping subjects: whether the child's knowledge that English doesn't allow a missing subject — that "is raining" and "went to the store" aren't sentences, where the Italian kid's equivalents are fine — is built in or learned. The awkward parts, which I'll lay out on stage: nobody ever tells the kid that subjectless sentences are ungrammatical, the input just never contains one, and "I never heard it" doesn't by itself distinguish *forbidden* from *nobody happened to say it*; on the other hand the input is stuffed with overt subjects including the meaningless ones ("it's raining," "there's a dog outside"), so there's a positive cue you could point at, and there's a measurable difference between the English and Italian inputs; and English two-year-olds do go through a stretch of dropping subjects themselves ("want cookie," "doggie barking"), which could mean they haven't figured anything out, or could just mean thier mouths and working memory are worse than their grammar. Some people also claim that if you count carefully, English two-year-olds produce overt subjects more often than you'd expect by chance, which if true seems to cut the other way again.

So: predict her answer. What I actually want is the machinery behind it, because her record seems to me to fit at least three different rules — something like "if positive evidence can't disconfirm the wrong hypothesis, the right one is innate," something like "only universal structural principles are innate and anything that varies between languages is a switch you set from the data," and something like "let the child's own statistics do as much work as you can possibly get away with" — and I honestly can't tell which one she's running, since they agree on everything she's ruled on so far. Name the candidate rules that are consistent with her whole record, say what each of them predicts for the subject-dropping case and for the early telegraphic stage, and give me your single best guess about what she'll say on stage plus the reason you prefer it over the others. Then tell me whether her entrenchment answer about "goed" commits her to something here she'd rather not be committed to — i.e., whether the dummy "it" in "it's raining" counts as a competing form that preempts the subjectless version the same way "went" preempts "goed," or whether that analogy quietly breaks down, and where. And finish by giving me one further case I could actually put to her live, a construction or a developmental finding where at least two of those rules give different answers, so the panel isn't just two people agreeing in different vocabularies. If you think she has no consistent answer available, say so, and say which verdict she'd have to give up to get one. I've already got the reading list, so please don't send me one — I just want the reasoning.
