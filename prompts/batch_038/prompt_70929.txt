I've been in a reading group on Augustine on lying for about two years and the argument has gone nowhere, which bugs me, because everyone's read the same two treatises — the early one on lying, and the later one he wrote to Consentius about whether you may pretend to be a Priscillianist in order to smoke out heretics. We've got two camps. One says the wrongness comes from the nature of the sign: lying is having a double heart, putting out a word that doesn't match what you hold inside, so it perverts what speech is for. The other says it's flat-out divine command — scripture pronounces a universal sentence against those who speak falsehood and that's the end of it. They fight constantly and nobody budges.

My hunch, offered only as a hunch: they aren't disagreeing about the same thing. One is answering what explains the wrongness, the other what makes it a sin, and both quietly assume the definitional work — insincerity rather than falsity — is neutral when it may already be doing the normative lifting. Then there's Rahab and the midwifes, where as I remember it he says the reward was for their mercy rather than for the lie and treats the lie itself as a lesser fault; each camp reads that as an embarrassment to explain away instead of as data.

So is this verbal, substantive, or two questions under one label? And what would actually move it — I'd guess you'd need a case where the sign-perversion ground and the divine-command ground give different verdicts about the same utterance. Tell me whether such a case exists, or argue that it can't.
