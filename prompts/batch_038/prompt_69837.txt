I teach a third-year seminar on well-being and every week I pair a technical reading with a novel, so the students have something concrete to argue about instead of a diagram on the board. Next week is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: if pleasure is supposed to be the one thing that makes a life go well, the hedonist owes us an account of what all the pleasures have in common, and there isn't an obvious answer, since a hot bath, finally getting a proof to work, a really good mango, being envied, and religious transport don't look like one kind of state. I've got them reading the usual spread of responses -- the felt-quality line, Feldman's split between sensory and attitudinal pleasure, Heathwood's desire-based alternative, Labukt's unity thesis that there's a single hedonic tone present in every pleasure at different intensities -- plus the affective neuroscience angle, since half the class assumes the lab has settled that pleasure is one measurable thing when actually there's Berridge's separation of liking from wanting and hedonic hotspots rather than a pleasure centre. I also put Aristotle on the list as the wildcard: pleasure not as a separable inner state at all but as something that supervenes on the unimpeded activity of a faculty in good condition.

The novel is The Picture of Dorian Gray, and I want a proper critical reading of it against that debate rather than a summary. Two places seem to matter most. First, the long catalogue chapter (11 in the twenty-chapter text, I think) where Dorian spends years assembling his researches: perfumes and their effects on the mind, music and unfamiliar instruments, jewels and the superstitions attached to them, embroideries and tapestries from everywhere. Is that list one kind of thing repeated many times over, or a heap of heterogeneous states that share nothing but a name and a narrator? Wilde reports all of it in the same flat enumerative register, which feels like it's doing work -- either evidence for the unity view, or evidence that English happens to have one word for a loose cluster and the novel is exploiting that fact. Second, the back half, where Dorian keeps doing all the same things and stops enjoying any of them, and ends up in the opium den near the docks chasing something he can't get. On a felt-quality account that's a puzzle, because the sensations should still be available to him; on the Aristotelian account it's trivially explained, because his faculties are wrecked and the pleasure goes with them. Does the novel end up arguing for the activity view against both Feldman and Heathwood, or is it just a morality tale with a mood problem? Related: is the new hedonism Lord Henry preaches actually hedonism? Dorian seems driven by wanting to have had every experience, which looks a lot more like desire fulfilment or the intuition behind Nozick's machine than like pleasure-maximising, and I'd like that taken seriously rather than assumed away. And there's the paradox of hedonism -- the direct pursuit fails -- but I want to know whether the book can tell us why: because pleasure isn't the sort of thing you can aim at, or because Dorian is simply a ruined agent. Those give different lessons to my students.

Please be honest in both directions. Say plainly where the book resists the value-theoretic reading: the portrait is a supernatural moral device doing symbolic work no theory of pleasure needs, the catalogue chapter may just be pastiche and showing off, and Wilde's aestheticism may not be interested in well-being at all, in which case I'm bolting a contemporary question onto a text doing something seperate. One of my students will certainly say the whole thing is aestheticism and not hedonism, and that I've mislabelled the week -- deal with that objection rather than waving at it, and then say what the novel actually adds to the heterogeneity debate that argument alone can't deliver, if anything. Don't bother with quotations, they all have the book, so point to scenes and chapters roughly; and no reading lists or bibliography, I've got the technical side covered. British English please, since I'm handing an extract of this round as the discussion sheet.
