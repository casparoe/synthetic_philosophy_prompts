I have been following the arguments about when the Anthropocene began, and there is a recurring feature of them that I cannot name. The competing candidates all seem to win or lose on the same kind of ground. Take the proposal that the epoch starts in the early seventeenth century, with the collapse of Indigenous American populations after contact and the small, decades-long dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide that followed and was recorded in ice. The objection that does the real work against it is not that the event was unimportant, or that colonialism should not count as epoch-making; it is that a signal that faint, that gradual, and preserved in that kind of archive cannot be pinned to a year and correlated by people working in different places. Now take the leading rival, the mid-twentieth-century marker left by nuclear-weapons fallout, which is sharp, unmistakable, and appears in the same layer everywhere at once — and which has the effect of installing the industrialized world after 1945 as the agent of the epoch, with everything before it, including several millennia of farming and land transformation, reduced to background. The same happens to any slow and distributed cause: salinization building up under centuries of irrigation, forest clearance spreading across a continent over a thousand years. These are not defeated by an argument about their significance. They are ruled out by criteria — synchrony, global correlatability, legibility in a datable layer — that only abrupt and concentrated events can satisfy.

The pattern is this: the question of when the Anthropocene began, and therefore of who began it, gets settled inside a procedure that presents itself as purely methodological, and whose admission rules cannot receive certain kinds of cause however consequential those causes are. Nobody has to make the political argument, because the argumentative work is done by the evidentiary standard, and the standard looks like a fact about rock and ice rather than a choice anyone made.

Does this configuration have an established name? I know it sits somewhere near the general claim that observation is never neutral, and near the worry that the way we measure something quietly fixes what we turn up; and I know there are people who call the whole periodization debate political, or Eurocentric. Those labels feel too coarse to me: they would apply to almost any dispute, whereas what I am describing is a specific structure — a substantive commitment about the contested question carried by a discipline's admissibility rules, so that following the rules looks like staying neutral while in fact deciding the matter. I want to know which existing concepts come closest, how what I have described differs from each of them, and which, if any, fits best. If nothing fits, say so plainly and coin a term for it, with a definition and boundary conditions: what would count as a case of it, what would be merely the ordinary fact that evidence depends on what you already believe, and what would be plain bias wearing a technical mask rather than a genuine methodological constraint.
