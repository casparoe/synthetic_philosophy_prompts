Our reading group on the foundations of inductive inference has been going round in circles for two terms now, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of the argument itself rather than a summary of Solomonoff induction — everyone in the room already knows the machinery.

Two camps. The universalists hold that algorithmic probability settles the philosophical business: the universal prior dominates every computable semi-measure up to a multiplicative constant, so occams razor isn't something you assume, it falls out of the counting of short descriptions; the objection that you had to choose a reference machine is handled by the invariance theorem, since any two universal machines yield priors differing only by a constant factor; and the uncomputability is no problem at all, it's an idealisation in the way a frictionless plane or a Carnot cycle is, with Levin search, the speed prior and context-tree weighting standing in as the tractable shadows it casts. The sceptics say none of that is a solution. The universal prior builds in a substantial contingent claim about the world — that whatever generated our data is computably describable — and that claim, not any theorem, is doing the work. No-free-lunch says that averaged over all possible worlds every learner performs as well as every other, so the supposed privilege of the universal prior is just an artefact of which worlds you chose to average over. And an ideal no finite agent could implement can't function as a norm for anybody: you can't tell someone they ought to hold the beliefs an uncomputable function would hold.

As far as I can tell, the stall is mutual question-begging. The universalists say the no-free-lunch average over all worlds is itself a prior, and a uniform one at that, so the sceptics have no standing to object to priors. The sceptics say the idealisation move already assumes that shortness tracks truth, which is what had to be shown. Two years, no movement, and both sides think they've won.

Three hypotheses of mine, offered strictly as hypotheses:

(1) They're answering different questions behind one slogan. The universalists are answering: which semi-measure dominates all computable ones, and what happens in the limit? The sceptics are answering: what entitles a finite, embodied agent to project this regularity forward? "Solves the problem of induction" is doing double duty.

(2) There's a hidden empirical question — whether the world's regularities are computably describable — that both sides treat as closed. And I suspect the idealisation analogy fails, because in physics you're entitled to an idealisation once you've shown it doesn't change the predictions in the regime you care about, whereas throwing out every non-computable source does change what you'd predict. It isn't a smoothing approximation, it's a restriction on the hypothesis space.

(3) The real crux is about what a constant is allowed to do. I tried this one myself and got shot down. I said: take a handicapped machine, one where every program has to begin with a million bits of compulsory junk; its prior is then 2^-1000000 times the standard one, so for any creature with a finite lifespan the invariance theorem buys nothing. Someone pointed out that a uniform rescaling cancels when you form the conditional predictive probability, so the handicapped machine makes exactly the same predictions as the standard one and my objection was empty. I think they're right about that. But I don't think the whole line is empty, because the theorem only bounds the ratio between two universal priors — it doesn't say the ratio is the same for every string. So your choice of machine can shift finite predictions, and can shift how long the error bound takes to bite. Whether that's a new objection or a restatement of the old one, its not obvious to me.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Verbal, substantive, empirical in disguise, two questions wearing one coat, or a case where each side is legitimately entitled to accuse the other of circularity because no non-circular answer is available at this level? Tell me which of my three hypotheses is closest and correct me where I'm wrong, arguing from what the two camps actually say rather than from what their labels suggest. Then be concrete about progress: is there a distinction that would break the deadlock, a theorem somebody could prove — say, about how far a change of reference machine can move finite-time predictions on a natural class of sequences — an experiment, or a reframing that would count as resolving the problem rather than dissolving it? And if your honest answer is that nothing will move this and the circularity is just what disputes at this depth always look like, say so and say why.
