I write a monthly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently drafting a piece of roughly two thousand words on the ethics of insect control — pesticide applications, mosquito fogging, and the broader question of what we owe the enormous numbers of invertebrates our food system and public health measures routinely kill. The argument I want to make is unconventional enough that I do not trust my own judgment about whether it holds, and I would value a critical reader before I commit to it in print.

What follows is the argumentative spine of the piece in draft. I have numbered the paragraphs so that your comments can point at specific places. It is not yet prose I would publish; some of it is deliberately compressed. Please treat it as a draft and read it adversarially.

1. Every summer a truck drives through the coastal neighborhoods after dusk, trailing a fog of fine oil and insecticide. Residents are told to bring the dog in and shut the windows. The notice names a mosquito species and a virus. Nobody counts the dead, and the fog is not selective: it takes the mosquitoes and also the moths, the lacewings, the bees caught away from the hive, and the midges that were food for everything else. The county measures success by trap counts and by human case numbers. Both fall. By every administrative standard available, the truck is a success.

2. The public conversation about insect decline is conducted almost entirely in the register of loss. A long-running survey using standardized traps in German nature reserves — places set aside to protect nature — found that the total biomass of flying insects there had fallen by more than three-quarters over roughly three decades. Pollinators, birds that feed on insects, whole food webs: the story is one of subtraction, and it is told as though the death of an insect were self-evidently a harm and the disappearance of insects self-evidently a tragedy.

3. A smaller and stranger conversation runs alongside it. Recent reviews of the neural and behavioral evidence have concluded that the old confidence that insects cannot feel pain is no longer safe to hold. If some insects are subjects of a life, however thin, then insecticide is not environmental management but a method of killing on a scale no other human activity approaches. A single season of broad-spectrum spraying on a single farm may end more individual lives than all the wars of the twentieth century combined.

4. My claim is that this framing, though sincere, gets the moral structure of the problem backwards, because it treats headcounts as the currency. It assumes that each insect killed is one unit of harm and then adds units together. Insect lives are not built the way that arithmetic requires.

5. A female moth may lay hundreds of eggs, and in a good year a very small fraction of her offspring will reach adulthood. Most die as eggs, as newly hatched larvae, as pupae: to weather, to starvation, to fungal infection, to predators, and above all to parasitoid wasps and flies that lay eggs in a host and consume it from the inside over the course of days. This is not a marginal feature of insect ecology; it is the central fact of it. For most insects, death is not an interruption of the life cycle. It is the default route through it.

6. I want to name this condition mortality saturation: where background pre-adult mortality is already close to total, an additional cause of death subtracts far less than it appears to. Killing a larva that had a one-in-fifty chance of becoming an adult does not remove an adult. It removes a one-in-fifty chance of one. A spray that kills a million caterpillars may therefore have destroyed far fewer than a million insect lives, in the sense that should matter — lives that would otherwise have been completed.

7. Saturation has a second consequence, and this is where I hope the piece earns its keep. If the intervention mostly does not destroy completed lives, then the harm it does cannot be measured in lives at all. It must be measured in durations and in the manner of dying. The question stops being how many insects die and becomes how they die. That reframing inverts some familiar rankings.

8. Consider two families of insecticide. Contact neurotoxins — the pyrethroids, the organophosphates — knock an insect down quickly and indiscriminately: a great many non-targets die, but they die fast. Insect growth regulators, the chitin synthesis inhibitors and the juvenile hormone analogues, are marketed as gentler and more specific, and are usually presented as the ethically preferable choice. They do not poison the animal so much as interrupt molting and metamorphosis, so that the target dies at its next molt and many non-target species are left alone. If headcount is the currency, the growth regulator wins, and it is treated as winning. If duration and manner of dying are the currency, the ordering may invert: the growth regulator delivers a slow, disorganizing death, and in some cases the insect does not simply die but develops into an intermediate form that fails over a longer period. Spraying less, and spraying more slowly, is not automatically better.

9. It follows that letting nature take its course is not a neutral baseline. Declining to spray a caterpillar outbreak and allowing the population to be regulated instead by parasitoids and pathogens does not avoid harm; it substitutes one manner of death for another, usually a slower one, undergone from the inside. The pastoral default is not a no-harm option. It is a choice among harms.

10. And the baseline problem is worse than that, because there are three baselines and the piece has to keep them from collapsing into one another. The first is the same field, unsprayed. The second is the natural life history of the individuals in question, which is what saturation appeals to. The third is the future population: suppressing a pest this season means fewer eggs next season, so an intervention can harm no one and still be a harm, by preventing anyone. If insect lives are worth living, prevention is a loss; if they are net negative, prevention is a benefit; and the same spray can be both at once, because the individuals killed now and the individuals never born are different individuals.

11. Where I land is not a policy recommendation but a shift of the burden of argument. Any account of insect ethics that cannot say what makes one manner of insect death worse than another is not yet an account of harm; it is a tally. And the tallies currently driving the conversation — biomass, trap counts, individuals per hectare — are measurements of precisely the thing that matters least.

12. The failure mode I am trying to avoid is a reader compressing all of this into insect lives are cheap, so spraying is fine. That is not my argument and it would be a bad one, since it slides from the claim that an individual larva's expected future is small to the claim that vast numbers of small futures can be waved away. If the draft reads as a defense of the fogging truck, I have failed.

I would be grateful for comments organized under the following headings.

First, the central move. Does mortality saturation survive scrutiny, or is it a fallacy wearing a technical name? I can see at least two attacks on it — that it applies expected-value reasoning to individual animals rather than to populations, and that saturation is itself produced by the density of the population, so that a spray which lowers density changes the very number the argument depends on. Are those the right attacks? Is there a better one?

Second, paragraph eight. Does the ranking inversion hold, or have I built a paradox out of a mechanism I have described too loosely? Where would a trained entomologist say I have gotten the biology wrong, or overextended it?

Third, paragraph twelve. Is my worry warranted by what I have actually written? If so, propose a repair that is structural rather than a disclaimer, since a paragraph saying but of course this does not mean lives are cheap will not survive editing.

Fourth, the seam between levels. Please identify every place where the draft quietly changes from reasoning about individual insects to reasoning about populations, and tell me whether each transition is licensed or smuggled.

Fifth, structure and economy. Which paragraph is doing the least work and should be cut? Does paragraph one earn its place, or is it decoration that a reader will experience as a delay before the argument?

Sixth, the strongest case against me. Write the best objection available from someone who takes insect welfare seriously — not the objection I have already anticipated in paragraph twelve, but one I have not seen. Steelman it in a short paragraph.

Two constraints on your reply, please. Do not send me a reading list, a bibliography, or named studies; I have my own reporting to do and citations I cannot check are worse than none. Where you believe I have a fact wrong, say what you think is wrong and why, and flag your confidence level rather than pointing me at a source. And do not pad: I would rather have six sharp observations than twenty courteous ones. Somewhere between seven hundred and one thousand words, under the headings above.
