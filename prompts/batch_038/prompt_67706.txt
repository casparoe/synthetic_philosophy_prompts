Question: "Emotions have been analysed as judgments, as perceptions, and as feelings. Explain what each analysis claims, and say what recalcitrant emotion is supposed to establish about them. Which analysis comes out best, and why?" (25 marks; rubric is 8 for accurate exposition of the three analyses, 9 for the recalcitrance discussion, 8 for the comparative verdict and its defence.)

Answer:

"There are basically three ways to say what an emotion is. The judgement theory says an emotion is an evaluation — to be afraid of the dog is to judge that the dog is dangerous. The big advantage is that it explains why emotions can be reasonable or unreasonable. A judgement is the sort of thing that can be supported by evidence or contradicted by it, so fear can be assessed in a way that a shiver can't. It also explains why emotions are about things, and why you can argue someone down out of one: if the judgement goes, the emotion should go with it.

The feeling theory says the emotion is the felt character of the episode — the tightness in the chest, the racing heart, the way everything feels different while you're in it. On this view being afraid just is feeling your body getting ready. The third option, the perception theory, is a more developed version of the same idea: it says emotions are perceptions, so what you're registering is a felt change in you rather than a thought about the situation.

Recalcitrant emotions are cases where the emotion hangs on after the belief has gone. I know the spider on the wall is harmless, I have no doubt about it at all, and I still won't go near it. Frequent flyers know the plane is statistically the safest way to travel and are still white-knuckled through takeoff. This is meant to be a problem for the judgement theory, for exactly the reason given above: if the emotion were the judgement, then removing the judgement would remove the emotion, and it doesn't. It's a problem for the perception theory too, becuase you can't be perceiving danger and simultaneously believing there's none — the two states have contradictory contents and one of them has to give. It's also the same problem as the objection that animals and babys have emotions without being able to make judgements.

So the judgement theory and the perception theory both fail, and what's left standing is the feeling theory: the emotion is just the feel of the episode, and whatever it's about gets supplied by the beliefs that happen to be going on at the same time. This is confirmed by anxiety, which is the one emotion with no object at all — you just feel bad — and it fits perfectly, since on the feeling theory there was never any requirement that an emotion be about something in the first place. The feeling theory therefore comes out best: it doesn't have to explain the spider away, it just says the fear is a real state of the person even though it doesn't match what they believe."
