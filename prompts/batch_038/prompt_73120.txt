For the past couple of years I have been following a debate in ML-adjacent writing about whether large language models are, in the relevant sense, sphexish — named after the digger wasp, whose nesting routine is apparently sophisticated but collapses into an endless loop if you drag its paralyzed prey a few inches while it is checking the burrow. I work on inference infrastructure rather than on evaluation, so I come at this as an engineer, but I have read enough of both sides to be frustrated: the arguments are all familiar, the evidence cited by each side is well documented, and nobody has moved an inch.

One camp holds that anything whose behavior is cued by surface form rather than governed by a representation of the goal is a sphex, however fluent. Their evidence is behavioral and, to my eye, not weak: format brittleness across trivial rephrasings, confidently repeated errors after correction, benchmark contamination, and the way a model will happily re-run an entire failed plan because the failure looked slightly different than the one it was told about. The other camp holds that the sphex charge is either an unfalsifiable moving of the goalposts — any finite test can be dismissed as the system having gotten lucky — or a scale argument in disguise, amounting to the claim that enough of the wrong kind of thing can never add up to the right kind, and that the impressive transfers and novel multi-step solutions we already see are being discounted on principle rather than explained.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about models, and that pulling it apart requires separating at least three distinct disputes that everyone is running together:

1. A verbal one about the word flexible — is it behavioral generalization over a held-out task family, or a counterfactual/dispositional property about how the system would respond to perturbations nobody has yet tried? These come apart constantly in the arguments, and I suspect each side is using a different one while thinking they are using the same one.
2. A dispute about what counts as data — one side treats a brittleness case as evidence of a categorical property of the architecture, the other treats the identical case as a performance parameter that improves with scale or with better training data. Under those two readings the same observation supports opposite conclusions, which would explain why evidence never lands.
3. A deeper question about whether the issue is even empirically decidable, or whether the standard being applied is one that no physically implemented system could ever satisfy because it is implicitly defined in terms of a designer's-eye view of what the system ought to be doing.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on the models. Specifically: sort out which of these three disputes is doing the most work in the actual exchange, and say whether the impasse indicates that the question is ill-posed, or merely hard. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. I am especially interested in whether there is a decidable fragment of the question — something like a moved-prey protocol, where you interrupt a system mid-routine with a controlled perturbation drawn from a specified distribution and measure whether it resumes by adapting to the new state or restarts the whole script — that could be run against current systems and would actually discriminate between the two camps rather than being absorbed into whichever side's prior. If the answer is that no such experiment could ever be decisive, I want the argument for why, stated precisely enough that I could check it, rather than a restatement of the intuition.
