Malthus died in 1834, a decade before the Irish famine and more than a century before Bengal in 1943 and the Great Leap Forward. Suppose he were given the record of those three catastrophes along with Sen's entitlement analysis — the claim that mass starvation typically reflects the collapse of particular groups' capacity to command food rather than an aggregate shortfall, and that famines do not occur in functioning democracies with a free press — and asked for his considered reply. Reconstruct it. I want his verdict case by case: which famines he would still file under the principle of population, and where he would concede that distribution and political choice did the causal work. I want his answer to whether Sen's distinction between the food a society possesses and the food a given labourer can command is something he had already argued, since on his own account a man whose labour nobody wants has no claim on the parish's granaries however full they are, or whether he would treat it as a mechanism smuggled in place of a law. I want the single feature of the Bengal evidence he would find hardest to absorb, bearing in mind that the wartime official inquiry and Sen read the aggregate rice figures very differently. And I want the condition under which he would revise the principle of population itself rather than merely annex a further check to it.

Argue in his own vocabulary — preventive and positive checks, population pressing against subsistence, effective demand for labour — but flag explicitly wherever you are extrapolating beyond what he actually committed himself to, since a confident fabrication about Malthus is worse than an admitted gap.
