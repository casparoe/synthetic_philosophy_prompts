I sit on the grants panel of a community foundation in a mid-sized old manufacturing town — nine of us, mostly local business people, a couple from nonprofits, and me becuase I've apparently been on the board long enough to be institutional memory. This cycle there's one proposal that has split the panel down the middle, and I've been asked to circulate a written recommendation before Thursday's meeting so people have something concrete to react to instead of arguing in the room. The proposal: a workers' center wants roughly $340k over three years to pay about 120 low-wage people — warehouse pickers, home health aides, grocery stockers, line cooks — the equivalent of six weeks of their wages, straight to them, with no employer involved and no string attached to a job, so they can simply not work for six weeks. The part nobody can agree on is the design. The application asks each recipient to submit a short written plan for what they intend to do with the time (rest, look after a parent, take a course, finish something they started), and the foundation's evaluator insists the program be measured on whether recipients are still employed nine months out, whether their earnings rose, and whether they used fewer sick days.

Two of us think the plan requirement inverts the entire idea: the moment someone has to account for their free time, leisure has become another unpaid project with a deliverable, and the one thing this money is supposed to buy back is time that doesn't have to be justified to anybody. The evaluator's answer is that she has to raise the renewal money, the only language funders here speak is outcomes, and if we can't show that six weeks off makes someone a steadier worker there is no fourth year — which awkwardly means we'd be defending leisure by proving it improves work, which is exactly the concession half of us are trying not to make. There's also an eligibility fight I don't think the panel has noticed is the same fight: the proposal limits eligibility to people with verifiable hours from a single employer, which cleanly excludes the two most obviously overworked groups we can name — the aide piecing together shifts at four different agencies, and the full-time caregiver who hasn't had a paycheck in eleven years. On top of that, one trustee who lived in Japan for a few years keeps raising death-from-overwork and wants the memo to say something about health risk rather than only about dollars, and there's an unresolved argument over whether to allocate by lottery or by a weighted need score.

So: draft the memo I'll send around. It needs a clear bottom line up front — fund it, fund it with conditions, or send it back for revision — and the conditions have to be ones the workers' center could realistically accept or bargain over, not gestures. It should engage the evaluator's position seriously, because she isn't wrong about the money and if the memo steamrolls her the panel won't adopt it; I want the strongest version of her case stated in a way she'd recognize. It should also say, in plain English and without jargon, what the disagreement underneath is actually about, since I suspect five of the nine people don't realize we're arguing over whether free time has to earn its keep, and whether "work" is even the right category for the caregiver. And be honest about what a memo like this can't settle. No citations, no reading list, no bibliography — I couldn't check them and I'd rather they not be there; my whole repertoire on this is one party trick about a famous economist who predicted we'd all be working fifteen hours a week by now and worried the hard part would be what to do with the rest. Please write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just paragraphs, the way you'd write a letter to colleagues — somewhere around 900 to 1200 words.
