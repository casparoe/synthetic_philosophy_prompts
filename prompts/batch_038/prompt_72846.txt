So I'm one of six voting members on my city's automated-decision review board, and Thursday we have to rule on a Parks & Rec proposal that has the whole board arguing past each other. I've been asked to write up the recommendation memo, and I want to get it right.

The proposal: instead of surveying residents about which classes and programs they want, Parks wants to *infer* it. A vendor system learns a reward function from behavior — signup abandonment, how long you hover on a listing, waitlist joins, no-shows, cancellations — and then ranks and recommends programming accordingly. Technically it's inverse reinforcement learning dressed up in marketing language, and they call the output "preference learning."

A neighborhood coalition invoked our policy clause requiring "meaningful notice of the criteria used to rank or allocate services affecting a resident," and they're demanding that each resident recieve a plain-language statement of what the system thinks they want, plus a way to contest it. Parks' technical lead says that's impossible in principle, not merely expensive: a reward recovered from behavior isn't pinned down uniquely, because whole families of reward functions that differ in ways that leave the optimal behavior untouched are equally consistent with the data, so any printed statement picks one arbitrary member of an equivalence class and attributes goals to people they'd disavow — while the system's recommendations would be byte-for-byte identical under the alternative. Also, they say, there's no per-person reward function to begin with; it's one shared model conditioned on features.

Board is split. One member says the underdetermination point makes the coalition's demand incoherent and we should just approve. Another says that's precisely why we shouldn't: a system that can't say what it takes you to want can't be contested, and contestability was the point of the clause.

Two wrinkles neither side is addressing. First, most of the training behavior comes from last year's pilot, which was itself recommending things — so a chunk of the "revealed" preferences were manufactured by the system, and the vendor's headline accuracy number is holdout agreement with behavior-under-recommendation, which looks circular to me. Second, subsidized kids' swim slots get allocated by the same ranking, so opting out costs you access.

Draft the memo the board could actually act on: approve, deny, or approve with conditions. If conditions, spell out concretely what Parks would have to disclose or offer residents that *survives* the underdetermination objection — I suspect the answer lives at the level of what the system does differently under some counterfactual rather than any statement of a reward, but argue it rather than assuming it — how you'd handle the feedback-loop problem and the people who can't opt out, and what the timeline should be for pilot versus full rollout. And give the strongest case for whichever side you don't land on, in a form that side would recognize as their own argument.
