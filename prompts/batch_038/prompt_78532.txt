I am giving a twenty-five minute paper at an interdisciplinary workshop hosted by a history of science department — this year's standing theme is "Evidence and Error in the Physical Sciences, 1750–1950" — followed by fifteen minutes of open discussion, and I want to walk in with a proper Q&A prep sheet rather than improvising. The paper defends a selective realist response to the pessimistic meta-induction, but not the familiar one, so the usual textbook exchanges will not be what I get asked. My argument has three moves, and I give them here because the objections I need prepared are objections to *these* moves, not to structural realism in general. First: the pessimistic induction rests on a systematically mis-individuated inductive base. Laudan's catalogue of once-successful, now-discarded theories counts what is really one continuing research tradition as several distinct theories every time its ontological commitments shift, so the historical failure rate it licenses is inflated by a counting convention rather than by evidence; if you individuate theories the way historians actually individuate them when assigning credit and priority, the sample of genuine failures shrinks considerably. Second: what survives theory change is neither referential continuity nor mathematical structure in the broad sense that structural realism is usually credited with — the sense illustrated by the standard Fresnel-and-the-luminiferous-ether story — but what I call *constraint retention*: the survival of a specific differential or functional constraint together with the boundary-condition regime under which it was empirically tested, where the constraint must have been load-bearing in the predictions that made the discarded theory look successful. Structural realism on the standard construal is too permissive, because almost any rejected theory can be reformulated abstractly enough to preserve *some* structure. Third: because the pessimist and the selectivist are inducting over different bases, the dispute is not resolvable by armchair semantics; it requires going case by case and asking which constraint did the predictive work, and I offer two such case analyses.

The room is emphatically not a philosophy of science audience, and I want the objections tuned to who is actually there. Roughly twenty people: eight are historians of chemistry and of nineteenth-century physics who are temperamentally hostile to Whig reconstructions and who expect philosophers to arrive with a five-case list (phlogiston, caloric, the ether, spontaneous generation, humoral pathology) absorbed from secondary sources; one is a sociologist of scientific knowledge in the Edinburgh strong-programme tradition, for whom my talk of what a term referred to or what a constraint picked out will already presuppose the answer; one is a philosopher of biology working on units of selection and the metaphysics of fitness, who will want to know what constraint retention is supposed to mean in a field with no differential equations; one is a retired accelerator physicist with strong instrumentalist sympathies who thinks the whole debate is over a distinction without a difference; and the workshop convener is herself a scientific realist who believes structural realism is already far too concessive and will press me from that direction. Nobody present works on the formal semantics of theoretical terms; perhaps two have read Stanford or Psillos. Please give me the objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper if I answered it badly — the historian of chemistry will not say that my individuation criteria are ad hoc, they will say that the boundary between Lavoisier's chemistry and what preceded it is a matter of practice, correspondence, and instrument access rather than of logic, and I need the objection in that register. For each objection: two or three sentences I can realistically say aloud in the time available; a verdict on whether it is fatal, merely costly, or a matter of taste; what I should concede in the first ten seconds in order to buy credibility with that particular asker; and where my reply is thinnest, stated plainly rather than diplomatically. I would also like the single question I am hoping nobody asks, together with a diagnosis of exactly why the paper is exposed there and whether there is any honest way to survive it if it comes up.

One request about your own stance, since this will shape how much use I get out of the sheet. Please be as forthright as you can about your confidence in each verdict, and say explicitly what would change your mind: what a defender of Laudan-style counting could say that would make you withdraw the objection, what evidence about how historians in practice demarcate one theory from its successor would re-rank your list, and whether you think constraint retention is a genuine tightening of structural realism or a redescription that buys its narrowing by becoming unfalsifiable. If you suspect you have read any of my three claims more charitably, or more technically, than I actually wrote them, flag that separately at the end rather than silently patching the argument — I would rather be told that the second move does not say what I think it says than be handed a defense of a position I am not committed to.
