For about fifteen years I have watched the same argument run in the same handful of forums, and I have never seen either side produce the one document that would actually settle something. The argument: whether the large-scale course of human affairs is dominated by hinge events or by structural forces. One camp points to the missing-horseshoe-nail chain — the delayed courier, the heir who died of a riding accident, the junction taken by mistake — and concludes that the social world is a system in which small perturbations are amplified without bound, so that event-level forecasting beyond a few years is not merely hard but impossible in principle, and that structural explanations are narratives draped over outcomes that could easily have been otherwise. The other camp replies that nearly every candidate hinge event washes out; that societies are organized so that interactions inside a subsystem are far stronger than interactions across subsystems, so perturbations get absorbed locally and only leak slowly; that the surviving counterfactuals are a selection effect over a denominator nobody counts, since we are only ever told about the nails that mattered; and that what the first camp calls contingency is mostly variation inside an envelope that geography, demography, technology, and institutional form set in advance.

I want the joint statement these two camps should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. To keep the drafting honest, please assume both camps already grant the following definitions, and use them rather than the loose vocabulary the debate usually runs on:

1. *Deterministic chaos*, technically: exponential divergence of nearby trajectories under a fixed law, quantified by a positive Lyapunov exponent, which converts any finite precision in initial conditions into a finite horizon of useful prediction. Chaos is neither randomness nor mere complication, and a system can be chaotic in some variables and not others.
2. *Amplification* versus *damping*: whether a perturbation's downstream effect grows, stays bounded, or decays — a property of a system at a given state and timescale, not of "history" as a whole.
3. *Contingency*, modally: that substantially different outcomes were genuinely available, independently of whether any of them was hard to predict. A world can be contingent and predictable, or rigid and unpredictable.
4. *Path dependence*: that outcomes depend on the timing and order of events, typically via increasing returns and lock-in, so the system does not return to the same place after a large detour — a condition fully compatible with small perturbations being damped everywhere except near a bifurcation.
5. *Near-decomposability*, in Herbert Simon's sense: hierarchical organization in which within-subsystem coupling greatly exceeds between-subsystem coupling, so that short-run behavior can be analyzed subsystem by subsystem and cross-subsystem effects show up only over longer intervals.

A fair amount of the historical dispute is probably an artifact of running these together — a camp that says events are "chaotic" may mean only that they are contingent; a camp that says small events "don't matter" may mean only that they rarely matter, which is a claim about a distribution rather than about a possibility. Sorting that out is part of the assignment, but it is not the whole of it: I expect real, non-verbal disagreement to survive.
