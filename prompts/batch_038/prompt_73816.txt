**Working title: Between-node and within-node variance, or what the Great Man debate is actually disagreeing about.**

My claim is that structuralists and Great Man theorists are not making competing estimates of the same quantity, which is why the debate feels interminable. Introduce two distinctions. A *node* is a position in an institutional-causal network that some individual must occupy: the chancellorship, the leadership of a mass party, the head of an army. Total variance in a macro-outcome then decomposes into between-node variance, governed by the parameters that determine what kind of person is selected into the node and what the node permits, and within-node variance, governed by which particular individual fills it.

Applied to Germany between 1918 and 1933, the structuralist wins decisively on the mean. Given the terms of the peace settlement, the hyperinflation of 1923, the deflationary response to the Depression, the collapse of the liberal middle parties, and a völkisch antisemitic tradition that long predated Hitler, running from Schönerer's Pan-Germanism through Lueger's municipal antisemitism in Vienna, an authoritarian and antisemitic German regime was highly likely. That is a statement about the center of a distribution.

The Great Man theorist wins on the within-node variance, and it was unusually large. A Strasser-led party, weighted toward its anti-capitalist left wing until Strasser was murdered in the 1934 purge of the SA leadership; a Hugenberg or Papen government, which is a conventional conservative-authoritarian regime with the old elites still in charge; a Schleicher experiment in corporatist military government: these differ not merely in the degree of repression but in the kind of regime produced, and they differ in whether an invasion of the Soviet Union and industrial-scale racial murder sit inside the option set at all.

The interesting move is the explanation of *why* within-node variance was so large. It was not because structures were weak. It was because one structural variable took an extreme value: the attenuation coefficient of the leadership channel. A party organized around personal allegiance to a single leader, rather than around a program or a committee, transmits that leader's private dispositions into state policy with very little loss. Kershaw's description of subordinates working toward the Führer names exactly this mechanism, since anticipation and preemption of the leader's wishes allow his dispositions to propagate without ever being issued as orders.

So the conclusion is a dissolution rather than a synthesis: Hitler mattered enormously not in spite of structure but because of a structural peculiarity that turned his office into a low-attenuation channel. The Great Man thesis is not a rival of structuralism; it is a claim that a structural parameter took an outlier value.
