My brother-in-law Marcus and I have been going around on one thing for years and I want a read on him, not really on the topic.

The thing we argue about is reasons that have nothing to do with the thing the attitude is about. Like if somebody offers you $500 to genuinely admire his hideous ceramic lawn gnome — not fake it, actually admire it — is the $500 a reason to admire the gnome? We've piled up a bunch of these over the years and here's how he's come down on them.

1. The gnome. He said yes, obviously the $500 is a reason. Then he said, but notice it's not a reason about the gnome. I asked what that's supposed to mean and he changed the subject, so I don't know if it meant anything.

2. My mom tells people who are sick that believing you'll get better helps you get better. Marcus says that is not a reason to believe you'll get better, it's a reason to want to believe it. I called that hair splitting and he said no, that's the whole ballgame.

3. I'm scared of the deep end of the lake and I know it's stupid, I'm a strong swimmer and it's completely safe. He said I have every reason to be scared and got irritated when I pointed out the deep end isn't dangerous. His line was, fear isn't a verdict about danger, it's a seatbelt.

4. There's a genuinely bad watercolor in my hallway that my late grandfather painted. I said, well, at least I have a reason to admire it, it's his. Marcus said no. That's a reason to keep it on the wall and a reason to wish you admired it. His painting it doesn't make it any better.

5. My uncle paid my tuition and has never once let me forget it, and I'm fairly sure he did it so he'd have something over me. Marcus says I still have reason to be grateful. Gratitude answers to what somebody did for you, not to what was in his heart.

And one time in passing he said something like: if you can actually sit there and work on yourself until the admiration is real, then the money was a real reason; if all you can do is put on a show and hope nobody checks, it wasn't. I couldn't tell if he meant that or if he'd take it back.

Here's the case I want to spring on him Saturday, and I want to know what you think he says before I do it. My daughter has a piano recital coming up and she's already decided she's going to freeze. My wife's whole approach is, believe you can get through it and you'll get through it. By #2 Marcus should say that's not a reason for her to believe it, it's a reason for her to want to believe it. But there's a difference here I can't get around: in this case her believing it is one of the things that makes it true. She freezes *because* she's sure she'll freeze, so if she believed she wouldn't. So is the fact that believing it helps make it true a reason to believe it? It doesn't feel like the $500. It's not about how she'll feel afterwards, it's about whether the thing is true. And if he says no, I'm going to ask him why #3 gets to be an attitude with a job — fear is a seatbelt — while this one doesn't. Why does an attitude get to have a job in one case and not the other? Also #5 seems to say the attitude answers to the deed and not the heart, which cuts the other way again.

So: what does he say, and why? I want more than one candidate rule, because I think at least two or three different rules come out right on all five of those and I can't tell them apart from the inside. Say which of my five is the closest thing to the recital case and whether he's actually entitled to use it, whether the five even pin him down, and if they don't, give me the case that would — one where two of the rules that have fit everything so far finally come apart and he has to pick. And be honest about whether he's just contradicting himself between 3 and 4.

I'm not in school and I don't want a reading list, I'd never look at it. I just want your best guess at what's going on in his head and what would settle it.
