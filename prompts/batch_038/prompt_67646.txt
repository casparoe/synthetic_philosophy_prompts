Last term, after my single lecture on expected value, a first-year student stopped me in the corridor to ask whether she should switch in the two-envelope problem, and I could not give her a clean answer; I have now been handed the fifty-minute section in which that paradox is the scheduled topic, for students whose probability background is coin flips and one definition of expected value. I want a plan for the section rather than an explanation of the paradox. Tell me the order in which the pieces must go in and what depends on what — in particular, whether the naive switching argument should be presented and allowed to succeed before anything is said against it, and whether I first need them to accept that both envelopes are already filled and fixed. Then tell me which single example should carry the session: I am torn between the plain version with an unnamed amount and a version in which the organiser rolls a die to select the smaller amount from 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and students are handed real envelopes. Say what the example must contain — I assume some case in which the number you see tells you which envelope you are holding — and what it must not contain to avoid pre-empting the reasoning I want them to do. I also want a map of the misconceptions with the precise point in the sequence for heading each off: that this is the Monty Hall problem again; that expected value has simply told them to switch and there is nothing further to discuss; that the whole difficulty is an artefact of unbounded or improper priors; and the one I expect to be hardest, from a student who concedes the boundary cases and then insists that at every remaining amount switching still looks favourable, so it looks favourable overall. State which of these I should let form and then dismantle, and which I should prevent outright. Finally: one closing question whose answers would separate the students who understand why the argument fails from those who have merely learned to distrust it; and what I should leave out of fifty minutes and announce that I am leaving out. Be explicit about how confident you are in these pedagogical claims, especially your ranking of the misconceptions by how quickly they will surface in a live room, and single out the point you are least sure of.
