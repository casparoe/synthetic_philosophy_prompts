I'm TAing a grad seminar on risk regulation and for the last session I've been told to make the two camps that have been sniping at each other all term actually produce a document together, instead of another round of the same seminar-room theater. One camp defends a *qualified* precautionary principle: when an activity threatens serious and irreversible harm and the probability of that harm can't be credibly bounded from above, the burden shifts to the proponent to make the safety case, and the regulator is entitled to a proportionate response short of a flat ban — containment, staged rollout, sunset clauses, monitoring conditions. The other camp thinks that's incoherence with good PR. Their line is that "can't be credibly bounded" is quietly doing all the normative work, that every precaution has its own risk tail so a principle that forbids risky action forbids its own application, and that once you write down what the qualified version actually licenses in a real decision, it collapses into ordinary decision analysis under ambiguity — maxmin expected utility, robustness criteria, that sort of thing — with a rhetorical wrapper left over. Both sides have read Sunstein's *Laws of Fear*, and both think the other is the one who read it badly.

The case I want them to work through is not one from the literature; I invented it so nobody can just recite a canned position. Call it the Symbiont File. A regulator in a drought-prone agrarian country has one statutory window, roughly a planting season, to decide whether to authorize aerial inoculation of an engineered fungal root symbiont for the staple grain. The upside in the file is large: the agronomic model says that skipping inoculation means a shortfall over two lean seasons that the public-health annex converts into a very big excess-death number. The downside is a recombination pathway. The organism is supposed to be self-limiting via a kill-switch — it depends on a micronutrient that has to be supplied and that local soils lack — but a wild relative of the grain is common in field margins, and if the symbiont transfers into that wild grass and loses the switch, the file says you could end up with a persistent wind-borne root pathogen across the whole belt, with no eradication technology in prospect and no realistic timeline for developing one. Crucially, nobody knows the probability of that. There are no field data at all; every number in the safety case comes from models built by the proponent's own lab, and the regulator's in-house reviewers can't reproduce them and have said so in writing. There is a contained-trial option — fenced, instrumented, three seasons — that would yield actual transfer-rate data, but the window means choosing the trial is choosing to eat the projected shortfall, and the trial isn't perfectly contained either: the file concedes a nonzero escape probability that goes up with plot area. Two legal wrinkles that the seminar keeps dodging: the constitution has both a precaution clause obliging the state to act against threats of serious or irreversible damage even where the science hasn't settled, and a justiciable right to adequate food, so the state can be sued either way. And one more, which I want them to fight about explicitly: the micronutrient for the kill-switch comes from a single foreign supplier, so "self-limiting" depends on a supply chain the regulator doesn't control. One camp thinks that changes the epistemic situation — it turns a biological safety property into a political contingency, which is exactly the kind of thing that can't be bounded by a model. The other camp thinks it's just another risk with a price.

Draft the joint statement the two camps could genuinely both sign. I want (a) a numbered section of claims both accept — about what the file does and doesn't establish, about what irreversibility means here and whether the famine toll is irreversible in the same sense as the pathogen, about how much evidential weight the proponent's unreproducible models carry, and about what distinguishes this case from one where probabilities are known and merely unwelcome; (b) a disagreements table where each row gives the contested question, the precaution camp's formulation in wording they'd sign, the critic camp's formulation in wording they'd sign, and a verdict on whether that row is a substantive disagreement or a verbal one, with a reason; (c) the cruxes — the sub-questions where an answer would actually move at least one side — and for each crux a specific test, case, or piece of evidence both agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction recorded in advance so it's falsifiable later; and (d) one thing each camp would count as evidence against its own position. Don't give me the polite mush where every row resolves to "more research needed," and don't hedge the table. Commit: say how much of this dispute you think is verbal, and then append a short drafter's addendum, signed by you rather than by the camps, stating which camp's central claim survives the exercise and which one you think is doing nothing but rhetoric, with reasons. If your view is that the qualified precautionary principle really does collapse into decision theory under ambiguity, say so and point at where the residue is; if you think there's a genuine residue, name it precisely and explain what work it does that a maxmin-EU or robustness criterion can't do.
