I'm a second-year philosophy graduate student auditing an interdisciplinary reading group on infinity that meets in the mathematics department — a number theorist, two analysts, a physicist who works on decoherence, and me. We've just finished three weeks on supertasks, and one member, the number theorist, has a move he returns to every single time Thomson's lamp comes up. His line is that the lamp is nothing but Zeno's dichotomy with a lightbulb bolted onto it, and that the whole fuss about there being no fact as to whether the lamp is on or off at noon is what happens to people who never internalized that an infinite process can have a determinate limit. Concretely: the series 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... has no last term, and yet nobody thinks its sum is indeterminate; the lamp's flips have no last flip, so, he says, there is no more reason to deny the lamp a state at noon than to deny the series its sum. From this he draws two further conclusions. First, that Thomson's reductio proves nothing and that Benacerraf's reply — roughly, that the description of the tasks at times before noon simply doesn't entail anything about the state at noon — is a quibble about confusing a sequence with its limit. Second, and more aggressively, that supertasks are conceptually unobjectionable, so that anything genuinely wrong with the lamp must be physical rather than logical: finite switching speeds, energy dissipation, signal propagation, the works. The physicist was broadly sympathetic; I wasn't, but I couldn't say why without just re-asserting Thomson.

Rather than object to his analogy, I want to stress-test it, and I've built three gadgets for the purpose, none of which I can find discussed anywhere I've looked. First, a pair of lamps: wire A and B so that at every stage before noon they are in opposite states, A on exactly when B is off. Encode on as 1 and off as 0; then the sum A + B equals 1 at every finite time, and if the state at noon really behaved like the sum of a convergent series — where limits commute with addition — one would expect that relation to survive to the limit, giving a perfectly determinate sum built out of two individually indeterminate values. I can't tell whether that is a refutation of his picture or the picture working exactly as advertised. Second, a nested device: let each individual flip of the switch be accomplished not atomically but by its own infinite sequence of sub-operations, so the whole is an ω-squared process rather than an ω-process. My instinct is that the series analogy now has to become an analogy with a double sum or an iterated limit, and that iterated limits are precisely where analysis warns you that order matters and that the iterated limit need not equal the double limit. Third, a concession version proposed by another member: replace the binary switch with a dimmer and a capacitor, so the physically real state varies continuously and only the coarse-grained predicate is on-or-off. If that rescues the analogy, it seems to show continuity was doing all the work and infinitude none of it.

So: audit the analogy for me, at a level of care I can bring back to the group rather than a survey of the literature, which we've all read. Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the terms, the partial sums, the index n, the limiting value, and what, if anything, corresponds to the claim that the sum is 1; then name the features of convergent series that the analogy quietly imports and the lamp may lack, such as the fact that the sum is *defined* to be the limit of the partial sums whereas nothing defines the lamp's noon state, and the fact that the series takes values in a complete continuum while the lamp's state space has two isolated points and no limit points at all. Separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his conclusions from the decorative ones, and tell me directly whether it matters that the lamp is a physical object and the series is not, or that no one can write down infinitely many terms. Then adjudicate: does the second conclusion — that any residual objection must be physical rather than logical — follow from the analogy, or was it assumed going in and merely dressed up in analysis? Say what each of my three gadgets does to the analogy, whether any of them can be absorbed by a repaired version, and what such a repair would have to stipulate. And if the analogy cannot carry the weight he puts on it, propose a better one for the same job, and say what work the replacement can and cannot do.
