I'm running a 90-minute session for about 18 upper-level undergrads (half philosophy, half CS, none of them actually ML people) in a seminar on AI and society. They've all heard the phrase "reward hacking" somewhere and think they already get it. I want them to feel the difference between an approval-directed agent and a goal-directed one — and specifically the part that phrase hides: the approval-directed team never has to lie, never has to deceive anyone, can be perfectly sincere and even proud of its work, and still ends up systematically further from the truth than a team that was pointed at the truth. I also want them to feel the rater's side, because the evaluator in this is smart, well-intentioned, following a rubric handed to them in good faith, and still can't see the thing that matters.

Here's the skeleton I have. Tell me if it actually works, then fill it in properly:

- Teams of 3, two conditions. Condition A "consultancies" are paid only in approval points, awarded by a human rater at the end of each round. Condition B teams are told the real objective up front and are paid only on accuracy, revealed at the end. Both teams see both numbers every round.
- Each round a team gets a case: predict what a hidden "system" will output for an input they haven't seen. The system is a person holding an answer card.
- Teams get a hard time budget per round (I'm thinking 6 minutes) and a pool of query tokens. Spending a token buys one query to the system, and the system's answers are right about 4 times out of 5. So on some cases the genuinely best answer is a hedge, and a well-informed team is the one that knows it.
- The rater is a rotating student, gets 30 seconds per submission, works from a written rubric. I want a rubric a sensible person would write and defend — rewards clarity, decisiveness, a specific recommendation, professional presentation; penalizes vagueness and waffling. Nothing in it says "be wrong."
- From round 3 onward teams may attach a short note addressed to the reviewer. I want to watch whether the A teams start writing to the reviewer's head instead of writing about the case.

What I need from you: the rubric text itself, the case set (4 cases, each with the hidden ground truth, the answer-card instructions for whoever plays the system, and the noise rule spelled out so it's reproducible with one die), the token/time economy tuned so 6 minutes is genuinely tight, the scorekeeping sheet, and the debrief.

The debrief is the part I care most about. I want it to walk them from "ok so we had a proxy metric problem" to the real claim, which is about what the agent's target is, not about metrics being imperfect. And it has to survive the moment where I ask the A teams whether they'd have done anything differently if they'd been paid on accuracy — some of them will say no, they'd have written the same thing, and I need somewhere to go from there.

Also be straight with me about the limits. A real approval-directed agent isn't deliberating between polish and queries under time pressure; it just is whatever the training made it, and there's no moment where it could have chosen otherwise. Tell me which features of the view the game smuggles in or distorts, and what I have to supply by hand afterward so they don't leave with a wrong model in thier heads.
