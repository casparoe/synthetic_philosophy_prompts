I am drafting the framing chapter of a thesis in formal epistemology, and I have stalled on the dispute over whether rational credence must be sharp. I have now worked through the arguments on both sides several times, and my impression is that the literature has settled into a stable equilibrium: every move has a standard countermove, the countermoves have standard rejoinders, and nobody changes position. I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement — not a verdict on which side is right, and not a survey of the field.

The set-up as I understand it. One camp holds that an ideally rational agent's doxastic state is a single probability function; anything else is either a defect or a useful fiction about an agent who has not finished making up her mind. The other camp holds that some bodies of evidence simply do not determine a unique probability, so a rational state is a family of probability functions — a convex set of priors, or upper and lower previsions in the sense developed by Walley, or Levi's indeterminate probabilities — and that forcing sharpness smuggles in information the evidence does not supply, typically a principle of indifference where none is licensed. The sharp side is associated with Elga's insistence that subjective probabilities should be sharp; the behavioral fuel for the imprecise side is ambiguity-aversion data of the Ellsberg kind and the multiple-priors decision theories built to model it. Practitioners working on decisions under deep uncertainty (robustness-based planning, say) mostly bypass the whole question, which I take to be data for a diagnosis rather than a neutral fact.

The argument families I keep re-encountering:

(a) Accuracy. Relative to a strictly proper scoring rule, an imprecise state is accuracy-dominated by some sharp state — a line Joyce pressed and then complicated, his later work being friendlier to imprecision than the argument suggests. Reply: scoring a set requires an aggregation rule across its members (best case, worst case, average, or something spread-sensitive), and fixing one already presupposes an account of what the object under assessment is; moreover dominance is a claim about whole states, not about the questions the agent actually faces, and the dominating sharp state may be one the imprecise agent has no grounds to regard as her own.

(b) Dynamic coherence. Imprecise agents suffer dilation — their intervals widen toward total ignorance on learning information that is intuitively irrelevant, as the results of Seidenfeld and collaborators suggest is unavoidable — and they face problems with reflection and with money-pump-style sequences of choices. Reply: dilation may simply be the right verdict about what the evidence licenses after an uninformative observation; and the dynamic criticisms require pinning the imprecise agent to a decision rule (maximin, E-admissibility, Γ-maximin) that the camp has not agreed upon, so the objection targets a decision theory rather than a credal state.

(c) The reverse charge, that sharpness is arbitrary. Under symmetric or nearly empty evidence, no particular sharp value is picked out by anything; and the representation theorems deliver precision only because they assume completeness of the agent's comparative confidence, or of her preferences — an idealization that makes the mathematics work rather than a requirement of rationality. Reply from the sharp camp: an agent whose comparative confidence is incomplete cannot be reasoned with, scored, or updated; incompleteness is not a richer description but an abdication; and the claim that evidence fails to determine a unique probability equivocates between a statement about evidence and a statement about the agent.

(d) Behavior. Ambiguity-aversion shows that competent agents are not sharp. Reply: that is psychology, and the dispute is normative; or, ambiguity attitudes can be represented sharply with higher-order probabilities.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and to be tested rather than indulged: the whole dispute turns on (c) — completeness is the hinge, the other families are downstream of it, and the debate looks broad while in fact resting on one contested axiom about what rationality demands of an ordering of uncertainty. A rival hypothesis I want weighed: the camps are answering different questions, one about the normative structure of an ideal agent's doxastic state and the other about what a bounded inquirer is licensed to represent and act on, with the appearance of a single debate an artifact of a shared word and a shared formal idiom.

What I want from you:

1. A diagnosis. What kind of disagreement is this — verbal, resting on a single hinge axiom, two questions under one heading, a disguised empirical question about agents, an irreducible normative standoff, a single well-posed question that happens to be unresolved, or an ill-posed one?
2. An argument-by-argument assessment of (a) through (d): which genuinely engage the opposing camp and which beg the question, and against whom. I want to see where the dialectic has traction and where it spins.
3. Whether my completeness hypothesis survives. If it does, explain why the dispute has not been recognized as resting on it; if it does not, say what the real hinge is.
4. What would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a theorem, a change of question, an experiment, or nothing — specified tightly enough that I could tell whether it had been achieved.

Two standards. First, do not give me a bibliography or a reading list, and do not attribute claims to particular papers unless you are confident of them; I will handle sourcing, and I am after structure. Second, separate clearly what is settled among people who work on this, if anything is, from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position — on either side — as the settled view. If any of my descriptions above is unfaithful to how the position is actually held, correct it before building on it.
