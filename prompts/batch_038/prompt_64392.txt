I'm working on an ideas piece about what general relativity did to the old quarrel over whether space is something in its own right or merely a scheme of relations among material bodies. The gravitational physicist I interviewed has a vivid line for it: spacetime, he says, is the luminiferous aether back under a new name — it has degrees of freedom of its own, black hole mergers shake it, and the resulting waves carry energy we can now detect on Earth — so relationism is finished and Newton's bucket is settled. He added that Einstein himself, a few years after 1915, allowed that the theory endows space with physical qualities and was willing to speak of an ether of sorts, provided it had no mechanical properties and no state of motion.

Could you audit the analogy for me? I'd like the correspondences stated explicitly — what in the theory plays the medium, what answers to a wave in it, what answers to being at rest with respect to it, and what answers to the drag effects that nineteenth-century aether theories were built to explain — and then the disanalogies, sorted into those that genuinely threaten his inference and those that are merely picturesque. In particular: what does the analogy quietly assume about spacetime points having identities of their own, and does the awkwardness of localizing gravitational energy cut against it even though the waves plainly deliver energy? Finally, if "aether" is the wrong hook, what would be a better one — and could a relationist accept that better analogy without embarrassment? Please say how confident you are in the claims you make, and flag the single point you are least sure of.
