I'm assembling an essay for a collection on political aesthetics, and my piece pairs Rousseau's *Letter to d'Alembert on the Theater* (1758) with Peter Weir's *The Truman Show* (1998). The obvious pairing is too easy — spectacle bad, spectator passive, Christof as tyrannical playwright — and I want to push past it. So I'd like a close reading of the film that treats the Letter not as a mood but as a set of specific claims with mechanisms attached: that theater does not purge or purify the passions but feeds and legitimizes them; that dramatic pity is a sterile, self-congratulating feeling whose function is to license the spectator's inaction rather than to move him to act; that plays cannot teach virtue because virtue is a matter of will and deed while spectacle can only stir sentiment, and comedy in particular makes the honest man ridiculous by turning his scruples into the audience's entertainment; that the actor's trade is a form of self-alienation, a person publicly playing another for pay, which Rousseau links to deceit and to a loss of civic standing; and that spectatorship gathers citizens together while leaving each of them alone, in contrast to the open-air festival in which a people see themselves and so become participants rather than an audience. Please test each of these against the film rather than merely listing them, and be concrete about where each lands: the two viewers who, moments after Truman walks out of the dome, ask what else is on and reach for a remote; the man in the bathtub and the drinkers in the bar; Christof conducting the storm from the control room in the artificial moon; the final bow and catchphrase; and above all Seahaven itself, a town of parades, neighborliness, and small-scale sociability that looks for all the world like Rousseau's festival and is in fact a set.

The puzzle I can't resolve is that Seahaven seems to satisfy Rousseau's *positive* criterion almost perfectly. There is no audience inside the fiction, no ticket, no division between stage and seats; everyone performs together in a self-enclosed community shielded from a corrupt outside world, and Christof's defense of the dome — that the world beyond it is no more truthful and considerably more frightening — sounds uncomfortably like Rousseau's defense of Geneva against Paris. And yet Seahaven is a horror. Does the film thereby show that Rousseau's diagnosis targets the wrong feature, so that what corrupts is not spectatorship but unfreedom and deceit? Or can Rousseau absorb Seahaven by insisting that a gathering whose performers are paid, whose central participant never consented, and whose whole life is staged for absent watchers is not a festival at all but theater wearing festival's clothes? I want you to argue for one of these, not to split the difference politely.

I also want the honest counter-pressure, since a reading that only confirms Rousseau is worthless to me. Two places the film seems to resist him: first, Sylvia's watching converts into organizing, and the campaign she joins is precisely the audience-member-becomes-actor transformation Rousseau says theater cannot produce; second, Truman leaves by way of a theatrical gesture — a bow, a signature line addressed to the camera — as though the film cannot imagine an exit that is not itself a performance, which may say something deflationary about Rousseau's own ideal of a people transparently present to itself. Tell me whether either of these actually defeats the Rousseauian reading or can be reabsorbed, and address the reflexive problem as well: the film indicts its characters' viewing, but it is watched, for a price, by people who leave the cinema feeling enlightened about the evils of watching — so does it turn out to be exactly the kind of work the Letter objects to, a machine for producing virtuous feeling at no cost? Finally, end by saying what the film adds to the argument that Rousseau's own reasoning does not supply: some case, distinction, or mechanism he has no resources for. Please paraphrase rather than quote anything from the Letter or the script, since I have both in front of me and mistrust recalled wording, and don't give me references or a further-reading list — I want your reading, not citations.
