Anonymous peer review in most academic journals runs on two channels: a set of comments addressed to the author, and a separate confidential one addressed to the editor. The norms governing them are asymmetric. In the author-facing text, a negative judgment is expected to be hedged, softened, and repackaged as a suggestion; reviewers are instructed to be constructive, and open harshness counts as a breach of professional etiquette even when it is accurate. In the editor-facing text, the same reviewer is expected to say plainly that the paper will not work and cannot be fixed, and vagueness there counts as a dereliction of duty. All the while, the stated purpose of the author-facing review is to help the author improve the manuscript.

Reconstruct the theory of honesty and of communication this arrangement presupposes. Does it assume that truthfulness is owed to whoever must act on the information rather than to the person the information is about? Does it assume that a misleading-but-kind utterance can be permissible in a way a misleading-and-cruel one is not — and if so, what is doing the work in that asymmetry: consequences, the relationship between speaker and hearer, or the hearer's entitlement to know? Which rival views of lying and of respect does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it merely happen to sit comfortably with? Tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, whether the two channels can be squared with the claim that the review exists for the author's benefit, and what would have to change — in reviewer instructions, decision letters, and what an author may reasonably infer from a warm review — if the confidential channel were abolished and every reviewer had to write a single text read by both parties.
