I'm in a third-year seminar on democratic theory — twenty-two of us, one seventy-five-minute session per week — and next week's topic is the rationality of voting. The reading has been the familiar arithmetic: multiply the probability that your single ballot decides the result by the difference the result makes to you, subtract the cost of turning out, and notice that the product is vanishingly small while the cost is not, so a self-interested calculator should stay home; then the equally familiar replies that something has been left out, either a term for the intrinsic worth of participating or the thought that voting is less an instrument than a declaration of whose side you are on. Our last session went badly in an instructive way. Everybody could reproduce the arithmetic, nobody was moved by it, and the discussion collapsed into people agreeing that voting is irrational in principle and that they will of course vote anyway. My suspicion is that the arithmetic is simply never felt, because a room of twenty-two is exactly the wrong place to feel it: in a room our size the chance that any one of us decides the outcome is on the order of one in six, so any vote actually run inside the seminar will vindicate voting rather than problematize it. What I want is an activity that puts twenty-two people into the situation of one voter among a couple of hundred thousand, and leaves them unable to say afterwards that they didn't know what it felt like.

I have a proposal, which I would like you to develop, repair, or replace if it doesn't work. The idea is an amplification device: each real ballot cast in the room is joined by a body of "phantom" ballots whose size is determined by a draw from a distribution announced to the class in advance, with the realized draw sealed and revealed only at the count — so nobody is voting blind about the odds, they are voting knowing exactly how unlikely they are to matter. One structural worry I can't settle on my own: should the phantom electorate be produced by a single common draw for the whole room, or by an independent draw attached to each real ballot? My instinct is that only the common draw is honest, because in a real election there is one world in which everybody else votes, and my ballot is decisive or futile in that same world rather than in a private lottery of my own; independent per-ballot draws would hand each of us an individual chance and quietly destroy the very structure the activity is meant to exhibit. But I don't trust the instinct, and I want it argued through, including the objection that a common draw makes my ballot's relevance feel like pure luck rather than like a judgment about my causal contribution. Beyond that, three constraints. The matter being voted on has to be something the class genuinely cares about — the form of the final assessment, or how the last two sessions are spent — so that the gap between the outcomes is a loss we would actually resent rather than a hypothetical good. Turning out has to cost each of us something real we would rather keep, though nothing involving money and nothing punitive; propose whatever you think is honest without being cruel. And abstention has to be a live, unpenalized, recorded option, so that at the end we have an actual turnout figure to stare at.

What I need from you, then, is a runnable design: rules, a minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes, materials, the stakes and cost scheme, and whatever the facilitator has to say and not say while it is happening — the whole point being that the group walks into the problem through what they do, and only hears it named afterwards. Then a debrief that does three things. First, it should put the turnout we actually produced next to the arithmetic we were given before voting and make the room explain the gap, including for those who abstained whether their reason was that it wouldn't matter or that they expected enough of the rest of us to go without them, since I suspect those are very different reasons and only one of them is a genuine answer to the paradox rather than something that would unravel if everybody adopted it. Second, it should map specific moments in the activity onto specific moves in the argument, so that when someone says the extra term for the worth of participating is just a fudge, we can point at the minute in the game where they behaved as though it weren't. Third, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I can already see four candidates: someone reverse-engineers the device and announces the decisive probability out loud, at which point the feeling dies; a bloc organizes before the vote and pushes pivotality back up to a level where voting is obviously sensible; a few people refuse to participate in protest at being made into an experiment; and, worst of all, the phantom draw lands unluckily and the real ballots decide the result anyway, so the seminar walks out believing the paradox has been refuted in front of them. Tell me how to design against each of those, or why I should instead accept them and use them. And close with a frank account of what the device cannot reproduce at all — I suspect at least that in a real election the difference between outcomes is diffuse, contested and largely counterfactual rather than a single felt loss, and that real voters are never handed the odds in advance the way my version requires.
