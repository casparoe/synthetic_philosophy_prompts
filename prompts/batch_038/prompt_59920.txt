I built an argument for a philosophy-of-biology reading group with the narrow purpose of embarrassing the spandrels critique, and it worked far too well: as far as I can see, it now shows that no claim about the origin of any trait is ever evidentially supported, which I am not prepared to accept. I want a diagnosis of the reasoning, not a defence of it, and not a survey of the adaptationism debate — assume I know its general shape.

The case I built the argument around is invented but meant to be realistic. In a clade of forty fossorial rodents, a lateral bony shelf on the cheekbone — call it the zygomatic flange — scales against skull length with an allometric exponent of about 1.6, so that relatively larger skulls carry disproportionately larger flanges. Ontogenetically the flange is the last element of the lateral skull to differentiate, appearing only after the masseter attachment area has been fully laid down. Two stories cover these facts. Adaptationist: the flange is a lever arm maintained by selection for forceful chewing in hard substrates. By-product: selection acts on the extent of the masseter attachment field, and the flange is what bone has to do at the margin of an expanding attachment field, so it is a consequence of that expansion rather than a target in its own right.

The argument:

(1) The force of the spandrels critique is that a trait's good design does not license an inference to direct selection, because the trait may be a structural concomitant of some other feature that is itself under selection.

(2) For any trait T and any body of comparative evidence E — allometric series, ontogenetic sequences, phylogenetic distributions — a by-product hypothesis B can be constructed: pick any trait S that E correlates with T and posit T as a structural consequence of S.

(3) In my case, the adaptation hypothesis A and the B constructed from E entail exactly the same allometric exponent and exactly the same ontogenetic order.

(4) Where two hypotheses entail the same evidence, that evidence assigns them the same likelihood and so cannot favour one over the other.

(5) Hence E does not favour A over B; and since the construction in (2) is always available, no comparative evidence ever favours an adaptation hypothesis over a by-product hypothesis.

(6) The construction is symmetric: for any spandrel claim, posit instead a selection regime that would produce the same correlation. So no comparative evidence ever favours a by-product hypothesis either.

(7) Therefore no claim about trait origins in functional morphology is ever supported by evidence — including the positive claims Gould and Lewontin make about particular structures, which stand or fall by the same comparative data.

(8) Hence the spandrels critique is self-undermining, and so by parity is any methodological critique of functional inference, since each must rest on trait-level inferences of the kind it declares unsupported.

I cannot live with (7), and I am not willing to buy (8) either. I am most attached to (1) and to (4) — (4) strikes me as a bare fact about likelihoods that no one should be asked to surrender — and least attached to (6). So if your diagnosis lands on (4), I want to be shown precisely what is lost rather than being told that it is false.

Please open with a single sentence naming the step or steps you reject, then give me four sections in this order:

**The failing step** (about 200 words): locate the slide — equivocation, quantifier order, category mistake, whatever it is — and state it with enough precision that I could see it happening in the inference. Write out the corrected version of the step if a corrected version exists, and say so explicitly if none does.

**Why it looked sound** (about 200 words): explain the pull of that step by working through the rodent case, and say what is genuinely right about the intuition behind it.

**What each repair costs** (about 450 words): take at least three candidate steps to reject — including (4), whether or not it is your diagnosis — and for each say what has to be given up or taken on if it goes, and whether the position that survives is one Gould and Lewontin could coherently have held.

**What the critique keeps** (about 200 words): restate the strongest version of the methodological point that survives your diagnosis, and say what commitments it now carries that its critics were entitled to demand of it.
