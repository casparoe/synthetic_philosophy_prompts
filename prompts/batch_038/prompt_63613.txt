Right, so I've been stuck in this argument for the better part of a year with two friends, one Swedish and one Norwegian, about which of their countries is actually the more liberal one. It started on a train from Gothenburg to Oslo and it still hasn't ended. Over Easter we got properly silly and built a spreadsheet: press freedom rankings, drug policy, alcohol policy, church/state arrangements, guns, parental leave, immigration and citizenship rules, hate speech law, how easy it is to home-school, whether you can behave like a weirdo in public without being stared at. And the spreadsheet settled nothing, because for every row one of them could point to another row that flipped the answer. Norway has topped the international press freedom index for most of the last decade, Sweden did it for a few years before that. Sweden seperated its church from the state in 2000; Norway only formally untangled its church in 2017. Both have a state alcohol monopoly, so that's a wash. Norway has way more guns per head, nearly all hunting rifles, but you need permission to carry one in public just like in Sweden. Norway wrote the right to roam into statute in the fifties while in Sweden it's unwritten custom — which my Norwegian friend says means Norwegians have a right and Swedes have a permission, and my Swedish friend says that's lawyer-brain and makes no difference on the ground, and honestly its a fair point.

Then each of them turned out to have a theory of the other, and that's where it got unmoveable. My Swedish friend leans on that state-individualism story: the Swedish welfare state was deliberately built to make people individually autonomous by dissolving dependence on family, church and charity, so Swedish liberalism is about the individual being free of everyone, whereas Norwegian liberalism is really about the local community being free of central authority. My Norwegian friend says that's exactly backwards — Swedish conformism, everyone agreeing, Jantelagen — means social pressure does the policing the state doesn't have to do, while Norway is a country of self-sufficient stubborn individualists with a big state that mostly leaves you alone provided you don't make a scene. So each of them accuses the other of confusing a liberal state with a liberal society, and neither will grant that the state/society distinction is neutral ground. I tried to break the tie with something I made up and it backfired: a cost-of-deviance test, where you take a handful of specific non-standard life plans (off-grid hunting cabin, home-schooling, home birth, visibly orthodox religion, refusing state childcare, a man who wants to be a full-time house husband, a Roma family, someone who wants to publish deeply unfashionable opinions) and ask in which country the friction of living that way is lower, counting legal, fiscal and social friction as one number. My Swedish friend said social friction shouldn't count at all because that isn't politics; my Norwegian friend said it's the only thing that counts because law on paper is cheap. Which makes me suspect my test was smuggling in a whole conception of freedom under the guise of being neutral, and that's why nobody budged.

So here's what I want from you, and please answer in British English. Don't give me another list of policy differences between the two countries — we have that list, it's on the spreadsheet, it's useless to me. Give me a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal, i.e. are all three of us just using 'liberal' for different things so that there's no fact of the matter? Is it substantive but about the unit of analysis — a property of states versus a property of societies versus a property of individuals' actual option sets? Is it a hidden measurement problem, given that both countries sit at the ceiling of every index we could find so the indices literally cannot discriminate between them? Or is one of my friends simply wrong about their own country, which is possible and would be funny? Tell me which of these you'd bet on and defend the bet by pointing at what the two of them actually said above, not at generic philosophy. Then say what would count as progress from here: a stipulated operationalisation, an argument that one conception of freedom is the right one to use, a dissolution into several separate questions each of which does have an answer, or nothing at all — in which case explain why this argument should be enjoyed rather than settled. And be honest about whether the cost-of-deviance test can be repaired into something both of them would accept in advance of the results, or whether any such test gets rejected by whichever side it doesn't favour. No citations or reading lists, I can't look anything up right now anyway.
