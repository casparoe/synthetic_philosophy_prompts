I write novels for a living, and last month I was one of four authors on a panel at a small festival in a cathedral town. Afterwards, in the bar, I found myself behind two panellists who had not previously met: Helen, who spent eleven years drafting policy documents for a government department and now teaches non-fiction, and Idris, who reviews poetry for a Sunday paper. The panel had been about what one puts in front of first-year students, and Orwell's *Politics and the English Language* (1946) had come up; they resumed the argument at the next table and went seven rounds. I said nothing, I took no side, and I have tried below to report what they said accurately rather than favourably. I should be very grateful if you would referee it.

1. Helen opened by saying that the essay's real claim is not aesthetic but evidential. Prose of the kind Orwell catalogues — the dying metaphor, the pretentious diction, the phrase that can be pasted into a paragraph without anyone having decided what it asserts — is produced by a writer who has not settled what they mean. A page is therefore evidence about the state of mind that made it, which is why the essay belongs in front of people who will one day draft something the public must rely on. As a specimen she quoted a line from a consultation document she had once been asked to clear: "the department is minded to explore a range of interventions aimed at optimising outcomes for those cohorts most likely to experience poor outcomes," and said one could tell from that sentence alone, without ever meeting its author, that nobody in the chain had been willing to state who would be affected and how.

2. Idris replied that the inference is invalid, and that Orwell's own opening paragraph invalidates it. The essay says the decline of the language has political and economic causes and that bad habits spread by imitation — which makes officialese something caught, like an accent, rather than something chosen. A junior official who wrote that sentence may know precisely who will suffer and be forbidden from saying so; a reporter on deadline writes in clichés while thinking with complete precision. Style is a property of a community and an institution, so diagnosing a person's sincerity from their syntax is like diagnosing their honesty from their vowels.

3. Helen said that on this reading the essay makes no sense at all, since Orwell also insists the process is reversible and that the habits can be avoided "if one is willing to take the necessary trouble" — a claim about choice, and about effort expended sentence by sentence. And her test was never about ugliness. It is this: ask the author to say the same thing in short, concrete words. If they can, the original was padding; if they cannot, or if the plain version turns out to be something they will not put their name to, you have learned something true about the document — whatever the private clarity of the individual who typed it.

4. Idris said the paraphrase test quietly assumes that everything worth saying has a plain propositional equivalent, which is false of a great deal of good writing, including Orwell's own. Those essays run on hyperbole, on rhythm, on the strategic withholding of the point until the final pages, and on figurative inventions — the man who takes to drink because he feels a failure, the cuttlefish spurting ink, prose assembled out of ready-made sections like a prefabricated hen-house — none of which survive paraphrase. A test that convicts the man who proposed it is not a test.

5. Helen said his category mistake was the other way round: Orwell's target is not figurative language but the dead figure, the phrase already in print which can be inserted without the writer picturing anything. A fresh image whose job can be stated sits on the far side of that line — ask what the cuttlefish is doing in the sentence and Orwell can answer: it marks the evasion as reflex, as something that happens to the writer rather than being decided by him. She added that the essay closes by conceding that any of its rules may be broken rather than commit an outright barbarism, which shows he understood them as rules of craft serving an aim, not as taboos.

6. And that concession, Idris said, is precisely what destroys the diagnostic. A rule that yields whenever keeping it would make the prose worse has no independent content: it cannot convict, because the defence is always available (I broke it deliberately) and the accusation is always available (your departure was barbarous), and which of the two applies can be settled only by judging the passage on other grounds first. The essay is therefore not an instrument but a bundle of preferences wearing the costume of a moral test — and the costume is politically expensive, because a doctrine claiming to detect insincerity from texture hands a cheap dismissal to whoever happens to own the plain style. "He cannot say what he means in short words" is the easiest way in the world to avoid answering someone you have not understood, and Orwell supplies the alibi in a beautifully quotable form. Idris noted, too, that Orwell himself writes that the great enemy of clear language is insincerity — meaning a gap between one's real and one's declared aims — which is a fact about a person, whereas Helen's test claims to find it in a fact about a sentence.

7. Helen's last move was that a defeasible test is still a test. What the closing concession does is shift the burden: a writer who departs from plain usage must be able to say what the departure buys, and if they cannot, the departure was not made, it merely happened. She had never claimed a bad sentence proves a bad person; only that a draft in which no one can state what its sentences commit the department to is a draft that nobody has yet thought about, and that this is discoverable by interrogating the text rather than the author's soul.

Please adjudicate move by move rather than in the aggregate: for each of the seven, say whether it lands, half-lands or fails, and justify the verdict in a sentence or two. Beyond that I want three things — whether move 6 disposes of Helen's diagnostic or only of a grander version of it than she was still defending by move 7; which single move in the exchange did the most damage, and to whom; and what neither of them said that ought to have been said, since I suspect the argument ran some way past the point at which the real disagreement lay. If you can spare one closing sentence: I am four months into a novel whose narrator is a permanent secretary and whose entire idiom is that of Helen's specimen, and after hearing this I cannot tell whether that constitutes a defence or a confession. Please write your answer in British English.
