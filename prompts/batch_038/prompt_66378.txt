I am one of the two student editors of my high school's weekly newspaper. We publish roughly 2,500 words a week, written by four staff writers who, like me, have no background in political science, and everything goes to print only after a twenty-minute pre-publication meeting that our faculty adviser, a history teacher, has to sign off on. This term we have had a run of complaints — two from parents in the letters column, one from the adviser — all amounting to the same charge: that we use the words "liberal," "libertarian," "classical liberal," "neoliberal," and "progressive" as though they meant one thing, when in fact the same word picks out opposite positions depending on whether the article is about the United States or about Europe, and depending on when it was written. She is right, and I want to fix it with a procedure rather than with a lecture nobody will read.

Please write that procedure: an ordered list of questions to ask of any sentence containing one of those labels, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the point at which the procedure terminates in "strike the label and describe the position instead," and the points at which it hands the decision up to me or to the adviser because the question is one of editorial judgment rather than fact. Two constraints matter. First, it has to be runnable in under three minutes per sentence by a sixteen-year-old in that meeting. Second, the writers may not look anything up, because we have no research time and roughly half our copy concerns countries none of us have studied; the procedure therefore has to say what to do when the answer is not already known to the person applying it. Every output should be a phrase a writer can paste straight into copy, not a note to self.

Begin with a grounding section of no more than about a hundred words stating the distinction or distinctions the whole procedure rests on, since that is the most our writers will read. Then separate three ways the word fails: a false friend, where the term exists in both languages but does not carry the same meaning; drift inside a single language over time; and the case where the extension is roughly constant but the word is praise in one discourse and blame in another. Tell me which of those three a mechanical rule can actually fix and which can only be fixed by rewriting the sentence, and let that difference shape the steps.

Then run it on the six pieces in this week's issue and give me the sentence we should print in each: a brief on Danish coalition talks mentioning Venstre, whose name literally means "Left"; a short item on Norwegian education policy mentioning that country's Venstre; a translated French article that calls a candidate "libéral"; a profile of Argentina's president, who calls himself a liberal and a libertarian, proposes dollarizing the currency and abolishing ministries, and rejects being called a neoliberal, while much of the foreign press calls him a right-wing populist; a trade story quoting a backbencher from Australia's Liberal Party; and a piece we are co-running with the history department on the nineteenth-century British Liberal Party.

Please be candid where the procedure goes against me. Last term I imposed a house rule that none of these words may appear in the paper without the sense being specified on the spot, and I expect that rule will not survive contact with a 2,500-word weekly; if it will not, say so plainly and propose the narrower rule that should replace it. I also want a rule for when a contested, pejorative-leaning label such as "neoliberal" may stand in a news story as opposed to an opinion column. The adviser cut it from a news piece last month and I thought she was wrong, but I would rather have a procedure that sometimes rules against me than one engineered to rule for me.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot settle: the cases where accuracy and what our readers will inevitably infer genuinely conflict, the cases where the subject's own country disagrees about the label, and anything that depends on a writer correctly identifying a discourse community they have never encountered. A reading list or sources would be useless to us; I am asking only for the procedure and the worked cases.
