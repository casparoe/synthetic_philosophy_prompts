I am revising the central argument of a chapter in the philosophy of psychiatry, and it has become unusable: stated carefully, it appears to establish a conclusion I cannot hold. Here it is as tightly as I can manage.

(1) Some classifications are indifferent: applying them leaves the thing classified as it was. Others are interactive: the people classified can learn the classification, reorganize their self-understanding and their conduct around it, and so alter what it takes to fall under it. This is the distinction the looping-effects literature turns on.

(2) Psychiatric classifications are interactive in the strongest available sense. Not only does their extension shift as classified people change; the criteria themselves are rewritten in response to how the classified now present.

(3) The evidence for (2) is uncontroversial: a bereavement category was added to the American manual's most recent text revision with a duration threshold — grief persisting past a cutoff stated in months — which the two international classification systems set at different lengths; a category covering same-sex attraction was struck from the manual by institutional vote rather than on the strength of a discovery; adolescents have been documented acquiring the motor and verbal repertoire of a neurological diagnosis from short-form video and then presenting with it in clinics.

(4) If a taxonomy's criteria are rewritten to match how its subjects currently present, then at every stage in the taxonomy's history its criteria fit the population to which they are then applied.

(5) A criterion that fits whatever population it is applied to cannot fail to fit, and a classification that cannot fail to fit admits of no incorrect application.

(6) Therefore psychiatric diagnosis is immune to error: whoever meets the manual's criteria for a disorder has that disorder.

(7) The same reasoning runs unchanged through every other interactive kind. Income is defined for tax purposes; taxpayers restructure their affairs in light of the definition; the legislature amends the definition; repeat. Likewise the legal category of the refugee, the marketing category of the teenager, the financial category of the creditworthy borrower, the clinical category of the food allergy. In each of these we obviously get particular people wrong — someone is coded into the wrong bracket, wrongly refused credit, wrongly certified.

(8) Since (6) is false in those cases and the inference is identical, (6) is false in the psychiatric case; so something in (1) through (5) has gone wrong.

I want a diagnosis, and I want it priced. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the chain is in fact valid as stated, then say which premise is least expensive to abandon and argue for that judgment against the alternatives. For each premise you keep, explain what makes it look unobjectionable when considered on its own, since I have been over this argument repeatedly and each step survived inspection individually.

Two constraints on your answer. Rejecting (1) or (2) turns psychiatric kinds into indifferent ones, which is what the looping literature denies and what my examples in (3) seem to show; if that is what you propose, you owe me an account of what those examples are really evidence of. Rejecting (7) — holding that tax brackets and psychiatric categories are not interactive in the same way — looks like special pleading unless there is a principled difference, so name it.

Finally, tell me what the repaired argument leaves standing. Whatever survives, can it still distinguish a revision of criteria that improves a taxonomy from one that merely enlarges it — a threshold lowered, or a criterion broadened, so that more people meet it, whether for billing convenience, professional campaigning, or genuine accumulation of evidence? If making that distinction requires a vantage point from outside the loop, say where such a vantage point is supposed to come from, and why occupying it does not simply restore the picture of disorders as kinds wholly independent of our classifications that I set out to dislodge.
