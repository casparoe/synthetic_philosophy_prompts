For some thirty-five years I worked as a physicist, the last dozen of them largely in administration, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more seriousness than I expected of myself. I belong to a continuing-education seminar that spent this past term on the formal study of knowledge and disagreement, and last Thursday our discussion began with Aumann's result that two rational agents sharing a common prior cannot knowingly disagree, and then became considerably more heated than our discussions usually are. Two members — a statistician, whom I shall call W., and a philosopher of economics, N. — argued for the better part of an hour while the rest of us kept silent. I confess that by the end I could no longer tell who was winning, or even what was being decided. I should be grateful if you would keep the score for me.

W. began:

(1) Aumann's theorem shows that if two Bayesians share a common prior and it becomes common knowledge between them what each believes about some question, their posteriors must coincide. What interests me is the contrapositive. Where two people have genuinely absorbed the same body of evidence and remain of two minds, and are aware that they remain so, at least one of them is either failing to reason as a Bayesian or failing to treat the other as one. Ordinary stubborn disagreement is therefore not a neutral feature of the intellectual landscape but a diagnostic sign. The phrase 'reasonable people can disagree,' taken literally, is false.

N. replied:

(2) The theorem is correct and close to empty, because its hypotheses cannot be met. A common prior is a modelling stipulation rather than a discovery about persons, and common knowledge is unattainable. Any actual conversation, however candid and however prolonged, yields mutual knowledge to some finite depth: I know your view, you know that I know it, we each know that the other knows that, and then the chain simply stops. A theorem that fires only in circumstances that cannot arise cannot diagnose the circumstances that do. So move (1) is a misuse of it.

W.:

(3) That is far too strong. There is a substantial body of work on approximate common knowledge, and its upshot is a continuity result: if it is common p-belief, for p near one, that each of us holds a posterior near some value, then our posteriors must in fact be close with high probability. Mutual knowledge to a large finite depth therefore buys most of what common knowledge buys. That is all I require. Two people who have argued in good faith for an hour possess mutual knowledge of one another's views to a very high order, so the conclusion of the theorem should hold approximately: their beliefs should have converged. Wide and lasting disagreement still indicates that something has gone wrong.

N.:

(4) Rubinstein's electronic mail game refutes the continuity intuition you are leaning on. Two players do well if both choose a certain action when each is sure of the state of the world, and each prefers the other action if he suspects the other may be in the dark. A message is sent, and its receipt acknowledged, and each acknowledgment acknowledged in turn, and every message may fail to arrive with small probability. The striking result is that for any positive probability of failure and any finite number of acknowledgments — a thousand, a million — the unique equilibrium is the inferior action, while at common knowledge the preferred action becomes available. The equilibrium correspondence is discontinuous precisely at the point you insist makes no difference. Knowledge that is almost common, in the sense of holding to arbitrarily high finite order, does not behave almost like common knowledge.

W.:

(5) You have changed the subject. The email game concerns what people do when each one's payoff depends on a guess about the other's guess; there is a channel of strategic feedback passing through every level of belief, and it is that channel, not the epistemic shortfall, that generates the discontinuity. The account of robustness given by Kajii and Morris makes the point exact: an equilibrium of the complete-information game fails to be robust precisely when it admits 'contagion', where my incentive to abandon it depends on my belief about your action, which depends on your belief about mine, and so on up the tower. Aumann's theorem contains no such channel. Posteriors are fixed by the evidence and the prior, not by what I expect you to expect. The continuity result for agreement of beliefs stands untouched.

N.:

(6) Two answers. First, the feature you dismiss is exactly what makes your continuity result weaker than you suppose. Those theorems guarantee agreement with high probability, and the probability weight they concede to failure is where all the interesting cases reside. A guarantee that two agents agree except on a set of states of measure one in a million is not a guarantee that this pair of people, in this argument, should expect to agree; the exceptional case may be the one in front of them, and each may know that the other knows it. Second, and to my mind more damaging: even in the purely epistemic setting, finite mutual knowledge can be worth nothing whatsoever. Take the muddy children. If the father's announcement is common knowledge, the induction runs and the muddy children step forward on the appointed day. If it is mutual knowledge only to a finite depth, the induction can run only that many steps, so when there are more muddy children than the depth of the mutual knowledge, the reasoning breaks down and no child ever concludes anything, however plainly the mud is there for everyone to see. In such cases the number of levels is not a measure of approximation at all but a question of whether a particular inference is available. The right question is not 'how many levels?' but 'which inferences does this epistemic situation license?', and for some inferences the answer changes completely between every finite level and the limit.

At that point the hour ended and they shook hands without having moved.

I should like you to go through moves (1) to (6) one at a time. For each, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and give your reason; and where a move rests on a technical claim about a particular result, tell me separately whether the claim is correctly stated and whether, correctly stated or not, it actually supports the conclusion drawn from it. Then tell me which of the two has the better of the exchange as a whole, and where each of them overreaches. Finally — and this is what I most want, since it is the question I could not settle in my own head — say whether there is a substantive disagreement here at all, or whether they are arguing about the value of an idealisation, which in my old line of work was the kind of argument that ended as soon as somebody sat down and wrote out the estimate. If there is a real issue underneath, please state what it is in terms that do not presuppose either man's framing of it. I am not asking for references or suggestions of further reading; I want your judgement on the arguments exactly as they were made.
