I serve on the committee that sets the written qualifying examination in philosophy of mind for our doctoral candidates, and this cycle I have been assigned the section on consciousness and its metaphysics. I should like you to compose one essay question on epiphenomenalism about phenomenal properties, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The item must be unanswerable by competent recitation. Every candidate has read the standard material; if the question invites them to rehearse the causal-closure argument, the knowledge argument, the zombie argument, and a definition of the position, they will all score well and the item will tell us nothing. I want the crux to be an internal tension rather than a tally of the view's costs. The tension I have in mind is roughly this: the reasoning that motivates the view leans on judgments we take ourselves able to make about our own experience — what it is like, and what could be absent while every physical and functional fact stayed fixed — yet the view, if true, appears to sever precisely the connection between experience and those judgments that would entitle us to trust them. The difficulty is discussed under some such heading as the paradox of phenomenal judgment, but I do not want a question that a candidate can field by reproducing that discussion. I want one that makes them determine whether the position pulls the ground out from under its own central argument, and what would have to be true of knowledge, or of judgment, for it not to.

Please supply three things.

First, the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with any sub-questions clearly marked. It should be answerable in ninety minutes by a well-prepared second-year doctoral candidate. At most one sub-question: I do not want the item to fragment into a checklist.

Second, a model answer of 800 to 1,000 words. It must reach a defended verdict on the question posed rather than balancing considerations against one another, and it must be self-contained — candidates sit the paper without library access, so neither the question nor the answer should require or reward knowing who advanced which argument, when, or where, and the answer should contain no quotations.

Third, a marking scheme out of twenty-five marks, arranged as five or six required moves, each with its mark value and a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates botch it. State plainly what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one. My own expectation is that the difference lies in three places, though I leave you to judge how much weight each deserves: recognizing that the problem is not a species of general skepticism and not simply the knowledge argument run in reverse; saying something substantive about what kind of epistemic relation knowledge of one's own experience could consist in, instead of assuming a causal theory of knowledge as though it were the only game in town; and distinguishing the vulnerability of a judgment about the character of an experience from the vulnerability of the modal judgment that such an experience could have been absent. If you think one of these is a red herring, say so and justify marking accordingly.

Finally, if you judge the question unfair as I have framed it — too broad for ninety minutes, or collapsing into something our candidates have already sat — tell me why and propose the amendment you would make in its place.
