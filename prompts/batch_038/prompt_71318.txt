In the philosophy-of-religion reading group I belong to, one member is defending a version of the cosmological argument that runs through Hilbert's Hotel: a hotel with infinitely many rooms, every one of them occupied, which can nevertheless accommodate a new arrival by asking each guest to move one room up, and which can absorb an infinitely long busload of new guests by asking everyone to double their room number. He treats the hotel as an analogy for an infinite past — the rooms are the moments or events that have already occurred, the guests are what fills them, and the manager's shuffling is the passage of time through a completed infinity. His conclusion is that since the hotel's behaviour is absurd (a fully occupied hotel with room to spare, guests who have all moved and yet nobody has left), a collection of that shape cannot be instantiated in the real world, and therefore the past cannot be infinite and the universe began to exist.

Another member replies that the same story cuts the other way or does no work at all: if you are an eternalist, every past moment is equally real, so the past is exactly the coexisting collection the hotel depicts — and in that case no guest ever needs to be shifted and no bus ever needs to be seated, because the paradoxes only arise when someone is made to perform an operation on the completed infinity. On this reading the hotel is at most a warning about supertasks, not an argument that infinite totalities are impossible.

I would like you to audit the analogy against both uses. Please give me an explicit correspondence table — rooms, guests, the manager, the new arrival, the infinite bus, the shifting operation, and the fully-occupied-yet-vacant upshot, each matched to whatever is supposed to play that role in the past — and then identify where the mapping fails. I want the disanalogies sorted into those that are load-bearing for his conclusion and those that are merely decorative. In particular, say what the hotel story quietly assumes that the past may not supply: simultaneity of the whole collection, manipulability of the totality as an object, the requirement that traversal be completed in finite time, and any tacit restriction on what may be done with infinite quantities.

Then adjudicate this variant of mine, which is the case I care about most. Strip the story down to a ledger hotel: the manager keeps a record of every arrival, one entry per night, and the record has no first entry. Nobody is ever asked to move, no new guest is ever turned away or seated, and no infinite task is performed; the only infinite thing is the set of nights already written down. Does the force of his argument survive that stripping, or was it located precisely in the operations my version deletes? If it survives, name the feature of the ledger that carries it; if it does not, say what the original hotel was really an argument against.

Finally, tell me whether the inference from the absurdity of the hotel to the finitude of the past holds at all, since the hotel seems to bear on the possibility of an actual infinite collection while the argument needs the impossibility of an infinite temporal regress — and, if you think one side or the other would be better served by a different analogy, offer one and say explicitly what it can and cannot support. Note too what a defender of the hotel could concede without losing the argument.
