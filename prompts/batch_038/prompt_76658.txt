**Working title: Who reads the archive? Orwellian and Stalinesque revision without a reader**

**1.** The finding. Two small dots of different colours are flashed one after the other at different places on a screen. Within a certain range of separations and intervals, you don't get two flashes — you get one dot travelling between the two locations. And the striking part: the travelling dot seems to change colour roughly halfway along the path. (The experiment was run by Kolers and von Grünau in the mid-seventies because Goodman had asked what a colour change would do to apparent motion, which is a nicely philosophical way for a psychology study to get started.) The puzzle is that at the moment the change seems to happen, the information about the second colour is not yet available anywhere in the system. Nothing at the halfway point carries it.

**2.** Two ways to make the books balance. Call them the Stalinesque and the Orwellian. On the Stalinesque story, the discrepancy is settled before anything goes on the record: the first frame is held in a buffer, the second arrives, the colour change is resolved, and only then is the finished content produced. The revision happens pre-publication. On the Orwellian story, the content goes out as it comes: at the relevant moment the subject really does have something like an experience of a dot of the first colour travelling the whole way, and then, when the second frame lands, the trace of that experience is overwritten — so what the subject subsequently has access to says the change happened midway. The revision happens post-publication, to the archive.

Dennett and Kinsbourne's punchline is that these aren't rivals. The two theorists agree about every physical event, about where and when each of them happens; they differ only over whether a certain stretch of processing gets labelled pre-experiential or post-experiential. And they agree it should feel the same from the inside either way. So no experiment separates them, and the choice between them is empty — which is meant to show that the picture generating the choice, a finish line somewhere in the brain where processing turns into experience, is what we should drop.

**3.** My complaint, part one: look at the metaphors. Both of them are censorship metaphors, and censorship always has an audience. The doctored photograph and the rewritten back issue do their work on someone — there is a reader for whom the revision constitutes a lie, and that reader is the point of the whole exercise. Take the reader out and "revision" stops having a normative shape at all: an in-place overwrite of a memory trace isn't a deception of anybody, it's just a write. So the vocabulary in which the puzzle is posed smuggles in a spectator — exactly the inner audience the argument is supposed to be demolishing. I don't think this is just a quibble about naming, because it changes what the argument can prove. If Orwellian-versus-Stalinesque only makes sense relative to a reader, then "there is no fact about which sort of revision occurred" is a very cheap result: it follows from the choice of words, not from anything about the brain.

**4.** My complaint, part two: don't spend that cheap result on eliminativism, because there is one channel where there is obviously a fact. The report. When the subject says the colour changed halfway, that is a committed output — one token, produced at one time, in one channel, causing one thing. There is a fact about when the utterance happened and about what its temporal content was. So the live question is not whether the revision was pre- or post-experiential but: how does a system with no global clock come to emit a report with a definite temporal content in it?

**5.** Proposal. Temporal content in reports isn't read off a timeline; it is reconstructed from a sparse set of markers. Version numbers, not clock times. Given two markers (first frame, second frame) and a prior that apparent motion is continuous and roughly uniform, "halfway" comes for free, whether or not anything was ever represented at the halfway point. On this reading "halfway" is neither a misremembered experience nor a pre-resolved one — it is the best available reconstruction, and it is no more false than the history a merge commit displays is false.

**6.** If temporal content is reconstruction rather than readout, then confabulation about *when* should be systematic and predictable rather than arbitrary, and it should come apart from confabulation about *what*. Three predictions:

(a) the reported change point should be attracted to the midpoint of the path even when the physical timing is skewed — e.g. when the first dot's offset is delayed so the actual temporal midpoint is well off the spatial one;
(b) order judgements should be more robust than colour judgements under a manipulation that degrades colour information specifically;
(c) my favourite — in a variant where the second dot's colour is cued in advance, before the first dot is even shown, subjects should still report a midway change. That version needs no backward trickery at all, because the reconstruction only ever needed the markers.

**7.** What I'm not claiming. Not that there is no experience, and not that reports are all there is. The claim is narrower: "when did it become conscious" is a question that requires a clock discipline the brain doesn't have, while "why does the report say halfway" is a question with an answer, and the two get fused because English makes them look like the same kind of question about times.

**8.** Worries I already have, in case you don't: (i) section 4 might just be a behaviourist reading of the report in a nicer coat — the report is what there is, everything else is decoration; (ii) I'm not sure prediction (a) separates me from the Stalinesque side, since a buffer plus a uniform-motion prior could produce the same midpoint attraction; (iii) "version numbers not clocks" may be carrying more weight than it can bear, and I don't know what independent evidence there is that temporal order judgements are marker-based rather than trace-based.
