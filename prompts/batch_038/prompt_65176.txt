I was elected chair of my university's Faculty Research Conduct Panel in July — I am a philosopher of science, so everyone evidently assumed the job would come naturally to me — and on Tuesday I have to sign my first determination. I am not sure what it should say, and for the first time I suspect that the answer depends less on the evidence than on an arithmetic question nobody on the panel has ever had to face. The panel has five voting members: me, Ibrahim, Ndiaye, Sandoval, and Tsai. Our Code makes a finding of research misconduct turn on three elements, each of which the university must establish by a preponderance of the evidence: (i) that the respondent omitted, altered, or fabricated data in reporting results; (ii) that this was done knowingly or recklessly, rather than as honest error or a difference of opinion; and (iii) that it was a significant departure from accepted practice in the relevant field. Two further clauses matter. The Code says the panel "shall record a finding on each element," and it says that "the determination of the panel shall be that of a majority of the members voting." It never says whether the panel's finding on an element means the majority view on that element considered by itself, or whether the panel's single determination is whatever a majority of members conclude when each of them applies the three elements to their own findings. The secretary has always kept the internal ballot sheets showing each member's element-level votes, even though only the disposition appears in the public determination, so I have the raw data. General counsel has already told me that this determination will be appealed and that the review board will be interested in how we got from five sets of private findings to one institutional sentence.

There have been five cases under the current Code. In each line below the three entries are that member's findings on (i), (ii), (iii), with y for established and n for not established:

1. Altered calibration records: me y/y/y; Ibrahim y/y/y; Ndiaye y/y/y; Sandoval y/n/y; Tsai n/n/n. Disposition: misconduct. The determination recorded findings on all three elements and then the conclusion.
2. Unattributed text carried over into a proposal: me y/y/n; Ibrahim y/n/y; Ndiaye n/y/y; Sandoval y/y/n; Tsai n/n/n. Disposition: no misconduct. The determination recorded findings on (i) and (ii) as established and stated that (iii) was not.
3. Fabricated survey responses: me y/n/y; Ibrahim y/n/n; Ndiaye n/y/y; Sandoval y/y/n; Tsai n/n/n. Disposition: no misconduct. The determination found (i) established, (ii) not established, and expressly declined to reach (iii).
4. The same figure published twice without disclosure: me y/y/y; Ibrahim y/y/y; Ndiaye y/y/y; Sandoval n/y/y; Tsai y/n/n. Disposition: misconduct, with Tsai filing a separate statement saying that because she could not find (ii) or (iii) established she could not join the finding.
5. An unreported outlier exclusion in a clinical dataset: me y/y/y; Ibrahim y/n/n; Ndiaye n/y/n; Sandoval n/n/y; Tsai n/n/n. Disposition: no misconduct, even though I had found all three elements. That case is the reason I know my own vote carries no special weight; the Code gives the chair no tie-breaking power that I can find, and says nothing at all about abstentions.

It looks to me as though every one of those five dispositions comes out identically however you count, which would mean the record has never actually tested the question — but I am no longer confident in my own counting, so please check that rather than take it from me. I also cannot tell whether the difference between case 2, where the panel recorded findings on all three elements, and case 3, where it declined to reach the last one, reflects a principle or merely style.

The new case: the respondent is an associate professor who ran nine experimental sessions, reported seven in the published paper, and did not disclose the exclusions. The two unreported sessions were the ones that attenuated the headline effect. A notebook entry documenting an equipment fault exists for one of them and is dated after publication. The hearing closed last week and the members' confidential element findings are already filed with me: me y/n/y; Ibrahim y/y/n; Ndiaye n/y/y; Sandoval y/y/y; Tsai n/n/y. So I voted that the omission happened and that it was a significant departure, but not that it was knowing or reckless; Ibrahim thinks it was deliberate but immaterial; Ndiaye thinks it was material and reckless but is not satisfied the act element is met as charged; Tsai finds only the third element. Sandoval alone finds all three. Separately, Ndiaye has asked whether she may abstain on element (ii) only, on the ground that she supervised the respondent's dissertation and doubts her impartiality about state of mind while feeling able to judge the other two. A finding would go to the dean and to the sponsor whose money supported the work, and would bar the respondent from supervising graduate students for three years.

Here is what I need. First, work out which aggregation rules are actually compatible with the five precedents. At minimum consider: taking the majority view on each element separately and then applying the Code's conjunction; taking the majority view on the overall disposition alone; deciding the elements in the Code's order and, at each stage, counting only those members who accepted the elements already established; and the possibility that the panel runs no rule at all but deliberates until it finds a disposition everyone can sign. Say whether the record admits still other rules, and be blunt about whether five cases of this shape could ever discriminate among them. Second, tell me what each surviving rule implies for Tuesday, showing the counting rather than asserting the result, and deal explicitly with the tie I think arises on the third element under the third rule — including who, if anyone, could break it, and whether a chair who has herself dropped out at element (ii) can possibly do so. Third, predict the disposition and the shape of the determination I will end up signing, say which hypothesis you are backing and why, and give me a rough confidence. Fourth, tell me what I should propose if I want the panel to be consistent with its own record: name the closest analog among the five precedents, or tell me honestly that there isn't one, and state what principle the panel would be committing itself to in each direction — in particular, whether one option amounts to the panel asserting a belief about the respondent's state of mind that four of its five members, including its chair, do not hold. Fifth, redo the analysis for the contingency in which Ndiaye abstains on element (ii), say whether a partial abstention is even coherent under a preponderance standard, whether it changes the outcome, and whether it would be legitimate for me to want it to. Finally, tell me what single further case — or what feature I might still dig out of the older files — would settle which rule the panel has been running all along. Please write in American English and give me no bibliography or list of sources; I have six days and no time to read anything, so I need your reasoning laid out, not your references. I have since learned that this configuration has a name in the literature on group judgment, but the name will not help me write the sentence I have to sign.
