I cover housing and urban policy for a national magazine, and over the past year of zoning hearings I have heard the sentence "housing is a human right" used by three parties whose positions are otherwise incompatible: a tenants' federation opposing a 200-unit market-rate project next to a transit stop, a state legislator sponsoring a constitutional amendment, and, increasingly, the developers themselves, who argue that the same right obliges the city to let them build. I want to write a piece that takes the slogan apart rather than repeating it approvingly or dismissing it as empty. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct claims it could be making, ranging from the near-platitude to the radical: who holds the right and who bears the corresponding duty; what its object is — shelter of some specified quality, secure tenure at a price the holder can actually pay, or a claim to housing in a particular neighborhood; whether it is a claim to be provided with housing or a claim against the state's obstructing it; and whether it is enforceable or aspirational. For each reading, say whether it is true but trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then tell me two further things: what the word "right" is doing in each speaker's mouth that "urgent need" or "policy priority" would not do, since that seems to be where the rhetorical force lives; and which single reading, if any, could support all three of my speakers at once.
