A friend of mine has gotten deep into the doomsday argument and every single time it comes up he reaches for the same comparison: it's like an urn. Every human who will ever live is a ball in a giant urn with their birth rank printed on it, and your being born at around number 60 billion is just you drawing a ball blind. From there it's ordinary statistics — if you pull a low-ish numbered ball you should think the urn wasn't all that big to begin with. He uses this to shut down anyone who pushes back: an urn is an urn, the sampling is transparent, and if you don't like the conclusion you're just bad at probability. Half our friend group has sort of caved to it and I haven't.

Can you do the audit properly? I want the correspondence laid out explicitly — what in the urn story plays the role of the ball, the number printed on it, the drawing, the size of the urn, the "blindness" — and then the places the mapping actually gives out. And when you list the breakdowns, sort them for me: which ones are real load-bearing problems for the inference, versus which ones are cosmetic quibbles that a statistician would wave off. The reason I ask is that I suspect the crucial bit is that a real urn has its contents settled before anyone reaches in, whereas the thing he's estimating is still being made as we go, and the analogy just papers over that gap. Tell me if that's the actual weak point or if I'm barking up the wrong tree. And if the urn genuinely can't carry the weight he's putting on it, give me a better comparison to hand him — one where the part that's doing the work is visible instead of smuggled in.
