Dana's an econ PhD, Marcus does social theory. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Dana: Marx's law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall is just wrong as economics. Okishio's theorem — a cost-cutting technique gets adopted because it's profitable at prevailing prices, real wage held fixed — shows the new equilibrium general rate of profit is *higher*, not lower. So mechanization can't push profitability down. Marx has the sign backwards.

(2) Marcus: The theorem assumes circulating capital and compares two static equilibria. Marx's claim is about the path: the innovator pockets an extra surplus profit, the technique generalizes, the organic composition of capital rises, and since surplus value only comes out of living labor, the general rate falls as it spreads. Okishio is computing profitability in physical/Sraffian terms, Marx in labor-value terms. They aren't measuring the same quantity, so the "refutation" is a category mistake.

(3) Dana: That's the tell, not a defense. If the falling rate only shows up in a unit that never appears on any balance sheet, the law is both unfalsifiable and inert — it can't explain a single real crisis, because crises get observed in money prices. And empirically the profit rate in advanced economies has been broadly stable over very long stretches, which is what Okishio predicts and not what Marx predicts.

(4) Marcus: Fine, I'll concede the tendency. The interesting automation claim is the fragment on machines in the Grundrisse: once the general intellect is objectified in fixed capital, direct labor time stops being an adequate measure of wealth and the law of value undermines itself from within. That's a claim about the form, not a numerical prediction. On that reading, automation producing abundance with no profit-rate collapse is exactly what the theory expects — so your empirical point never touches it.

(5) Dana: Now you have a framework predicting both a falling rate of profit and a self-dissolving value form, and any outcome whatsoever — boom, bust, stagnation, robot plenty — gets slotted into one of the two. Plus general intellect is a metaphor Marx never converts into a mechanism. It explains everything, so it explains nothing.
