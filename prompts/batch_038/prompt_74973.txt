I sit on a four-person working group at a structural engineering practice of about three hundred people, and for five months we have been going through the firm's legacy internal rules ahead of a merger, with a mandate to recommend which of them survive. One is a rule, unbroken so far as anyone can establish for forty years, that no change to a live project's design may be released without the countersignature of an engineer who has no other involvement in that project. Everyone, including me, has always understood it as a check against error and against client pressure, and my draft recommendation was to replace it with a software-assisted review that would catch more of both at a fraction of the cost. Then, pulling promotion histories for an unrelated part of the review, I noticed that every project director the firm has had since the mid-eighties was formed by the countersignature rotation, that the rotation is the only mechanism the firm has ever had for producing engineers who understand disciplines other than their own, and that the firm has consequently almost never needed to hire a director from outside. I also found, in a retired partner's notebooks, that when someone proposed in the mid-nineties relabelling the rotation as a formal development programme, the standard of the sign-offs fell apart inside a year and the change was quietly reverted. The rotation seems to work because the people doing it experience it as a duty owed to a project rather than as a course being taught to them. Our report goes to the partnership in three weeks, and there is a live decision about whether that section stays in it. What I want from you is not a description of my firm, which is full of irrelevant politics, but a clean designed case I can argue with.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. (i) Setting: an ordinary private firm or public body of realistic size; no futuristic technology, no perfect information, no state-of-nature staging, no machinery beyond the kind of institutional facts I have just described. You may change my practice entirely if some other one carries the structure better — I want the structure, not my firm. (ii) Two reasoners, whom you may name: a conservator who argues only from Chesterton's fence, and a reformer who argues only from an act-consequentialist assessment of outcomes over whatever horizon they like. Both are intelligent, both are fully informed, both accept exactly the same non-normative facts, and both agree that the practice's real function is worth having and worth paying something for. Neither may be a fool, a hypocrite, or a sentimentalist. (iii) Neither may appeal to any independent moral constraint — no rights, no duties of honesty or fidelity, no fairness, no respect-for-persons premise. Whatever force their conclusions have must come from their stated principle plus the facts. (iv) The required verdicts, and this is the point of the exercise: the conservator must conclude that the practice should be abolished and that the finding about its real function should be circulated to everyone it affects, while the reformer must conclude that the practice should be preserved and that the finding should be suppressed or rewritten. Both conclusions have to be forced by the premises, not merely permitted by them. (v) The mechanism generating the split must be that the practice's function is produced by the participants' understanding of it as serving some other, quite ordinary purpose; nobody is lying to anybody, and the misunderstanding is the uncoordinated, everyday kind that institutions run on. (vi) There must be a genuine fork: a document stating the finding exists, there are real alternatives about whether it becomes common knowledge, and the suppress option must be feasible without obvious collateral disaster that would settle the question by itself. (vii) You must explain the belief-dependence mechanistically — why knowing the function degrades it — in a way that a sceptical organisational psychologist would find at least arguable rather than magical.

For the avoidance of doubt about what would count as cheating: the case is meant to test whether Chesterton's fence has any content beyond a counsel of epistemic delay — specifically, whether satisfying the fence's own condition can ever *require* removal rather than merely *permit* it, given that Chesterton frames understanding as the price of permission rather than as a warning. A case in which the conservator's conclusion secretly depends on a smuggled objection to deceiving people, or the reformer's on a smuggled distaste for change, fails the brief even if it is well written. If you think the target is genuinely unreachable under these constraints, tell me so and diagnose which constraint is doing the damage; I would much rather have that verdict than a case that quietly relaxes one of them. Please organise the response in these sections, in this order: **The case** (the scenario only, no analysis, ending at the decision point, about 300 words); **Common ground** (a numbered list of the facts and evaluative commitments both reasoners share, about 150 words); **The conservator's derivation** (numbered steps from the fence principle to abolition-and-publication, about 300 words, with the crucial step — from "I now see the use of it" to "therefore it must go" — singled out and defended explicitly, since I expect this is where the case will break if it breaks); **The reformer's derivation** (numbered steps from outcome-assessment to preservation-and-suppression, about 300 words); **The hinge** (the single feature of the case that makes the two derivations diverge, about 200 words — I suspect it is the publicity-sensitivity of the function, but if the work is really being done by something else, such as the existence of the document or the fact that the conservator is reasoning about their own standing rather than about outcomes, say so); **The removal test** (take that feature out and say which standard, uninteresting case you are left with, about 150 words); **Cross-examination** (each side's best attack on the other's reasoning and the best reply, about 250 words — please include the reformer's charge that the conservator has turned a maxim about evidence into a principle about authorization, and the conservator's charge that a consistent consequentialist about institutions ends up arguing exactly as Burke argued when he defended inherited prejudice, so that the reformer's position is conservatism with a guilty conscience); and **Confidence** (about 150 words). No citations, no bibliography, no named books or articles; if you think real institutions contain practices with this structure, describe them generically. In that last section, tell me how confident you are that the case actually delivers the forced verdicts rather than merely dressing them up, on what basis you are calibrating that confidence, and single out the one step in either derivation you are least sure of and why.
