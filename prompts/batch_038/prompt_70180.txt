I'm putting together a talk for a film-night-with-philosophy thing — mixed crowd of grad students and non-philosophers, all of whom have seen Everything Everywhere All at Once — and I want to build it around David Lewis's modal realism rather than the vaguer multiverse talk people usually reach for. Can you write me the reading?

Here's the angle I keep circling, and I want you to either develop it properly or convince me it's wrong. Everyone assumes the film is a Lewisian pluriverse: many concrete worlds, all equally real, all the Evelyns counterparts of one another. But the film's own machinery looks anti-Lewisian in three specific ways, and I'd like each worked through instead of waved at.

First, its worlds branch. Every choice spawns a new universe, so all of them share a trunk and a common history. Lewis is adamant that worlds don't overlap and don't branch — that's the whole reason he needs counterpart theory instead of trans-world identity. So is the film's pluriverse actually closer to what Lewis would file under an ersatzist construction, a branching tree of descriptions, than under genuine concrete plurality? Does that distinction change how we should read the film, or is it bookkeeping?

Second, verse-jumping hands Evelyn the skills, memories, and body of her parallel selves. That's trans-world identity plus bodily transport, both of which Lewis forbids. If the film needs Evelyn to be literally the same person in every world, does it forfeit the thing Lewis most wanted — that de re possibility is qualitative similarity between distinct individuals, not one person smeared across worlds? And there's the Humphrey-style worry underneath: when Evelyn watches the movie-star version of herself, is the film inviting us to think that's what she could have been in a sense Lewis can support, or is it smuggling in a stronger notion of a self that counterpart theory denies? My sense is the film wants the emotional payoff of both readings at once, and I'd like that diagnosed rather than resolved cheaply.

Third, the jumping rule itself: you reach another world by doing something wildly improbable for you. That's an inverse-similarity rule, which sits very strangely next to Lewis's counterpart relation being a matter of weighted, context-sensitive comparative similarity. Is there anything philosophically interesting in that mismatch, or is it a gag that happens to have a metaphysics-shaped hole in it? Say which.

The part I actually care most about is Jobu Tupaki's nihilism. Her argument looks like a standard objection to modal realism — if every possibility is actual somewhere, nothing you do is special, so nothing matters — and Lewis's answer is supposed to be that actual is an indexical, like here or now, and that plurality doesn't dilute anything. Work out whether that answer is available to Evelyn in the film, or whether the film has already given it away: Jobu experiences all worlds at once, which is just about the one state of mind the indexicality move is supposed to rule out. Is Jobu someone who has lost the indexical, with the ending being Evelyn getting it back? Or is the film's optimism about a failing laundromat doing metaphysical work Lewis's view can't license? Related: what do we make of the everything bagel — a world where everything is true, which Lewis can't admit since worlds have to be consistent and recombination is limited accordingly, or rather a place where all worlds are superimposed, which would violate isolation instead of consistency? And the rock canyon scene, the universe where life never got going and the two of them are just rocks with all the dialogue as on-screen text — is that the film's most Lewisian moment, since nothing in it is instrumental to Evelyn's own world, or is it a sentimental use of a world Lewis would insist is a world like any other?

My hunch, which you should push on hard rather than flatter: the film is a modal realist in its imagery and a thoroughgoing actualist in its heart. Nothing in it ever asks us to grieve anyone who doesn't exist in Evelyn's world, and every other universe functions as a store of information she reads off. If that's right, the film isn't dramatizing Lewis at all — it's an ersatzist pluriverse shot on a realist budget — and the honest critic has to say so out loud.

Constraints: no reading list, no citations, and don't quote the film or Lewis verbatim, I want your own formulations throughout. Don't reduce the film to an illustration of the theory, and don't bend the theory to fit the film; where it resists the reading, say so and say what the resistance costs. End by telling me what the film adds to the modal realism debate that the arguments alone don't, if anything. Around 1500-2000 words.
