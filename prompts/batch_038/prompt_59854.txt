I am chairing a book symposium at a law school this spring, and the author we are discussing defends a position I will call, as she does, the record theory of judicial review. I will state it to you as precisely as I can, since I want your questions keyed to this view rather than to any neighbor of it.

Her claims: courts have no authority to invalidate legislation on the ground that its outcome is unjust, or that the legislature misidentified what a right requires. The only legitimate ground of invalidation is that the legislature failed to treat the measure as one requiring public justification. Concretely, a court may look for a short list of institutional artifacts — a committee report that engages the objections actually raised against the measure, a recorded vote rather than voice passage, a bill that stood on its own rather than arriving as a rider on unrelated must-pass legislation, an amendment rule that permitted the minority to propose changes — and may hold a statute unenforceable when the artifacts are missing. Her remedy is not permanent invalidation but remand: the measure returns to the legislature, which is free to re-enact it once the record exists. She argues this dissolves the counter-majoritarian objection because the court never substitutes its own view of the good, or of rights, for the legislature's; it polices only the preconditions of lawmaking's being lawmaking at all. She concedes, and defends as the price of democratic legitimacy, that a properly recorded statute she considers substantively wrong will stand. She also holds that courts are institutionally better at diagnosing procedural defects than at moral reasoning, and she treats that as a comparative empirical claim rather than an article of faith. She is content to be compared with the representation-reinforcing tradition, but insists she goes further than it, since she refuses to identify any list of substantive rights for courts to protect.

I have sixty minutes with her and an audience of law students. Draft eight to ten questions for me.

Requirements:
- No warm-up questions and nothing of the form of asking what drew her to the view. Every question must turn on the content of the record theory as I have stated it, and every question must be pointed enough that she cannot answer it by restating her thesis.
- For each question, supply the follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, and state in one clause what that reply is likely to be, so that I know which branch of the exchange I am in. Where her most likely reply forks, give me the fork rather than a single follow-up.
- Include at least one question each on the following, and make each of them specific to her machinery rather than a general worry about proceduralism: (a) how she tells genuine deliberation apart from the performance of it, given that a legislature can manufacture any artifact on her list in a single afternoon; (b) the regress, since a court judging whether a report "engages the objections actually raised" is judging the quality of reasoning, which looks like the substantive judgment she disclaims; (c) what she says to the litigant whose rights were violated by a statute carrying a flawless record; (d) emergencies, where the absence of a record is sometimes what responsible legislating requires, and where her theory would license courts to slow the fastest responses; (e) what her remedy amounts to if the legislature ignores the remand, or re-enacts the identical measure with a thin report attached, and whether the statute remains enforceable against my litigant in the meantime; (f) the source of a court's authority to impose procedural requirements that no constitutional text mentions.
- One question should press her on what evidence would count against the comparative claim about courts, and what she would do with her theory if that evidence came in against her.
- One question should ask whether her own theory has ever been adopted by a deliberating body that recorded its reasons, and what she makes of the answer.
- If an objection I have asked for is already familiar in the literature, note that in a clause; do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a survey of who has said what. I want questions.

After the list, tell me how confident you are that each question identifies a genuine difficulty rather than one she has a clean answer to — a word or a short scale per question is enough — and then single out the one question on which you are least sure you have characterized her position correctly, explaining what would turn on getting that characterization right.
