I am in my final year and currently taking a seminar on the foundations of probability. We have just spent two weeks on the reference class problem, and the accompanying assignment was to select a single, specific future event, state its probability, and defend the number from frequency data. Two classmates — I will call them Marta and Elias — independently chose the same case, produced different numbers, and then argued about it for close to an hour in the café. I was at the adjacent table, said nothing, and have tried to reproduce their exchange as accurately and as evenhandedly as I can.

The case: Elias's grandmother, 78 and diabetic, is deciding whether to undergo a revision total knee replacement. Registry data give roughly 9% for serious complications across all revision knee replacements and roughly 15% for revisions in patients over 75; the surgeon's own published series reports 2 complications in 43 comparable operations, about 5%. Elias's draft used the surgeon's series; Marta's used the over-75 registry subgroup. Their disagreement went well past the numbers:

(1) Marta opened by saying the assignment is ill-posed as written. Because the grandmother belongs to indefinitely many classes with divergent frequencies, defensible answers range from about 5% to about 20%, and which one you produce depends entirely on which class you nominate; so the exercise rewards motivated selection rather than inference.

(2) Elias replied that there is a standing discipline for this, roughly the rule we were given for direct inference: reason from the narrowest reference class for which you can still make a warranted and reasonably precise frequency judgment. That, he said, is exactly why the surgeon's series beats the registry — it is more specific and still has enough cases to be usable.

(3) Marta said "narrowest usable" is indexed to our evidence, not to the world: as the data improve, the correct answer to what the probability of her operation is would change, which is absurd if that probability is a fact about the case. She added that the candidate classes form a lattice rather than a chain — narrower by surgeon, by hospital, by year, by age, by diabetes, by which knee — so there is no unique narrowest class in any event.

(4) Elias said to impose a relevance constraint instead: keep narrowing only along factors that are statistically relevant to the outcome, and stop when further narrowing leaves a class whose frequency is swamped by sampling noise. He pointed out that the far end of narrowing is clearly unavailable, since the class consisting of this one operation has a frequency of either 0 or 1, so a stopping point both exists and is required; his rule supplies it.

(5) Marta said statistical relevance is defined by reference to the outcome in question, so the rule presupposes knowing which factors matter before it can tell you which class to use. She then said the surgeon's series is worse than merely narrow: the surgeon decides whom to operate on, so her series is a filtered class, and a 78-year-old diabetic is precisely the profile a surgeon declines — meaning the frequency may fail to transfer even if the series had hundreds of cases. This, she said, is why the reductive frequency analysis of single-case probability should be given up rather than patched further.

(6) Elias said her diagnosis proves too much. A subjectivist must also decide which statistics to defer to when setting a prior, so the same selection arises on any account; if the reference class problem is fatal to frequentism, it is equally fatal to credences, and so cannot be a decisive objection to either.

(7) Marta's final move: a credence is hers to set and makes no claim that some uniquely correct class exists in the world, whereas his number does make that claim, and it is that claim which needs a uniquely correct class. Elias said this simply relocates the problem and immunizes her view by definition. They stopped there.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than as a whole. For each of the seven numbered moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right but overstated, or clearly right, and justify the rating briefly; where a move bundles several claims, split them and rate each. Then tell me who is right about which specific issues — in particular, whether the evidential-indexing charge in (3) is genuinely damaging or answerable, whether the homogeneity constraint in (4) resolves the circularity Marta alleges in (5) or repeats it, whether the selection point about the surgeon's series is a new objection or just another instance of the original one, and whether (6) is a real tu quoque. I would also like to know what both of them are missing, and whether there is a resolution available that neither considered. If either of them has misstated a doctrine they are leaning on — I have in mind the direct-inference rule in (2) and the homogeneity-based alternative to it that we were also assigned, and I may have reported them imperfectly — correct the characterization rather than accept it as I have rendered it. I am not looking for references; I want the arguments assessed as they were actually made.

One last request about your own stance. Please state how confident you are in the ratings you assign overall, and single out the point in your adjudication that you are least sure of, explaining what would settle it.
