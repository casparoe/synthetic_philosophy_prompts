I am taking a medical ethics seminar this term and we have just spent two weeks on the determination of death. I want to get clear on what our actual transplant practice commits us to, rather than on the textbook summary of the criteria, and I would appreciate a careful reconstruction.

Here is the practice as I understand it. In controlled donation after circulatory death, the patient is a ventilated person with a devastating and non-survivable neurological injury who has not been declared brain dead. The family and the team decide to withdraw life-sustaining treatment; extubation takes place in or immediately adjacent to the operating theatre. In some programmes, an anticoagulant and a vasodilator are administered to the patient after the decision to withdraw but before death is declared, purely to protect the organs for the recipient. When the heart stops, the team observes a fixed standoff interval — commonly on the order of two to five minutes — after which death is declared on circulatory grounds and procurement begins, often with cold perfusion, within a minute or two. No resuscitation is attempted during the interval, and the protocol provides that if circulation returns, procurement stops. Meanwhile, elsewhere in the same hospital, a patient presenting the identical rhythm who is not a registered donor receives prolonged compressions, drugs, and possibly extracorporeal support, and is not declared dead for far longer; the difference in treatment tracks donor status and nothing else. There is a second strand to the picture: the same institutions also declare death on neurologic criteria, where a beating heart is held not to matter, and there are reported cases of bodies declared brain dead being kept on somatic support for weeks in order to carry a pregnancy, and of declared-dead patients being kept perfused for days for organ procurement. In at least one much-litigated American case a hospital continued somatic support for weeks on a patient it had already declared dead, citing a state statute concerning pregnant patients.

So, working from that description, tell me what conception of death the practice presupposes.

First: the statutory formulations most of us work under speak in terms of irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function. But the DCD donor's circulation is plainly not irreversible in the sense of incapable of being restored — the team is standing by in a position to demonstrate otherwise the moment anything stirs, and ordinary ICU treatment would restart that heart. What has ceased is permanent, in the sense that it will not be restored because we have resolved not to restore it. Does the practice therefore rest on a different criterion from the one it officially invokes, and if so, what happens to the idea that death is a biological fact we discover rather than a status we confer? How much work is being done by the fact that the resolution not to restore is grounded in the patient's own prior refusal of treatment?

Second: the dead donor rule is supposed to guarantee that procurement does not cause death. In this setting procurement begins minutes after an arrest that would be reversed by the treatment given to the non-donor upstairs. Does the rule survive only if the standoff interval is read as a bright line adopted for reasons other than the patient's biological condition at that minute — the avoidance of autoresuscitation, the avoidance of the appearance of killing, the protection of public trust? What does each of those readings presuppose about the rule's function: is it a moral constraint on killing, a rule of reassurance addressed to the public, or a legal safe harbour for clinicians? Which of those is compatible with the interval being a round number that varies between protocols and jurisdictions?

Third: what does the premortem administration of organ-preserving drugs presuppose about the interests of the dying patient — that a patient this close to death has no remaining interest that such a drug could set back, that valid consent covers small risks undertaken for a third party's benefit, or that the donor has ceased to be a patient at the moment of the withdrawal decision? Is any of that consistent with the fact that the same drug, given to the non-donor upstairs, would require a benefit-and-risk justification addressed to that patient?

Fourth: we treat loss of the whole brain as sufficient for death and loss of circulation as sufficient for death, in the same building, in the same week. Do the two criteria presuppose a single underlying account of what makes an organism dead — loss of somatic integration, loss of the capacity for consciousness, loss of the organism's own work — or two different accounts that happen to coincide in ordinary cases and visibly diverge in these? Which account, if any, would license a five-minute interval rather than a two-minute or a twenty-minute one, and what does the answer to that tell us about what the practice is really tracking?

Throughout, please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits well with. I am especially interested in which of the standard accounts — whole-brain, higher-brain, circulatory, organism-as-a-whole, irreversibility-based, permanence-based, and the conventionalist view on which death is settled by communal stipulation — can accommodate all of the elements I have listed, which are ruled out by them, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that several accounts would license it equally. If two accounts make identical predictions about every protocol parameter, say so rather than manufacturing a difference. Finish by telling me what would actually have to change — interval length, drug administration, the structure of consent, who is permitted to declare and when — if an institution adopted a strictly irreversibility-based criterion in place of the permanence-based one it appears to be using, and whether that change would end controlled circulatory donation altogether or merely slow it down. Please also flag anything in my description of the practice that you think is inaccurate, oversimplified, or varies substantially by jurisdiction.
