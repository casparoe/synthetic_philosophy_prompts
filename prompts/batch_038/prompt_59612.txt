Last month a doctoral workshop in Atlantic history turned on a single sentence. My chapter reads the diary of an eighteenth-century Antiguan sugar planter as an exercise in self-deception: he logs floggings in the flat vocabulary of bookkeeping, and I argue the flatness is itself the evidence, that he half knew what he was doing. A senior faculty member called this presentism — importing our concept of cruelty, and a whole moral psychology along with it, into a mind that had no use for either — and when I asked how one could describe the practice at all without some such vocabulary, he closed the exchange with the first sentence of L. P. Hartley's The Go-Between: "The past is a foreign country; they do things differently there." Ten minutes later someone pointed out that the very same sentence turns up in op-eds defending plantation-house tours and monuments to slaveholders, where it is made to carry a verdict rather than a caution: that we have no standing to judge the dead.

Please treat the sentence as a piece of language rather than as a quotation. Give exactly five distinct readings of it, numbered, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion, ordered from the most deflationary to the most radical. Each reading should state the precise claim the sentence could be making — about what past people actually believed, about our capacity to understand them, about whether moral concepts survive translation across centuries, about what a historian may assert as opposed to describe, or about something else the image is reaching at — and should then append three short verdicts: whether the claim is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or false; whether it would license my professor's objection to the words cruelty and self-deception in my chapter; and whether it would also license the op-ed use, since I suspect no single reading can support both. Let at least one of the five test what the metaphor commits its user to, given that the sentence arrives as an image and not a bare assertion — a foreign country, with Hartley's narrator reaching it by way of a diary he can still read.
