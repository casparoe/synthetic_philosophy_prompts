So I work on a small backend team at a company that makes a recidivism risk score that a few US states use at parole hearings, and last week the whole "are we the bad guys" argument blew up in our retro. A nonprofit that reviews our tool wrote up something calling it the medical model in a trench coat — meaning we dress up a coercive classification as care. My PM pushed back with two analogies back to back. First one: we're not punishing anybody, we're doing triage, the score just tells you who needs the treatment bed. Second one, when someone said triage still means the state grabs you: it's quarantine, not retribution — you isolate someone carrying something contagious and you don't hate them for it, in fact you're supposed to be nice about it, and the humane version of quarantine is exactly the right target. Then a third person pointed at Halden, the Norwegian prison from 2010 that every documentary does, and said see, that one basically is a hospital and that's why it works, so the analogy is fine, you just have to build the nice hospital instead of the nasty one.

What I want is an audit of the two analogies separately, prison-as-hospital and prison-as-quarantine, because they're being used to defend the same thing but I'm pretty sure they're not the same claim. For each one, give me the correspondence table explicitly: who's the patient and who's the carrier, what's the disease, what counts as a diagnosis and who's qualified to make one, what's the treatment, what's the discharge criterion, what plays the role of informed consent, what plays the role of a malpractice suit, and what plays the role of the fact that a hospital is supposed to be worse for the doctor if the patient dies. Then tell me where each mapping breaks, and please sort the breaks into two piles — the disanalogies that actually bite for what my PM is using them for (deflecting a coercion objection) and the ones that are cosmetic and would just be noise in the meeting. And be explicit about what each analogy smuggles in that the target doesn't have: I'm thinking stuff like the assumption that the state's motive is beneficent, that the person in front of you is a case rather than an agent, that there's a determinate clinical endpoint, that the expert and the subject's interests point the same direction. Say which of those the quarantine framing keeps and which it drops.

Then the part I actually care about: does quarantine get my PM what he wants? It's supposed to make the score non-punitive, but my gut says it's worse, because quarantine doesn't care what you did, so it's comfortable detaining someone who scored risky and hasn't harmed anyone, it has no natural upper bound, and it has no lower bound either — release the moment someone stops being contagious means possibly never, or possibly letting out someone who did something awful and looks clean on the metrics, which no parole board will ever actually do. Tell me whether that gut feeling is right or whether I'm missing a version of the quarantine argument that constrains it. And seperately: does the hospital analogy explain Halden at all, or is Halden built on some other idea entirely — something like the punishment just being the loss of liberty and everything else staying deliberately normal — that neither hospital nor quarantine captures? If there's a better analogy for what a parole risk score actually does in a system like that, propose one and tell me what it would commit us to. Its fine to conclude that none of them hold. I have no philosophy background so define terms as you go, and don't send me a bibliography or a who-said-what list, I can't go look any of it up — just reason it through in front of me.
