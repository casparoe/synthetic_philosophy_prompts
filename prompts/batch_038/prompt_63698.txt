I am in the last round of copyedits on a novel, and I have reached an impasse with my editor that cannot be settled on grounds of craft, so I would like to know whether the principle she is appealing to is sound.

The book contains two minor characters, whom I will call A and B. They never share a scene; they are never spoken of to one another; nobody in the book knows of both. I built them from a single character sheet and, being fond of the exercise, I varied nothing in it: the same age, the same trade, the same father's trade, the same two mannerisms, and the same four sentences of physical description wherever description was called for. The only things that set them apart are relational, and supplied by the surrounding story. A frequents a tavern in the third chapter and is being bled dry by a man whose path never crosses B's. B appears in the ninth and eleventh chapters, is unmarried, and is once taken by a stranger for someone else.

My editor's note amounts to this: you have not written two characters, you have written one character twice. If everything true of A is true of B and everything true of B is true of A, she argues, there is nothing for a difference between them to consist in — their being two rather than one is not itself a feature of either. She adds that "the one who turns up in chapter three" and "the one who is being blackmailed" are not features of A in the way his height is: they are features of the book, or else they are a disguised way of pointing, and pointing at A only succeeds if there are already two of them to point at. She wants me either to differentiate them properly or to lose one.

I have resisted, but I would rather test the resistance than defend it, so I have two questions and I would like them kept apart.

First: is her principle correct? Suppose I went further than she asks and stripped out the blackmail, the tavern, the marriage and the mistaken identity, so that nothing at all distinguished them beyond the fact that the story contains two of them. Would that manuscript be one character described twice, or two characters? I cannot tell whether her reasoning proves that the pared-down version collapses, or proves something I am not willing to accept about the version I actually wrote.

Second: whatever the answer to that, what should I do at this stage? My readers, unlike philosophers, will meet A and B on different pages, in different company, months apart in the reading, and I suspect that this makes the whole dispute academic for the purposes of the book — but I am not certain that "readers won't notice" is a legitimate answer to "there is no fact of the matter."

One request as to the shape of the reply. Please separate what is settled here from what is genuinely disputed among people who work on this. If the principle my editor invokes is one that a substantial number of such people reject, or accept only with significant qualifications, say so plainly; do not present her position — or mine — as the agreed-upon one simply because it is the more convenient to argue with.
