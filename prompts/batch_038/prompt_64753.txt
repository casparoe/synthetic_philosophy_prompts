I teach a graduate seminar in moral psychology and metaethics, and our final session takes up a question I find genuinely unsettled: whether the descriptive psychology of political moralities — moral foundations research above all — tells us anything whatever about which side of a left–right dispute is right. My students assume the answer is an easy no, on grounds of the genetic fallacy and the is–ought gap. The colleague whose work we will be reading, a philosopher I will call H., has written a series of papers in which she rules, case by case, on whether a given empirical finding has normative upshot, and her rulings look inconsistent to my students. I suspect they are not, but I want the suspicion tested before I put it in front of a room. Here is the track record as I have compiled it.

On the studies in which incidental disgust — a foul smell in the room, a filthy desk, a nauseating film clip — makes subjects' moral condemnations harsher, she holds that these do real normative work: not that the judgments so produced are false, but that the subjects lacked warrant for them, so that any political argument leaning on purity intuitions loses its evidential support. On the questionnaire finding that conservatives endorse all five foundations roughly evenly while liberals weight care and fairness most heavily, she holds that this is no evidence that conservatism is right, though it is evidence against any normative theory treating care and fairness as jointly exhaustive of the moral domain — descriptive breadth, she says, bears on theory choice rather than on particular verdicts. On the inequity-aversion experiments with capuchin monkeys, she holds that continuity with other primates buys nothing: a monkey's refusal of a cucumber while its neighbor receives a grape does not tell us whether inherited inequality is unjust, and naturalness is no mark of truth. On the several-fold cross-cultural variation in ultimatum-game offers, she holds that the variation counsels modesty about treating one population's fairness intuitions as data, but does not lower confidence in fairness-based claims, because the variation is explained by market integration and the scale of cooperation — an explanation, she insists, that is silent on whether such claims are true. And on a large multi-site comparison reporting that harm-based judgments show the least cross-population variance and the least movement under incidental manipulations, she holds that this makes harm-based claims better warranted than purity-based ones, an asymmetry she states outright.

The new case is a preprint that arrived last week, and I want to know what she will say about it before I ask her. A team working with a small-scale pastoralist population reports, first, that their instruments detect robust loyalty and authority effects and robust harm effects but essentially nothing that looks like a fairness foundation: no inequity aversion in their distribution tasks, and no condemnation of cheater scenarios that involve neither harm nor betrayal. Second, and more interestingly, the team reports that when their own undergraduate subjects are run through a manipulation designed to reproduce features of that ecology — anonymous and non-repeating allocators, acute scarcity framing, no reputational audience — the subjects' fairness judgments move substantially and reversibly in the same direction, while their harm judgments do not move at all. The question I will put to H. is whether this finding bears on the credibility of our own convictions about distributive justice, say the conviction that a society permitting vast inherited inequality is unjust, and if so, how.

Predict her verdict. Then do the harder work. Lay out the candidate rules that would generate all five of her earlier rulings; I claim there are at least three live ones — one keyed to the reliability of the belief-forming process, one keyed to whether non-moral causes are of a specifically incidental or arousal-based kind, and one keyed to whether the moral fact is doing any explanatory work in the first place — and they do not converge on this preprint. Then evaluate a fourth rule of my own devising, which I have not yet published and which I would like you to break if you can: what matters is not the reliability of the process but whether the non-moral cause is an irrelevant cue, external to the situation judged, or a morally relevant parameter of that situation, one to which the correct verdict might itself be responsive. On that rule the disgust cases debunk, the ultimatum-game variation does not, and the capuchin case is untouched; but I confess that her fifth ruling, crediting harm's stability with positive warrant, is the one that strains the proposal. Say whether the rule can absorb that ruling, and if not, which of the two must give. Close by telling me the single cheapest further case — one study, one manipulation — that would discriminate among the leading rules, and say what result would favor which.

Please write to me as you would write a letter: continuous prose throughout, with no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, and no tables. Do not trouble yourself with citations or a bibliography; I know these studies and will supply the references myself. Twelve hundred words is ample.
