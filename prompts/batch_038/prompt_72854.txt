You are to work as a senior political philosopher whose specialty is the theory of public reason — the family of views about which kinds of reasons may legitimately be offered in support of coercive political decisions. Please think carefully and work in stages rather than delivering a verdict immediately.

**Stage one, diagnostic.** Before you write the final deliverable, spend a few hundred words taking the sentence in question apart. Identify which of its words are doing ambiguous work, and list the independent choices any precisification of it must make: who is bound by the requirement, what exactly must be offered, offered to whom, by when, on what underlying normative ground, and with what consequence if the requirement goes unmet. Note which combinations of answers are stable positions and which collapse under pressure, and flag any reading you judge to be a strawman rather than a live option someone might actually hold.

**The sentence.** At last Tuesday's meeting, one of my colleagues leaned across the table to a member who is also an ordained minister and said: "You're entitled to your view, but if you want it to count in here you have to translate it into public reasons first." He has said some variant of this at least four times this year. It has never once been argued for. It is used as a rule of order, and people accept it as though it were self-evident.

**The case it was used in.** The council was voting whether to renew a contract with a faith-based overnight shelter. The contract obliges the shelter to house anyone who presents at the door; the shelter's own settled practice is to require attendance at a twenty-minute chapel service before dinner as a condition of receiving a bed. The minister opposed renewal and gave as his reason that worship exacted as the price of a bed is not worship at all, that it makes the city a party to a transaction that degrades the guest's standing before God, and that a shelter bought with public money ought not to be in the business of harvesting assent from people who have nowhere else to sleep. That is when my colleague made his remark. The minister, taking the point, offered what seemed to me a clean translation: public funds should not purchase coerced participation in a practice whose value depends on its being freely undertaken, and the city should not run a service whose terms are set by one confession and imposed on residents of every other confession and none. My colleague replied that this changed nothing, that it was the same religious view in a different coat, because the only reason to think coerced participation destroys the value of the practice is that one already accepts the minister's account of what the practice is for.

Two complications. A second member, who supported renewal, argued directly from scripture and made no attempt at translation; nobody objected to her. A third member argued against renewal on broadly Kantian grounds about autonomy and about not using people merely as a means to someone else's ends, which struck me as a comprehensive philosophical doctrine if anything is, and nobody asked him to translate either.

**Stage two, the deliverable.** I am drafting the record, and more urgently I want to know whether that sentence has any content at all that would let me rule one of these three contributions in and another out. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing as a numbered menu — not a general essay on public reason, and not a summary of the debate. The object of analysis is that one sentence and the work it was made to do in that room.

For each entry give the thesis in a single bolded sentence stated precisely enough to be true or false, a verdict tag in brackets (one of: platitude; substantive but defensible; substantive and contentious; false; not a coherent claim as stated), two to four sentences of explanation, and then an explicit answer to both of these questions, which I suspect my colleague is running together: does this reading license dismissing the minister's *original* argument, and does it license dismissing his *translation*?

Make sure the menu covers, at minimum: whether the demand is for a different vocabulary or for a different justificatory ground, and what follows when a faithful translation turns out to be weaker, or less widely accepted, or possibly false once detached from the doctrine that generated it; whether the requirement binds the private citizen, the office-holder, or the deliberative body in its official capacity; whether it must be satisfied in advance of speaking or whether satisfaction can come later in the same debate or afterwards in the official justification; whether its ground is the legitimacy of coercion, civic respect for persons who do not share the doctrine, or the quality of collective deliberation; whether it is a rule of admissibility or a rule of weight; whether it applies to comprehensive doctrines generally or only to religious ones, and if only to religious ones, whether any principled line can be drawn that also handles my Kantian colleague; and what would make a restatement a genuine translation rather than a re-description, and whether that notion can be given determinate content at all.

**Stage three, closing.** Say which reading or readings my colleague is most plausibly invoking, whether he can hold them simultaneously given what he actually did in the case — accepting the scriptural argument for renewal while rejecting the minister's two arguments against it — and then give me a short list of the moves the minister could make that would satisfy each reading you judged live.
