Back in my working life I ran a spectroscopy lab for 35 years, retired two winters ago, and I'm now reading history and philosophy of science properly for the first time. Next month I'm giving an informal talk to a history-of-science reading group: mostly social historians of the lab and the field station, one person who's published on Hayden White, and a couple of grad students doing archivial work. My line is that Ranke was roughly right — the historian's job is to pin down what actually happened — and that the scepticism from Carr, and especially White's argument that narrative form does the real work, doesn't license the conclusion that historical claims aren't objectively true or false. The argument I want them to attack is a physicist's: I want to say that White's scheme of emplotments and tropes functions like a choice of coordinates or a gauge fixing, and the historical content is whatever stays invariant when you retell the same events as tragedy, as irony, as farce. So form is real, but it doesn't swallow the facts.

Give me a prep sheet rather than a defense: the six to eight objections this particular crowd will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each does to my talk, each phrased the way they'd phrase it — their vocabulary, not mine — with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply goes thin. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so I don't come across as someone who's never opened the books (I've read Carr, White only secondhand), and finish with the one question I'm praying nobody asks. American English, please, and go light on the jargon — I'm still learning it.
