I'm running a 90-minute philosophy of mind seminar next Thursday — sixteen third-years, a bright room, no projector or computer, just index cards, coloured paper, and a phone stopwatch. They will have read the colour phi material beforehand, so they already know the labels Orwellian and Stalinesque; what they lack is any grip on why the question "did the colour change happen before the second spot or after?" is supposed to be empty. Last term I taught the same passage and three students spent twenty minutes arguing over the answer while I stood there unable to show them the dispute was vacuous rather than merely subtle.

Design the activity. I want them to generate the illusion themselves with hand-flipped cards, commit on paper to a timeline of when the colour change occurred, and then have that commitment measured against something independent — a sealed report, a forced choice under time pressure, a leading question posed after a delay — so a discrepancy can be produced in the room and scored, and so students can lose points for being confident about the timing of their own experience. Give me the card parameters (size, separation, the interval that makes apparent motion fire and the one that makes it fail), a minute-by-minute timeline, a money-free scoring scheme, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the two models and lands the point that both predict everything they just did.

Two things to handle explicitly. The obvious design puts one student in the editor's chair, which just reinstates the Cartesian theatre with a person in the seat: either avoid it or make that failure part of the lesson. And tell me what will go wrong on the first run — students who never see the motion, those who conclude only that memory is unreliable, and the room leaving with the wrong moral — plus the feature of the case your activity cannot reproduce that I must supply by argument.
