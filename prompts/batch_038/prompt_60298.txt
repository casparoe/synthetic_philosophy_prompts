You are, for this exercise, a historian of patristic moral theology who also publishes on the contemporary analytic literature on lying — someone equally at home in Augustine's *De Mendacio* and in the modern argument about whether lying requires an intention to deceive. Please think carefully before drafting, and in particular begin by working out the logical space of possible positions from a small set of variables, rather than by listing doctrines you remember and then fitting them together afterwards.

I convene a reading group of eight final-year philosophy and theology undergraduates, and this week's text is *De Mendacio* alongside a recent paper on the definition of lying. The session went badly. One student holds that the fugitive-at-the-door case refutes Augustine outright; another thinks the whole thing collapses because a novelist does not lie; a third, who has read something about the later casuistical tradition, insists that Augustine already allows equivocation and simply does not admit it. My supervisor's criticism of the handout I circulated was fair: it was a list of named positions, essentially an encyclopaedia entry in disguise, and the students picked whichever label they already liked without discovering anything about what they actually believe. What I need instead is a diagnostic instrument that sorts them by what they say about cases. I should like you to write it.

The deliverable is a branching quiz of eleven questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, followed by a scoring key and a taxonomy. The requirements are these.

1. Build the tree from four branch variables, each phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing any label: whether the speaker believes what she says to be false; whether she intends to produce a false belief in the person addressed; whether the utterance is offered in a register carrying the implicit warrant of assertion at all, as against joke, fiction, irony or stage line — Augustine sets jokes aside on something like this ground, and my students need to see that this is a real move and not a dodge; and whether the moral assessment of the act depends on anything beyond the relation between what is in the heart and what comes out of the mouth, including consequences for third parties and the questioner's entitlement to an answer. Add a fifth variable if you judge one necessary, and say briefly why.

2. The cases must include: the fugitive and the man who comes looking for him, split into the private questioner and the judge, since the seventh of Augustine's classes carries an explicit exception where a judge interrogates; the midwives of Exodus; Abraham and Sarah on the sister business; a person who says something true while believing it false, and its mirror, a person who says something false while believing it true; the reflexive "I'm fine"; a first-person narrator in a novel; someone who feigns agreement with the doctrines of a sect that itself permits lying in order to get close enough to confute it, which is the Priscillianist problem the later *Contra Mendacium* was written to answer; a doctor's placebo; the sixth class's own example, the person who denies knowing where another's money is hidden; and — my own addition, and I want it handled squarely rather than waved away — an artificial-intelligence system that issues a confident false statement while, so far as anyone can tell, believing nothing whatsoever. That last case is a test of whether the axes survive the loss of the heart.

3. There should be at least nine leaves, each carrying a name, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and an indication of which of the eleven answers are diagnostic of it. Among the leaves I want located: graded Augustinian rigourism, on which all eight classes are sinful but rank from the lie in religious doctrine down to the lie that shields someone from sexual violation; the concealment variant that shifts the entire practical burden onto silence and non-assertion; the internalist double-heart view on which lying is fixed by the mismatch alone; contemporary non-deceptionism; deceptionism proper; a register-or-genre conventionalism; broad mental reservation; the consequentialist threshold view on which gravity can cross over into permissibility; and the deflationary view that there is no distinctive wrong of lying beyond deception or the breach of assertion's warrant.

4. The scoring key should map patterns of answers, not tallies, and should say what a mixed pattern means — for instance, a student who gives the internalist answer on the definition but the consequentialist answer on the midwives.

5. Then give me the taxonomy itself: the combinations of answers the four or five variables permit, with each cell marked occupied or unoccupied. For every unoccupied cell, say whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your instrument cannot really separate — I suspect concealment against broad mental reservation is one such pair, and I want your honest verdict on whether that is a defect of my design or a genuine indeterminacy in the positions.

Roughly fifteen hundred words for the whole handout. Please do not include quotations, page references, a bibliography or any further reading: I will supply those from my own texts, and guesses would be worse than nothing. British spelling throughout.
