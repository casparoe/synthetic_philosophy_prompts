I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm finishing a profile of a behavioral ecologist who has spent her career on signal honesty — the Zahavi/Grafen apparatus, indices versus badges, honest signals versus cheap talk. She is unusually disciplined about which displays she will call handicaps, and I have careful notes from three weeks of interviews plus her graduate seminar, where she worked down a list of cases and ruled on each. Her verdicts:

- The peacock's train: no. It records what he managed to be last season. The female reads a résumé, and nothing is being paid while she reads it.
- The bowerbird's bower: no. Weeks of solitary labor, after which the female arrives to inspect a monument. Whatever was spent, she did not see it spent.
- Thomson's gazelle stotting: yes. He pays in front of the animal he is paying to — energy and exposure, burned while the predator watches — and a gazelle in poor condition cannot keep it up.
- Red deer roaring contests: yes. The rate is the message. By the twentieth roar a stag is spending something he cannot conceal, and his rival is counting.
- The low pitch of a male toad's call: no. It is set by his size and a small male cannot fake it, but a measuring stick is not a price. Unfakeable is not the same as costly.
- The house sparrow's black bib: no. Cheap felt. The honesty lives in the beating a bluffer takes from rivals, not in the badge. That is a police force, not a handicap.

The case my editor wants me to put to her before we go to press is one she has not ruled on. Courtship feeding in a fish-eating seabird — say a tern, though the species doesn't matter. The male hunts for himself and brings the female fish through courtship and early laying; females consistently prefer the males who feed them most often; and the food is not symbolic, since better-fed females lay more and better eggs. Every delivery happens in front of her, and a male in poor condition cannot hold the rate up for long.

Predict her verdict on the tern, and predict what she would call it if not a handicap, since she evidently keeps more than one name for a non-handicap. Then do the harder part: lay out every rule that is compatible with all six of her prior rulings — I suspect at least three survive, and one of them is not the textbook account her verdicts first appear to endorse — and state what each predicts for the new case. Tell me which rule you would bet on and why: which of her stated reasons carry the most weight, and which are consistent with several candidate rules at once. Finally, name the further cases I should ask her about to separate the survivors, what each rule commits her to saying about each of them, and whether any such answer would force her to reopen one of the six verdicts above.
