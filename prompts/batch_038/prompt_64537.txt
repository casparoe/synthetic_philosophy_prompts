I am finishing the third draft of a novel whose protagonist, Desmond Vance, is a former options market maker who has come to regard the Kelly criterion not as a staking rule but as a complete account of how a person ought to live, and who has founded a small fund around that conviction. The book alternates chapters of narration with documentary artifacts — a prospectus, a deposition transcript, an email thread with a former partner — and the artifact I need now is the fund's final quarterly letter to its limited partners, written after a disastrous year. Vance is fictional and I would like him to stay that way: the satire should aim at a style of argument rather than at any real person, firm, or school of thought, and I am not asking for references or a bibliography. The letter must stand entirely on its own, from whatever you already know about the Kelly criterion, geometric versus arithmetic averages, and the recent controversy over time averages versus ensemble averages in finance.

The piece should run roughly eight hundred words and be formatted as a real letter would be: dateline, salutation to the limited partners, three or four headed sections, a signature block, and a legal disclosure paragraph at the end whose wording is technically true and quietly devastating. Vance writes courteously, in a wounded, patient register, and never once notices that he is funny; the reader has to do that work. Here is the fund's situation, which the letter must engage with rather than evade. Its flagship strategy stakes one-sixth of capital each round on a repeated gamble that pays sixty percent on a win and loses forty percent on a loss at even odds, so that one-sixth is precisely the Kelly fraction and the strategy compounds at roughly two percent per round. In the year just ended the true odds drifted below the odds Vance had estimated, the edge was real when it was measured and gone when it was traded, and the fund lost most of its capital without ever being, in his words, mathematically ruined. I need the letter to contain all of the following: a correct observation that staking the whole bankroll each round would have made expected wealth rise linearly in the size of the stake while sending the typical path geometrically toward zero, deployed by Vance as proof of his own prudence; an admission that half-Kelly staking would have cost about a quarter of the growth rate while cutting the dispersion of that growth by about three-quarters, which Vance treats as a bad bargain he rightly refused; the claim that the strategy cannot reach zero, stated as reassurance; and a digression, perhaps parenthetical, dismissing critics who invoke utility functions, bequest motives, or the need to eat along the way as people who have mistaken themselves for a distribution. Above all, the load-bearing move should be Vance's habit of switching between the time-average and the ensemble-average framing — always selecting whichever one flatters the quarter in question, congratulating himself on the typical path when the average outcome would embarrass him and on the average outcome when the typical path would, and never flagging the switch. Do not invent mathematics, fake a theorem, or hand him a claim no practitioner would make; every assertion should be a real one stretched a little past the assumptions that make it true.

After the letter, please give me a short audit of it as a single JSON object with exactly these fields: `title`, the letter's heading; `beats`, an array of at least six objects, each with `phrase` (a verbatim snippet from the letter of twelve words or fewer), `doctrine` (the genuine claim the snippet invokes, in one sentence), `accurate_as_stated` (one of "yes," "partly," "no"), `assumption_traded_away` (the condition the claim needs that the letter quietly drops), and `who_pays` (which party absorbs the cost of dropping it); `the_switch`, two or three sentences identifying the passages where Vance changes averages and what each change buys him; `strongest_defense`, the most honest reply Vance could make to the sharpest objection buried in his own letter; and `consistency_note`, one sentence naming a fact about the fund's history that the audit implies and that the rest of my manuscript must not contradict.
