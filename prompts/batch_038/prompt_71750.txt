I wrote this up for a reading group on modality and it backfired — it was supposed to be a tidy argument for brute first-person facts and instead it seems to eat itself. I can't tell whether one of the steps is rotten or whether I'm actually forced to swallow the conclusion.

(1) If you can coherently conceive of a scenario — fill in the details however you like without hitting any contradiction or conceptual confusion — then that scenario is metaphysically possible.

(2) I can coherently conceive of everything being exactly as it is: same universe, same particles arranged the same way, same people with the same bodies and the same memories and the same psycology — except that the one having these experiences, the one behind these eyes, is my roommate rather then me. Nothing in the world differs. Only who's doing the looking.

(3) So by (1) it's possible for the complete physical and psychological story to be exactly as it actually is while the fact of who I am is different.

(4) So facts like "I am NN" aren't entailed by any physical or psychological facts. They're further, brute facts about the world, and a complete physical description of a world leaves them out.

(5) But now run it again for everyone else. For each person in the room it's possible that all the physical facts be just as they are while that person is one of the others. And possibility isn't relative to who's doing the conceiving: the world in which I'm my roommate and the world in which he's me are the same world, just described from different places inside it. So no possible world contains any fact that answers to "who I am."

(6) And there I have it both ways. (4) says the first-person facts are extra ingredients in worlds; (5) says no world can contain them. Same premises, both directions. So either conceivability reasoning licenses contradictions, or (1) is false, or one of these steps isn't doing what it looks like it's doing.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1) if I can — I lean on that kind of reasoning elsewhere, and conceiving of a situation where the clear, drinkable, lake-filling stuff around here isn't H2O feels to me like exactly the same move as (2), so if (1) goes I want to know what's left of that and whether it's just gone too. Be specific about what the surviving principle permits and what it forbids. And whatever your diagnosis turns out to be, tell me whether it transfers to the zombie argument against materialism or whether that one is insulated from the problem — I keep hearing it's a different case and nobody ever says exactly how.
