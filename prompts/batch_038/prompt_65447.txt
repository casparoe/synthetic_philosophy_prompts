I am in the second half of a philosophy of mind seminar on naturalizing intentionality, and for my term paper I have committed myself to a fairly specific version of teleosemantics that I would now like to stress-test rather than merely defend. The version is consumer-side and etiological: a state counts as a representation at all only if it is produced and consumed by mechanisms with proper functions, where those functions are grounded in phylogenetic selection history; and the content of the state is fixed by the mapping relations between it and conditions in the world that the consuming system requires if it is to perform its function, assessed against Millikan's Normal conditions as indexed to the environment in which the mechanism was selected. I also want to retain the standard resources for hard cases, namely derived proper functions for states produced by systems whose function is itself to produce further states, plus some appeal to ontogenetic or cultural selection processes for traits acquired during a lifetime. My seminar partner, who works in a systems neuroscience lab, keeps insisting that this package either does no explanatory work beyond a causal-informational account or has been patched into invulnerability, and I would like to know which of the two charges actually lands.

Please map the view's exposure. For each of the following pressures, tell me whether it is fatal, seriously damaging, merely awkward, or no threat at all once properly formulated: (a) the grain problem for the classic frog bug-detector, i.e. whether the selected-effect story picks out flies, dark moving specks, small moving things, or frog food, and whether the answer changes for a lab-reared frog that has only ever snapped at pellets; (b) representations whose content has no selectional history behind it at all, such as concepts of quarks, of one particular building, or of a chess structure; (c) the repairs available for (b), and specifically whether admitting ontogenetic selection or derived functions quietly collapses the view into Dretske's learning-period indicator semantics or into a two-factor theory; (d) mismatch cases such as hatchling turtles steering toward beachfront lighting, moths holding a celestial bearing, and insects navigating by a band of sky that no longer serves them, where content fixed in an ancestral environment appears to entail massive systematic error today; (e) the possibility that the relevant trait was selected for flexibility rather than for any mapping, so that selection history cannot determinately fix content at all, which is the line of thought associated with Godfrey-Smith's environmental complexity thesis; (f) the neuronal recycling picture from the cognitive neuroscience of reading and arithmetic, on which culturally recent skills take over circuits with quite different evolutionary jobs — does that vindicate the view, since it would explain why such learning is constrained and effortful, or undercut it, since the recycled circuit plainly comes to carry contents its selected function does not license; and (g) swamp duplicates, which I suspect an advocate must simply swallow. For each item, distinguish what a committed defender would themselves concede counts against the view from what critics merely assert.

Beyond these case-by-case objections I want two structural things. First, what does this view predict that its main rivals — Dretske-style indicator semantics, Fodor's asymmetric dependence account, and functional-role or interpretationist approaches — do not? I am especially interested in whether it yields any empirical or conceptual prediction about where misrepresentation can arise, about how fast content can shift within a single lifetime, or about which errors a given cognitive system should be prone to; if it yields none, please say so plainly, since that strikes me as the core of my partner's objection. Second, an honest verdict on insulation: once Normal conditions, derived functions, and appeals to ontogenetic selection are all on the table, is there any conceivable finding about a real organism that the theory could not accommodate after the fact? If it is unfalsifiable in that sense, is that a defect for a naturalizing project that claims continuity with biology, or is the demand for falsifiability simply misplaced for a theory of this kind, and what would the view have to give up in order to risk something? Please end by ranking the defeaters from fatal to irrelevant, and tell me which single one I should be most worried about in a paper, given that I would be most persuaded by an argument showing that the view's own repairs leave it explanatorily equivalent to a rival it was supposed to improve upon.
