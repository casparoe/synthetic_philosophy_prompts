So I've read enough toxin-puzzle takes over the years (Gauthier's dispositional thing, the precommitment replies, "you can't intend what you know you won't do") that the original barely registers anymore, which is why I built a variant and dragged it to a meetup last week. Two people there ended up actually fighting about it and I couldnt tell who won. Please engage with the variant as stipulated rather than sliding back to the classic one.

The variant: same billionaire, same vial, same deal — $1M already in your account by 12:01am if a scan taken at exactly midnight finds that you genuinely intend, right then, to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon; drinking makes you miserably ill for a day with no lasting damage. Two additions. First, the billionaire knows about and permits any self-modification: his exact line is that he's paying for the midnight state and "however you get yourself into it is fine with me." Second, there's a clinic ten minutes away that will, for $100k cash tonight, install a compliance implant at 11pm — irreversible, 99.9% reliable, and its brochure says the target act becomes "automatic: you won't have to decide."

Here's the exchange, roughly in order. Marcus (pro-chip) and Devi (anti-chip):

1. Marcus: this isn't a puzzle anymore, it's a shopping trip. Kavka's impossibility claim was just that you can't intend what you're certain you won't do; the implant makes drinking something you're certain you *will* do, so at midnight you can sincerely intend it. Net +$900k minus one bad day. He said the original only looks profound because it has no precommitment market in it — add one and it collapses into ordinary instrumental rationality.

2. Devi: that assumes the contested point. "Automatic, you won't have to decide" sounds like the act bypasses intention, the way a reflex or a deeply habituated movement does. If chipped-you never forms an intention to drink, the midnight scan finds nothing and you're out $100k plus the vomiting.

3. Marcus: then use the billionaire's own stipulation. Either the implant produces the paid-for state — in which case whatever it produces counts as an intention and Devi's distinction isn't doing any work — or it doesn't, in which case the clinic is just fraudulent and that's a fact about a made-up clinic, not about Kavka. He said there's no third option where the chip makes you drink but leaves you unintending at midnight.

4. Devi: suppose he's right and you *can* genuinely intend after chipping. Notice what you've bought: $100k to become someone who drinks a toxin that, at noon tomorrow, you'll have no reason whatsoever to drink. The bite of the original was that a rational agent can't decide her way into that state. If you can be moved into it for a price, the conclusion isn't "the puzzle dissolves," it's that agency is a tradable asset and every pointless action has a number attached at which becoming the sort of person who does it is rational. She asked whether he actually believes that.

5. Marcus: yes, and so does she. Odysseus and the mast, alarm clocks, antidepressants, paying a trainer in advance so you can't skip, a savings contract with a withdrawal penalty — all of them are paying, in money or freedom, to make your future self do something her future self will experience at the time as a hassle or a mistake. If her objection kills the chip it kills those too, so she owes a principled line. At noon tomorrow he also has "no reason" to go to the gym, only a prior arrangement.

6. Devi: the line is endorsement. With the mast, the alarm, the trainer, the contract, the future action is one the payer still endorses as worth doing at both ends. The chip is bought solely so you do something that at noon serves no goal of yours, that you'd refuse if asked, and whose only point was to trigger a payment already received. That's not delayed self-governance, it's renting your body to a stranger's whim for a day. And the gym case helps her: if at noon there were genuinely no reason to work out, the contract wouldn't make the workout rational, just unavoidable.

7. Marcus: then say that instead of pretending it answers Kavka. "Unavoidable but irrational" is precisely the state the original says you can't intend your way into — and the chip shows you can be *moved* into it for $100k. So either intentions can attach to actions you'll have no reason to perform (puzzle solved, expensively), or they can't (the clinic is useless). Devi wants the second reading for the original case and the first for the chipped case, and she can't have both.

Can you adjudicate this move by move — rate each of the seven from clearly wrong to clearly right with a line of justification each, and say who's right about which sub-question? Three I care about specifically: (a) does the brochure's "you won't have to decide" really open the gap Devi needs in move 2, i.e. can an action be reliably caused while the agent still has a genuine, scan-detectable intention that plays no role in producing it; (b) is the endorsement line in move 6 principled, or is it a redescription of her discomfort that Marcus's gym counterexample already breaches; (c) does move 7 actually catch an inconsistency, or is it equivocating between "having a reason to act" and "having a reason to have arranged to act."

Then the bigger verdict: who's more right about what they were really fighting over — whether the chip variant dissolves the original puzzle or just relocates it somewhere else. And tell me what neither of them said that they should have.
