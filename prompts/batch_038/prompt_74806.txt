I sit on the product review board at a company that makes a commitment app — the kind where you stake money on a habit — and we have to rule on a feature that is already built and waiting on us. Users escrow a sum that is automatically forfeited to a political organization they have said they despise if they miss their weekly target, and cancelling a pledge requires a 72-hour waiting period that the user configures at enrollment. Consent looks clean on paper: it is opt-in, the stake and the beneficiary are disclosed before signing, and there is no marketing pressure. So one board member's objection is not about deception. It is that the feature is engineered to exploit a predictable failure of the person who shows up at 11pm on Thursday, and that approving it means enforcing the will of whoever the user happened to be on Monday against whoever they are now. The engineer who built it argues the reverse: refusing to enforce the pledge overrides the user's most considered decision in favor of their least considered moment.

Our telemetry makes both positions awkward. Cancellation requests cluster in the ten minutes after a missed check-in, so the self asking to be released is precisely the self the pledge was aimed at. But pledge size correlates strongly with signing during a spike of motivation — the first week of January, the day after an annual physical — and those are the users who later describe their own stakes as absurd. Neither the earlier nor the later self looks like a neutral authority.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before Tuesday's vote. It should carry a clear recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and if it lands on conditions, those conditions should be specific enough that an engineer could implement and monitor them, including some telemetry trigger that would suspend the feature. I want the reasoning to actually take a position on whose preference the board should treat as the user's, rather than noting that reasonable people disagree, and I want the strongest version of the losing argument stated at length enough that the board can see it has not been strawmanned.
