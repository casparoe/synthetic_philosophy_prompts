I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on classical Indian epistemology, and I also work eight hours a week as a volunteer intake screener for a small regional environmental-health nonprofit. Residents send us reports of possible local hazards — a solvent smell from tap water, a creek that has changed colour, a cluster of illnesses near a facility — and for each one I have to record one of three dispositions: open an investigation, decline with reasons on file, or park pending clarification. I receive ten to fifteen reports a week and have about thirty minutes per report. We have no in-house scientists and no laboratory budget to speak of, so I almost never have a measurement in hand when the decision is due; what I have is what people say, what they describe seeing, and whatever published material they attach. A two-person committee reviews my dispositions afterward, the file rationale has to be intelligible to volunteers who have never taken a philosophy course, and because we occasionally receive legal threats the rationale has to show the reasoning rather than just the outcome. Declining a genuine hazard harms people; opening frivolous investigations exhausts our budget and creates liability.

What I want is a triage procedure built on the Nyāya account of the pramāṇas, which I have been reading this term. Here is my crib sheet, in functional terms rather than Sanskrit, and I would like you to correct it where I have got it wrong or where I am importing something that belongs to a neighbouring school — I know the three conditions on a sound reason are codified more explicitly by the Buddhist logicians, and that the conditions on sentence comprehension are shared with Mīmāṃsā. Tell me plainly rather than working around my errors.

First, a report should be sorted by which source it actually rests on, because the checks differ by source: direct perception, inference from a sign, identification by comparison with a known case, and testimony. Second, before any of that, a report has to be comprehensible at all — the words must hang together, must be semantically compatible, must be mutually expectant, and the speaker's intended purport must be recoverable. Third, an inferential reason must be present in the case at hand, present in the known positive instances, and absent from the negative instances; the defective-reason catalogues in my readings differ by author, but they cluster into a reason that is too wide, a reason that is too narrow because it has no positive instances outside this one case, a reason that is non-exclusive because the subject term sweeps in everything, a reason that is itself unverified, a reason that actually supports the opposite conclusion, a reason counterbalanced by an equally strong opposing reason, and a reason that is mistimed — true of the target once, but no longer. Fourth, testimony carries warrant only from a trustworthy speaker, analysed as someone who is competent, honest, and communicating what they actually know; I want to treat competence, honesty, and access as three separate screens, since a witness can be impeccably honest about something they could not possibly have perceived.

The deliverable I am after is a procedure a volunteer can run in thirty minutes: gates in a fixed order, what counts as passing each, what each failure routes to (decline, park, or escalate to the committee), and the exact wording of the file rationale, which I would like to follow the five-member form — claim, reason, the generalisation together with a known instance, the application to this case, the conclusion — because that form forces the screener to name the pervasion and produce a positive instance, which is where weak reports tend to fail. Tell me if that form is a straitjacket on paper and, if so, what to cut.

Then run the procedure on four cases. (1) A retired teacher with a long record of accurate reports and no discernible grievance says her tap water has smelled of solvent for three weeks and that her neighbour's dog died the same week; she has no measurements and cannot say what is in the water. (2) A community group submits a 200-signature petition about a cluster of childhood asthma cases near a plant, with an attached study from another country published in the late 1990s, arguing that the plant emits a substance known to cause asthma and therefore caused the cluster; a clinic report in the same packet shows local asthma presentations falling over the same period. (3) A report consisting almost entirely of comparison: the smell, the discoloured creek, and the dead fish are said to be exactly like an incident in another town a decade ago, which the writer treats as settling what the cause is here. (4) Briefly, a voicemail transcript so fragmentary that I cannot reconstruct what is being claimed.

I want one of these to come out against my own sympathies — if the procedure declines or parks the retired teacher's report in case (1), say so and show me why, rather than bending the gates to open it. Mark every point where judgment cannot be eliminated, and state which distinctions from the theory must survive the simplification and which can be dropped without loss. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: I suspect Nyāya tells me when a cognition is veridical and which source produced it, but says nothing about how much warrant is enough to spend money on, nothing about how to rank two pramāṇas when they conflict, and nothing about prevalence or base rates. If that is right, identify precisely where the procedure stops being epistemology and hands the disposition to the committee's values, and draft the sentence I should put in the file at that point.
