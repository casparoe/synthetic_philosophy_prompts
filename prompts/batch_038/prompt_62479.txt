I'm a postdoc at a middling UK university and for about eighteen months I've been running an interdisciplinary reading group on nations and nationalism that has gone wrong in the most tedious way imaginable. It's really just two people plus an audience. One is a medievalist who thinks the English were a nation by the time of Alfred's court and probably before it, and who gets cross when anyone suggests she's a romantic; the other is a historical sociologist of the hard modernist persuasion — Gellner, Anderson, Hobsbawm — for whom a nation is at absolute oldest a couple of centuries old and is downstream of print, mass schooling, conscription and labour mobility. Everyone else, me included, mostly watches them talk past each other while eating too many biscuits. They've both grudgingly agreed to do a proper adverserial collaboration over the summer and to sign whatever comes out of it, and I've volunteered to draft the document, which is where you come in. I don't want a dialogue, I don't want a verdict on who's right, and I definitely don't want a reading list — I want the joint statement the two of them could actually put their names to.

So: first, a numbered section of claims both can genuinely sign. This should include stuff about what would even count as evidence that a nation existed at a given date, about the difference between a dynasty, a kingdom, an ethnie and a nation, about what the invented-tradition thesis is and isn't claiming (my medievalist thinks it's a cheap shot; I suspect she's partly right that Hobsbawm's own examples were doing something more specific than she gives him credit for), and about what print capitalism can and cannot carry on its own. Then the disagreements, each phrased so the side holding it would accept the wording — no vacuous filler like they disagree about how old nations are, and nothing slanted. There's one case I want them both pushed onto because neither of them actually works on it: the Swiss Confederation in the centuries before the revolutionary wars. My own hunch is that the meadow oath and the crossbowman business are inventions that turn up in writing well over a century after the events they supposedly describe, bolted on afterwards to something real, which looks like a gift to the modernist — but she'd say a league of peasant valleys with a shared written legal culture and no common printed language is exactly the case modernism can't digest, and honestly I think that's a decent point. Use it. Also say explicitly where you think the quarrel is verbal: I suspect a large chunk of it is a fight over the word nation doing work that some defined term like mass-identification political community with an authoritative centre would dissolve, and I want to know which bits survive that renaming and which don't.

Format it as: (a) numbered agreed claims, (b) a markdown table of the disagreements with the columns topic | wording the modernist would sign | wording the medievalist/ethnosymbolist would sign | genuine or verbal | why, and (c) after the table, a JSON array of the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody. Each object in the array should have these fields exactly: crux_id (a short slug), question (the crux in wording both accept as the live question), whose_position_it_threatens (modernist, medievalist, or both), test (one concrete case or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it — archival, linguistic, demographic, whatever, but it has to be something you could in principle go and look at), modernist_prediction, medievalist_prediction (what each writes down before looking, ideally falsifiable), concession_trigger (what result would force that side to give ground rather than reinterpret), and verbal_component (null if there isn't one, otherwise the part of the crux that evaporates once the terms are fixed). Three to five cruxes, no more.
