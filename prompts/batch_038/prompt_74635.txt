I work in quality assurance at a plant that makes matte-finish panels for appliance fronts, and last Thursday two of my colleagues got into an argument that I was unable to settle and have not managed to stop thinking about since. Our colour-matching specification is written in terms of a single number, ΔE, computed from the measured colour of a production panel and of a reference sample: zero means the two are identical, around one means a difference that is not visible even side by side under good light, and by around five most people will simply say the panels are different colours. The disputed case was a batch sitting at 3.4 — visible, but only to someone who knows to look for it, and only under the inspection booth's lighting. Owen, who runs the metrology lab, said the batch either matches the reference or it does not. Ines, who writes the inspection software, said that for a case like this there is no answer to give. They talked past each other for an hour and I could not tell which of them was making the mistake.

Here is the exchange as closely as I can reconstruct it. (1) Owen began: either the panels match or they do not, and there is some exact value at which matching stops; the fact that we cannot locate it says something about us and our instruments, not about the panels, and on Ines's account a claim about that batch is neither true nor false, which is not a theory of vagueness but a refusal to have one. (2) Ines replied that the precision Owen wants would have to come from somewhere, and nothing supplies it — not the physics of the two surfaces, not human colour vision, which issues no verdict at 3.4, and not our practice, which had fixed no number at all until the committee wrote one down last week. Her own view, she said, keeps everything worth keeping: there are many equally admissible ways of sharpening 'matches' into a precise rule, and a sentence is true outright when it comes out true on every one of them, so 'the batch matches or the batch does not match' is still true in the only sense that matters, while the bare claim 'the batch matches' is neither true nor false. (3) Owen said that relocates the problem rather than dissolving it: on her account there is still a boundary, namely the place where cases stop being borderline and become determinate non-matches, and either that boundary is itself sharp, in which case she has accepted precisely the cutoff she objected to, only one storey higher, or it is vague as well, in which case she needs a further boundary for that one, and so on without end, and her theory never says anything definite about anything. (4) Ines said vague all the way up is a feature, not a bug, but pressed two costs of her own against him: his view is committed to an exact threshold — presumably some real number with infinitely many decimal places — that no amount of measurement, reflection, or linguistic investigation could ever reveal, and a truth that is unknowable in principle explains nothing; and when the committee finally entered 3.0 into the specification, nobody in the room experienced that as discovering where the line already lay, we experienced it as making a decision, which is exactly what one does when there is nothing there to find.

They asked me to referee and I was useless: I found each of the four moves compelling while it was being made and unpersuasive ten minutes later. Please go through the exchange and tell me who is right about what. I would like exactly five numbered items — the first four adjudicating moves (1) through (4) in order, and the fifth saying what, if anything, both of them were missing (if you think the disagreement turned on something other than what either took it to be about, that is the place to say so). Each item should open with a rating — for items one through four, one of clearly right, mostly right, a wash, mostly mistaken, clearly mistaken applied to that move; for item five, one of they missed a great deal, they missed something, they missed nothing much — followed by one or two sentences of justification. No introduction, no conclusion, and no restatement of the debate: just the five items.
