I'm in a book club and we're on a trade-press book about free will, and the chapter on Harry Frankfurt goes roughly like this. I typed the gist up from my notes so the wording isn't his exactly, but I'm pretty sure I kept every claim he's making:

> "For centuries philosophers assumed that you can only be blamed for something if you could have done otherwise. Frankfurt demolished the assumption with a single thought experiment. Black wants Jones to steal a wallet, and has secretly implanted a device in Jones's head that will step in and make him do it if Jones shows any sign of hestitating. As it happens, Jones steals the wallet for reasons of his own and the device never fires. Jones is plainly responsible for the theft — and yet he couldn't have done otherwise. So the ability to do otherwise turns out to be irrelevant: all that matters is whether the action actually came from you. Which means the whole quarrel about determinism was a red herring. Frankfurt showed that moral responsibility survives determinism, and practically everyone accepts this now; the remaining holdouts just insist that the device would have made Jones's decision determined anyway, which misses the point that the device never activates. Free will is dead, blame is not, and blame is the part that matters."
