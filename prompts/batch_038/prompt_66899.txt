I sit on the working group of a regional federation of worker co-operatives that is drafting a position paper on the quality of working life in our member enterprises. At our last session an outside consultant advanced a framing that several members found compelling and that I find troubling without being able to say precisely why. I would like a rigorous audit of it before we vote on whether to adopt his language.

His central move is an analogy: employee disengagement is what Marx called alienation, restated in the vocabulary of management science. He argued that the section on estranged labour in the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844 is essentially a first draft of the engagement literature, and he quoted the line that "The devaluation of the world of men is in direct proportion to the increasing value of the world of things," glossing it as an early formulation of the finding that output rises while the person producing it deteriorates. He then set out the following correspondences:

- the worker's product, which confronts him as an alien power, corresponds to the outputs, metrics and assigned intellectual property an employee generates but does not control;
- estrangement in the act of production corresponds to low task engagement, presenteeism, and the experience of work as externally compelled;
- estrangement from species-being, from one's own free life-activity, corresponds to the absence of autonomy, mastery and purpose in the role;
- estrangement from other human beings corresponds to weak team cohesion, siloing, and internal competition for ratings.

From this he drew two conclusions. First, that because alienation is a matter of how work is organised and experienced, it is remediable from inside the firm: job crafting, participative goal-setting, recognition schemes, and rotation out of low-autonomy roles. Second, that because ownership restores the worker's relation to the product, a genuine worker co-operative is structurally immune to alienation, so our members are already on the right side of the problem and the position paper should say so in those terms.

There is a difficulty he did not resolve. Across two rounds of a standard twelve-item engagement survey in eleven member co-operatives, we score well above the sector average on items concerning meaningfulness, voice and pride in the organisation, while simultaneously recording high exhaustion, entrenched long-hours norms, difficulty recruiting, and exit rates comparable to those of the conventional firms we compete with. One co-op has the highest engagement score in the federation and the worst turnover. His explanation was implementation lag.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture on Marx. Specifically:

1. State the correspondence explicitly and assess each of the four strands separately: is the managerial item the same phenomenon under a different name, a related but distinct phenomenon, or a strand with no genuine analogue at all?
2. Identify what the analogy quietly imports from the source domain — self-report measurability, variation between individuals within the same firm, addressability by managerial decision, and the presumption that the condition in question is a malfunction rather than the ordinary operation of the system — and say which of these imports do the damage.
3. Rank the disanalogies by what they cost, treating the two conclusions separately. I want to know whether any single disanalogy is fatal to the claim that co-ops are structurally immune while leaving the claim about internal remediation largely intact, or whether the reverse is true.
4. Assess our survey-and-turnover pattern. Does the analogy have the resources to describe a workplace that reports itself engaged and is nonetheless alienated on Marx's own account, or does it define the possibility away? Take the implementation-lag reply seriously rather than dismissing it.
5. The analogy also carries something I would rather not lose: it tells our members that the harm of bad work is not exhausted by pay and hours, and that it is a feature of systems rather than of individual bad managers. Tell me how much of that survives the audit, and whether a different comparison would preserve it more honestly — and if so, what that comparison would have to keep fixed.

Be decisive about which of the two conclusions falls, and say what the cheapest revision of his framing would be if the federation still wants to use it.
