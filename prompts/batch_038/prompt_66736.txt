I am putting together a small hand-out of verse to close a seminar on intention and precommitment, and I would like a poem written on Kavka's toxin puzzle. The setup, briefly, so that the poem need not narrate it: an eccentric billionaire sets a vial before you and offers a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink its contents at three o'clock tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make you painfully ill for a day, without threatening your life or leaving any lasting effect. The money will be in your account in the morning, hours before three; you are told in so many terms that you may change your mind; and nothing you do tomorrow afternoon can alter the payment by a cent. Kavka's conclusion is that the intention is simply unavailable to you, since intending seems to require believing you will act, and you know you will have no reason at all to act once the money has cleared. Gauthier's is that if you could form it, tomorrow's deliberation would already be over — the commitment, not the payoff, would be what made drinking the thing to do.

The form should be a pantoum of five quatrains. Lines two and four of each stanza return verbatim as lines one and three of the next, and the closing stanza takes the poem's third line for its second and the poem's first line for its last. I will permit exactly one departure from the rule: a single carried line may come back with one word changed, and that change must fall where the reason drops out — the point at which a reader should feel the difference between a sentence that still binds its speaker and the same sentence returned as an echo.

As for content: stanzas one and two belong to the hour before midnight, stanza three to the morning the payment clears, stanzas four and five to the afternoon with the vial on the table. Write in the first person, addressing the vial. The distinction between having a reason to form an intention and having a reason to carry it out must be borne entirely by image and repetition — no proper names, no decision-theoretic vocabulary, and no last line that tells us whether the speaker drinks. Someone who knows the puzzle should be able to say, stanza by stanza, which of the two diagnoses the returning lines are supporting, and should not be able to settle it.
