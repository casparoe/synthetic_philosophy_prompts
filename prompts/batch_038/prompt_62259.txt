The student's answer (518 words):

"On Sunday, Beauty is put to sleep and a fair coin is tossed. If it lands Heads she is woken once, on Monday; if it lands Tails she is woken on Monday and again on Tuesday, and after each waking she is given a drug that erases her memory of that waking, so that at any waking she cannot tell which day it is or whether she has been woken before. At each waking she is asked for her credence that the coin landed Heads. Before the experiment begins she should of course say 1/2, and both accounts of the case agree about this: the disagreement concerns only what she should say once she is awake. Elga argues that the answer is 1/3. The centered possibilities open to her at a waking are Heads-and-Monday, Tails-and-Monday, and Tails-and-Tuesday. These are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, and from where she sits inside the experiment they are subjectively indistinguishable, so the principle of indifference applies to all three of them at once and each receives 1/3; summing the two tails possibilities yields P(Tails) = 2/3 and P(Heads) = 1/3. That indifference step is where all the substantive work is done, and the rest is arithmetic. It follows that Lewis's quarrel with Elga can only be a quarrel about indifference, since once indifference is granted the arithmetic is unavoidable.

Lewis's reply is that Beauty acquires no new information when she wakes. She was told the entire protocol on Sunday, so she already knew that she would be woken at least once and that she would be unable to tell which day it was; the fact that she is now awake therefore tells her nothing she did not already have, and conditionalizing on her total evidence leaves her Sunday credence where it was. Lewis accordingly keeps P(Heads-and-Monday) at 1/2 and splits what remains between the two tails possibilities, giving P(Tails-and-Monday) = P(Tails-and-Tuesday) = 1/4. What makes this position available to him is that Lewis does not treat beliefs about which day it is, or about which waking one is currently in, as beliefs proper: centered contents are not, on his picture, genuine objects of belief, so there is no question of Beauty's evidence having shifted. The dispute is therefore really a dispute about whether probability theory has any business with indexical belief at all.

Suppose that at each waking Beauty is offered the chance to pay some price for a ticket that pays $1 if the coin landed Heads and nothing otherwise. A thirder, valuing the ticket at her waking credence, should pay up to about 33 cents, while a halfer should pay up to 50. The dispute then looks settleable by experiment: offer the ticket and watch what she does. If a halfer really does buy at 45 cents, then across many repetitions she pays out 45 cents once on the Heads runs and twice on the Tails runs, while collecting a dollar only on the Heads runs, and so she loses on average roughly 17 cents per run; the thirder, buying at 33 cents, breaks even. Since both are stipulated to be rational and both have been told the same protocol, a position that reliably loses money where its rival does not is not a competing interpretation of the case but a straightforward failure of rationality, and should be given up. This is also why the thirder conclusion need not conflict with the Principal Principle: the chance of Heads remains 1/2 throughout, and Beauty's 1/3 is a credence in a centered question rather than a denial of the chance."
