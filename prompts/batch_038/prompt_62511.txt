I've been doing some copyediting for a student-run appeals group and their winter famine appeal has one image that comes back in every single draft: a famine is like a flood, it arrives without warning, it takes whoever happens to be standing in its path, and when the water goes down what's left is a landscape to be rebuilt. The organisers lean on it to make three moves at once, and they never separate them out — that giving is charity rather than something owed, so it can't be demanded of anybody; that what's called for is generosity and logistics rather than blame, because you don't prosecute a flood; and that the political stuff (who owned what, who was allowed to export, who had a vote and who had a censor) matters but is downstream, the kind of thing you get round to once the emergency is over.

I'm doing a global justice module at the same time and we've been reading Sen on entitlements plus the three cases — Bengal in 1943, Ireland 1845 to 1852, the Great Leap 1958 to 1962 — and my understanding is that at least in Bengal the aggregate food supply wasn't the main story: the rice was more or less there and the people who died were the ones whose claim on it collapsed, the rural labourers whose wages fell behind prices, the boatmen and coastal fishing families wrecked by the denial policies, so the flood picture gets the shape of the causation backwards. Though I also half-remember someone saying the Irish case is genuinely different, because the blight really did destroy the thing the poorest third lived on and the grain and livestock that kept leaving under guard weren't food those people could ever have bought back anyway. My flatmate thinks there's an obviously better analogy: a famine is like a house fire set by someone already inside the house, which turns it into a crime scene rather than a landscape. I worry that one has its own problem, since it wants a single arsonist with an intention and the causation here is smeared across administrators, merchants, landlords, procurement cadres and a war economy, none of whom meant for anyone to starve.

So can you audit both of them properly? I'd want the mapping written out explicitly — in the flood version, what's doing the work of the rainfall, the levees, the floodplain, the emergency services, and who are the people upstream who decide whether the levees get built at all. Then separate the disanalogies that actually bite against those three moves from the ones that are just decorative, and name what a flood quietly brings along that a famine doesn't have: no agents, no beneficiaries, roughly symmetric exposure, a natural timescale, an aftermath in which nobody is richer for the water having been there. Tell me whether the conclusion survives once those are on the table, and specifically whether "charity not justice" and "the politics can wait" are positions you can still hold, or whether the disaster frame was carrying them the whole time. Then do the same for the arson version — what it buys you, what it smuggles in, and whether it can stretch to cover diffuse and unintentional causation without losing the point of calling it a crime. And finally: does it matter which of the three famines you're picturing, is there a case where the flood analogy is roughly right, and if neither image holds up is there a better one — or is the honest answer that this thing resists being pictured at all and the analogy is a crutch rather than a tool? British English please, and don't worry about being even-handed, I'd rather you commit to a verdict.
