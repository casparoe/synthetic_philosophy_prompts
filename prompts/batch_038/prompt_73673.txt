My friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether Lakatos's methodology actually bites on real cases, and we ended up accusing each other of the very thing we were discussing. Here are the verdicts I was left holding, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. Le Verrier postulates an unseen planet to absorb the residuals in Uranus's orbit, and it turns up roughly where he said. Progressive; I regard this as the paradigm case.
2. The same programme faces an excess in Mercury's perihelion. Auxiliary hypotheses are offered for decades — an intra-Mercurian planet, a zodiacal dust cloud, a solar oblateness — and none is confirmed. I want to call this degenerating, yet I cannot say at what date it became so, and I certainly would not have said it in 1900.
3. A programme in social psychology has survived eleven consecutive failed replications, each time by positing a further moderator — cultural sample, stimulus intensity, participant mood — every one of which is measurable in principle and none of which was ever measured in advance. Degenerating, clearly. But note that this case satisfies the obvious formal test I would have proposed, that auxiliaries be independently testable.
4. A colleague's programme in evolutionary developmental biology has generated no novel prediction in thirty years, yet its vocabulary has been quietly adopted by three neighbouring fields that now produce results with it. I want to call this progressive, and my friend says I have no right to.
5. A rival programme is worse still: no predictions, no uptake, no technical apparatus. I find myself thinking the first programme's standing cannot depend on that fact, and then noticing that in practice it does.

The principle I kept reaching for was something like: a programme is progressive just when its auxiliaries are independently testable and at least one has borne out a novel prediction. Case 3 breaks the first clause and case 4 breaks the second, and I cannot patch both without the principle ceasing to say anything.

Please answer in British English, under these headings: **The pattern in my verdicts** (about 150 words) — what my five judgements have in common and where they pull against each other; **Candidate principles** (about 300 words) — two or three, each stated as explicit necessary and sufficient conditions, with the case that motivates each clause; **Pressure points** (about 200 words) — the case or pair that each candidate cannot accommodate, and how much complexity it costs to keep them; **The verdict I should drop** (about 120 words) — if a simpler principle forces me to give up one of 1 to 5, which and why; **One new case** (about 100 words) — a situation I have not described, drawn from the history of science or invented, on which your favoured principle delivers a verdict that would surprise me.
