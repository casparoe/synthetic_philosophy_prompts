Compose a dialogue, in American English, between two philosophers arguing over whether computational intractability bears on the content of epistemic norms. Anchor it in a concrete case: an analyst must assign credences across a large space of mutually dependent hypotheses on the basis of a substantial body of evidence, and computing the coherent credence function her evidence supports requires a combinatorial search that neither she nor any machine available to her can complete.

The first holds that the norm is fixed by the ideal — logical closure and full conditionalization — and that infeasibility is a regrettable practical obstacle that leaves the norm exactly as it was. The second holds that a standard no feasible procedure can approximate is not a standard of rationality at all, so that tractability enters into what the agent is required to believe.

Neither may be a strawman. The exchange must work through at least these three points: whether "ought implies can" transfers to belief at all, given that believing is not something one simply does; the idealizer's answer to the charge that her norm cannot guide anyone, paired with the tractabilist's answer to the charge that she makes epistemic requirements agent-relative, yielding conflicting verdicts about the same evidence; and the proposal that the tractabilist settle for the best feasible approximation, together with the objection that bounding an approximation's error can be as hard as computing the exact value. Close by having them identify the precise claim on which they part company, rather than agreeing to differ in general terms.
