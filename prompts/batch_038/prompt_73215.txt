**Question 3 (25 marks)**

Amara and Ben have run a weekly reading group together for two years. Amara has recently accepted a post at another university and has privately resolved to withdraw from the group at the end of the current term. She has told no one, and she has given no outward indication of her resolution. In the meantime she and Ben continue to organise the next session as usual: each intends that they hold the session together, each forms subplans for the session that interlock with the other's, and each is willing to take on part of the other's role if the other's subplan breaks down. Ben has no inkling of Amara's resolution.

(a) Set out Bratman's account of shared intentional activity and say whether Amara and Ben share an intention to hold the next session. Identify which conditions of the account, if any, the case puts under pressure, and why.

(b) Set out Gilbert's account of joint commitment and the plural subject and say whether Amara and Ben are jointly committed to running the group.

(c) Do the two accounts yield different verdicts in this case? If they do, say what in the accounts explains the divergence; if they do not, say what the real disagreement between them amounts to.

**Candidate's response**

Bratman analyses shared intention out of the ordinary planning attitudes of the individual participants rather than treating a we-intention as a primitive psychological kind. For Amara and Ben to share an intention to hold the session, three things must obtain. First, each of them intends that they hold it. Second, each intends that they hold it in virtue of these intentions together with subplans that mesh — that is, subplans that are the same on both sides, so that Amara's plan for the session matches Ben's plan for it. Third, the whole interlocking structure is common knowledge between them, which is satisfied here, since each can observe the other continuing to plan and can observe that the other can observe this. Bratman adds further conditions of mutual responsiveness and mutual supportiveness, requiring that each party actually assists the other's contribution to the joint activity; Amara and Ben meet this requirement, since the stipulation of the case is that each takes on part of the other's role when the other's subplan fails. So on Bratman's account they do share the intention to hold the session, notwithstanding Amara's secret resolution. Her resolution concerns the group's future rather than this session, and Bratman's conditions are conditions on the attitudes the parties presently hold and on their common knowledge of them; nothing in the account makes a concealed intention to exit a defect in a present shared intention.

Gilbert holds that two or more people are jointly committed to do something as a body when each expresses readiness to be so committed with the others and each believes the others have done the same, the resulting commitment being held by the parties jointly rather than severally — the plural subject. On this account, then, joint commitment too is built out of the participants' own commitments together with mutual belief about them, and, as with Bratman, it cannot obtain unless the parties' personal commitments are out in the open between them. A joint commitment therefore cannot survive a private resolution by one party to withdraw, because the mutual belief in each party's commitment is then false, or at any rate no longer mutually manifest. Gilbert and Bratman consequently agree about Amara and Ben: both hold that a shared intention (joint commitment) obtains with respect to the next session, because on both analyses the relevant attitudes and the relevant mutual belief are present at the time of the session.

The disagreement between the two authors is therefore not about when shared intention obtains but about what follows from it. Because Gilbert treats the parties as a single subject of the commitment, joint commitment carries obligations and correlative entitlements: were Amara simply to stop attending without explanation, Ben would be entitled to rebuke her, and she would owe him one. Bratman's account is reductive and situates shared intention within individual planning agency, so it can generate at most expectations of stability and the pragmatic pressure of consistent planning, not obligations to another person. The advantage of Gilbert's account in this case is that it can register Amara's concealed resolution as something normatively defective: obligations attach to the joint commitment itself, so a party who privately resolves to abandon the joint activity is already failing in something she owes the other, even while the group continues to function perfectly. Bratman's account, being a purely descriptive account of interlocking attitudes, has no resources for marking the secret resolution as a failing of any kind, which is why the two accounts give the same verdict about the session but only Gilbert's can explain what is wrong with Amara's conduct.
