I'm the TA for a senior seminar in formal political theory, and for our closing session I want to hand out something that will make the term's material land harder than another problem set would. We've just spent three weeks on Arrow's impossibility theorem and the standard repertoire of responses to it — restricting the domain of admissible preferences, objecting to independence of irrelevant alternatives, retreating to cardinal or partially ordinal ballots, randomizing, admitting interpersonal comparisons — and I've noticed my students oscillating between two errors: treating the theorem as proof that collective decision-making is a sham, and treating it as an abstraction that no actual institution ever collides with. I'd like a piece of satire that quietly embarrasses both attitudes without ever naming either.

The deliverable is a municipal procurement file of roughly 900 to 1,100 words, with numbered clauses and section headers in the visual style of real procurement paperwork, issued by an invented township — something plausibly drab, with an invented procurement officer, an invented clerk, invented council members, and invented vendors — which has decided to acquire a fair aggregation procedure the way it would acquire a snowplow. Build it in three parts. First, a Request for Proposals whose "Functional Requirements" section restates Arrow's four conditions entirely in bureaucratic boilerplate: unrestricted domain as a clause demanding the system accept any configuration of resident inputs without precondition or screening, the Pareto condition as a responsiveness warranty, independence of irrelevant alternatives as a stipulation that the system's treatment of any two options shall not be affected by the presence, absence, or promotion of any third option, and non-dictatorship drafted as an anti-single-vendor-dependency and anti-nepotism provision. Second, a bid evaluation memo in which the procurement officer works through the submissions and rejects each for the technically correct reason — a first-past-the-post bid and its exposure to spoilers, a points-based bid and its sensitivity to cloned or merely adjacent entries, a pairwise-majority bid that must be faulted both for cycles and for strategic manipulability, and so on. At least one bid should be rejected not for failing a requirement but for declining the question as asked: a vendor submitting approval-style or scored ballots, which the officer records as non-compliant with the input specification rather than as an answer to the town's problem. That distinction is precisely what I want the students to see, and it must be played dead straight. At least four of the recognized escape routes from the theorem should surface somewhere in the file — as bids, as amendments, or as clarification questions — each characterized accurately, including what the township would have to surrender to accept it; a bidder's question asking whether resident preferences happen to be single-peaked along one left-right corridor, and the officer's uncomprehending reply, would be welcome. Please also work in a reference to an earlier tender whose winning method proved manipulable in practice, so that the town has been burned once already and has responded by tightening the specification rather than by revising its aims.

The target is the township's insistence on a procedure satisfying every criterion it finds appealing while never once asking which criterion it would give up, and its habit, in the addendum, of answering impossibility by reissuing the same specification with one further clause appended. The file should end by recording a sole-source award to a longtime resident whose preferences the town agrees simply to adopt — the only submission the evaluation finds fully compliant — narrated in the flat, satisfied language of a successful tender, with no remark on what has occurred; then a brief addendum in which the council, responding to public outcry, strengthens the non-dictatorship clause, and the procurement officer reports that no responsive bids remain and recommends the matter be settled by a show of hands at the next meeting. Nothing may state a moral, name Arrow in the body of the document, or wink at the reader. The single place where the real issue may be brushed against is a one-paragraph dissenting note from a council member who complains that the whole exercise was doomed because nobody controlled the list of alternatives the bids were scored against — and she should be right in a way that conspicuously does not help. It ought to be funny to someone who knows the social choice literature and at least followable, as bureaucratic farce, to someone who has never seen the theorem. Keep every name, office, vendor, and document number invented, and do not quote from any real text.
