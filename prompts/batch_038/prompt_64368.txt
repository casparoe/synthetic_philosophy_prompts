I cover science and ideas for a small magazine — mostly the sociology of research, preprint culture, weird governance experiments in academic communities. Lately I've been reporting a piece on member-run scientific societies that govern themselves without any outside authority, and I ended up sitting in on the annual meeting of a mushroom club in the Pacific Northwest (400-ish members, runs its own open-access field guide, does all its business by its own written constitution). What happened there turned into the most interesting argument I've overheard all year, and I want your read on it before I write it up. Please take the role of someone who actually works on jurisprudence and constitutional theory — not a generalist summarizer — and think it through carefully. Before you give verdicts, spend a few lines brainstorming the space of possible resolutions (what could make the second amendment valid, what could make it void, whether there's a third option neither of them considered).

Here's the setup as I took it down. Their Article IX says any part of the constitution can be amended on 60 days' written notice and a two-thirds vote of members present. In 2019, during a fight over whether to publish GPS coordinates for rare species, the membership added Amendment 7, which entrenched the foraging-ethics article and said, roughly, that that article "shall not be amended, repealed, or superseded by any procedure whatsoever, including a unanimous vote of the membership," and that this entrenching sentence itself was equally unamendable. Last spring a younger cohort — mostly people who joined after 2019 and who want coordinates published for survey work — gave proper notice and passed Amendment 12 by about 68%, repealing the entrenchment and rewriting the foraging article. Nobody disputes the notice or the count. The fight is purely about whether a rule can bind the people who come after it. Two members went at it in the parking lot afterwards; I know both slightly, neither is a lawyer by training exactly, but one is a retired probate judge who has been their parliamentarian for a decade, and the other is a grad student in philosophy who does their newsletter. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Marjorie (the judge): Amendment 7 was adopted under Article IX when Article IX still had full force, so it's valid law; a validly adopted rule that limits future change limits future change; therefore Article IX's amending power no longer reaches the entrenched article or the entrenching sentence, and Amendment 12 is a nullity — the vote was an opinion poll, nothing more.
(2) Dev (the grad student): Article IX says the membership may *change* the constitution; it nowhere says the membership may *end* its own power to change the constitution, and you can't get the second from the first. Self-destruction isn't in the grant. So the self-entrenching half of Amendment 7 was void from the moment it passed, and Amendment 12 just tidied up the record.
(3) Marjorie: that's the same trick run twice. If you can't derive self-extinguishment from the amending power in 2024, you couldn't derive it in 2019 either — fine, I accept that, but then your "void from the start" is just an unwritten substantive limit on what members are allowed to vote for. Who gets to say what else is on that invisible list? On your view the officers could annul any amendment they found distasteful and call it ultra vires.
(4) Dev: there is a limit, and it's not invisible: a rule has authority only for as long as the community it governs goes on accepting it. The members have accepted Amendment 12 — the officers certified it, the newsletter printed the new foraging code, three forays have already run under it, and the 2019 cohort has complied without protest. That acceptance is what makes it law, not a supermajority from six years ago.
(5) Marjorie: then be honest about what you've conceded. Amendment 7 was worthless the day it was adopted, and every entrenchment clause anyone has ever drafted is a decorative suggestion. On your theory the constitution is a photograph of whatever the current majority wants, and there's no difference at all between the bylaws and the mood of the room.
(6) Dev: I'll own that. But look at the mirror image. If Amendment 7 really binds forever, then this society can never correct anything in that article no matter what it learns about truffle ecology — not by supermajority, not by unanimity, not by re-founding from within. The only exit is to dissolve the society and incorporate a fresh one next door with fresh bylaws. And that's literally what the 2024 faction threatened in their notice letter. So your entrenchment doesn't bind the members at all; it just routes them around itself. "Bound forever" and "dissolve and restart" come out the same in practice, which means your principle isn't doing any work either.
(7) Marjorie: there's a difference between a rule that people can evade by acting outside it and a rule that isn't a rule. Germany's constitution has provisions that can't be amended even by the supermajority it prescribes, and nobody thinks the Germans have decorative bylaws. Besides, your acceptance test can't tell Amendment 12 apart from a faction that ignores the tally and simply declares itself the winner — both are "the community going along with it."
(8) Dev: mine can, by scale and duration: proper notice, 68% of those voting, a full season of uncontested operation. But I'll grant the line is vague and I can't sharpen it into a rule. Vagueness is the honest answer here. Yours is a bright line, but you buy it by making a majority in 2019 permanently sovereign over everybody who joined afterward — including the majority of the current membership.

Neither of them budged, and honestly I left not knowing what to think, which is why I'm asking. Please go through the exchange move by move and rate each of the eight numbered moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating — I want the reasoning attached to the specific move, not a global verdict pasted on top. Then tell me: who is right about which particular sub-question (does the amending power include the power to abolish itself? does the second amendment's validity depend on how people have behaved since? is Marjorie's "decorative" objection fatal or just rhetorically strong?), who is more right overall, and what the two of them are jointly missing — I suspect there's at least one distinction that would have dissolved part of the fight and neither of them reached for it. Two follow-ups, if you can: does this puzzle have a name in the literature, and roughly which historical position is each of them closest to? (No bibliography and no citations, please — I can't check them and won't be printing them; I just want to know whether I'm walking into a well-trodden debate or something odd.) And finally: if the officers asked you what to actually do next spring — leave Amendment 12 in place, hold a fresh vote, draft a smarter entrenchment clause — what would you tell them, and is there any wording that makes entrenchment neither decorative nor absurd? Don't hedge into "both sides have a point"; give me real ratings and a real recommendation.
