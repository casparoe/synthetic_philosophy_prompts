I've been working through Scanlon's contractualism on my own — the idea that an act is wrong if it would be ruled out by principles no one could reasonably reject, and that when principles are compared you look at one individual's complaint against another's rather than adding them up. I keep worrying that this pairwise method might go round in circles, and I'd like a case that settles whether it can.

Please construct a scenario with three possible rescue options and three people such that pairwise comparison of individual complaints yields a strict cycle: the first option is less reasonably rejectable than the second, the second than the third, and the third than the first. Constraints: a realistic setting (a collapsed building, a clinic, a small boat — not a trolley); one paragraph; each option must leave exactly the same number of people harmed, and the total harm must be identical across all three, so that neither the numbers nor the sums can be doing any work; the harms must be ordinary losses a layperson can rank, with no appeal to incomparability; and nobody dies.

After the scenario, give me the three pairwise comparisons in full — whose complaint is strongest in each and why — then say what contractualism is supposed to do once the ordering closes on itself: is the verdict indeterminate, or does everything collapse into a tie? Tell me which single feature of the case generates the cycle, what happens if I remove it, and the strongest reply a contractualist could make (for instance, that complaints are measured against a common baseline rather than against each alternative in turn). Since what I want is a cycle produced by the structure of the complaints, a case that simply stipulates the verdicts is no use to me. Finish with how a utilitarian comes out given the equal totals, and which of the two predicaments is worse. Please write the answer in British English.
