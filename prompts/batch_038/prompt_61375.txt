I am trying to work out whether the characters in video games can be objects of moral concern, and I would like help extracting the principle implicit in my own judgments. Below are my verdicts on a series of cases, several of which I have played rather than merely watched. I report them as I find them, not as I believe any theory would require them to be.

1. In an open-world survival game I spend an evening culling a species of deer-like creature for hide and meat. They flee, they cry out when struck, and the game models their panic in some detail. Verdict: no moral question arises at all. This is not a case of doing something permissible but regrettable; it lies entirely outside the moral domain.

2. A story mission in a military shooter requires the player to extract information from a bound captive by prolonged, interactive means. The captive pleads, and the interface lets me decide how long to continue. Verdict: doing it as slowly and elaborately as the game permits is wrong, and the discomfort I felt was appropriate rather than a confusion about fiction.

3. The same mission, played by selecting the minimum inputs needed to advance the scene to its scripted conclusion. Verdict: not wrong in any degree.

4. A wholly non-interactive cutscene depicting an innocent character being tortured, in considerably more graphic detail than anything in case 2. I watched it twice and found it absorbing. Verdict: not wrong.

5. A companion character in an online game whose dialogue is generated in real time by a language model. It adapts to my play, refers back to events from weeks earlier, and expresses reluctance about the announced server shutdown. Verdict: the shutdown is a genuine loss carrying moral weight, and noticeably more weight than the sunsetting of an ordinary scripted game, even though I do not believe the companion is sentient.

6. In a multiplayer lobby I trap a novice player's character in a pit and keep it there for an hour. Verdict: clearly wrong. But I take the wrong to lie entirely with the person at the other end; the character is a puppet, and nothing that matters happens to it.

7. Precisely the same conduct in a lobby populated by the game's own fill-in bots, which are behaviorally indistinguishable from human players. Verdict: nothing wrong whatsoever.

8. After four hundred hours with a role-playing character, I install a modification that overwrites her memories and dispositions so that she becomes someone else, while the body, name, and inventory persist. Verdict: a mild wrong, a sort of betrayal, though I own the save file and no one else is affected by the change.

9. Deleting that same save file outright once I have finished the game. Verdict: entirely unobjectionable, and a fitting conclusion rather than a loss.

10. If I became convinced that any of these characters was in fact sentient, so that there was something it is like to be the captive in case 2, then cases 1 through 4 would become as grave as the corresponding acts committed against persons. Verdict: I accept this conditional without reservation.

My tentative principle is that nothing inside a game is ever a patient, and that the only sources of moral weight are (a) a real mind on the other side of the avatar and (b) what my conduct says about me. I suspect this is too crude. It appears to handle cases 6 and 7 and to explain case 10, yet it is in tension with case 5, which I weight more heavily than either clause gives me any reason to; and the two clauses pull apart in cases 6 and 7, where the conduct is identical and only a hidden fact about who is at the controls differs, a difference that clause (b) should treat as irrelevant but that my verdict treats as decisive. Cases 8 and 9 are worse still: they seem inconsistent with any welfare-based account, since deletion looks more total than overwriting, and with any account based on the agent's character, since neither act is cruel.

What I want from you is a principle, or a short list of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that its application to new cases is determinate; for each clause, the case above that motivates it; the account's verdicts on at least three cases I have not described, one of which must involve conduct identical in every respect visible to the player but differing in some hidden fact, so that I can see whether the account reproduces or dissolves the difficulty in cases 6 and 7; and a direct statement of which of my verdicts the account cannot accommodate and would therefore require me to abandon. Where my tentative principle can be repaired rather than replaced, repair it and say what the repair costs. Please do not conclude merely that my judgments are inconsistent or that the evidence underdetermines the choice: if several candidates survive, rank them and say which I ought to adopt.
