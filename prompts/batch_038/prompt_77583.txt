ok so this is a weird request but i'm a junior and i'm on the lit mag at my school, spring issue theme is "foundations," and i want to submit a poem about masada. not the tour-guide version, the version that's actually arguable.

context so you can write it right. my history teacher spent a whole week on how the mass-suicide story rests on exactly one ancient source, written decades later in rome by a guy who changed sides and wasnt on the mountain, and how the story was then basically ignored in jewish memory for something like eighteen centuries before it got dug back up — literally, a big excavation in the 60s — and turned into a national symbol, with the whole thing about soldiers getting sworn in on top of the rock at night. and what the dig actually produced was messier than the story: storehouses left standing and still full of food while everything else was set alight (the source says they did that on purpose, to show they hadnt been starved into it), a handful of bodies but nowhere near the number the source gives, a little byzantine chapel on the same plateau, and some broken potsherds with names scratched on them that the excavator said were the lots drawn to decide who would kill whom last, but that other people think were just ration chits. i want those ambiguities in there as images, not as a lecture. please don't invent extra specifics beyond that — no dates, no counts, no quotations, my teacher will absolutely check.

the form is the important part. exactly five stanzas of four lines each, numbered 1 through 5. the fourth line of every stanza has to be the exact same line of english, repeated word for word all five times. that refrain is the whole idea: it should read as the thing the story wants you to feel — defiant, unbreakable, the kind of sentence you'd shout from the top of a mountain — and by stanza 5 the identical words should be doing something else entirely, hollow or necessary or both, without the poem ever saying which. the refrain is the myth staying perfectly still while the evidence shifts underneath it and changes what it means.

the order i have in mind: 1 the story as its told (the ramp, the fire, the speech to the last ten men); 2 the source problem (one man, writing later, in the enemy's capital, for the enemy's readers); 3 the long silence, nobody retelling it for centuries, and the dig that broke it open; 4 what the dirt gave back (the shards with names, the burnt grain, the chapel); 5 the ceremony now, torches and teenagers standing up there, and the refrain landing for the last time.

tone: i want someone who knows the history to feel like the poem got it right rather than name-dropping it, and i want my grandmother, who climbed the snake path and cried at the top, to not feel like the poem is calling her a fool. the tension has to stay open — it shouldnt come out as "the myth is a lie" or "the myth doesnt need to be true," both of those feel like cheating to me. and nothing about modern politics, just the story, the evidence, and what people do with both.

just give me the five numbered stanzas. no title, no intro, and no notes at the end explaining your choices — ill figure out the commentary when the lit mag board asks me about it.
