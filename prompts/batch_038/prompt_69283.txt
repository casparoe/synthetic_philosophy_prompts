I'm auditing an intro set theory course for fun — I did philosophy as an undergrad and never shook the feeling that I missed something — and our TA has a bit he does every year about people who make a fuss over the axiom of choice. His line is that they simply don't understand the quantifiers. He laid it out in section last week and I wrote it down as best I could, and honestly it looks like it goes through, but it also looks like it proves way more than he wants and I can't put my finger on the bad step.

Here's the reconstruction:
(1) For every nonempty set x there is some y with y ∈ x. Nobody disputes this; it's just what "nonempty" means and it needs no choice principle.
(2) Whenever you have "for every x there's a y such that φ(x,y)", you may conclude "there's a function f such that for every x, φ(x, f(x))" — because a function is nothing over and above a thing that assigns each x one of the y's that φ allows, and the first half already guarantees each x has one. Demanding more than that is demanding a *recipe* for the assignment, which is a different and much stronger demand.
(3) So there's a function F such that for every nonempty set x, F(x) ∈ x.
(4) That's the axiom of choice, in fact the global version — one chooser for everything at once.
(5) So choice isn't a substantive assumption about the set-theoretic universe at all; it drops out of the meanings of "for every", "there is" and "function". That ZF can't prove it is a defect in ZF, namely its stingy habit of identifying functions with sets of ordered pairs.
(6) Therefore anyone who rejects AC — intuitionists, constructivists — isn't making a mathematical mistake but a linguistic one.

What makes me suspicious is that if (2) really were just logic, the same move would work for families indexed by proper classes too, and I'm fairly sure the course said the global choice principle can't even be *stated* in ZFC (no quantifying over classes), and that some of the class theories have to add it as a seperate axiom rather than derive it. So either (2) is smuggling something, or my TA has proved global choice for free on a whiteboard in twenty minutes, which nobody thinks is possible. Relatedly, the finite case really does seem to work: given finitely many nonempty boxes you can just pick from each one, and I believe ZF proves finite choice by induction — so (2) feels right because it's right there and the argument slides.

So: which step do you reject? Actually commit to one and argue for it rather than serving me a tasting menu of possible diagnoses, I've read enough of those and they leave me exactly where I started. I'd much rather keep the idea that AC is a real assumption than keep (6), so tell me what the intuition behind (2) is tracking if it isn't logic, and say straight out whether (5)'s charge against ZF — that treating functions as sets of ordered pairs is too narrow — has any force or none. Please keep the whole thing under 350 words, and start with a single sentence naming the step you're giving up before you explain anything.
