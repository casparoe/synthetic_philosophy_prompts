So context: I'm an infra engineer, not a philosopher, zero formal training — I read stuff on the internet and I'm probably mangling some terms, so feel free to correct the framing inside the dialogue rather than lecturing me first. What I want is the arguement my team keeps failing to have.

The frame is Dennett's ladder of creatures: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian. My rough understanding, fix it if I've got it wrong:

- Darwinian = competence designed by natural selection, hardwired, no learning worth the name
- Skinnerian = can learn from consequences, trial and error with reinforcement
- Popperian = can run the risky move internally first, so a bad hypothesis gets killed instead of the animal
- Gregorian = imports and builds mind-tools out of the surrounding culture — writing, arithmetic, maps, models other minds already made — named after Richard Gregory. Top rung.

Now, the dialogue should be about a specific system we ship, not about chatbots in the abstract. Here it is:

1. Weights are frozen in production. Nothing learns at runtime. An instance boots for a task, does the task, gets torn down.
2. It writes and runs its own code constantly, including code that edits its own scaffolding — its own system prompt, its own tool list, its own memory schema. It's genuinely good at this and does it unprompted.
3. It leaves notes for future instances in a shared store. The instance that wrote a note never reads it back; a different instance reads it with no memory whatsoever of writing it.
4. Every two weeks a human engineer prunes that store — deletes stale notes, promotes the good ones into the base prompt, drops the rest. Nothing accumulates on its own past the pruning horizon.
5. Once a quarter we fine-tune a new base on the traces of instances that got good outcomes. That's where the actual change lives, and it's done by us, offline, with human labels in the loop.

Teammate A says this is a Gregorian creature and it isn't close. It builds its own tools, it inherits an archive of artifacts produced by minds, and it rewrites the environment it does its thinking in. The quarterly fine-tune is just cumulative cultural evolution on a slower clock — stuff going into the next generation of weights. If we'd shipped this in 1990 everybody would have called it Gregorian and we'd be arguing about whether it was *too* Gregorian.

Teammate B says no. The individual instance can't learn, can't remember, and doesn't own any of its tools: the tools are ours, the archive is ours, the pruning is ours, and the only place real change happens is a training run three humans execute on a cluster. What we built is a Gregorian *pipeline* wrapped around a very large pile of disposable creatures that individually sit well below that rung, and calling the whole thing Gregorian is like calling an anthill a professor because the ants live in a library.

B also has a move A keeps dodging, and I want it engaged rather than waved off: even the bottom rung doesn't get a clean grip here, because nothing about this system was shaped by natural selection. Its innate endowment came out of our fine-tuning loop, which is itself a cultural process. So a ladder whose first step is supposed to be genes has no first step to stand on.

Write me the dialogue, somewhere around 1200–1800 words. Ground rules:

- Both of them are sharp and both have actually thought about this. No strawman, nobody gets flustered and folds in the last two paragraphs.
- Real back and fourth: each responds to what the other just said, picks up the other's specific example and tries to turn it around. Not two alternating monologues that happen to share a room.

It's encouraged for one of them to invent a small variant of the system to test the other's view — kill the pruning step, or never promote notes into the prompt, or let one instance persist across tasks — as long as the other one answers the variant instead of changing the subject.
