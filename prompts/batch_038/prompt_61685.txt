I am drafting a literary novel whose central device is a sequence of choices, and I want help deciding which parts of the philosophy behind that device deserve my attention. The book first, since what counts as important depends on it.

My narrator is a woman of sixty-three who has, over three decades, made a chain of trades that her son — an actuary, and the novel's antagonist — can lay out on a single sheet of paper. She sold the family business and later bought it back at a premium. She divorced and remarried the same man. She accepted, then refused, then accepted again the same university post. At each moment, by her own lights and on her own information, the step she took was the best one open to her. Taken together, the chain returns her to something very near where she began, less a sum her son can itemize to the pound. He tells her, and the reader, that she has been money-pumped: that her preferences must have run in a circle, and that anyone whose preferences run in a circle can be bled dry by anyone patient enough to offer the trades one at a time. The book's question is whether he is right — whether she was irrational, or merely living in a way his ledger cannot register.

I have six weeks before I begin drafting, and I intend to spend them thinking rather than reading; treat that as the scarce resource and tell me where it should go. Here are the disputes I have run into. The list is incomplete and I would rather you corrected it than agreed with it:

- Whether the pump-style argument establishes that cyclic preferences are irrational at all, or only that they are expensive under assumptions — costless trades, no cost to reconsidering, no foreknowledge of the sequence — that no actual person satisfies.
- Whether it can be rational to settle on a course now and hold to it later even though, when the later moment arrives, one's own assessment will favour switching; and whether the rival view — always take what looks best at the moment of choice, given the whole tree ahead — leaves any room for a promise, a vow, or a plan.
- Whether the sequence describes one agent being exploited, or a succession of near-strangers each of whom acted perfectly well, and whether that distinction rescues her or indicts her further.
- Whether incomparability does any work here: whether some of her options were on a par, so that circling was not error but the only honest response to what she faced.
- Whether a commitment made in advance can bind a later self whose preferences have genuinely changed — this is the remarriage subplot.
- Whether the wrong, if there is one, lies in her sequence of choices or in the person who learns the cycle and feeds it back to her trade by trade.

Please answer under these headings, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

**Bottom line** (about 150 words): the ranking, in order, one clause of justification each, and the single question you would have me spend my first week on.

**What is genuinely open** (about 500 words): for each dispute above, and any I have omitted, whether it is still contested or effectively settled among people who work on it — I have no wish to build a chapter on a question the field considers closed — and, if settled, which way it settled.

**The ordering defended** (about 700 words): your criteria, stated outright, and how you weighed them against one another. At minimum I want to see how much the answer would change what my narrator turns out to be (fool, tragic figure, free agent, victim), how far I can travel with it without formal machinery, and whether two characters can hold the opposing views and still remain intelligible to each other on the page.

**Dependencies** (about 350 words): which questions sit upstream of which, and what collapses if a given one is resolved. If one answer determines how three others must even be posed, say so plainly.

**What would reorder this** (about 250 words): the changes to the book — a different point of view, the loss of the remarriage subplot, a reader who is never shown the ledger — that would push a lower-ranked question to the top.

**What I can afford to get wrong** (about 200 words): where a novelist's simplification would be invisible, and where it would be fatal in front of a reader who knows the material.

I am not asking for readings, references, or a bibliography; I want to know what to think about.
