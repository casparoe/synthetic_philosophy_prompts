I have spent twenty-six years on a plant floor, the last nineteen of them as a shop steward, and I have no academic training of any kind; the philosophy I know I have picked up reading essays at night. So I will be plain about what I know well and what I do not.

What I know well is the vocabulary of my work: the classification and the job description attached to it; what is in scope and what is out of it; the difference between what a person can be written up for and what they can only be thanked for; discretionary effort; the tactic we call working to rule, where people do precisely and only what the description says and the place falls apart within a fortnight, which tells you how much of the real work lives outside the description; and past practice, the way a favor granted three times becomes something you can grieve over when it stops. I have argued about each of these across a table from company lawyers.

Recently I came on a philosophical distinction that seemed to me to be about exactly this, though it never used my words. The claim, as I understand it, is that some acts are morally good and deserving of praise without being morally required: you cannot be blamed for leaving them undone, and yet doing them is genuinely better than not doing them. Running into a burning building for a stranger, giving a kidney to someone you have never met, that order of thing. And there is an objection to the claim, which I also found: that if such an act produces far more good than the acts that are required and costs the doer comparatively little, then calling it optional is only a genteel way of excusing ourselves, and the category collapses back into duty. There are replies defending the category, and I have read a couple of them without being sure I followed the reasoning.

Here is what I want. Restate that whole exchange — the claim, the objection, and the strongest replies to the objection — in the vocabulary I set out above, as faithfully as you can manage. Do not import my framework's assumptions and hand them back to me as translation: where a step in the argument depends on there being an employer, a negotiated description, a grievance procedure, or a wage at the end of it, flag that dependence rather than quietly relying on it. For each of the main claims, give me three things: the closest counterpart in my vocabulary, what you had to bend to make it fit, and what is simply lost in the bending.

Then the residue report, and I would rather you be precise than agreeable. My own suspicion — which I want tested, not flattered — is that my vocabulary cannot hold the distinction steady. On the floor, praise works as a ratchet: the moment management thanks people publicly for going above and beyond, "above and beyond" begins migrating toward the description, and within a year the person who does not do it is treated as a problem, whether or not anyone writes it down. If something like that ratchet operates in the moral case too, then the philosophers may have a category their own framework cannot keep from leaking, in the same way ours cannot, and the objection may be less a mistake than a description of what actually happens to any distinction of that shape. Tell me which it is: whether the residue you find marks a limitation in the way I talk about work, or is evidence that the distinction itself is less stable than it first looks. If it is the second, say what would have to be changed in the distinction for it to hold, and whether the change costs the defenders anything they wanted to keep.
