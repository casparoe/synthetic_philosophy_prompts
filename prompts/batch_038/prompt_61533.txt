Background, because I want this anchored to something concrete rather than the textbook version. I first hit "should the numbers count?" via a rationalist blog thread years ago and never fully got over it. The two parties for the collaboration:

- **Party A, a committed Taurekian non-aggregationist.** There is no entity that loses five lives; there are five separate people each of whom loses everything, from their own perspective, and equal concern for each person's separate point of view gives each an equal claim on the rescue, which is why a fair coin looks like the right procedure and why summing looks like a category error.
- **Party B, a committed Scanlon-style pairwise-comparison contractualist.** Numbers count without any interpersonal summing: you compare individual complaints one-on-one, and a principle that saves the one is reasonably rejectable by each of the five in a way that a principle saving the five is not rejectable by the one, so the five's complaints go undefeated.

They have been arguing past each other for a decade. Write the document they should have produced.

**Here's the case I want the whole thing to run through — it isn't in the literature as far as I know, so please actually engage with it rather than reaching for the standard rescue vignette.** Call it the Chamber case.

A private hyperbaric training facility suffers a pressure-system failure. Two sealed chambers. Chamber A holds five people; Chamber B holds one. There is exactly one reserve of breathable gas, enough to bring one chamber — and only one — back to safe pressure. Absent it, all six die, equally and soon. You are a visiting engineer with access to the reserve valve and no relationship to any of them.

The twist: all five in Chamber A were paid volunteers in a compensated risk study. They signed up, they were told the failure probability in plain language, they were paid a premium above market for exactly that risk, and they understood and accepted it. The person in Chamber B is a maintenance contractor who was routed into that chamber by a scheduling error ten minutes before the failure. She had no idea the drill was live, took no compensation, and assumed no risk knowingly. Nobody's preferences have changed: all six want to live, nobody is waiving anything, nobody is being noble. Strip out everything else — same ages, same health, no dependents, no differential social value, no friendship or partiality anywhere in the picture.

Sub-cases I also want handled explicitly:
1. Replace death with graded harm: the five volunteers each lose a hand, the contractor dies.
2. Dilute the voluntariness: the five "volunteered" by not opting out of a checkbox in a waiver nobody reads, for a $20 gift card.
3. Scale it: five hundred volunteers in Chamber A instead of five.
4. Make *you* the operator who ran the study, rather than a visiting engineer.
