I am applying for a semester of research leave, and our faculty committee requires a proposal of roughly fifteen hundred words describing a project that can realistically be completed within the time available. The project is in formal epistemology, and the half-formed idea I want the proposal to discipline is the following.

Consider an agent whose reasoning is fully transparent to it and which endorses a course of action only when it can prove that the action yields an outcome it values. Suppose such an agent contemplates replacing itself with a successor that reasons in the same way. To license the replacement it must pass from the premise that the successor will act only if it has proved the act good to the conclusion that the successor will act only if the act is good; that is, it must rely on the soundness of its own proof system. Löb's theorem blocks the inference. For any consistent and sufficiently strong theory T and any sentence φ, if T proves that the provability of φ in T implies φ, then T already proves φ. A system can therefore adopt a blanket trust in its own deliverances only at the cost of adopting trust in everything. The consequence, discussed in this area under the name of the Löbian obstacle, is that an agent which trusts its own reasoning cannot coherently deliberate about improving that reasoning, and that reflective self-modification appears to demand a systematic self-distrust instead.

My suspicion is that considerably more is being read out of this result than is in it, and my question is whether the obstacle identifies a genuine constraint on the structure of justification or merely charges for three idealizations that no informal epistemology needs: deductive closure, transparency of the agent to itself, and the assumption that endorsement is exhausted by provability. More sharply, I want to know whether the obstacle collapses into a difficulty that epistemologists already know how to describe — that a basic source cannot confer warrant on itself by means of its own deliverances, which is the trouble at the heart of the bootstrapping and easy-knowledge objections — or whether it is a distinct phenomenon calling for distinct remedies. If the former, the informal literature has already mapped the responses available, and the project's task is to establish which of them the formal result actually permits. If the latter, we should be able to say precisely what the additional content is.

Please write the proposal. It should contain:

1. A precise statement of the question — one that admits of a determinate answer, and that is not merely the uninformative question whether the obstacle is a real problem.
2. An account of why the existing treatments leave it open. There are those who take the obstacle to show that reflective self-modification is impossible, or possible only at the price of abandoning trust in one's own reasoning, and there are those who dismiss it as an artifact of a toy model that no realistic agent instantiates. Say what each side has failed to establish.
3. The method, stated concretely and staged across the semester. What I have in mind is a family of models in which the idealizations are relaxed one at a time, with attention to the point at which the collapse to triviality disappears, and then an assessment of whether each relaxed assumption was doing essential work in any informal account of justified self-revision. Explain why this is preferable to the obvious alternatives — waiting for a technical fix from the formal side, or conducting the whole inquiry by armchair conceptual analysis.
4. What a result would look like, and what would count as the project having failed. Include the possibility of a well-supported negative conclusion, and say how I would distinguish having established that from having merely run out of semester.
5. An honest assessment of scope. I am not a proof theorist, I have no collaborator who is one, and the leave runs fourteen weeks. Say what must be cut.
6. A closing paragraph explaining why this is a contribution to epistemology rather than to the engineering of artificial systems: what someone working on reflective equilibrium, on self-trust, or on the revision of one's own standards should take from it.

Two constraints. Do not provide citations or a bibliography — refer to positions, arguments, and results by description, and I will supply the references myself. And write for a committee of generalist philosophers, most of whom will not know the technical literature; the proposal must be intelligible to them without becoming vague.
