I spent thirty-four years as a field ecotoxicologist, most of them tracing the fate of agricultural chemicals through small catchments, and since retiring I have been reading the philosophical and legal literature on risk with a group that meets at our county library. My training makes me impatient with vagueness but also suspicious of formalisms that get imported into policy without anyone checking whether their assumptions hold in the field, so before I circulate a short essay to the group I would like a rigorous consistency check on the position I have arrived at. I have read enough to know the general landscape — Sunstein's book arguing that the precautionary principle is incoherent and potentially paralyzing, the statement drafted at the Wingspread conference in 1998, and the European commentary on both — but the view below is my own construction, and I want to know whether it holds together rather than how it compares to anyone else's.

Here are my commitments, stated as plainly as I can manage. (1) In a regulatory setting I am a frequentist about probability: a risk estimate earns decision weight only if it derives from replicated data, or from a model whose assumptions can be tested against such data; I do not think a decision-maker's subjective priors should carry independent weight. (2) Nonetheless, the absence of a demonstrated harm is not evidence of safety, and where an intervention is novel the burden of establishing that it is safe falls on its proponent rather than on the objector to demonstrate danger. (3) I reject any rule that licenses treating a worst case as though it were certain — including the maximin reading one finds in the philosophical literature — because every course of action, inaction included, has a worst case, and a principle that permits decision by worst case permits paralysis. (4) At the same time I hold that irreversible and non-substitutable harms — the extinction of a lineage, the permanent loss of an aquifer or of a soil seed bank — can warrant refusal of authorization even when the best available estimate of the probability is very small. (5) I do not believe any harm carries infinite disvalue, and I do not accept lexicographic orderings of outcomes: the loss of one species may be many times worse than the loss of another, but the ratio is finite. (6) I also hold that the status quo is not a risk-free baseline; doing nothing is a choice with consequences, and any workable precautionary doctrine must not smuggle in a presumption in favour of the familiar. (7) My own proposal for when precautionary refusal is warranted turns not on the magnitude of the probability but on the topology of the harm. I distinguish systemic risks, whose damage propagates through coupled subsystems in a way that no quarantine, recall, or remediation can bound, from local risks, whose worst case can be contained and, should it occur, compensated. Only the former, on my view, justify refusal irrespective of the estimated probability; the latter should be governed by ordinary comparison of expected costs and benefits.

Finally, three verdicts I feel confident about, which I regard as fixed points that any revision of (1) through (7) must reproduce. A proposed release of a self-propagating engineered organism to suppress an invasive insect pest was, I think, rightly refused notwithstanding favourable modelling, because once released it could not be recalled and its ecological effects could not be bounded. A proposed geothermal development that would have drained a single thermal spring, eliminating an invertebrate found nowhere else on Earth, was wrongly permitted, because the loss was permanent and the organism could not be restored by any expenditure. And a proposal to apply a mobile but readily degraded chelating agent across a watershed — one that would disperse widely and could not be recalled once applied, but whose effects on the sediment community would be reversed within a few decades by natural recovery at no cost to anyone — is a case in which refusal would have been excessive.

What I want from you is an audit, not an essay on the principle. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which only look as though they do? Order the real tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what abandoning it would cost me elsewhere in the list. Two hunches of my own, which you should treat as data about my confusions rather than as guidance about where the trouble lies: my reading group insists (1) and (2) cannot both be held, and I half-suspect they are right; and I worry that (5) leaves me unable to explain why extinction should matter differently from a loss that can be compensated. Tell me whether either worry survives inspection. Then answer three further questions: whether (7) does independent work or is merely (4) restated; whether my three verdicts are jointly entailed by (1) through (7) or whether one of them is quietly appealing to a principle I have not stated; and which single revision would repair the largest number of problems at once. Please do not recommend giving up a commitment because it is unpopular in the literature, and do not supply references or a reading list — I want the trace of what commits me to what.
