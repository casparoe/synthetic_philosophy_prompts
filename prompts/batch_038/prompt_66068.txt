Studying for my quals on 4E/embodied cognition next month and I've build myself a little battery of invented cases, to check whether I can actually apply the standard criteria for extended cognition or whether I'm just pattern-matching on Otto's notebook. Could you run the battery and tell me where my intuitions are off?

The seven cases (all made up by me, so don't go hunting for literature versions):

A. Marta's phone assistant stores her medication schedule, but it also argues with her, adds reminders she never asked for, and sometimes buries a stressful appointment so she won't see it until later. She never checks what it's done.

B. Ray is an air traffic controller. His strip board is always within reach, he never questions what's on it, and glancing at it costs him about as much as glancing at his own hand.

C. Nadia is a composer who can only work out a passage by pacing a specific four-metre loop in her studio. She says the loop is part of the thinking, and she's right that she gets nowhere sitting still.

D. A chess player who thinks by physically shoving the pieces around — not to record anything, just to see the patterns. He's hopeless without a board and fine with one.

E. A lawyer whose entire case-law library lives in a search database. She has memorised nothing and trusts the database completely, but she's barred from bringing any device into court, so during a hearing she simply cannot get at it.

F. An elderly couple, married 45 years, who've split their memory down the middle: he holds all the money, she holds every social obligation and family date. Neither functions well alone and both know it.

G. A man who has outsourced all moral deliberation to a chatbot. It gives him a considered verdict, he reads it, endorses it without a second thought, and acts on it. He describes the setup as his conscience, living in his pocket.

The criteria I want ranked (glossed so we're using the same things):

1. parity — if the same process were happening in the head we'd call it cognitive without blinking
2. glue-and-trust — the resource is reliably on hand and the agent treats its deliverances as given
3. automatic endorsement — content pulled from the resource isn't critically scrutinised
4. constant availability — retrievable whenever the task needs it
5. personal-level integration — the process serves the agent's own ends and is woven into her other states rather than running as a bolt-on module
6. the bloat worry (the Adams and Aizawa line) — that criteria like these are so loose they'd let half the environment count as cognitive

What I want back: first a verdict on each case — is the external resource part of the cognitive process or not, how confident you are, and which single criterion does the deciding work. Second, and this is the main thing, a ranking of the six criteria by how much explanatory work they actually do across the battery. Score each on two axes: discriminating power (does it separate my yes-cases from my no-cases, or does it just say yes to everything?) and reliability (when it fires, does it fire right?). If a criterion is definately just a relabelled version of another one, say so and dock it.
