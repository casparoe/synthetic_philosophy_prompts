I teach a 50-minute discussion section of intro political philosophy — 24 students, six tables of four — and next week is Machiavelli. They've read Prince chs. 6–7 and 25 but we haven't discussed 25 yet. I want a game, not a lecture: they should run into the problem before anyone names it.

Design a table game on fortune and virtù with full rules, a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money (I've got candy and can offer a small particpation bump), materials I can prep in under an hour, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 50. Two things I care about most:

1. Each player starts locked into a fixed mode of proceeding — cautious-and-patient vs. impetuous-and-bold, say — and the "times" each round sometimes reward one and sometimes the other, so that what wins is the *fit*, not the better character. I want two players with opposite modes to both finish near the top and two with the same mode to split badly, which is the pattern the chapter itself describes. Make the times partly readable (omens, rumors, other tables' results) but never certain.

2. There has to be a fair-weather stretch where you can spend points building barriers and defences that only pay off if a flood round actually arrives, so that preparation feels like pure waste right up until it suddenly isn't. And switching modes mid-game must be possible but genuinely costly and lagged, in a way that mimics his claim that a man can't deviate from what nature inclines him to, and can't be persuaded to abandon the way that has been working for him.

Then give me the debrief: which moments map onto which claims, including whether the final tally actually supports handing fortune the arbiter's seat over "the other half, or perhaps a little less." Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — especially if it degenerates into a lesson about just being bold — and be honest about which feature of Machiavellian virtù a points game structurally cannot reproduce, that I'll have to supply by hand.
