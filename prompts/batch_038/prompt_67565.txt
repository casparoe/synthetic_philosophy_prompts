I'm a second-year master's student in philosophy, and the seminar I'm in each term pairs a piece of metaethics or normative theory with a work of moral psychology; my slot this week is the question of whether the empirical literature on the moral differences between political left and right has any bearing on which side is right. I want to arrive having actually decided something rather than having surveyed positions, so I've been drafting an essay in advance, and I'd like you to write a full first draft of it that I can then argue with. The empirical backdrop, as I understand it: moral foundations theory holds that moral judgment rests on several distinct, innately prepared sensitivities — care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation, with liberty/oppression proposed later — and the architects of the theory have recently reformulated parts of it and issued new measurement instruments. Studies using the associated questionnaire have repeatedly found that self-described liberals, in the American sense, lean disproportionately on the first two foundations, while self-described conservatives draw more evenly across all of them; and the popular book that did most to spread the theory draws an explicitly normative lesson from that pattern, namely that the right has the broader moral palate, perceives more of the moral landscape, and is therefore better equipped both to build durable moral communities and to get policy right. I want the essay to take that inference seriously rather than wave it away.

Two reconstructions of my own are doing most of the work in my draft, and I'm not confident either survives scrutiny, so please interrogate them directly. The first I've been calling the receptivity argument: if two rival normative outlooks disagree, and the adherents of one reliably register a wider range of morally relevant considerations than the adherents of the other, then ceteris paribus the wider outlook is better supported, on analogy with a perceptual faculty — a creature sensitive to more wavelengths has access to more of the evidence about its environment, and is likelier to be right about it. The obvious worry is that the analogy smuggles in a presumption we cannot grant: that the extra sensitivities are cognitive rather than motivational, when threat reactivity, disgust propensity, and system-justifying motives are all live alternative explanations of why someone registers authority violations or desecration at all; and detecting a consideration is not the same as its being there. The second I've been calling the confabulation bind: the theory is usually bundled with the social intuitionist model, on which moral reasoning is largely post-hoc rationalization of intuitions that have already arrived — the machinery behind the dumbfounding results, where people firmly condemn a harmless but revolting act and cannot say why. If that model is right, then the philosophical argument that conservatives see more of the moral landscape is itself likely to be confabulation in the service of a prior intuition, in the mind of whoever finds it compelling; and if reasoning is reliable enough to carry that argument, the intuitionist model is overstated. Can the essay escape the bind, or does it force a deflationary verdict on the whole enterprise?

Please also make the essay face three empirical complications head-on, because I do not want to be ambushed in discussion: that the instruments separate rating how relevant a consideration is from agreeing or disagreeing with moral statements, and that the loyalty, authority, and sanctity items look loaded with parochial, group-bound content, so a liberal might readily agree that betraying one's country is wrong while rating loyalty as barely relevant, which leaves it unclear whether the left/right difference tracks moral concern or question-answering style; that fairness may not be a single thing, so if equality and proportionality come apart, items pitched in one register will systematically favor one temperament over the other; and that there is a rival monist account, associated with Gray and his collaborators, on which all moral judgment is a matter of harm perceived as done by an agent to a patient, such that desecration and insubordination may simply be elaborate ways of seeing harm to a community, a self, or an inheritance — which would pull the pluralist premise out from under the conservative-advantage argument. As for the deliverable: an essay of roughly 1,800 to 2,200 words, with the thesis stated early and a defended verdict by the end. The verdict may be narrow or conditional, but it may not be a refusal to choose, and "descriptive psychology cannot settle normative questions" is admissible only if you have actually shown why the indirect routes fail — which means working through wide reflective equilibrium and the evidential weight of considered intuitions, the epistemology of moral disagreement between epistemic peers, whether breadth or scope counts as a theoretical virtue among rival normative accounts, and whether an evolutionary genealogy of a moral sensibility can debunk or vindicate it. I have all the primary papers, so please include no bibliography, footnotes, or citations, and do not attempt to reconstruct sample sizes or effect sizes from memory; I want the argument. Finally, one epistemic request: as you go, distinguish what is robust among people who actually work on this material from what remains a live dispute, and do not let the modularity of the foundations, the durability of the left/right questionnaire difference under reframing, or the standing of the harm-monist alternative be presented as settled when they are not — and if either of my two reconstructions is a straw man of the theory, say so plainly rather than defending it for the sake of the assignment.
