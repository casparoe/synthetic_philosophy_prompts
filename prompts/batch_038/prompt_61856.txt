Our department is nominating a candidate for a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship in quantitative history, and I have been asked to turn a methodological worry into a fundable proposal. Please write the proposal itself, roughly two pages, on the following lines.

The worry concerns structural-demographic theory, and in particular the causal weight it places on elite overproduction: the accumulation of more aspirants to elite status than there are positions of power to absorb them. My contention is that this single phrase bundles three mechanisms which differ not merely in emphasis but in functional form, and that current empirical practice cannot distinguish among them:

1. A crowding mechanism, on which what matters is the ratio of aspirants to positions. This enters as a level: instability risk should rise with the ratio even if every aspirant's absolute prospects are improving.
2. An expectations mechanism, on which what matters is the shortfall between the trajectories aspirants were socialized to anticipate, through credentialing and family strategy, and the trajectories they actually obtain. This enters as a change term: a high ratio that has been stable for a generation should be benign, whereas a sharp reversal should destabilize even at a moderate ratio.
3. A coalition mechanism, on which frustrated aspirants matter only insofar as they can organize and induce defection from incumbent elites. This enters as an interaction: overproduction should be destabilizing only where elite cohesion is low, and a cohesive elite should be able to absorb or co-opt a very large aspirant surplus.

The proposal should state a single, adjudicable research question emerging from this trilemma, and should argue that the ambiguity is a substantive methodological problem rather than a terminological quibble: make explicit what differs, both for the explanation of particular historical crises and for the credibility of forward-looking forecasts, depending on which mechanism is correct.

Then give me the following. A concrete plan of work for the twelve months, acknowledging that for premodern cases elite numbers must be reconstructed from thin proxies, and that discriminating between a level, a first difference, and an interaction term is precisely the kind of task that demands more observations than historians typically possess; say how the plan copes with that. A defense of the chosen method against the obvious alternatives, namely adding another case study, adding further proxies to an existing regression, or building an agent-based model in which I simply stipulate whichever mechanism suits me. A specification of what a successful result would look like, together with the conditions under which I would have to concede that the three mechanisms are observationally equivalent in the available evidence, which should be presented as the failure of the project rather than as a finding. Finally, a candid paragraph on scope: where a one-year project has to stop, and what would be left for a follow-on grant.

Please do not produce a bibliography, and do not name specific datasets, articles, or archives; where a claim would need a source, mark the spot with a bracket and I will supply the reference. Argue from the logic of the mechanisms and from the general character of the historical evidence.
