My topic is Fitch's paradox of knowability. The way I've got it: if you say every truth is knowable, and you also say there's some truth nobody actually knows, then take that unknown truth, which has the form "p and nobody knows p." The first principle says it could be known. But knowing it would mean knowing p while also knowing that nobody knows p, and you can't do both. So the two principles can't both be true, which is wild because neither of them sounds that controversial on its own.

The response I keep running into is to restrict the first principle — only apply it to some truths, like ones that wouldn't be made false by being known, or ones that aren't self-referential, or whatever. And my whole pitch is that nobody ever checks whether a given restriction is actually any good. I've been thinking about it like unit tests in programming. You build a test suite: a bunch of specific sentences where we're confident about whether they SHOULD be knowable in principle, then you run each candidate restriction against them and see which ones pass. Like "the person who died alone in that apartment 200 years ago was in pain at the end" — seems like something the anti-realist really wants to count as knowable-in-principle even though obviously nobody will ever verify it. Or "there are an even number of stars in the Milky Way." If your restriction accidentally makes those unknowable, then it isn't doing the job the knowability principle was hired for, which was ruling out truths that are in principle beyond us.

So the question I want to propose on is something like: can you specify a test suite that a restricted knowability principle has to pass, and is there a general tradeoff where the more thoroughly a restriction blocks the paradox the less anti-realist work it can do?
