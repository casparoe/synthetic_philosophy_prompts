Right, so I'm chairing a two-day workshop on verbal disputes at my uni and I've got to give the opening remarks on the Thursday morning, and I'm quietly dreading it because I've accidently invited exactly the wrong mix of people. Room'll be about forty: a solid chunk are quantifier-variance types who think most of ontology is people talking past one another, another chunk are the neo-Aristotelians who think there's a privileged way the quantifier can go and that structure is a real feature of the world, a couple of deflationists who won't commit either way, and our head of department who's only turning up to find out why the whole budget went on biscuits.

Could you write the opening remarks for me? Three or four minutes when spoken, so call it 550–650 words, and please make it sound like a person at a lectern rather than an essay — I'll be reading it out, so it needs rhythms, a couple of places to pause for a laugh, and a welcome at the top and a "let's get started" at the bottom.

The thing that makes it hard: I promised in the call for papers that the chair wouldn't beg the question against anybody, and half that room will be listening specifically for it. So the speech can't just assert that there are (or aren't) composite objects, numbers, heaps and so forth, and it can't sneak a substantive ontological commitment in through the welcome, the housekeeping or the jokes. Every time I say something like 'there are forty people in this room' or 'there's a problem with the projector' or 'we've got a full programme', somebody out there has a view about whether that's innocent. I want the speech to notice this, to try and fail to get one genuinely neutral sentence out, and to make something of the failure rather than paper over it — and I'd like it to float the idea of stipulating a harmless 'there are' for the weekend, then immediately register that the stipulation is itself going to sound like a concession to one side and a capitulation to the other.

It should also: get the factions right and let both of them laugh at themselves without either feeling mocked; work in Carnap's internal/external questions and the method-of-elimination move that Chalmers does, lightly, as things the room already knows rather than as a lecture; gesture at the statue and the clay and maybe at mereological universalism without turning into a seminar; and close on the genuine worry that our dispute about whether the dispute is verbal might itself be a verbal dispute. I want people to feel that regress, not have it explained to them with a diagram. The head of department bit should be affectionate — he's a good egg, he just thinks this is all nonsense about nonsense.

No verbatim quotes from anybody, please, I don't want to get caught out mis-saying someone's phrase from memory, and definitely no reading list or suggested further work at the end. It's a speech, not a bibliography.
