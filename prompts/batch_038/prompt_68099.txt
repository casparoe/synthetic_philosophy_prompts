I am writing the methods section of a comparative economic history course in which students will assess answers to questions of the form: why did sustained growth in output per head begin in Britain rather than in the lower Yangzi Delta? Before assigning any answers, I want the standard they will be judged against, stated once and neutrally, so that a referee who held it could apply it while remaining agnostic between the familiar candidates — coal, colonies, high wages, institutions, culture.

Set out the conditions a satisfactory answer must meet, in three groups. First, what has to be settled before the question is well posed at all: how the foil is chosen, whether the two sides are units of the same kind, which indicator fixes the divergence and when the two series actually cross. Second, what the proposed cause must do beyond being present in the site that grew: that it be independently identifiable rather than a redescription of growth itself, that it come with a mechanism and a chronology, and that it be shown absent or inoperative on the other side. Third, what the evidence permits on that last point, given that the records of the two sites are not of comparable depth, so that absence of evidence in one is doing real work.

For each condition, give the consideration or example that motivates it, and say which are non-negotiable and which are in truth the commitments of one school. Then tell me whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect that demanding a cause which is a genuine difference between the two sites, which pins down the timing of the takeoff, and which is general enough to travel beyond this pair may be too much — and finish with the single condition that disqualifies the most of the familiar answers.
