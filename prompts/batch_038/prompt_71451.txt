I work in health technology assessment — the analysis that decides whether a public payer funds a drug or a screening programme. Not a philosopher, and I don't want a philosophy lecture; I want you to tell me what my own rulebook is committed to.

Here's the practice, described as neutrally as I can:

1. When we compare an intervention that costs money now and delivers health benefits forty years out against one that delivers them next year, we discount the costs and the health at the *same* annual rate — call it 3%. A life-year gained in 2065 counts for maybe a third of one gained in 2026.
2. The reason usually given for using one rate on both is that money compounds: a pound now can be invested and comes back larger, so future pounds have to be shrunk before they're comparable, and health has to be shrunk on the same schedule or you'll systematically overfund prevention.
3. But health is not allowed to compound. There's no rule anywhere that lets us bank a life-year now and spend it in 2060, or invest in health and earn returns on it. We treat health as strictly non-transferable across time.
4. The rulebooks also say the 3% is made of two separate pieces — one for the fact that future people will probably be richer than us, one that's just impatience about people who don't exist yet — and they say the second piece should be very small on ethical grounds. In practice nobody can ever tell me which piece a given number came from.
5. And in the sister guidance for long-run climate and environmental risk, the rate isn't constant at all: it steps down the further out you go, and irreversible or catastrophic losses sometimes get exempted from discounting entirely. So the rate stops being a function of *when* the harm happens and becomes a function of *what kind of thing* is being lost.

So: what theory of value does 1–3 actually presuppose? Does shrinking future health at a rate whose whole justification is capital markets commit us to treating health as a fungible investable stock after all, or is there a coherent story in which the discounting and the no-banking rule sit together — and if there is, what has to be true about time, and about the identity of the person who benefits, for that story to work? Then take 4 seriously: if growth went to zero permanently, does the practice still have any content, or is its entire ethical payload parasitic on an empirical forecast? And take 5 seriously: what does a rule that switches discounting on and off by *category of loss* presuppose, and is it consistent with 1 at all? My hunch is that 5 quietly abandons the monistic, single-metric picture that 1 assumes.

Commit to a verdict. I don't want a menu of possible readings with the picking left to me — tell me which reading you'd defend, whether the practice is ultimately coherent or is doing two incompatible things under one number, and where you're reconstructing charitably rather than describing what's really there.
