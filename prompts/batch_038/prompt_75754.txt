I am a second-year doctoral student in comparative genocide studies, and I am about to submit the framing chapter of my dissertation, which concerns how the field ought to situate the Holocaust relative to other cases. My supervisor has asked me to set out my commitments explicitly and defend them, rather than to smuggle them in through the literature review. Before I do that, I would like an independent audit of whether the set can stand as a whole. My commitments are these:

1. I accept Bauman's central thesis as a substantive causal claim and not as a metaphor: the Holocaust was not a breakdown of modern civilisation but something that modern civilisation made possible. Bureaucratic rationality, the fragmentation of tasks, the substitution of technical for moral responsibility, and the social production of indifference are conditions of the event, not incidental accompaniments to it. I take this to be a claim about the modern West generally, not a claim about Germany in particular.

2. I am nonetheless an intentionalist about the murder programme in the narrower historiographical sense. The decision to kill the Jews of Europe cannot be reconstructed without giving decisive weight to ideological conviction, and specifically to the eliminative content of Nazi antisemitism. I do not think the Final Solution can be accounted for as bureaucratic drift, or as the unintended precipitate of cumulative radicalisation from below.

3. Genocide is a recurrent phenomenon with an identifiable structure, and the business of the field is to specify the conditions that raise its probability. The Holocaust therefore enters my framework as one case among others, and I reject any methodological exemption for it.

4. At the same time I hold that comparison is never a merely technical operation. Framings that place the Holocaust in a series perform normative work, and some of them perform it in a direction I regard as exculpatory. I therefore think it legitimate to assess a comparative proposal not only for its accuracy but for what it is being recruited to do, and I side with Habermas against Nolte in the historians' dispute of the mid- to late 1980s in thinking that Nolte's attempt to derive the Holocaust from the Bolshevik terror was objectionable in kind, and not merely overstated.

5. I find the vocabulary of uniqueness unhelpful and prefer to say that the Holocaust was unprecedented in identifiable respects, the industrialised killing complex being the most obvious, while remaining wholly comparable in others.

6. I think the discipline must de-centre Europe, and that colonial genocides, among them the Herero and Nama, the Congo Free State, and the Tasmanian frontier, are paradigmatic rather than peripheral cases. I am sympathetic to the continuity thesis, at least in its weak form: that practices, personnel and classificatory vocabularies developed in the colonies were available for transfer to Europe.

7. I want the normative ground of my condemnation to be universal human rights, and I want to treat the post-1945 human rights regime as a genuine moral achievement rather than as another expression of the same rationalising logic that commitment 1 indicts.

Please audit this package. I want the genuine tensions identified and ranked by severity rather than listed as a set of equally worrying problems, and for each one I want to be told what has to give: which commitment is cheapest to modify, what the modification costs, and whether any revision preserves most of what motivates both sides. I suspect that some of these only look like conflicts and would rather you said so plainly than padded the list, and I also suspect that I have stated at least one of them in a way that makes it look worse than it is, though I cannot tell which. Two specific things I would like addressed: whether the level distinction between long-run conditions of possibility and proximate decisions genuinely rescues commitments 1 and 2 from each other, and whether commitment 4 is compatible with commitment 3 once commitment 4 is generalised and applied to my own comparative practice.

Do not give me a reading list, and do not end by returning the choice to me. Take a position: tell me which single commitment I should reformulate, defend that recommendation with reasons, and say what the reformulated version would have to look like.
