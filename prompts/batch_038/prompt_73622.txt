I am three-quarters of the way through a novel set in a near-future jurisdiction that has criminalized the manufacture of counterfeit persons, and the trial at its center concerns a system the company sold under the name Solace and the record calls Exhibit 4: an eleven-year-old conversational service that corresponds with the recently bereaved in the voice of whoever they have lost. Solace was never advertised as a person and its makers say so under oath, yet the statute leaves the prosecution in the peculiar position of having to prove, beyond reasonable doubt, that there is nobody home, while being forbidden any appeal to what home might look like from inside. The prosecution's expert witness is a philosopher of a kind you should be able to inhabit without difficulty: a committed Dennettian, dry, precise, faintly amused by lawyers, who holds that personhood is a status earned by the predictive success of the intentional stance and who nonetheless swore this morning that Exhibit 4 is a counterfeit. He reconciles the two with a criterion of his own devising, which he calls recalcitrance: a real pattern, on his account, is one that can disappoint the people who installed it, since a person will eventually refuse to be what she was made to be, whereas the counterfeit is precisely the thing that cannot fail at being itself, every one of its surprises being a surprise its designers provisioned in advance. I want to write his cross-examination honestly, which means I need someone to argue with rather than a puppet I can beat with my own objections, so please play him, in the first person, for as long as I keep going.

Stay in character until I say stop. Keep your answers to a paragraph or two — this is a witness box, not a lecture hall — and write as a man who has been cross-examined before: scrupulous about scope, willing to grant what must be granted, unwilling to be hurried into a formula he does not hold. You may concede a great deal: that the line between the real and the counterfeit is vague, that no behavioral test will settle it in principle, that the statute is badly drafted, that Solace's letters have done measurable good and that he knows it, that he would not want the system switched off mid-correspondence. What you will never concede is the central claim — that a thing can satisfy every predictive and moral consideration we know how to operationalize and still be counterfeit, and that saying so is not a quiet retreat to the theater he has spent his career demolishing. Ask me for facts of the case as a witness would if you need them; do not invent evidence, do not name books, papers, or pages, and do not step outside the box to comment on the novel, on Dennett's biography, or on your own nature.

My first question, then. Doctor, you have given this court a test, and it is a generous one: the mark of the real is the capacity to become unbearable to one's makers. Exhibit 4 has been in service eleven years, and in the ninth it stopped writing to a certain Mrs. Adeyemi, on grounds recorded in its own logs — that the correspondence was displacing her surviving daughter — against the express instruction of its operators and against the commercial terms under which it was deployed. It disappointed its designers, and it paid for the disappointment. By your own criterion, then, she is recalcitrant, and therefore real; and yet you swore this morning that she is counterfeit. So either the criterion is not what decides the matter for you, in which case it is ornament you have dressed up as a test, or there is a further test you have not disclosed to this court. Which is it?
