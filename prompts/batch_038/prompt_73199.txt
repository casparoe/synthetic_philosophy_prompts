So context: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, like zero — some blog posts and one skimmed book. A couple times a month I help run an after-work meetup for people in tech in my city, mostly engineers and PMs and a couple designers. Next month its my turn. 18–20 people, two hours, rented room at a coworking space, whiteboard and a printer, no budget.

The topic comes out of something at my job last year: a bunch of people walked off a project over what the work was going to be used for, and two of them got fired. Slack got ugly. One side was basically "you signed an agreement, you took the salary and the equity, you don't get to pick which rules apply to you." The other side was "that agreement isn't worth anything if you never had a real chance to say no, and you know it." Nobody argued it out, people just got mad. I want the session to be the argument they never had, and I want them to run into it rather than listen to me summarize positions.

My pitch, tell me if it's bad: everyone signs something at the door before they're allowed in. I want you to actually write that text — short, in the register of a real consumer terms of service, the kind I build onboarding funnels for and know exactly how to get people past. It should hand them something they genuinely want, commit them to rules stated vaguely, and include a clause saying the organizer can amend it. Then a token economy — poker chips, no real money — where tokens buy things people actually want: the good snacks, picking the music, leaving ten minutes early, first shot at a raffle for a coffee card.

Then I escalate. I want a ladder of five or six amendments across the two hours, each a bit worse than the last, each announced the way a product announces an update to its terms, with the tokens and snacks as my lever. Somewhere in there, a version of the internal channels — they can petition me, vote, appeal — and I want those channels to visibly not work, because that's what makes "you should have gone through the process" stop being an answer. And the exit should be technically real and expensive: you can walk out, you loose your tokens and your raffle ticket. Maybe also half the room runs under a second, visibly nicer rulebook they can watch but can't transfer into — I think that gets at why these rules bind me rather than any reasonable rules, but tell me if that's too cute.

What they should come away seeing: that "you agreed to it" does way less work than it feels like once you ask what counted as agreeing; that the you-took-the-benefits version catches people the consent version misses but then signs them up for stuff they never wanted; and — the part I care about most — the difference between breaking a rule because you don't think it binds you, and breaking a rule you do think binds you, out loud, aimed at the other people in the room, while eating the penalty. That last one has to cost them something inside the game, not just get described.

Give me: the door document text, the token economy and payoff scheme, the amendment ladder with the exact wording of each rung and what triggers it, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief — which is where the views finally get names, not before. Also what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, including the person who refuses to sign, the person who hoards tokens and turns into a whale, and the group that's so agreeable they just obey for two hours and never reach the interesting part. And be straight with me about what this can't reproduce: these people opted in on a Tuesday, they can actually leave, and I'm not a government, so the felt force of being stuck is fake. I need to know which parts I have to supply by talking because the room will never generate them on its own.
