I've been on our state-and-federal allocation commission for three years now — we're the body that rules on whether a given competence should sit with the municipalities, the state, or the federation. Our enabling statute says we should apply "the subsidiarity principle" and then helpfully stops, so a decade of rulings is basically the whole content of the doctrine here. My colleague who came over from an EU desk keeps saying we're reinventing the standard three-part test badly and without the paperwork. Anyway, I'm trying to work out where we land on next month's docket and, honestly, whether our own record commits us to landing there.

Here's the record (compressed, dissents omitted because they're long):

1. Interstate river basin — moved up to the federation, unanimous, after twenty years of failed compacts between the states.
2. Local zoning and land use — left with municipalities, even though the majority conceded that exclusionary zoning drives up housing costs for people who don't live there. The line it drew: those costs are "diffuse and unpriced," not the sort of spillover that triggers an upward transfer.
3. Occupational licensing in low-variance trades (barbers, that crowd) — left down despite licenses not transferring across state lines and despite heavy worker mobility. Rationale: reciprocity agreements between states handle the friction, and the panel will only move a competence up once horizontal coordination has actually been tried and actually failed.
4. Minimum standards for the treatment of a religious minority in public schools — moved up, 6-1. The group was concentrated enough to be a permanent local minority everywhere it lived. The rationale was that you can't leave a question to the level whose electorate structurally excludes the people most affected by it.
5. Data protection for private companies — moved up. National firms, fifty compliance regimes, no plausible horizontal fix.
6. What gets taught about the country's wars in history class — left down, narrowly. There was a live argument that a local majority shaping its children's beliefs harms outsiders and future migrants, and the panel refused it: it would not treat "the formation of attitudes" as a cross-border effect at all.
7. Pandemic measures — split competence. Federation got a floor on interstate travel restrictions; states kept discretion over closures and gathering limits.
8. State tax incentives aimed at poaching firms from neighbors — moved up and effectively banned. Textbook prisoner's dilemma, everybody defects, everybody loses.

Now the new case. A dozen cities have passed ordinances banning camping and sleeping in public space, with the stated aim of making themselves a less attractive destination than the city next door. Two adjacent metros have a voluntary cost-sharing agreement on shelter beds that has actually held for four years; nowhere else does anything like it exist. The people affected are overwhelmingly not registered voters in the jurisdictions excluding them, a large share aren't residents of them at all, and they are not concentrated anywhere — there is no city in the federation where they'd be a local majority or even a pivotal bloc. Public opinion on the ordinances is genuinely and sharply split across regions in a way opinion on data protection never was. And the cities argue, plausibly, that their the level with the real information about which blocks and which shelters matter.

Two things I want. First, predict the verdict — but don't hand me a coin flip. Tell me which rule we're implicitly running, because I can count at least four that are consistent with everything in 1–8 and come apart right here: something about material versus non-material spillovers, something about exhausting horizontal remedies first, something about preference heterogeneity being the entire point of having lower levels, and something about the political standing of the affected group. Which of my precedents actually pins each one down, which are still live, and is 2 or 6 the closest analog to the camping ordinances? Second, if the record genuinely underdeterminates this, tell me what I should argue for and be honest about what principle we'd be adopting either way — including whether it matters that this one is really three-tiered, which none of the others were.
