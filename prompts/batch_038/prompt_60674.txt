I've been trying to pin down what I actually mean by understanding versus just being good at the thing — the competence/comprehension split, plus that inversion move where you get design without a designer. (Amusing that the inversion phrase started life as a hostile reviewer taunting Darwin, and Dennett later wore it as a badge of honor.) Here are my gut calls on cases. Tell me what principle they add up to.

1. A chess engine plays a genuinely beautiful endgame. Competent, zero comprehension. Easy call.
2. My grandmother made a flawless souffle every week for fifty years and could not tell you why it rose. Ask her and she'd say you just know by the feel of the batter. I want to sort her with the engine, but that verdict makes me queasy, and I suspect the only reason is that she's a person.
3. A classmate who memorized a proof that the reals are uncountable, could recite it flawlessly, and fell apart the second you changed one hypothesis. No comprehension, just retention.
4. Someone who executes long division perfectly and has no idea why the algorithm works. Here's my problem: I want to say they don't comprehend either — but the comprehension is clearly somwhere, in whoever devised the algorithm, and they're borrowing it. Whereas with the octopus eye there was never comprehension anywhere in the ancestry, and I still want to call that design brilliant. So "is there comprehension upstream?" seems to be doing work in my verdicts and then not doing work.
5. A large language model that answers the question "does the chess engine understand chess?" well, and also correctly reports that it can't count letters in a novel word. I want to say that self-inspection is a real step up from the engine. But it's the same kind of system underneath, so I worry I'm just being swayed by the fact that it talks.
6. Natural selection building the eye. I happily call this intelligence without a mind.
7. But when an economist says the market designed this price signal, I think that's a sloppy metaphor and he should be embarrassed. Cases 6 and 7 look like the same move to me and I'm ruling opposite ways.

My tentative principle is roughly this: a system comprehends to the degree that you can interrogate it in ways its training or selection never targeted and it still tracks the truth — call it the off-distribution interrogation test. It's why I credit the model in 5 above the chess engine, and why case 3 fails outright. But it wrongly denies comprehension to my grandmother (you can't interrogate her about the souffle at all) and to me about riding a bike, which I also can't explain, and I don't want to accept that consequence.

So: give me one principle that handles as many of these as it honestly can, or tell me flatly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up and why I was wrong to have them. I don't want a tour of the options — commit to something and give your reasons. Then tell me what your principle delivers on two cases I didn't list: a model that solves a competition problem by a route its own authors cannot reconstruct afterwards at all, and a detection dog that flags a cancer marker with 95% reliability and has no concept of cancer. And finish by saying which of the two — my grandmother or the self-inspecting model — your principle ranks as understanding more. Be willing to be wrong about that out loud rather than hedging.
