For eleven years I have run the records room for a county government in the Midwest, and for the last three I have also been the staff person on a task force the commissioners put together after the state finally released the files from a residential facility this county operated for children with disabilities between 1937 and 1979. The task force has eleven volunteer members, a county attorney, and one recommendation to make: whether to build a permanent memorial, whether to publish a searchable register of the residents' names online, whether to keep the case files sealed for another fifty years, or some combination. The meetings have become an argument between two camps, and both camps argue from a single case. One side says the county's obligation is to the dead and to the survivors who want their names said out loud, and that anything less is a second abandonment; the other side points to three former residents who have written in asking that nothing be published, that the register would expose them to their own families, and that a plaque by the courthouse door would let the county feel finished with the matter while the living former residents still cannot get medical records corrected. On my own time I have been reading around this, mostly whatever the library has: Margalit's book on the ethics of memory, which I understood to be arguing that shared memory is what makes our closest obligations possible at all; David Rieff's short book in praise of forgetting, which I understood to be arguing that organized remembrance often makes conflicts worse rather than better; and, much earlier, Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. I have found all three illuminating and none of them usable. Nobody on the task force can act on a thesis; we need to know what we are required to do, for whom, and what would let us stop.

So before I bring another proposal to that table, I would like the specification rather than the positions. What does a theory of the duty to remember, and of when forgetting is permitted or even required, actually have to deliver in order to count as adequate? I want the requirements list, each with the case or consideration behind it. It should cover the phenomena such a theory has to explain: why the demand to remember feels urgent for some past wrongs and merely academic for others, even when the scale of harm runs the other way; why a person can feel bound by events no one in their family lived through; why the fixed public formula of remembrance tends to start sounding hollow within a decade or two; why remembrance sometimes hardens a conflict it was meant to close. It should cover the distinctions the theory may not collapse, and I would like you to say why collapsing each one is a real failure and not a quibble: remembering as a state of mind versus memorializing as a public practice versus merely preserving records nobody consults; the difference between knowing that something happened, knowing it accurately, and knowing it as wrong; a duty to remember versus a duty not to repeat versus a duty to repair, which people in my meetings treat as one obligation and I think are three; and, most importantly, to whom a duty of this kind could be owed, given that the dead cannot be addressed, the survivors are not unanimous, and future people have no interest in us yet. It should cover the results the theory has to accommodate rather than ignore: that human memory is reconstructive and degrades, so a requirement of accuracy may be impossible to discharge; that selection is unavoidable whenever a budget is finite, which means any account that cannot also say what ought to be forgotten is incomplete; and that whatever is required must be something an institution can actually be required to do. Please mark, for each requirement, whether you take it to be non-negotiable for any account in this area or whether it is really a commitment of one camp dressed up as a criterion, the way I suspect the demand that remembrance be valued for its own sake and never instrumentally is doing work that has not been argued for. Then flag any subset of the requirements that you think cannot be jointly satisfied, because if the list itself turns out to be a no-go result I would rather know that now than discover it in month eight of a memorial process. And tell me which single requirement eliminates the most of the familiar answers, since that is the one I would want the task force arguing about explicitly instead of past it.

Two constraints on how you answer. First, adequacy here is relative to a purpose, and the purpose is a document that eleven volunteers and a county attorney could actually apply to a real decision under a fixed budget and a records-retention statute, not a theory that is merely internally elegant; if some requirement is worth having for philosophy but useless for that purpose, say so and keep it anyway if it matters. Scoring the existing positions against your list is welcome but should be a short closing paragraph at most, since the criteria are what I am after and I can look up the positions myself. I do not want a reading list, a bibliography, or citations of any kind, and please do not put words in anyone's mouth with quotation marks unless you are certain of them; paraphrase instead. Second, and this matters to me more than the rest: be explicit about where you are on solid ground and where you are guessing. Separate what people who work seriously on memory and obligation treat as settled from what is genuinely disputed among them, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus, or an idiosyncratic one of your own as though it were standard. I have sat through two years of meetings in which confident people on both sides asserted as obvious things that turned out to be live arguments, and I would rather have a frank statement of what is unresolved than a tidy answer I cannot defend.
