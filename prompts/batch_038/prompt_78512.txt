For the past year I have been the data manager on a longitudinal study of well-being in patients who recently started a drug whose most common side effect is affective flattening. Part of the protocol is a daily diary: participants list up to three things from the day that felt good and rate each from 0 to 10. Six weeks ago the postdoc who wrote the coding manual left, and the definition of that item is now mine to defend. It says only that the item captures experiences of positive hedonic tone. That single clause turns out to be a grenade.

At a group meeting, two of our senior people argued for forty minutes over a batch I had flagged, and nothing was settled. The batch contained, from one participant: the first sips of coffee after the overnight fast (8); finally getting a pivot table to behave (6); sitting on the bench in the sun with no particular thought (7); the headache letting go around three in the afternoon (9, the highest score in the batch); being told their talk was the best of the day (7); a slow Bach movement on headphones (8). It also contained, from a different participant: watching the dishwasher run (8), who wrote in the free-text field that they had not enjoyed it so much as been absorbed by it.

Here is the exchange as I recorded it.

1. **Nadia, the psychologist:** All six share one thing, a distinctive felt quality, and that is what the scale measures — its intensity. This is the only thing that makes one number comparable across entries and across participants. If the entries share no property, the diary is not a measurement instrument, it is a diary.

2. **Emeka, our ethicist, who reads philosophy:** There is no such shared quality. Bach and the pivot table have nothing in common as feelings; nobody could pick the pivot-table satisfaction out of a lineup. What they share is not a sensation but a stance — the person is glad it is happening. He wants the item rewritten as "how good was it" rather than "how much did it feel good," on the grounds that the current wording sends participants hunting for a sensation and returns noise. The headache entry clinches it: what felt good there was the absence of something bad, and an absence has no positive feel of its own. The 9 is relief, which is a change, not a quality.

3. **Nadia:** Being-glad severs the very link that makes pleasure matter. Plenty of people are glad their day went to plan without any experience worth calling pleasant, and one participant in this batch rates the sun on the bench a 7 while telling us she would never ask for it to be prolonged, because asking costs too much. If pleasure is being-glad, then whether something is a pleasure turns on mood and second thoughts, and the account cannot explain why pleasure is what people seek and pain what they flee. Attitudes can diverge from how things feel; feelings cannot. As for relief: our own participants distinguish "the pain stopped" from "the pain stopped and it felt good that it stopped," and only the second phrasing attracts the 9s. So something is felt there, however hard it is to name.

4. **Emeka:** The bench case is an argument for his view, not against it. She has shown only that "wanting it to continue" is the wrong gloss; the right gloss is "pleased that it is happening while it is happening," which requires no desire for prolongation. Then the deeper objection: if there is one quality, name it, and say how a person identifies that same quality in Bach and in coffee. If she cannot, the 0–10 numbers are ordinal preference dressed up as an interval scale. His own account does not escape heterogeneity by magic but by using the right unifier — fear of spiders and fear of bankruptcy share nothing phenomenally, yet they are the same kind of state because they share a formal object. Pleasure is unified the same way, by what it takes as favorable, not by how it feels.

5. **Nadia:** She is willing to concede that there is no single simple quality. What there is, she says, is a determinable with many determinates — one shade of tone in the coffee, another in the Bach — and that is enough to license a single ordering, the way color licenses a wavelength scale. Then she turns his unifier against him: if being pleased that the Bach is playing and being pleased that the coffee is warm are held together only by the abstraction of favoring, his account has given up the claim that pleasure is a feature of experience at all, and this study is about experience. She also presses the dishwasher entry: on his account that participant was not pleased about anything, since they were not attending to the dishwasher at all but absorbed in it, so the 8 should be discarded. On hers, some tone was present and is being reported afterward, which is what the item asks for.

6. **Emeka:** He grants that the dishwasher is a real problem for him and refuses to explain it away — but says it is worse for her. If the participant was attending to nothing, the entry is a reconstruction, and her scale therefore measures remembered tone rather than tone. He then offers what he calls the decisive empirical point: in the high-dose subgroup, ratings do not shift downward, they compress, with almost everything landing between 5 and 6. That is what you would expect if the item tracked an evaluative stance that has gone quiet. It is not what you would expect if it tracked the intensity of a sensation, since a weakened sensation should lower the numbers rather than narrow their spread.

7. **Nadia:** Compression is exactly what you would expect if the instrument has lost resolution. A dulled participant can no longer discriminate fine intensities and regresses to the middle of the scale; that is a measurement artifact, not a metaphysical finding. His rewrite, meanwhile, would destroy the instrument in a different direction: "how good was it" invites a global evaluation, so the diary stops sampling experience and starts chopping life satisfaction into fragments, and the study ends up measuring the considered judgment about how one's day went that the protocol was designed to bypass.

Several of these moves struck me as answering a claim the other person never made, but I cannot tell which ones, and I am the person who now has to write the tie-breaker into a manual. Please adjudicate move by move rather than in aggregate: for each of the seven, say whether it lands, lands only in part, or fails, and why, and where a move is directed at a position the opponent does not hold, say what the position actually is. Then take up, in order:

(a) Whether any part of the dispute is verbal, and if so which part, and what wording in the manual would expose it rather than paper over it.

(b) The dishwasher entry. Should it score 8? I want to know whether the answer depends on which account of pleasure is correct, or whether both accounts can absorb it.

(c) The compression pattern. Is it evidence for either side, or is that kind of argument simply unavailable to an instrument of this design? Say what additional measurement would be needed before either of them is entitled to claim it.

(d) Whether Nadia's determinable/determinate move is a genuine third position or a redescription of the quality she has already admitted she cannot name — and, if it is genuine, whether it earns her the interval-like scale she wants at the end of move 1.

(e) What both of them are missing, given that the question in front of me is not "what is pleasure?" but "what does this item measure, and is it capable of measuring that?" If the honest conclusion is that the philosophy and the instrument can come apart — that either account could be true without changing a word of the manual, or that both require the same change — say so plainly.

Close with the single paragraph you would put in the manual in place of the current definition, written so that neither Nadia nor Emeka could reasonably refuse to sign it. I would rather you reason the case through than point me to the literature; I have no way to look anything up, and the argument as transcribed above contains everything I think you need.
