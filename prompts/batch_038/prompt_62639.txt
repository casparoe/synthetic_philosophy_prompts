ok so I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about this and I want a third party in the room, because I couldn't answer him and I have to answer a version of this at work about once a quarter.

I'm in the scenario/strategy group at a large asset manager. Twelve of us. We feed 30-year capital allocation decisions, and roughly quarterly somebody senior asks us something like: will the current political order in Beijing still be standing in 2050? How unusual is the US right now, historically? Is the post-1945 system ending? Every time, an analyst disappears for a week, reads a pile of history, comes back with a narrative, and then we argue for an hour about whether the narrative is evidence or just vibes with a bibliography. Last quarter a number from one of these went into a client deck and I genuinely cannot reconstruct how we got it.

What I want is the outside view turned into something we can actually run: reference classes, base rates, survival from the past, the whole Kahneman/Flyvbjerg reference-class-forecasting idea, but as ordered steps that a smart analyst with one working day and no historian on staff can follow, and that leave behind an audit trail a partner can sign off on in ten minutes. Sometimes the output goes in a deck with real money attached, so "we think probably not" isn't going to cut it — but neither is a fake precise number, and I'd rather the procedure tell me to publish nothing than publish a number it can't support.

Here's the fight I lost, and the procedure has to either handle both of these or say plainly where it concedes:

1. My friend says the reference class is always chosen after you already know what answer you want. Great powers, personalist regimes, bureaucratic empires, hegemons, party-states — each gives a different duration distribution and nothing in the method tells you which is the honest one. He says the entire exercise is a way of laundering a preference into an average.

2. He also says the historical sample is not a fair sample twice over. We have good durations for the polities that lasted long enough and kept records long enough to be written about, so the short ones are missing. And if I'm standing in 2025 asking about an order that has already lasted 75 years, the unconditional average lifespan of empires is simply the wrong quantity — I want expected remaining life given the age it's already reached, and I have no clue whether that's larger or smaller. He called it length-biased sampling, something about how if you drop in at a random moment you disproportionately land in the long-lived ones. I nodded like I understood and I didn't.

Given that, the deliverable needs:

- the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each one, what the analyst writes down at each one, and the points where the procedure stops cold with "no number" rather than grinding out a guess.
- a pre-commitment stage: the reference class written as one sentence plus an explicit membership test plus a start rule and a death rule, all fixed before anybody opens a single case. Because the coding rule does all the work — depending on what you're counting, Rome ends in 476, or in 1453, or arguably never, and that one decision swings the sample mean by centuries. I want the procedure to force that decision into the open and make the analyst defend it, not discover it in a footnote.
- a requirement to build two classes, one narrow and one wide, and to report the spread between the two results as the answer instead of a point estimate. Tell me how wide the spread can get before the honest output is silence.
- a rule for how far the base rate may be adjusted on the grounds that modern states have capacities older ones didn't — mass literacy, fiscal extraction, surveillance, nuclear deterrence, medicine. Cap the adjustment, make the cap written-down and checkable, and tell me straight whether the cap is arbitrary.
- an explicit account of where the procedure hands the question to a human because what's at issue isn't a fact but what we're even asking about.
- every step that is really a judgment call wearing an arithmetic costume, flagged as such. Don't be gentle about this.

Then run it, showing the analyst's actual worksheet, on three cases: (a) the political order in China over the next 25 years; (b) the US question, where what the client wants is a comparison to history rather than a probability; and (c) a third case you pick where the procedure produces an output I would hate to send — one where it either forces us to publish a survivial number that makes our flagship 30-year assumption look comfortable when I privately think it isn't, or forces "no number" on the exact question a partner has already promised a client we'd answer. Don't pick a soft one.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: the questions it will look like it's answering and isn't, and the failure mode we should expect to get burned by first.
