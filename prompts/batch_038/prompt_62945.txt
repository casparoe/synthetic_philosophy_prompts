I'm a third-year econ major sitting in a historical sociology seminar this term, and I've argued myself into a corner. Our Weber unit keeps circling the same fight — can "culture" be a cause at all? — so I tried to settle it by building an argument, and now I think it proves way more than I wanted:

(1) For a claim to be a causal explanation it has to rule some possible world out: it must say what would have happened had the cause been absent.
(2) Any claim of the form *these people's values caused them to do X* is compatable with the opposite outcome too — if the Calvinists had consumed lavishly instead of ploughing profits back in, you'd cite stewardship of the calling; if they'd hoarded, asceticism.
(3) So value-claims rule nothing out, and by (1) they explain nothing.
(4) Weber's Protestant ethic thesis is a value-claim of exactly that form, so it isn't a causal explanation, just the outcome redescribed in theological costume.
(5) But revealed-preference economics works the same way: preferences get read off choices, so any choice is compatible with the preference claim that rationalizes it.
(6) So by (1)–(3) supply and demand explains nothing either.
(7) (6) is false, and I'm not giving up economics to save Weber.

Somewhere in there it goes wrong, but I can't tell whether the problem is that (1) is too strict a test for anything in the social world to pass, that (2) is unfair to how specific Weber's actual mechanism is (predestination anxiety, work in a calling, reinvestment rather than consumption), or that (5) is just a bad analogy. If I have to pick, I'd rather keep (1) than lose (7).
