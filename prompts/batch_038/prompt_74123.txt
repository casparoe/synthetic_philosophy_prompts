OK so in my philosophy of maths seminar a mate put this argument up and I walked out unable to say what's wrong with it, even though I'm pretty confident the conclusion is rubbish. It's meant to show that talk of "same size" snaps once you allow actual infinities, so you can't keep both Hilbert's hotel and Cantor.

(1) If two collections can be matched one-to-one with nothing left over on either side, they're the same size.
(2) Hilbert's hotel is full: one guest per room, rooms 1, 2, 3, and so on without end.
(3) Now shift everyone — whoever is in room n goes to room n+1. Nobody's evicted, nobody's doubled up, and room 1 is free for the new arrival.
(4) By (1), the guests in rooms 1, 2, 3, … are the same size as the guests in rooms 2, 3, 4, …, and the second lot is a proper part of the first.
(5) But (3) shows the hotel taking in an extra guest without losing anyone, so after the shift it genuinely contains more guests than the part in (4) did. The whole is strictly bigger than that part.
(6) Nothing can be both the same size as and strictly bigger than one and the same proper part. So "same size" has no determinate meaning for infinite collections.
(7) The diagonal argument is just (1) applied over and over, so if (1) means nothing here, Cantor's theorem means nothing either — and there are no infinite collections at all.

My mate reckons (5) is the whole point: a hotel that's full and yet still has room is only possible if size-talk has come apart, and everything built on it goes too. I did the diagonal proof in week two and I'm definately not giving that up, and I don't want to give up (1) either — but say so if that's just stubbornness. Which step should I reject, why did it look airtight on the way in, and what do I have to hand over once I've rejected it? British English please, my tutor marks spelling.
