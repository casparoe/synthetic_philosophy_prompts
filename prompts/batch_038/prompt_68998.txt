I retired four years ago after a career in materials physics — most of it split between a diffractometer and the numerical codes we used to make sense of what came off it — and I have been reading philosophy seriously ever since, largely on my own and in a small group of six that meets on Tuesday evenings in the back room of a bookshop. Our present topic is the argument that the past cannot be infinite, in the form William Lane Craig gives it: that an actual infinite cannot exist; that a beginningless series of events would be an actual infinite; and, as a separate line, that such a series could not have been formed by successive addition, since completing an endless succession is not a thing that can be done. The discussion has been stuck for something like eight months, and I want to understand why. The positions in the room are roughly these. One member, a mathematician by training, holds that the question has been settled: Cantor showed that actually infinite collections are coherent, the arithmetic of infinite cardinals is consistent, and Hilbert's Hotel is not a refutation but a demonstration that our intuitions about the infinite are merely untutored; on his view the argument is not so much wrong as empty. Another accepts everything he says about sets and yet insists an infinite past is different in kind, because it would involve an actually infinite number of concrete events, each of which once occurred and is now gone, rather than an infinite collection whose members are all present together. A third thinks the real weight lies in the second line of the argument: whatever one grants about infinite collections, the present moment would have had to be reached by completing an endless succession. I incline toward the second or third position without being able to defend either cleanly, and I have noticed that nobody in the group has revised anything since we began.

Here is my working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and would much prefer to see tested than flattered. My suspicion is that we are not disagreeing about one question but about three, and that we talk past one another because we use the same vocabulary for all three: whether an infinite collection can be given all at once; whether an infinite collection can be produced by a process; and whether the past in particular is given or produced. If that is right, the members who find the Hotel harmless and the member who finds traversal impossible are each correct about his own question and neither has engaged the other. A second suspicion comes from my old profession. In numerical work one often discovers that an apparently well-posed result depends on the order in which two limits are taken, and that much of the controversy evaporates once the order is stated explicitly. I wonder whether something structurally similar is happening here, with the presence or absence of a distinguished present moment playing the role of the order of limits — that is, whether the argument about infinity is in truth a disguised argument about the metaphysics of time. On a view where the past is a completed block, an infinite past is simply an infinite initial segment with no traversal to perform; on a view where the present is a genuine moving boundary, traversal is precisely what is being demanded of the series. If that is the diagnosis, it would explain our impasse, because the member who finds the Hotel harmless is a block-universe man and the member who insists on the impossibility of completing a succession treats the present as metaphysically privileged, and neither has ever stated that premise aloud.

Acting as a professional philosopher with real competence in the philosophy of mathematics, the metaphysics of time, and this argument and its critics, please think carefully and diagnose the dispute rather than adjudicate it. I am not asking for a verdict on whether the past can be infinite, and I am not asking for a survey of positions or a reading list; I cannot verify citations, and I want your reasoning rather than your references. Begin with a short piece of brainstorming in which you lay out the candidate diagnoses you can see — including the possibility that the disagreement is merely verbal, the possibility that it is substantive but rests on unargued bedrock intuitions about what counts as metaphysically absurd, the possibility that a hidden formal or empirical question is doing the work, and the possibility that the question as posed is ill-formed rather than merely hard — and then commit to one, or to a ranked combination, and support it by showing what each of the three people in my group actually says and why those remarks are consistent with your diagnosis.

Finally, tell me concretely what would count as progress: a distinction we have failed to draw, a formal result about infinite order types and what they do and do not license, a reframing of the question that would let the group resume, or an argument that we should expect to remain exactly where we are together with the reason why. Write in American English, organize the response under a small number of clear headings, and aim for something in the region of two thousand words. Where you think I have misdescribed the argument or confused two things that are in fact distinct — and I expect I have — say so plainly at the outset rather than working around it.
