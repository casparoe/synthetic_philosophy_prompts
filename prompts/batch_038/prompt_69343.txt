I teach a second-year module on the philosophy of perception, and this term I am setting an unusual formative exercise: students must produce a piece of satire in which the humour derives entirely from getting the technical detail right. Before I set it, I should like a model exemplar of my own, so that I can show the class what a first-class submission looks like rather than merely describe one. I should be grateful if you would write it for me.

The form is a published adjudication by a fictional advertising-standards regulator, in the deadpan register of a genuine ruling: the advertisement summarised, the complaints listed, the advertiser's response set out, the assessment reasoned through, the ruling given, with a case reference number and the usual bureaucratic headings. Around 700 words, in numbered paragraphs.

The advertiser is a small firm supplying bespoke, artificially induced hallucinations to private clients. Its advertisement claims that its products are 'indistinguishable from the real thing', and the small print makes a further, more awkward claim in the same idiom.

The complaint is brought by a rival firm whose own products are marketed on the opposite principle: that what an artificially induced hallucination and a genuine perception have in common is precisely what makes them indistinguishable — a shared factor that can be specified, measured and advertised. The complainant alleges that the first firm's claim is misleading because it can produce no specification of any property its product shares with a veridical perception, and therefore no basis on which a customer could check the comparison.

The advertiser's defence is a thoroughgoing negative disjunctivism: its products are not to be characterised positively at all; there is nothing it is like to have them over and above their being indiscriminable from veridical perceptions; and the demand for a shared specification simply assumes that the two cases must fall under a common kind before they can be confused for one another.

The marks in this exercise are earned in the assessment section. The adjudicator must engage, seriously and in the correct order, with the following, and the comedy must come from the fact that a real regulatory framework cannot cope with them:

- the causal argument that motivates the complainant's position — a matching proximal cause and a matching neurophysiology, hence a matching state of mind — and whether a firm may legitimately advertise on the strength of it;
- the complainant's screening-off objection: if a common factor is present in both cases and accounts for the client's inability to tell them apart, the advertiser's talk of disjuncts does no explanatory work whatsoever. And the advertiser's reply, which must be given in full and must not be a straw man: that this objection assumes what it was meant to establish, since a shared factor however well specified redescribes the indistinguishability rather than explaining it;
- the reciprocal difficulty for the advertiser: if one of its products has no positive characterisation, what makes it the particular hallucination it is — of a lemon, say, rather than of a bellows — and can its catalogue be anything other than a list of things its products are not;
- whether 'is either a veridical perception or an indiscriminable hallucination' counts as a product property at all, or is a disjunctive predicate of the kind no self-respecting regulator would accept in a specification;
- the customer testimonial the advertiser submits in support — a client who reports that he could not tell the difference — which the adjudicator ought to recognise as evidence of nothing whatever on the advertiser's own theory, since on that theory indiscriminability is constitutive of the product rather than detectable in it.

Three further requirements. First, the piece must be fair: the adjudicator should plainly understand the position she is adjudicating, and I leave the outcome to you, provided it follows from the reasoning rather than being selected for the sake of a punchline. Second, the register must stay deadpan from beginning to end — no winking, no authorial aside — while remaining legible and funny to a reader who has never encountered the argument from illusion; the conventions of the genre must carry the accessibility. Third, name no real philosopher, living or dead, and offer no citations, references or bibliography; the firms, the products, the regulator, the officers and the case number are all to be invented. For your own orientation, the terrain is the disjunctivist tradition running through McDowell, Snowdon, Martin and Fish, together with the common-factor conception it opposes — but nothing in the piece should betray that you have consulted it. British English throughout.
