I'm editing a small annual anthology of philosophical fiction for a reading group I've run for several years, and the theme of the next issue is integrity. Two thirds of the submissions I've received so far are the same two stories wearing different clothes: a trolley-problem retread in which the protagonist pulls the lever and then weeps, and a portrait of an exhaustively selfless person who turns out to be hollow. I would like to commission something considerably better from you, and I want to be precise about the conceptual material it should work through. The idea is Bernard Williams's integrity objection to impartialist moral theories — his claim that consequentialist and Kantian frameworks ask the agent to stand outside his own life and justify it from a point of view that belongs to no one in particular, and that this demand can alienate a person from the very projects and attachments that give him a reason to go on. I have two of his illustrations in mind. One is the young chemist, recently trained, in poor health, with a wife and children who depend on his income and no realistic prospect of other work, who is offered a decently paid position in a laboratory conducting research into chemical and biological warfare; the job is one he would find abhorrent, but the utilitarian calculation seems to favor taking it, partly because a less scrupulous colleague would take it otherwise and pursue the work more zealously. The other is Williams's observation that a man who saves his wife from drowning and then explains that he acted because it was permissible, in situations of that kind, to save one's wife has had one thought too many — the thought of his wife should have been the whole of his motive, and reaching for a further justification is already a kind of failure. I want the story to be built out of this material without ever lecturing about it. No philosopher is named, no term of art is spoken aloud by a character, and no one delivers a speech about what it means to be human.

Here is the premise I'd like you to build on. You should develop and complicate it, but not replace it. In a mid-sized river city, some years after a catastrophic flood, the municipal emergency service has begun administering a scheme called Credit for Assistance. Anyone who intervenes in a rescue is logged by the service's dispatch record, and the log entry is not complete until the intervener answers one question on the record: why did you act? The scheme's designers are not villains and they are not fools. Their reasoning is actuarial and, within its limits, sound: outcomes are noisy, and a stated reason is a better predictor of future reliability than a single lucky rescue, because a person who acted because the victim was his brother will next time walk past a stranger, whereas a person who acted because a person in that water warranted help will come back for anyone. So the scoring is openly tilted toward generality. Reasons framed in terms that would apply to anybody earn full credit; reasons that identify the victim by their relation to the rescuer earn a fraction. Credit converts into small, unglamorous, life-shaping things — priority in the flood-defense lottery, a rebate on insurance, a better position on a housing list. Your protagonist, whom you may call Aurel, pulled his younger brother out of a drainage culvert in eleven minutes of cold water, answered the question honestly, and was scored at the lowest tier. The story covers the eleven days during which he is permitted to file an appeal, and the appeal requires him to supply a better reason — not exactly to lie, but to redescribe what actually moved him in a form the scheme is able to recognize. Also in the story, and roughly his equal in narrative weight: the appeals officer, who answered the same question about her own daughter in the scheme's first year, gave the general answer, was awarded full credit, and has never since been able to establish to her own satisfaction whether she was telling the truth.

Some specifications. Roughly two thousand words, give or take a few hundred; third person limited, anchored to Aurel, with one scene in the appeals office rendered from inside his perception rather than hers. Keep it physical and procedural — the culvert, the wet stair, the forms, the particular humiliation of being asked to explain yourself to a person who is being paid to be reasonable. The story has to turn on the actual content of the appeal: the reader should come away understanding what a compliant justification looks like as a piece of language, and what it costs a person to manufacture one about the worst eleven minutes of his life. Please do not let the piece collapse into a satire of bureaucracy for its own sake; give the designers at least one moment in which they are visibly right, a case on file where a rescuer who named a relation did in fact fail to come back for a stranger, so that the charge against the scheme is not that it is empirically mistaken but something harder to state. The ending should not resolve the question in Aurel's favor or in the scheme's favor, and the appeal can be granted or refused as the story requires; what matters is what he has done to his own memory by the time he files. No quotations from any real book, essay, or speech anywhere in the text, and no allusion that a reader would need a philosophy degree to catch.
