I spend two days a week in the cataloging office of a small regional museum and the rest of the week auditing a graduate seminar in aesthetics, and between the two I have accumulated a set of judgments about fakes and originals that I cannot get to cohere. I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. The case that started it for us was a Delft interior our founder bought in 1931 on a Berlin connoisseur's opinion; it was exposed in the eighties as the work of a restorer from Haarlem, and I think the painting became worth less when that came out — less in money, obviously, but also less as a thing to stand in front of, even though not a brushstroke changed between Tuesday and Wednesday. I have the mirror-image reaction and don't find it embarrassing: a panel that sat in our study collection as "follower of Rembrandt" was cleaned and reattributed to Rembrandt himself, and I am quite sure it looks better now than it did before. A restorer's inpainting of a water-damaged fresco seems to me the same sort of thing — the completed wall is better to look at than the ruined one, and I would defend the intervention; but if a copyist had painted that wall from scratch to the same standard, I would call it decoration. And an unfinished opera patched out from the composer's sketches by a competent editor is worth hearing, though I refuse to call it his work.

The other cases pull the opposite way and I cannot explain the difference. A violin long believed to be a Strad turned out on relabeling to be a superb late-nineteenth-century workshop copy from Mittenwald, and I want to say the fraud was in the paperwork: the instrument's musical value is untouched, and a blind listening test would settle it. But when I learned that a celebrated period-instrument recording had been made on modern instruments with the tuning and the room corrected in the studio, I felt cheated, and I now think that performance is worse — though a blind listening test would settle nothing there either. A bottle of old Bordeaux revealed to be a skilled fraud: the drinking was exactly as good as it tasted, and I would drink it again with no loss at all. A lab-grown diamond sold honestly: no loss whatsoever, and at equal price I would take it over a mined one; sold as mined at the mined price, I have been wronged, but the wrong seems to live in the transaction, whereas in the Delft interior the wrong seems to live in the object. An image I admired that turned out to be a machine-made composite of stock photographs: the admiration evaporates on the spot — yet a print I bought in a flea-market box, whose provenance is a total blank, I admire fully and feel no itch to find out who made it, so it cannot simply be that I require a history, or else I require nothing. And here is the pair I find most humiliating. A living painter's note-perfect reconstruction of a lost work, exhibited openly and labeled as a reconstruction, strikes me as a remarkable stunt and a minor work; the same painter's forgery of that lost work, caught in the act, strikes me as more worth looking at than the honest copy, though the conduct behind it is far worse. Finally, a verdict I suspect is indefensible: when the Delft interior went into the vault I thought it should stay out of the galleries permanently, better that nobody look at it — and yet I would happily set the fraudulent wine bottle on a shelf as a curiosity, and I would want the machine-made image in an exhibition about deception. I know the autographic/allographic distinction and I think it does some work here, but it should make the violin and the recording come out the same way, and for me they do not.

My own guess is something like this: the value of an object is the value of the properties it presents to me plus the value of the achievement those properties represent, where the achievement is measured against the problems that were actually there to be solved where and when the thing was made. Test that against the cases and repair it or throw it out. There is also one I keep going back and forth on, and it is not a museum matter at all: my grandmother's longcase clock, which an appraiser told me is a 1920s reproduction rather than the eighteenth-century piece the family always claimed. I still love it, but I notice that I now love it as family furniture and no longer as an antique, and I cannot tell whether anything changed in the clock or only in my bookkeeping. What I want back is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely and clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause and what goes wrong if that clause is dropped; its verdicts on two cases I have not listed — a medieval manuscript that turns out to be a meticulous nineteenth-century transcription of a lost original, and the fiberglass replica of our courthouse facade put up after the fire, which the town adores and which I find I cannot dislike — plus one further case of your own choosing that would discriminate between the candidates if it is not already obvious which one is right; and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts the principle cannot save. I would rather be told plainly that the Delft interior reaction, or the ranking of the forgery above the open copy, has to go, than be handed a formula with enough epicycles in it to keep every one of my intuitions alive.
