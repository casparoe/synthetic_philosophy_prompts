Nevada requires that a proposed amendment to its state constitution be approved twice, with an election in between. An amendment originating in the legislature must pass two consecutive sessions — and because that legislature meets only in odd-numbered years, those sessions are typically two years apart — before the measure may even go to the voters. An amendment originating in a citizen petition must win a majority at two successive general elections before it takes effect, which is why measures approved in one cycle reappear on the ballot in the next. Meanwhile the same legislature can enact an ordinary statute, and repeal it, within a single session on a bare majority; and in the large majority of American states one legislative vote plus one referendum is enough to amend the constitution.

What theory of the popular will does the double-vote requirement presuppose? I can see two readings and don't know which the practice actually commits to. On one, a single majority registers a wish rather than a will: the demos is a persisting agent, and its genuine choice is only what it holds to across time, so the second vote is not a check on the first but the first one finally becoming real. On the other, nothing so metaphysical is involved — the interval is a filter for error and enthusiasm, and repetition adds evidential weight the way a repeat measurement does. Does the design decide between these, or is it compatible with both?

Relatedly: is this precommitment at all? The second legislature or electorate is entirely free to refuse, so the first vote binds no one and constrains nothing that would otherwise happen. If a self-binding device has to leave the later self unable to do what it is now tempted to do, then this isn't one; it looks instead like a cooling-off period, or a heightened threshold. What would count as genuine precommitment in a constitutional order — entrenched clauses that cannot be amended, supermajority requirements, judicial review of ordinary legislation — and why would a framers' choice of the cooling-off device over those tell us something about whether they thought the danger was passion, haste, or something else?

Finally, three tensions I'd like worked through. The requirement seems to assume that the intervening election is a verdict on the amendment, yet candidates rarely campaign on it and the second assembly contains members elected for unrelated reasons — if the election isn't about the measure, what licenses treating the second vote as evidence of a durable will rather than as double-counting the same electorate? Is the double-vote rule consistent with the same state's readiness to let a bare majority make and unmake statute law in days, or does that pairing require two different conceptions of the same people? And which parts of this are strictly presupposed by the practice rather than merely fitting it well — where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival accounts of collective agency over time would equally license it?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single view of the popular will that the practice most plausibly presupposes, and whether the practice is internally consistent — and then give the reasoning, distinguishing what it strictly requires from what it merely tolerates. No preamble, and no restating of the question.
