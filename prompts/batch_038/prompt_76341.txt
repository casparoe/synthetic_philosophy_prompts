**Question (part b, 10 marks):** Set out Newcomb's problem. Identify the two principles of choice that Nozick says come into conflict in it, explain how each one generates a recommendation in the case, and say what the defender of each has to give up. Would the conflict dissolve if the predictor were merely very reliable rather than infallible?

**Student's answer (script 7):**

Newcomb's problem is the case Nozick came up with in 1969 where the two main principles of decision theory give you opposite advice, which is why its properly a paradox rather than just a puzzle.

Setup: Box A is transparent and has $1,000 in it, you can see it sitting there. Box B is opaque and has either $1,000,000 or nothing. Before you came into the room a predictor who is essentially always right predicted whether you would take only B or take both. If it predicted one-boxing, B has the million; if it predicted two-boxing, B is empty. You can't see into B and nobody tells you anything about what it predicted.

Principle 1 — strategic dominance. The prediction has already been made, so nothing I do now can change what's in B. Holding the state of B fixed, taking both always nets $1,000 more than taking only B: if B has the million, taking both gives $1,001,000 against $1,000,000; if B is empty, taking both gives $1,000 against $0. So taking both dominates, and that's the two-boxing argument in a nutshell.

Principle 2 — expected utility. People who one-box overwhelmingly walk away rich, people who two-box overwhelmingly walk away with the $1,000. So if you conditionalise on your own choice as evidence of what the predictor did, one-boxing has a much higher expected utility. This is the evidential way of thinking, and the two-boxer is basically a causal decision theorist, someone who says the act is causally independent of the contents of B so you should care about efficacy rather than auspiciousness (Lewis presses roughly this line).

My own view on where the real difficulty lies: the one-boxer is effectively giving up on expected utility maximisation, since no genuine expected-utility maximiser would turn down a free extra $1,000. And the paradox only really gets going if the predictor is infallible. Once you allow that it's only, say, 90% accurate, the dominance argument breaks down, because now there's a real chance that B has the million even though you two-box, so taking both is no longer better in every possible state — and at that point one-boxing clearly wins on the numbers. The standard one-boxing reply seems to be that taking both boxes makes it the case that the predictor predicted you'd take both, i.e. backwards causation, which I find hard to swallow. So I'd two-box, mainly because I don't see how a choice made now can affect something that has already happened.
