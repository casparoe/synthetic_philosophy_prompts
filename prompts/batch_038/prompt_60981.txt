Six weeks ago my marriage ended, and the part I have not been able to get past is not the ending but the six years that preceded it. In the middle of all of it, a friend of mine — she teaches philosophy of mind and is the sort of person who keeps lists — produced a list of eleven things she had said to me between 2019 and 2024, each of which I had answered, sincerely, with some version of "he's under a lot of pressure right now." My therapist has a word for what she thinks I was doing and I don't trust it; my friend, more usefully, told me to write the cases down and see whether I was applying one standard or several. She also pressed the two standard positions on me over a weekend — the divided-mind picture I associate with Davidson, and the deflationary line I associate with Mele, on which nothing so exotic is going on and self-deception is just motivated belief formation with the usual biases doing the work — and I found the second far more attractive than I expected to. So I have a file. It contains my own case and about a dozen others, some invented, each sorted into self-deception or not, and the sorting now looks like it has a shape I cannot state.

The file. A man who insists there is nothing to suspect and who has checked his partner's phone weekly for two years, always finding nothing, which he takes as confirmation: self-deception, clearly. A woman whose partner has stopped initiating intimacy, who says it is work stress, who has looked for no evidence either way and has organized nothing around not finding it out: not self-deception — though from outside I struggle to say what distinguishes her from the phone-checker other than that she hasn't started checking. A person with a lump who decides not to think about it until the biopsy result arrives, and who genuinely does not: not self-deception, because she booked the appointment and intends to read the result. The same person after the appointment has lapsed twice: self-deception; same belief, same motive, and only her behavior around the evidence has changed. Someone whose partner really is faithful and who believes it, but believes it by leaving a letter unopened and steering every conversation elsewhere: still self-deception, since being right does not clean it up. A friend who says she remains at a collapsing organization out of loyalty to her colleagues and who has turned down two better offers without reading them: self-deception — and note that what she wants is not that the proposition be true but to see herself as loyal, so any principle stated in terms of desiring the content of one's belief comes out wrong here. A parent who says her child's poor recital was lovely: not self-deception, because that is not a belief report at all, which tells me the principle needs a clause about assertions versus expressions of attitude or it will sweep this in. An investor who received three written warnings, filed them unopened, and later says with complete sincerity that he never saw any warning signs: self-deception, and his later avowal is not a lie, so the sincerity of self-report is no test in either direction. A department that has described the same doomed program as "transitional" for eleven years: I do not know whether this belongs in the file at all, since I suspect group self-deception is only ever a metaphor, but if my principle is going to be forced to handle it literally I would rather know that now.

My own tentative principle, which I expect is wrong: self-deception is what happens when a person's motivation leads them to bring about and then sustain the evidential conditions under which a comfortable belief gets formed, so that the work is done upstream — in what one attends to, gathers, and reads — rather than in some impossible act of believing against one's own evidence. It explains the lapsed appointment and the unopened letter and the unread job offers, and it dissolves the puzzle that used to stop me, since nobody has to know the truth in order to avoid it. But it looks as though it swallows the woman in the second case, whose motivation surely shapes what she bothers to look for even when she does nothing, and it threatens to reduce the difference between keeping and missing an appointment to a matter of administrative diligence. So: what principle, or small set of candidate principles, actually captures these verdicts? State it or them precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which case in the file motivates each clause. Tell me what it says about cases I have not listed — I particularly want to know about someone who fabricates evidence in their own favor, and about someone who knows the truth, rehearses it constantly in private, and reports the opposite to everyone. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and which of those you would overturn rather than complicate the principle to save. Then take up the one I cannot settle. In the past six weeks I have several times said, and meant, that I always knew. Either I did know and would not let myself acknowledge it, which is the paradigm case, or I never knew and my present conviction is memory rewriting itself so that my past self looks shrewder than she was, which is not self-deception but something else I have no name for. Tell me which of these my own principle assigns, whether any evidence could distinguish the two readings even in principle, and whether the distinction survives the fact that both are available to me now only through the same unreliable faculty. Finally, I would rather you be explicit about your own confidence than present me with a tidy synthesis: say which of my verdicts you would give up first, what argument or counterexample would move you to a different principle than the one you settle on, and where you think the reasoning above is most likely to have gone wrong.
