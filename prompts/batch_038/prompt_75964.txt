I took early retirement from a chair in low-temperature physics five years ago, after thirty-eight years spent largely on dilution refrigeration and with a lingering affection for the thermodynamics of irreversible processes, and I have since been reading political philosophy with a small group of former colleagues who meet on Tuesday evenings here in Oxford. My instinct, which I have not succeeded in disciplining out of myself, is to treat a proved theorem as a proved theorem: once the hypotheses are granted, the conclusion is not a matter of taste. That habit served me admirably with Bell's theorem, and I suspect it has served me rather less well with Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, so I should like a clear-minded assessment of precisely where the analogy between the two breaks down.

As I presently understand it, Harsanyi showed that if each individual's preferences over risky prospects satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and if society's own ranking of those prospects is likewise von Neumann–Morgenstern-rational and defers to unanimity — where unanimity seems to mean no more than that society is indifferent when everyone is indifferent, and prefers a prospect when everyone prefers it — then the social ranking must be representable as a weighted sum of the individual utilities; and since no individual can coherently claim a privileged position, the weights come out equal, so the social ranking is a sum of utilities and nothing else. What strikes me is that this has exactly the architecture of a no-go result: the hypotheses look like bare requirements of coherence plus a minimal deference to the people affected, and the conclusion forecloses an entire family of views. On that reading, distribution-sensitive positions — Rawls's difference principle, prioritarianism, the various egalitarianisms that object to the shape of a distribution rather than to its total — are not rival moral theories competing with utilitarianism on level terms. They are attempts to evade a theorem, and whoever holds one has either failed to notice which hypothesis she is discarding or is discarding it without declaring so. I also gather that the theorem disposes of a difficulty I had assumed was fatal, namely how one is entitled to add together quantities that belong to different persons: if each utility is fixed only up to a rescaling and a shift and the sum is nonetheless well defined, then interpersonal comparability has been secured rather than smuggled in.

My question is a practical one. A member of the Tuesday group, a retired crystallographer with firm egalitarian commitments and considerably more patience for argument than for formalism, has undertaken to defend the difference principle next month, and I have volunteered to respond. I have no wish to prevail by rhetorical means, and I should not be content with the observation that Harsanyi and Rawls merely disagree about what happens behind the veil of ignorance, which is where our discussions have hitherto stalled. What I want is the cleanest statement of why her position cannot be rendered consistent with the theorem: which single hypothesis she is obliged to surrender, and why surrendering it costs her something she will not wish to pay. Then I should like to know what, on that footing, is left for distribution to do once the sum has been fixed. And if it turns out that I have misstated either the content of the theorem or the standing of its hypotheses — if, for instance, the equal weights, the unanimity condition, or the well-definedness of the sum are carrying more weight than I have allowed — I would much rather learn it from you now than in front of the group on the third Tuesday of next month.
