Thirty-eight years as a soil chemist, retired four now, and I've fallen into political philosophy the way other retirees fall into golf. I've come out the other side an epistocrat of a particular stripe, and I want you to go after it properly — not the strawman version, the one I actually hold.

Here's the position, in two parts. Part one is the general claim: the authority of a decision procedure ought to track the competence of the people running it, and a system that hands equal decisive power to everybody regardless of whether they know anything about the matter isn't obviously just, it's just old. Part two is my own fix, which I haven't seen anybody propose — feel free to tell me it's been proposed before and is known to be bad. Don't test voters; I know how literacy tests were actually used in this country, I'm not an idiot. Instead make the ballot two-part. You vote, and you also write down in your own words the strongest objection a reasonable person on the other side would make to your choice. Ballots with no rebuttal, or a rebuttal that doesn't actually engage the choice, count at fractional weight, say a half. My thought is that this buys the epistemic benefit of a screen without taking away anybody's vote, because what you have to demonstrate isn't knowledge, it's that you've been in contact with a real argument.

What I want from you:

1. The defeaters, ranked. Which are fatal to part one, which to part two, which are merely annoying and which are fatal to both at once. I'd rather have one genuinely fatal thing than twenty quibbles.

2. What does this position predict that its rivals don't? Specifically against the view that legitimacy comes from equal power rather than good outcomes, and against the view that what a polity needs is dispersed local knowledge rather than expert knowledge. And which of those predictions have already been tested — I have in mind the finding that people who score high on political knowledge quizzes are often better at defending their side rather than better at getting things right. Tell me whether that cuts against me or whether I can absorb it, and be honest about which.

3. Where my two-part ballot collapses into something else. It seems exposed to the objection that it just measures verbal fluency and schooling and re-entroduces the class bias I was trying to dodge, and to the objection that it rewards people who can write a convincing-sounding rebuttal they don't believe. Are those fatal, or implementation details?

4. What defenders of the general epistocratic line would themselves concede counts against them, not just what critics say. And note that Mill's plural-vote idea is the version I'd sign up for, so anything that beats it beats me.

5. Whether the whole thing has been insulated into unfalsifiability. Every failed prediction seems absorbable — the screen was the wrong screen, the electorate was corrupted, the experiment wasn't run properly. Is that a problem here? A normative position might be allowed to be unfalsifiable in a way an empirical one isn't, but then what work is the empirical part doing in my argument, and can I keep it?

One more thing. I got into this by analogy with peer review: science works because claims get screened by people who know the field. Tell me straight whether that analogy is load-bearing for me or whether it buries me, given how slowly and badly science itself settles some questions.

No reading list, no citations, I can find my own books. Argue with me. And if the honest verdict is that part one is wrong but part two survives, or the reverse, say so — I care more about the structure than about winning.
