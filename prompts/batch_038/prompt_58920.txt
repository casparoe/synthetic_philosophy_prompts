I am the readers' editor at a midsized metropolitan daily. Last month we ran a four-part investigation into a county commissioner who steered grant money toward a nonprofit whose paid director turned out to be his brother-in-law; the reporting is documented, and no one inside or outside the paper disputes the underlying facts. The follow-up column was written by our longest-serving columnist, who in the late 1990s lost a job in a city procurement office over an irregularity of the same general family — he was never charged, he acknowledged it publicly at the time, and he has written about it himself twice since. Within a day a reader had written in, and separately one of our own board members remarked at a meeting, that people who live in glass houses shouldn't throw stones. The remark was received as though it settled the matter: several people nodded, the board member proposed that we pull the column and append an editor's note explaining why, and I was the only person in the room who felt something had gone wrong without being able to say what. What I need is the menu. The proverb obviously does not express a single claim, and I suspect that at least four or five quite different propositions are circulating under it. For each candidate reading, please state it precisely rather than impressionistically: which property of the blamer is supposed to do the work (committing the very same wrong, a wrong of comparable seriousness, any wrong at all, the public visibility of the wrong, the blamer's failure to have dealt with it), what normative consequence is then alleged to follow (that the allegation itself is undercut, that the act of blaming is impermissible, that the blamer lacks standing to voice a judgment whose content is nonetheless correct, that the blamer merely invites exposure and is likely to be ignored, that the blamer owes self-blame first, that the blamer must recuse himself from the office of critic), whether the claim is agent-relative or agent-neutral, and whether it is a platitude that nobody could dispute, a substantive thesis that can be defended, or simply false. Then tell me which of the readings, if true, would actually license the board member's two proposed remedies — pulling the column and attaching a note — since I expect most of them license neither.

Two complications should be built into the analysis rather than tacked on. First, the proverb is the secularized cousin of a line in the Gospel of John, where the challenged accusers are told, "Let anyone among you who is without sin be the first to throw a stone at her" — note that the scriptural version specifies who goes first, whereas the proverb drops that and turns what looks like a sequencing instruction into a flat prohibition. In the same passage the one figure who plainly meets the stated condition then declines to condemn at all. I want to know what that does to the readings on offer: whether an ordering reading survives independently of the prohibition reading, and whether the source supports a requirement about who may blame or a requirement about whether to blame in the first place. Second, there is an institutional twist that the board meeting never reached. In 2009 this paper killed a broadly similar story about a different officeholder after an advertiser intervened; I have confirmed it in our own morgue files and we have never disclosed it. So the question of whose glass house is in play is not trivial — the columnist's past is his own, the paper's is corporate — and I want the readings sorted by whether they attach to the individual who voices the judgment, to the institution that publishes it, or to both, including whether an institution can lose and regain standing in the way a person can, and by what means.

A few constraints on the answer. Please be explicit about which readings, if true, would have the consequence that no one is ever entitled to criticize anyone, on the ground that nobody's house is not glass, and how a defender of those readings could block that collapse — because that objection is the first thing our board member will reach for, and I would rather meet it prepared. Say also, for the readings on which standing really is lost, whether the columnist's public acknowledgment and more than two decades of clean conduct restore it, and whether the readings differ in what they predict here, since a reading that cannot tell me anything about this case is not worth bringing to the meeting. My own inclination is that the proverb is a stock tu quoque, irrelevant to whether what we alleged is true; tell me candidly whether any reading rescues it from being a fallacy, and whether the board member might be pressing a defensible point about something other than truth — proportion, role, timing, or the credibility of the paper as a critic. What I want back is a numbered list I can read aloud: the reading, its precise formulation, its status as platitude, defensible thesis, or falsehood, and one line on what it implies for our column. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no survey of which philosophers have held which view — I am not trying to teach myself the literature, only to stop one sentence from being used as a crowbar.
