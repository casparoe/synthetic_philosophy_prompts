Departmental colloquium in about three weeks and I've talked myself into presenting a view I fully expect to get mangled. Help me put together a Q&A prep sheet — I'm slow on my feet and worse when somebody in the second row starts tapping a pen.

The view, roughly. I want to deny that the thermodynamic arrow needs a cosmological explanation. The standard story in our corner of the field is that irreversibility has to be purchased with a special low-entropy boundary condition near the big bang plus a probability distribution over initial microstates, and that the same package is what licenses our trust in memories, tree rings, photographs, and the rest of it. My claim is that the package does a lot less work than advertised. The asymmetries we actually ever notice — records run one way, we can wiggle the future but not the past, interventions cost less in one temporal direction than the other — are facts about how measuring and storing devices couple to their environments, not facts about a boundary condition on the whole universe. So the arrow is local and record-relative on my account: it's the shadow cast by an asymmetry in the thermodynamic cost of acquiring and holding onto information, and the very same microphysics with a different observer-physics could host differently oriented arrows, or several overlapping ones. If there is a low-entropy boundary condition, fine, it constrains which records are on offer. It doesn't constitute the direction of time and it isn't what makes the second law true.

Three steps, so you know what they'll be taking aim at. First: any account that explains the arrow by a boundary condition owes us an account of why that condition is initial rather than final that doesn't just redescribe the asymmetry it was invoked to explain — and the usual fallback, that it's the condition on the earliest time-slice, already presupposes an oriented temporal ordering. Second: the only evidence anybody has ever had for any arrow is a record, and records are physical systems whose reliability is a matter of the cost and stability of the information stored in them, so whatever arrow we can meaningfully talk about lives inside record-bearing subsystems. Third: therefore the global boundary condition is explanatorily inert with respect to everything we mean by time's having a direction, and it's a live option that a closed subsystem containing no records at all has no arrow — entropy still rises in it, but nothing in it is rising *toward* anything.
