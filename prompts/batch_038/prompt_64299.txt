A reading group on predictive processing has divided over a single point, and I would like your help adjudicating it. Assume for the sake of the discussion the standard apparatus: an agent carries a generative model of its sensory input, perceptual inference consists in minimizing a variational free energy that upper-bounds surprisal, and active inference holds that movement is selected to minimize expected free energy, whose decomposition separates an epistemic component (expected reduction in uncertainty about hidden causes) from a pragmatic component (the divergence between predicted outcomes and the agent's prior preferences over those outcomes).

The disagreement concerns the status of the perception–action distinction inside this framework.

Reading A (the collapse objection): active inference is presented as unifying perception and action because both reduce prediction error, one by revising the model and one by revising the world. But the framework seems to supply no non-arbitrary principle for telling the two apart. Since any sensory trajectory can be rendered unsurprising either by believing that it occurred or by making it occur, the distinction looks like a stipulation bolted on afterwards rather than a consequence of the theory. On this view the so-called dark room problem — an agent that minimizes surprisal should seek out a featureless, entirely predictable environment — is not an isolated embarrassment but a symptom of the missing principle: the theory has no place for the difference between a world that confirms expectations and an agent that has reasons to bring about some confirmations rather than others.

Reading B (the deflationary reply): the objection mistakes a presentational feature of the framework for an explanatory gap. The asymmetry is already explicit in the mathematics. Perceptual inference is conditioned on a fixed action sequence, while policy selection is conditioned on a fixed generative model; the two differ in the direction of the fit and in what is held constant during minimization. Preferences enter as the prior over sensory states, and precision weighting determines which error signals are permitted to drive belief revision and which are instead absorbed by action, so nothing is merely stipulated. The dark room problem dissolves once one notices that the relevant quantity is expected free energy under a model that assigns low prior probability to being in a dark room in the first place.

Please write an explanation that does the following.

1. Reconstruct the collapse objection as precisely as you can. In particular, distinguish the claim that the framework cannot represent the difference between perception and action from the weaker claim that it cannot explain why that difference matters for behavior, and say which of the two claims a defender of Reading A can actually sustain.

2. Identify where in the formalism an asymmetry genuinely does, or does not, survive. Address at least: the conditioning structure of the two minimizations; the role of precision in arbitrating between belief revision and action; and the function of prior preferences in expected free energy. Be explicit about which of these asymmetries are substantive and which are relabelings of the ordinary distinction between prediction and control that any optimal control account would also possess.

3. Assess whether the appeal to prior preferences over sensory states answers the dark room problem or merely restates it. Here it is worth separating at least three charges that tend to be run together in the literature: that minimizing surprisal entails seeking dull environments; that a framework that cannot rule out the dark room has no account of motivation; and that an account on which everything depends on an empirically unconstrained prior cannot fail to fit the data. Say what it would take to solve the problem as opposed to re-describing it, and whether the framework meets that standard.

4. Step back and evaluate the broader charge that the free energy principle is unfalsifiable or vacuous, on the ground that it is an identity claim about any system that persists rather than a contingent hypothesis about brains. Distinguish the case in which a principle is trivially true but earns its keep by licensing specific, checkable models from the case in which it is trivially true and does no such work, and say which description fits the present dispute about perception and action.

Throughout, keep the reasoning tied to the conceptual structure of the framework rather than to its history or to who has said what, and state clearly where you judge Reading A and Reading B to be partly right and partly wrong rather than defaulting to a compromise for its own sake. Do not include a bibliography or a list of recommended readings.
