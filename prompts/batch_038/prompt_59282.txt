Right, so here's a position I've basically talked myself into, and I want it stress-tested properly rather than nodded along with.

It's the hard-line deflationary view about Gödel's incompleteness theorems outside arithmetic: that they say nothing whatsoever about minds, machines, physics, law, theology, or the limits of reason in general, and that every extramathematical appeal to them is either an equivication or a metaphor wearing a theorem's clothes. But I don't hold it vaguely — I hold it via a specific sorting device I use in arguments, and that's the bit I want picked apart. Whenever someone offers me a Gödelian claim, I ask which of three buckets it belongs in:

(a) genuine unprovability inside a fixed, recursively axiomatised theory with a decidable proof-checking procedure;
(b) uncomputability or undecidability in the Turing sense — no algorithm decides the question;
(c) informal talk of a framework being open, inexhaustible, unable to capture all the truths of its own subject matter, no complete account of X possible, and so on.

My claim is that only (a) is a theorem, that the famous Gödelianism about consciousness and AI is really (c) dressed up as (a), and that whenever someone produces a real (b) result and calls it incompleteness, they've quietly changed the subject. On my view, that trichotomy plus the observation that informal disciplines never actually fix a proof-checker is enough to dispose of the whole literature.

So: what would count against this? Give me a ranked map of the defeaters, fatal at the top and merely awkward at the bottom, and say for each whether it attacks the trichotomy itself or just my confidence that everything sorts neatly into it. Please work through at least these candidates, since they're the ones I get pushed on most:

- The result showing that the spectral gap problem for certain families of translation-invariant quantum lattice Hamiltonians is undecidable. I take that to be a (b) result about a physical question, hence no threat at all — but I've also seen it claimed that the same construction yields Hamiltonians whose gapped-or-gapless status is independent of a set theory rather than merely algorithmically undecided. If that's right, does an (a)-flavoured result about a physical system land inside physics, or only inside our formalisation of it, and what would settle that?
- The strengthened finite Ramsey result that is true of the natural numbers yet unprovable in Peano arithmetic. I lean on the idea that incompleteness is an artefact of diagonal trickery and so licenses no general conclusions; does a natural, non-self-referential-looking combinatorial statement being underivable undercut that retort?
- Löb's theorem and the fixed-point machinery people use for agents that reason about their own future reasoning — reflective oracles, logical-induction style approaches, the alleged obstacle to a system safely trusting its own improved successor. Is that an (a) result with actual engineering bite, i.e. a live case of incompleteness constraining something outside pure maths? If so, does my bucket scheme have to admit a fourth category, and would that be a repair or a surrender?
- Paradox constructions that seem to generate contradiction without any single sentence referring to itself. If self-reference isn't doing the work there, does that weaken my claim that the informal uses all depend on a self-reference that was never licensed in the first place?
- The disjunction Gödel himself drew in his Gibbs lecture — either human mathematical capacity outruns any finite machine, or there are number-theoretic problems unsolvable in principle rather than merely unproved so far. Can a deflationist of my kind actually stay neutral there, or does my view owe an account of why the second horn is the live one?
- Self-amendment paradoxes in legal theory, and Chaitin-style algorithmic-randomness results about the incompressibility of specific mathematical facts. Are these even in play, or am I right to wave them off?

First, tell me which of these a committed defender of my position would themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to which ones only critics insist on — I suspect the honest answer is that my view concedes more than I've been letting on. Second, and this is the part that worries me: has the trichotomy insulated my position into unfalsifiability? Its obvious function is to guarantee that every proposed counterexample gets sorted into (b) or (c), where by construction it can't hurt me. If that's what it's doing, is it a gerrymander, and would it be better described as a stipulation about the word incompleteness than as a thesis about the world? Please address whether that's a genuine problem here or whether purely negative, debunking theses are entitled to a kind of immunity that positive ones aren't — and what it would concretely look like for an informal discipline to be blocked by incompleteness as such, rather than by the limits of whichever formalisation someone happened to pick. I'd find that last thing most decisive, honestly: a single worked example where practitioners are stopped in their reasoning by the theorem itself, not by a formalism they chose.
