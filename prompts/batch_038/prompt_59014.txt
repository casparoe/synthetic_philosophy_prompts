I do intake review at a city agency — we look at maybe fifteen proposed rules and program changes a month before they go to council, anything from a character clause in a vendor licence to a shelter policy where a caseworker can relocate someone at her discretion. Lately the pushback we get is a particular kind: someone says the rule is fine, the officials running it are decent, nobody's actually been harmed, so what's the complaint. I want a screening procedure I can hand to a reviewer who's never read any political theory, built out of the republican idea of liberty as non-domination rather than non-interference — the bits about arbitrary interference, about whether a power has to track the interests of the person it's applied to, and about freedom meaning you can meet the other person's gaze without deferring or being afraid. Twenty minutes a proposal, one reviewer, no lawyers in the room until sign-off, and the output has to be a verdict plus flags, not an essay.

Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the points where it should stop and escalate to counsel instead of pretending to decide. It especially has to handle three things we keep hitting, which I've started calling sleeping clauses: a discretionary power that's never actually been used, contest rights that exist on paper but cost enough time or exposure that nobody uses them, and constraints the affected people voted for themselves.

Then run it on two live ones. First: a hardship waiver in a business licence where the deciding officer has no obligation to give reasons and the waiver is revocable at will. Second: a lottery for subsidised units where nobody has any say but also nobody gets singled out. Take a position on which is worse and defend it — one of those results will annoy me and I'd rather you say that plainly than balance it out. And be straight about where the procedure will misfire, and which distinctions I'm losing by compressing it into something a non-specialist can run.
