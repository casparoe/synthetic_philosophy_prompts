I sit on the Clinical Decision Support Oversight Committee of a regional nonprofit health system — six hospitals and about forty outpatient clinics, roughly a quarter of our patients Black, most of the rest white, with a growing Somali and Hmong population that the existing race field does not capture at all. In August we are scheduled to deploy a vendor's readmission-risk model that ranks discharged patients for enrollment in a nurse-navigation program with hard capacity limits: about four hundred slots a month, so the model is effectively a rationing device. It uses some sixty variables, and one of them is self-reported race. Here is the complication that has deadlocked us. In validation on our own data, including the race indicator improves discrimination only marginally overall, but it materially narrows the gap in false-negative rates between Black and white patients — that is, dropping race makes the allocation worse, not better, for exactly the group our Office of Health Equity exists to protect. One of our biostatisticians has proposed a third path: remove race and substitute a neighborhood deprivation index plus two genetic ancestry principal components drawn from a biobank subsample, on the grounds that this replaces a social artifact with the underlying variables that were doing the causal work in the first place. The committee is split three ways, several of our nephrologists believe the question was already settled when the race coefficient was dropped from the kidney-function equations a few years back, and I have been asked to circulate the written opinion that will frame Thursday's vote.

What I have come to think, after a philosophy of science course I took as an undergraduate and a long-running argument with a friend in our sociology department, is that the committee is not actually disagreeing about accuracy or about equity trade-offs, though that is how the dispute keeps presenting itself. We are disagreeing about what kind of thing race is, and therefore about what it means to enter it into an equation that will decide who gets a nurse. One camp holds that race is socially constructed but perfectly real — that it has genuine, measurable downstream effects through differential treatment, exposure, wealth, stress, and trust in institutions — and that a model aimed at correcting for those effects is entitled to name the variable that tracks them. Another camp holds that the reality of the category is beside the point: encoding it in a clinical algorithm converts a historical and social artifact into something that looks like a biological parameter, and teaches both clinicians and patients to read biology back into it. And the ancestry-principal-components proposal claims to be the metaphysically honest middle route, though I suspect it may simply smuggle in the naturalized version of race under a more scientific-sounding label, while the deprivation index may be no less a proxy than the thing it was meant to replace. I want the memo to force the committee to see that the three options are three commitments about what the variable means, and that if we choose, we should choose on those grounds rather than by accident.

Please draft the memo — call it nine hundred to twelve hundred words, addressed to the committee, in a register a busy hospitalist will tolerate on a phone. It should open with a bottom-line recommendation (one of the three options, or a conditional variant if you can specify the conditions crisply), then give the reasoning, then a section that engages the strongest case for the option you reject; I want the ancestry-principal-components proposal in particular treated seriously rather than waved away, because it is the one that will sound most rigorous to the physicians on the committee and it deserves a real answer. Include a concrete implementation and review schedule: what gets monitored after deployment, which metric moving in which direction triggers re-review, and who signs off. Finally, because our patient portal will carry an explanation of the model, close with two or three sentences of plain-language disclosure that a patient could read without being misled — this is where I expect the metaphysics to become unavoidable, since almost any honest sentence about why race is or is not in the model commits us to a claim about what race is. Two housekeeping constraints: no footnotes, no bibliography, and no named academic authorities, since none of us will look them up and a physician audience reads citation as hedging; and one colleague keeps saying we should just let the data decide, so I would like the memo to explain briefly, and without condescension, why that phrase cannot settle a question about which variables belong in the model and what they are supposed to mean.
