I'm assembling a sorting instrument for a mixed group of philosophy students and policy people who keep talking past each other on whether misleading-by-literal-truth is morally on a par with lying. Write a decision tree of nine to eleven branch nodes, each node a concrete case the respondent can answer without knowing any of the labels — no node may ask whether a lie requires an intention to deceive, or anything else phrased in the vocabulary of the positions. Please use at least these case types: a literally true answer that the speaker knows will be heard as answering a different question; a false statement the speaker believes true, which the hearer disbelieves anyway; deception accomplished by silence rather than by speech; a statement whose literal content is true but whose presupposition is false; and one in which the same selective-disclosure move is made by a product label or an AI assistant rather than by a person, so the tree has to say whether its categories survive the move from individual speakers to institutions.

The leaves should be positions on two things at once — what makes an utterance a lie, and whether the lying/misleading distinction is one of kind, of degree, or of nothing at all. Name each leaf where a name exists and mark it "unnamed" where nobody defends it. Follow the tree with a compact key mapping sequences of answers to leaves, then list every pair of leaves your nodes cannot actually separate, and note which cells of the answer space come out empty, with a one-line diagnosis of whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Begin with the tree itself, with no preface or framing. Close with a short postscript giving your confidence that respondents will read each node the way you intend, and singling out the one branch you are least sure is splitting anything real.
