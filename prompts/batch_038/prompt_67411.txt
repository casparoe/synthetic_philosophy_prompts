**Draft: §4 — Are interventions counterfactuals in disguise?**

**¶1.** On the Lewisian picture, one event causes another when the second counterfactually depends on the first, or when a chain of stepwise dependencies connects them. The counterfactuals are read off a comparative similarity ordering over possible worlds that keeps the actual laws intact and tolerates only small, localized departures from them where those are needed to make the antecedent true. Causation, so understood, is a relation among events in the world, settled entirely by how the world is; no one's interests, questions, or capacities enter into it. In his later work Lewis recast the analysis in terms of influence rather than bare dependence, partly so that transitivity could be retained in cases where a preempted backup would have produced the effect anyway.

**¶2.** On the interventionist picture, X is a cause of Y when some possible intervention on X would change Y, or the probability of Y. An intervention here is not something anyone could necessarily perform: it is an exogenous process that sets X to a value, taking X off whatever pathway normally fixes it, that does not affect Y except through X, and that is not otherwise correlated with Y. What matters is invariance — the stability of the X–Y relationship across a range of changes in background conditions and in other variables. On this picture causal claims are answers to questions, and a question comes with a variable set; a claim that is invariant with respect to one set of variables may fail to be invariant with respect to another.

**¶3.** The literature then spends a great deal of energy on a scoreboard: which account handles early preemption, which handles double prevention, which can say anything coherent about omissions, which must give up transitivity and which must keep it at the cost of unpalatable verdicts. I want to suggest that this scoreboard is close to beside the point. The two accounts are not offering rival answers to the same question. Lewis's is a theory of singular causation among particular events. Woodward's is a theory of when a generalization licenses a certain kind of reasoning about what would happen if something were varied, and it reaches token claims only by adding machinery — in particular, by holding fixed every variable that does not lie on a directed path between the two.

**¶4.** Consider how much the two accounts share. Both allow substitutions that the actual laws forbid: Lewis by permitting small miracles in the evaluation of counterfactuals, the interventionist by refusing to require that an intervention be physically possible. Both allow that something can be a cause of an outcome even when, in the actual sequence, changing it would have changed nothing: Lewis through chains of dependence and through influence that is exercised along routes the effect does not ultimately depend on, the interventionist through the distinction between a contributing cause and a total cause, only the second of which is transitive. Where the counterfactuals are not in dispute, the verdicts coincide. Where they are in dispute, the disagreement is about the similarity ordering, which neither account derives from its own machinery.

**¶5.** The real disagreement concerns relativity, and here the interventionist is usually cast as the relativist. Add altitude to the variable set alongside temperature and boiling, and a generalization that looked invariant no longer is; the causal claim is thereby only as good as the choice of variables. Lewis, by contrast, gets claims that are true simpliciter. But this contrast is overstated. The similarity ordering performs a function that is structurally identical to a choice of what to hold fixed: to say that a difference between worlds is small and localized rather than large and widespread is to rank which features of the world are negotiable and which are not. Lewis's account is relative to a variable set too; the difference is that the set has been chosen for the speaker by the semantics of the counterfactual, rather than by the speaker's question.

**¶6.** A second apparent disagreement concerns reductiveness. Because interventions are themselves characterized in causal terms, the interventionist account does not reduce causation to anything non-causal, and critics have pressed this as a failure. But the counterfactual account is not causally innocent either. To decide which worlds are closest in a case of preemption or of symmetric overdetermination, one must already know a good deal about which processes are productive of which outcomes. If both accounts lean on causal notions they cannot discharge, the circularity objection stops being a way of ranking them.

**¶7.** This raises a difficulty for my own framing. The interventionist's route to token claims proceeds by holding fixed every variable off the relevant path, which is exactly the operation that the counterfactual account performs when it fixes background conditions and asks what would have happened. Likewise, the contributing-cause/total-cause distinction is deployed precisely where Lewis invokes transitivity, and for the same reason: to keep track of which dependencies survive when other routes are blocked. Perhaps, then, the difference is not between token and type causation at all, but between two different regimentations of the same body of dependency facts — one organized around a similarity metric, the other around a directed graph.

**¶8.** If that is right, the interesting question is not which account handles the difficult cases but what causal discourse is doing: tracking a relation that obtains in the world independently of us, or answering a practical question about what to vary. I am inclined to say both, at different levels of description, which makes the two accounts complementary rather than rival. I worry that this conclusion is too conciliatory to be worth arguing for, and that a referee will reasonably ask what work the distinction between the levels is doing.
