Braudel's split of historical time into deep structures, medium-length conjunctures, and short events makes something close to a testable claim about 1789, and I want you to price it. Here's the counterfactual. Hold the structures fixed: same royal debt from the American war, same seigneurial dues and tax privilege, same demographic pressure, same large class of legally trained men locked out of office. Hold the conjuncture fixed as well: the July 1788 hailstorm and the failed harvest still happen, bread prices still spike. Now excise only the events — no Réveillon affair in the spring, Necker isn't sacked in July, the Estates-General opens in some unremarkable fashion and the Tennis Court Oath never happens.

Your credence that France still gets something we'd happily call a revolution — a decisive break in who holds coercive power and how land and dues are held — before 1800. I want the decomposition, not the headline: how much turns on whether the fiscal-constitutional crisis was overdetermined enough to force a confrontation regardless of timing, how much on whether "revolution" is even the right label for what replaces it (reform from above, a ministerial coup, a long fiscal unraveling), how much on whether Braudel's thesis here is a substantive empirical claim rather than a methodological preference for weighting slow variables heavily, plus any other terms you think belong. A number and a line of justification each, then how they combine, then which term the total is most sensitive to. For what it's worth I sit at 0.7 and a colleague of mine sits at 0.3, and I'd like to know which of your terms the two of us are most likely split on, and what sort of thing would actually move me down rather than just make me feel clever.
