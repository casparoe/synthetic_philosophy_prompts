I work in public-sector integrity — desk reviews and field visits to municipal licensing and works offices, in two quite different countries — and I am drafting an internal methods paper about a pattern my colleagues and I can only describe with hand-waving phrases like "the office has gone relational" or "it runs on who you know now." I have been unable to find a term in the political science or the ethics-of-corruption literature that fits it, and the terms I do know all seem to be adjacent without being it, so I would like to know whether an established concept exists, and if not, how the thing should be carved.

Two typical instances. A business-licensing clerk works under a written rule providing that applications are handled in order of receipt, subject to an explicit discretionary clause permitting expedited handling in documented hardship or public-interest cases. Over five years she invokes the clause several hundred times; each invocation is properly documented, the hardship is genuine in each case, and no one could identify a rule she broke. But nearly every expedited file belongs to someone inside her personal network — former classmates, relatives of the men who carried her father's coffin, shopkeepers who have lent her brother money — and the cumulative effect on the queue is that a person with no connection to her can no longer realistically expect a licence. She takes no cash and her own material position does not change; what she accumulates is a stock of owed favours, which she then spends on her family. The same shape appears in a district works department: an engineer reprioritises road resurfacing under a clause permitting him to do so on safety grounds, every reprioritisation is defensible in isolation and no safety rationale in his memos is ever successfully challenged, yet after three years the map of resurfaced roads is legible as a map of his obligations.

Three features seem to defeat the labels I have run across, and I want you to take them seriously rather than wave them away. First, nothing unlawful occurs at the act level and nothing lawful occurs at the aggregate level, so definitions built on rule-breaking and definitions built on lawfulness both misfire. Second, the payoff is relational rather than pecuniary or status-conferring: an unspent balance of obligations, which is reinvested rather than consumed, and I do not know whether that counts as private gain at all. Third, there is no discrete decision to be partial — the partiality is assembled from individually legitimate exceptions, so no moment exists at which anyone chose to run a particularist office. Given that, please tell me: (1) whether an established concept covers this, and if one comes close, where exactly the fit breaks; (2) a ranked list of the three nearest concepts — clientelism, patronage, nepotism, state capture, conflict of interest, "institutional" or "legal" corruption, and the norm-erosion idea from the democratic-backsliding literature are the candidates I have, but you may propose others — and for each, whether it requires money, requires a discrete act rather than an aggregate, or requires an explicit bargain, since my cases seem to lack all three; (3) if you judge that nothing covers it, a coined term with a definition, the conditions an instance must satisfy, and two or three boundary cases that resemble it but fall outside — for example a clerk who expedites for co-ethnics to whom she owes nothing personally, a clerk who takes a single cash payment for a single expediting, or a clerk whose network is genuinely open to anyone who will later return the favour; (4) an observable signature, something a reviewer with the case files and the queue data could measure that would distinguish this office from one that is merely generous with its discretionary clause and impartial about whom it is generous to; and (5) whether your verdict changes depending on whether corruption is understood as deviation from the formal duties of a public office for private-regarding gain, or as deviation from the norms actually operative in that office — because in my cases the operative norms have drifted, and the two definitions then disagree, which is also why I want to know whether any term for this can be stated without silently taking the audit mission's side against the neighbourhood's. Please finish with one line beginning "Verdict:" containing either the established term you think covers this or your coined term, as a short phrase and nothing else.
