I work on the perception stack for a head-mounted display that overlays moving graphics on the real world, and my team has just spent two months arguing about something that turned out not to be an engineering question. To keep a virtual object glued to a real one that is moving, you either extrapolate — render where it probably is right now, and eat the error when the prediction goes wrong — or you hold each frame in a buffer for a hundred milliseconds or so, wait until you can see where the object actually went, and then render the whole trajectory correctly at the price of latency. We chose the buffer. Reading up on why that felt like the more honest option, I ran into color phi: two dots flashed at different places and in different colors are seen as a single dot that moves and changes color somewhere in the middle of its path, where the color it is seen to change into is not yet available to anyone's visual system at the moment it is apparently seen. Dennett and Kinsbourne's labels for the two ways out — Orwellian, where the experience really was of the first color all the way across and the record is altered afterwards, and Stalinesque, where nothing conscious is settled until the second flash arrives and the whole sequence is then staged with the change interpolated into the middle — map exactly onto extrapolate-versus-buffer, and our buffer is a Stalinesque device. When I put that in a draft chapter, my advisor's response was that I cannot cite the brain's solution as a vindication of ours until I can say what would count as an adequate account of the temporal structure of experience in the first place. That is what I want from you.

So: write the specification rather than the survey. Lay out the requirements any theory of when and how we are conscious of change and succession must meet, with the case or consideration motivating each. I want the phenomena covered to include color phi itself, and with it the control question of what it is like for the subject between the first flash and the second, in the two-dot condition as against a condition in which only the first dot is ever shown, and whether there is any fact of the matter; the cutaneous rabbit, where taps at the wrist and the elbow are felt as hops along the intervening skin, an illusion that collapses once the separation exceeds roughly three tenths of a second, which an account has to explain as the closing of some particular window rather than wave away; the old cortical-stimulation result in which a train of pulses has to run for something like half a second before any sensation is reported at all, yet the sensation is dated by the subject to the first pulse; the family of effects in which a stationary marker flashed in alignment with a moving object appears to sit behind it; and the utterly mundane case of hearing one note give way to the next, which sets the outer bound on whatever interval the theory says experience spans. Then the distinctions it may not collapse: the timing of an experience versus the timing of its content; what it is like at a moment versus what one is disposed to say a moment later; experiencing versus remembering experiencing; a content being determinate versus a subject being able to discriminate it. Say explicitly whether "the subject is simply mistaken about when the change seemed to occur" is a permissible move or a way of evading the phenomenon, since I have seen it used both ways. Add the consistency constraints: no backward causation, neural conduction delays, the fact that different attributes of one and the same event are processed at different speeds, and the fact that people can be reliably wrong about their own experience. Add the requirement to explain why the illusion is undetectable from the inside by the person having it. And add the requirement I care about most, which is to say what, if anything, would make the difference between the Orwellian and the Stalinesque account observable rather than a redescription of the same process in two vocabularies. For each requirement, mark whether you take it to be genuinely non-negotiable or a preference smuggled in by one camp — I suspect several of those I have listed are the latter — and tell me which single requirement, if pressed hard, eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive views. Above all: if some subset of these requirements cannot be satisfied jointly, identify that subset and explain why. That would be a result I could actually use, because it would mean the extrapolate-versus-buffer choice is not settled by getting the theory right.

One further thing, because it is the case I care about and I have not seen it described anywhere, though I may simply not have looked in the right places. A colleague and I sketched a self-triggered color phi. The subject chooses the color and the position of the second dot before the trial begins, by which finger rests on which key, and then releases the key to start the trial; the first dot appears immediately and the second appears after a fixed short interval, so that throughout the interval the subject already knows everything the second dot is going to tell them. On one trial in five the apparatus betrays the choice and shows the other color. If the mid-flight change is generated only when the discriminating information arrives, foreknowledge should make no difference, and the betrayal trials should be indistinguishable from ordinary ones until the second dot actually lands. If the change is interpolated whenever the relevant content is available, foreknowledge should let the change be experienced during the interval, and the subject should be able to time a response to it, while the betrayal trials should yield something like a change followed by a correction of the change. Tell me whether your list of requirements licenses a prediction here, which of the two hypotheses the design really separates, whether any measure of the effect is admissible given that reaction times carry motor latencies of their own, and whether a theory on which the visual system is a continuous estimator — one that keeps publishing a best guess about the trajectory and revises it as late evidence arrives, the way a tracking filter does — counts as a genuine third option or collapses into one of the two. When you come to the existing contenders, keep it brief: a paragraph at the end on how the retroactive view, the buffered view, the view that there is no single determinate stream to be timed at all, and the estimator view fare against your list. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and if you want to enumerate the requirements, do it in running sentences. And no citations, no bibliography, no reading list — I cannot check them, and I would much rather have your reasoning than guesses about where it came from.
