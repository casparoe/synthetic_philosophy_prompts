I am in the second term of a graduate seminar on philosophy of language and computation, and our unit on machine translation has produced the most unproductive month of my academic life. The assigned texts were Douglas Hofstadter's 2018 essay in The Atlantic on the shallowness of Google Translate, his 1997 book Le Ton beau de Marot on translation as an art, and selections describing the Copycat and Fluid Concepts research program, which is itself an attempt to model the flexible, context-driven analogical process he says genuine translation requires.

Two camps formed in the room and neither has budged. The first holds that the objection has been settled empirically: nearly every failure Hofstadter exhibited — pronoun and gender resolution, ambiguous constructions, register — has been largely repaired by larger models trained on vastly more data; aggregate human quality judgments now place machine output near or above professional human translation for many language pairs and genres; and any surviving appeal to real understanding is an unfalsifiable retreat to a standard no system could ever satisfy. The second holds that this misses the point: the claim was never about error rates but about kind — a system with no model of the situation the text describes, no purposes of its own, and no life out of which its words issue cannot mean anything by them; BLEU-style metrics measure n-gram overlap and are therefore structurally incapable of registering the difference; and the fact that hand-picked adversarial passages are now handled shows only that the surface statistics have grown richer, not that a situation-model stands behind them. A third student says both are confused on Quinean grounds: if translation is underdetermined even for human speakers, there is no determinate fact about which internal structure a competent translator possesses, so the dispute has no subject matter.

My working hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than endorsed, is that at least three disagreements are wearing one name here: a verbal one about what understanding picks out, a dispositional-functional sense versus an intentional or experiential one; a genuine but unformulated empirical one about whether translation competence can be fully accounted for by surface-form statistics, which could in principle be probed by building test sets in which the correct rendering depends on world knowledge for which the source text supplies no lexical cue; and a disagreement about which data count as evidence at all, one side treating curated failure cases as probative, the other treating corpus-level aggregates as the only admissible measure. I also suspect something less tidy. Hofstadter says plainly that the prospect of machines replacing human translators would shake his regard for the human mind, and I wonder how much of the impasse consists in the two sides defending different kinds of thing — a hypothesis in one case, a self-understanding in the other — so that benchmark results are received not as counter-evidence but as an assault.

So I want a diagnosis of the structure of this dispute rather than a verdict in it. Which of my three readings, or the Quinean deflation, or something I have missed, does the most explanatory work, and what in what each side actually says supports that? In particular: does Hofstadter's own computational program undercut the way his defenders frame him, since he evidently thinks meaning is computable and objects only to which architecture computes it? And has the object of the dispute shifted underneath both camps, such that current models resemble what he wanted more than the phrase-table systems he was criticizing? Finally, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a stipulated definition, a discriminating experiment or formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and explain in that case whether the impasse is stable because the question is hard or because it is ill-posed, and what would dissolve it as against resolve it.
