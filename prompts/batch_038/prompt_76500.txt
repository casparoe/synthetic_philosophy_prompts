I'm auditing a grad seminar on the sociology of morality and there's an argument I keep losing. The class is basically all agreed that the honor/dignity/victimhood typology (the one Campbell and Manning run with, building on the older culture-of-honor research about the U.S. South) is a decent descriptive tool, and then they say the question "which of the three is morally superior?" is confused, because these are types of social control that fall out of material conditions — how dense the population is, whether third-party institutions exist, how much your livelihood rides on your reputation — not moral outlooks anyone chose. So you can't rank them any more than you can rank agriculture above pastoralism. My roommate, who's in the same seminar, adds that honor is obviously the worst of the three because it licenses violence over slights and it's built on exclusion, whereas dignity at least takes persons to be autonomous equals. My only comeback so far has been that honor makes promises credible in a way no committee ever has, and that's not nothing. I want a better argument than that, or I want to be told I'm wrong.

Here's the case that won't leave me alone. Imagine a mid-sized open source project — a few hundred regular contributors, nearly all strangers, no employer or university in common. For years it ran on what I'd call honor. Standing came from keeping your word in public: you said you'd take a patch, you took it. Reviews happened fast partly because nobody wanted to be known as the person who sat on someone else's work, and the sanctions were informal but real — flake twice and people quietly stopped assigning you anything. Then a governance overhaul brought in a written code of conduct, a small elected moderation committee, an anonymous reporting channel, and an explicit norm that disputes get handled privately and procedurally rather than in public threads. Call it dignity machinery. Four years on: the review backlog is worse than it ever was, contributors describe the process as slow and bloodless, and the loudest conflicts in the project are no longer about who flaked but about who was harmed by whom and whether the committee took the harm seriously — with people's standing visibly tracking how much moral credit they can claim as victims, or as the loudest defenders of victims. In the post-mortem threads everyone agrees the new system is fairer and that the project works worse.

Write an essay that uses this case to answer the seminar's dismissal: is any of the three cultures morally superior to the others, and if so which, on what grounds, and whats the unit of comparison — the whole social-control package, the norms it produces, the character it forms in its members, the outcomes for the worst-off, or something else entirely? Take the strongest version of the "the question is confused" position seriously rather than strawmanning it; if you end up agreeing with it, that's an acceptable thesis as long as you actually argue for it. I also want you to say whether my roommate's asymmetry survives the case study — is the damage in the story attributable to dignity or to victimhood, and does it even make sense to pry them apart like that, or are they not really seperate things? Please don't end on a both-sides shrug or an "it depends what you mean by superior" hedge. Conditional verdicts are fine, e.g. superior along X but not along Y, as long as you commit to the conditions and defend them.
