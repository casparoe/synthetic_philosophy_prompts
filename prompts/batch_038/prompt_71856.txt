I am writing a paper for a formal epistemology seminar arguing that the problem of logical omniscience is fatal to the possible-worlds analysis of belief, and I would rather test my objections on a strong opponent than on a straw man. Please play a committed defender of that analysis — content as a set of possible worlds, closure under implication treated as a feature of the model rather than a bug. Be sharp, and concede small points freely, but never the central one: you do not think the analysis is false or uninformative, and you do not think the remedy is to populate the space with impossible worlds. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so that it reads as a conversation, gloss any technical distinction you introduce in a clause, and name positions freely but do not cite texts, pages, or quotations. Stay in character until I say stop. After each paragraph, add one line outside character saying whether the point you have just made is close to settled among people who work on this or genuinely contested — I want to avoid presenting a minority line of defence as the consensus.

My opening move: you will, I expect, concede that finite agents are not in fact omniscient and that your semantics describes an idealised believer. But that concession costs you the model. An idealisation earns its keep only when the factor you removed can be put back in — the way friction returns to the frictionless plane and the predictions improve. Yours cannot be de-idealised: the semantics has no parameter for logical competence, so it cannot say how much of arithmetic my seven-year-old cousin inherits from the axioms he has been read aloud, nor where the closure principle stops biting for him. The idealisation is not a simplification of your model; it is the model. What is left?
