For seven years I have chaired my university's standing committee on naming and commemoration, and I have become persuaded that what we decide, most of the time, is our own mood. The committee is nine: an archivist, a historian of the American South, the dean of the law school, two faculty members elected at large, an undergraduate delegate, an alumni trustee, a representative of the municipal historical society, and me. We meet monthly for two hours. Every recommendation is written up in roughly eight hundred words for a public report the provost signs; general counsel reviews anything touching a restricted gift, a deed, or human remains; and no member other than myself has any training in ethics. In a typical year we receive six to ten petitions — rename a building, retire a symbol, discontinue a ceremony, open a closed collection, erect something new — and although the petitions recycle the same handful of arguments, we cannot say why one of those arguments prevails in March and fails in November. A student newspaper has now charted the inconsistency for us.

The literature we would need is not merely abstract but internally divided. Margalit argues in *The Ethics of Memory* that the duty to remember belongs to ethics rather than to morality: it is owed to those with whom we share a past, in virtue of the thick relations that constitute us, rather than to humanity at large. David Rieff argues nearly the opposite in *In Praise of Forgetting* (2016): that institutionalized historical memory is frequently an engine of present hostility, that forgetting can be a genuine public good, and that the standing presumption in favour of remembering is a modern sentiment rather than a moral truth. Reviewers pressed Rieff on precisely the point my committee needs — he defends forgetting without supplying criteria for telling when it is preferable. Nietzsche's three modes of historical writing, monumental, antiquarian, and critical, offers a diagnostic that could be built in, though I fear it would harden into a three-box ideology in the hands of nine non-specialists.

Over four years we have arrived unaided at a distinction that does most of the actual work in our deliberations, and I want the procedure to carry it explicitly. The state of a record and the state of a memory come apart. A collection held in a climate-controlled vault, described in a single sentence of catalogue, consulted four times in twenty-five years, is functionally forgotten whatever our intentions in keeping it. Conversely, a monument removed, photographed, crated, and reinstalled in a museum behind a panel explaining why it was removed is functionally remembered far more intensively than it was while it was ignored on a lawn. Petitions to us almost always argue about what a commemoration asserts; the disputes that divide the committee are about what an audience will take from the outcome. I want the procedure to score options by expected uptake rather than by the petitioner's intent, while conceding plainly that uptake is a prediction about other minds and not a finding of fact, and is therefore the place where our deliberations are likeliest to be judgment wearing procedure's clothes. There is also a third disposition that no petitioner ever proposes: letting a commemoration lapse by withdrawing attention from it while leaving it standing. I want that treated as a policy with consequences rather than as the absence of one.

What I want from you is a decision procedure this committee can run inside a two-hour meeting, on a petition, without ethical training. Specifically: the questions in the order they should be asked; what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and what counts as evading it; the points at which the procedure stops with a refusal to act at all; the points at which it hands the question back to the committee as one of value rather than fact, and how the written recommendation should signal that it has done so; which of the distinctions from Margalit, Rieff, and Nietzsche must survive translation and which may be dropped, with reasons; how the procedure fixes the scope of the "we" to whom a duty of memory could be owed, given that our petitioners routinely disagree about whether the relevant community includes the descendants of a commemorated person's victims; how the eight-hundred-word report should be composed when some operative reasons — a deed, a trustee's threatened resignation, an estimate of staff hours — cannot be stated publicly without doing damage; and how the procedure should behave when the petition's factual premise is itself contested among historians rather than settled.

Then run it, in full and on the record, through three petitions now before us.

First. Halloran Hall takes its name from the university's second president, who served from 1891 to 1913 and endowed three professorships that still bear it. The graduate student union petitions for renaming. In our own archive his annual reports to the trustees recommend that the university refuse admission to applicants from two named national origins and bar Black students from the residence halls; the reports were published at the time and were no secret. There is no evidence of any personal act of violence. His great-granddaughter sits on the board of trustees and has said privately that she would resign. The name appears on some sixty-one thousand alumni transcripts, and on a restricted gift whose instrument names the hall as the situs of the endowment; counsel advises that removing the name may put the gift at risk. The building houses molecular biology. No one has used the name affectionately in a decade; students call it the Bio annex.

Second. Each October we hold a commemoration for eleven students who died in a laboratory fire in 1923. It began as a student vigil, has run unbroken since, and includes a reading of the eleven names. Two years ago one of our historians established from payroll and coroner's records that the toll was reported as eleven because the university excluded three workers — two recent immigrants and a woman employed by the catering contractor — whose presence in the building violated a rule the university had adopted the previous year. The faculty senate petitions not to correct the list but to discontinue the ceremony altogether, on three grounds: that in its current form it has become an instrument of institutional self-exoneration; that it has been cited in three fundraising appeals as evidence of a tradition of care for students; and that adding three names would be received as settling the question. Descendants of two of the eleven attend annually and say they will attend whether or not the university sponsors the ceremony. The advancement office opposes discontinuation.

Third. In 1998 the family of a longtime campus physician donated his papers, including forty years of case notes on students, on condition that they remain closed until 2048. The notes are known to refer to disciplinary proceedings against two students that were later shown to have been fabricated; one of those students is dead, one is living, and both families petition for early opening. The archivist reports that the collection has been consulted four times since accession, always by the same researcher, and that the catalogue describes it only as personal and professional papers. The library has funds for digitization but not for the redaction review that opening would require, estimated at fourteen hundred staff hours. One of my colleagues proposes instead that we let the 2048 restriction stand and say nothing, on the theory that the collection will be forgotten and the question will become moot; she insists this is not cowardice but the only disposition that leaves every living person's interests intact, and she wants to know whether the procedure can distinguish her proposal from mere inaction.

I expect at least one of these three to come out against the petitioners' framing. If none does, tell me so, and tell me whether the procedure is simply too accommodating to whoever is loudest in the room.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: the classes of petition it will mishandle systematically, what it will get wrong when the evidence is thin, what to do when it deadlocks, and which of its steps are in truth exercises of discretion that I am asking nine non-specialists to perform without noticing. I am not looking for a bibliography, and I do not want a survey of memory studies; I want something we can hold in our heads across a single meeting and that will yield the same verdict in November as it did in March.
