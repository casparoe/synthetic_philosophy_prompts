I am in the second half of a seminar on social epistemology and judgment aggregation, and I have ended up writing my final paper on central banks. What I need help with is the conceptual reconstruction, not the economics: I want to know what theory of collective judgment these institutions are committed to by the way they are built. Please argue from the ideas rather than pointing me at references — I have the course readings and a reading list would not help me here.

The practice, as I understand it. Three major central banks face the same problem — a committee of experts must produce one interest-rate decision out of many individual judgments — and they have solved it in visibly different ways. The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee publishes the vote member by member, names whoever dissented, and, unusually, also publishes each member's own projection for growth and inflation. The Federal Reserve's FOMC publishes the vote with the names of those for and against, but releases its economic projections as an anonymous scatter of dots that cannot be traced back to any individual. The ECB's Governing Council publishes neither: the decision emerges as a single unsigned announcement, individual votes and dissents are never disclosed, and the meeting accounts it now releases summarize the discussion without attributing any view to any person. On top of that, since the euro area grew past a certain size, voting rights on the Council rotate — the governors of the largest economies share a smaller bloc of votes among themselves, the remaining governors share a larger bloc, and the six Executive Board members vote every time. The result is that a given national governor casts a vote at some meetings and sits without one at others. Correct me if I have the rotation mechanics wrong; I am working from memory.

The puzzle I cannot resolve is this. Everything else in the ECB's legal design insists that governors are not national delegates: they are to act for the euro area as a whole and to take instructions from nobody. Yet the rotation allocates votes by the relative size of member economies, which is weighting by stake — the logic of representation, not the logic of a body of individuals jointly tracking the truth about inflation. And unlike a court, whose single unsigned opinion at least expresses one collective judgment on one question by one fixed panel, the Council's electorate changes from meeting to meeting.

So, specifically:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does the ECB's arrangement presuppose that the Council forms beliefs of its own — a genuine group agent whose judgment is something over and above the members' — or is it equally consistent with the deflationary reading on which there is no collective judgment at all, only an output that has been strategically insulated from reconstruction? Which rival views does the design quietly rule out, and which does it merely happen to fit?

2. Where does the doctrinal paradox bite? Committees of this kind decide the rate directly, which is outcome-based aggregation, but then publish a coherent collective analysis that looks exactly like a set of premises. Are those accounts a report of what the Council collectively judged about inflation and the output gap, or a consistent premise-set assembled after the fact — possibly one no member individually holds? If the latter, is the publication doing epistemic work or legitimation work, and does that distinction matter to the theory?

3. Test a hunch of mine. Standard judgment-aggregation results assume a fixed electorate and a fixed agenda, and the premise-versus-outcome contrast is defined relative to a single profile of individual judgments. A rotating Council never presents a single profile, so strictly speaking the familiar results may not apply to it across time at all. Is that right? If the framework does have resources for a changing electorate, what does the extension cost, and does the epistemic case for deferring to a committee — the Condorcet-style argument that its decision is evidence about the state of the economy — survive the loss of anonymity plus the loss of a stable voter set?

4. Finally, tell me what would have to change, both institutionally and philosophically, if the ECB adopted the MPC's transparency, and what the MPC gives up in exchange: does publishing named votes convert deliberation into bargaining over recorded positions, and does that undermine the claim that the committee is tracking anything?

Please be disciplined about separating what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely harmonizes with, and say plainly where the practice is underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license it equally.

End your reply with a single line of the form "Verdict: <label>" where <label> is exactly one of GROUP AGENT, EPISTEMIC AGGREGATION, REPRESENTATIONAL BARGAIN, PURE PROCEDURE, or UNDERDETERMINED, naming the theory of collective judgment the ECB's design most nearly presupposes.
