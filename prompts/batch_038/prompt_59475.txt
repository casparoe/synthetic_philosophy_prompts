I am trying to settle for myself what the cogito actually proves, and the standard formulations have stopped helping, so I have instead written down my verdicts on a set of cases. What I want is the principle those verdicts add up to — specifically, an account that says what the certainty attaches to and what its content is.

My cases and verdicts:

1. Total doubt. While I am busy doubting that there is an external world, a body, or reliable arithmetic, the thought that I exist seems impossible to sustain as a doubt: entertaining it defeats it. Verdict: certain, and certain in a way nothing else I believe is.

2. The impersonal rewrite. The suggestion, going back at least to Lichtenberg, that all I am entitled to is that thinking is occurring rather than that I think — I grant that this looks equally secure and far less committal. But I also feel that if I stop there I have gained no knowledge about myself at all, which is what I wanted from the argument in the first place. I hold these two reactions together uneasily.

3. Someone else's cogito. When a friend reports exactly the same certainty about herself, I am happy to say it is certain for her. Her existence is not thereby certain for me; she may be a figment. And I do not feel that my knowing that I exist has taught me anything whatever about thinkers in general. That makes me suspicious of the familiar reply to the old syllogism objection — the reply that we grasp the general premise, that whatever thinks exists, only by attending to the particular case.

4. The amnesiac. A person who has lost their name, their history, and even the concept of a person, but who is still thinking. Verdict: still right, and just as secure as I am, even though nothing in their condition identifies which thing in the world they are.

5. The machine. A system that emits a first-person affirmation of its own existence on request. Verdict: no certainty attaches here, because nothing has been doubted. Yet when I imagine that same system genuinely attempting to doubt its own existence, my verdict weakens considerably, and I cannot say what changed.

6. Denial. I cannot sincerely think that I do not exist; but this does not feel ruled out the way a contradiction feels ruled out. It feels more like a performance that fails than like a proposition that is false. Verdict: the impossibility is in the doing.

7. Duration. As I understand him, Descartes holds that the certainty lasts only while I attend to the thought, and that recalled conclusions need a further argument about a non-deceiving God before they are safe. My own sense is quite different: the certainty does not strike me as expiring when I stop thinking about it, and my past existence feels as secure to me as my present one.

8. Fission. In the sort of case where I cannot tell whether one thinker continues as two, I still feel certain that I exist — but I cannot tell what that certainty guarantees: that a single persisting substance is present, or only that these thoughts have an owner from the inside.

My tentative principle, which I expect will not survive contact with the list: the certainty attaches to whatever thinking is occurring now, and to nothing that has the thinking. If that were right, cases 4 and 8 would be explained at once — but I see no route from it to any first-person conclusion, and it seems to render case 1 a piece of luck.

So: is there a single account of what the cogito establishes — a self-verifying utterance, an inference, a judgment immune to error through misidentification of the kind Shoemaker and Evans discuss, or something else — that delivers all eight verdicts? State it precisely enough that I can check it against the cases one by one. If it cannot deliver all of them, say which verdict it forces me to drop, which one you would give up first, and why that one rather than another. Then apply the account to three cases I have not listed: a being that has thoughts but no first-person concept at all; a dreamer who entertains the thought of his own existence while dreaming; and the second-person variant, in which you tell me that I exist — is that certain for you, and if so, certain in the same way it is for me? Finally, does the account explain why existence comes out certain while the further claims that I am a thinking substance, and that I am the same thing that doubted yesterday, do not?
