I am writing a novel set in a small northern European welfare state, and I have run into a wall that is not made of plot but of political philosophy. My characters are a couple with a first child, and I keep finding that the leave system I have built around them does a great deal of their thinking for them, in ways I can feel but cannot name. I would like your help naming them.

Here is the system, as I have designed it — closely modelled on real ones:

- The entitlement attaches to the individual worker and to the birth or adoption, not to the household. Each parent receives a personal quota of paid months. A quota cannot be transferred, pooled, or sold; if the second parent does not use his, it lapses. The family simply loses the months. They do not become the mother's.
- The payment is a fixed proportion of the parent's previous earnings, subject to a ceiling, with a low flat rate for the tail of months beyond it. A senior engineer's month at home is therefore worth considerably more in cash than a care assistant's month at home.
- Separately, the same state runs a heavily subsidised, near-universal childcare service from the age of one, described in official documents as part of the child's own entitlement to early education. And it also pays a modest flat monthly allowance to a parent who declines that place and keeps the child at home until three.

I am not asking for a reading list; please give me no citations, no titles, no names of scholars. I want the argument, and I am content for you to coin the vocabulary if mine fails me.

Treat that package the way one might treat a ritual or a scoring rule: as a thing that only makes sense under certain assumptions and that quietly excludes others. What view of the family does the non-transferable individual quota presuppose? A household in which one member forfeits months rather than hand them to the other cannot be a single decision-maker pursuing a single shared interest — but I want the candidate pictures laid out, and to be told which of them the design strictly requires and which it merely happens to fit. Relatedly: if the reserved months are meant to alter what fathers want, the system cannot be indifferent to how those wants were formed in the first place. Tell me what that costs it. Does a state that shapes preferences this openly forfeit any claim to neutrality among ways of life — and can a state that simultaneously pays people to stay home with their children make that claim at all?

Then take the internal strains seriously rather than explaining them away. Because the rate is earnings-related, the state pays more for a month of the engineer's care than for a month of the assistant's, though the care is the same care; and because the lower earner forfeits less by staying home, the transferable months flow toward her — which is precisely what the reserved months exist to interrupt. Meanwhile the cash-for-care allowance pays a parent not to use the service the same state calls the child's right. Are these one policy at war with itself, or several policies with incompatible theories bolted together? I would rather be told that the package is philosophically underdetermined, licensed by several rival theories at once, than handed a tidy reconstruction that is tidy only because you chose the tidying.

Next, two substitutions. If a state accepted the older economic story, in which a household specialises because specialisation is efficient and interference is a deadweight loss, what would its leave policy look like, and what would it refuse to do? And if a state treated the household rather than the individual as the unit of entitlement, letting months be pooled or traded between parents, what would become invisible under that design that is visible under mine?

Finally, the novelist's question: which of these commitments would be felt from inside rather than observed from outside? My character who lets his quota lapse is not, I think, choosing among options; the system has already withdrawn an option and left him a forfeiture. Tell me what such a person experiences — entitlement, shame, the sense that a benefit is really a command — and where the unexamined assumption surfaces as emotion rather than as argument.

Two closing requests. Be explicit throughout about the line between what this design presupposes and what merely sits comfortably with it. And tell me what would change your mind: which empirical fact about how such systems actually behave, or which argument I have not thought of, would move you to a different verdict? Say plainly where you think your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong, and flag any point at which you find yourself importing a feature of a real system that I did not describe.
