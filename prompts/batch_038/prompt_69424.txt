So I'm about halfway through a masters in environmental philosophy and my supervisor has told me, twice now, that my chapter on intervention in nature is floating free of the ground — apparently four positions summarised in a row with no case to break them against isn't a chapter, it's a lecture. Fine by me. The case I've landed on is the Australian rabbit business, and I'd like you to actually work through it with me rather than give me the generic on-the-one-hand-on-the-other-hand treatment.

The facts as I've got them, shout if I've botched any of it: European rabbits arrived with the First Fleet and were then released for sport in Victoria in the 1850s, spread faster than any mammal anywhere on record, chewed through a huge amount of native vegetation and are implicated in various plant and animal declines; the rabbit-proof fence in Western Australia didn't hold, and neither did shooting, poisoning or ripping warrens. So in the early fifties they deliberately released myxoma virus — 1950 I think, though it may have been a bit later. In the first seasons it killed essentially everything it infected, something like 99%; over the following decades the virus attenuated toward intermediate strains killing maybe half to ninety-some percent, and the rabbits evolved partial resistance, so the population clawed a lot of the ground back. Then in the mid-nineties they released a rabbit calicivirus to do the job again. The bit that matters for my purposes: myxomatosis is a wretched way to go — eyelids swell shut so the animal goes blind, discharge, respiratory distress, roughly ten days to two weeks of it — whereas the calicivirus kills much faster, often overnight.

What I want is that sequence analysed through the wild animal suffering / intervention-in-nature debate, running in both directions. First, what does each side get to say about it? The interventionist's stock move is that we already intervene constantly and at enormous scale, so the hands-off position is a disguised preference for the status quo — but does a deliberately released, self-spreading pathogen count as the kind of intervention that move needs, or is it something categorically different? And the laissez-faire side will say the release was driven by woolgrowers' margins and biodiversity targets rather than rabbit welfare, and that it made things worse for the rabbits for fifty-odd years: is that a fair reading, or is it retrofitting a welfarist objection onto a programme nobody framed that way at the time? Second, what does the episode do to the theories? A welfarist would obviously prefer the calicivirus to myxoma — same aim, similar scale, far less suffering per death — so does that suggest the real debate isn't about whether to intervene but about the manner of intervention, and if so, was everyone arguing about the wrong axis? And where do the theories go quiet? Nobody in the fifties was modelling resistance evolution, and the intervention degraded itself over time; that feels like an epistemics problem rather than an ethics one and I can't tell which it is. Then there's a half-baked idea of mine I'd like stress-tested rather than flattered: call it the suffering-swap test, on which an intervention is permissible only if the suffering it removes outweighs the suffering it adds, summed across every affected individual including the knock-on ecological effects, measured against a baseline of never having intervened at all. Run it on myxoma and on the calicivirus and tell me honestly whether it produces a verdict or just relocates the entire disagreement into the baseline and the discounting — and if it collapses at the coevolution step, because the attenuated-strain era was caused by the intervention itself, so the never-intervened world and the intervened world don't even contain the same population of rabbits, then say so plainly and tell me what if anything survives of the test. British English throughout please, and no reading list or references, I've got more than enough of those already.
