I administer a small emergency assistance fund housed at a community foundation: roughly thirty thousand dollars a year, forty or so applications per cycle, and one written eligibility criterion, which states that an applicant must be facing "severe financial hardship." Because that phrase does no work by itself, for the past three years I have ranked applicants by a single number — the monthly shortfall between a household's income and its costs for rent, food, utilities, and medication. Last cycle the money ran out at the fourteenth slot, which meant we funded a household $212 short and turned down a household $205 short. The two applications were indistinguishable in every other respect I could find, and I have not stopped thinking about the letter I sent the second one.

The board has now asked me to propose a written cutoff for the coming year, and I am stuck. Any figure I put down will be arbitrary in exactly the way last cycle's decision was: the applicant one dollar below it will be, so far as I can measure, just as deserving as the applicant one dollar above it, and I will have no way of telling which side of my own line the truly needy stop falling on. One of our intake volunteers, who read some philosophy as an undergraduate, offered what I found an oddly useful distinction. Either there really is a precise point at which hardship ceases to be severe and we simply cannot locate it — in which case the household I turned down either genuinely qualified or genuinely did not, and my ignorance does not change that — or else, in a case like theirs, the question simply has no answer, and my letter asserting that they fell short was not merely unkind but untrue. He insists this makes a difference to what I ought to do next cycle. I cannot see how; to me the two options sound like descriptions of the same predicament.

So I would like your advice on the actual decisions in front of me, and specifically on whether his distinction has any practical consequences or is decoration. First: should I publish a sharp dollar figure, retain the existing wording and adjudicate case by case, or do something else — several colleagues propose that we fund everyone down to some point and then run a lottery among the near-misses, and I cannot tell whether that is more honest or merely a tidier way of hiding the problem. Second: what may I truthfully say in a rejection letter to an applicant who sits in the region where the cases run together, and in particular whether I am permitted to tell them that there is no right answer about their eligibility. Third: was last cycle's choice — funding the household $212 short while refusing the one $205 short — a wrong done to the second household, or an unavoidable artifact of finite money that I should simply own? Please be concrete about which of your recommendations would change if one of the volunteer's two pictures is correct rather than the other, and tell me where you think the matter is in fact settled.
