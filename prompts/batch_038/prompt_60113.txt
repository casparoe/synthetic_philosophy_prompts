I sit on the board of a small groundwater district in an arid valley, and I have been asked to draft both the district's new pumping charter and the memo defending its decision procedure, ahead of a public hearing next month. Before either circulates, I want a consistency check on the package of views the draft rests on. I would rather find the cracks now than have a water lawyer or an economist find them at the hearing.

The case that drove the drafting is real enough that I have stylized it as follows. Two users draw on a single aquifer: A, on the upper terrace, and B, on the lower field near the aquifer's margin. Each season, each user either runs a high-lift pump or stays inside the shallow quota. That yields four seasonal states: z = neither pumps hard; x = A pumps hard and B does not; y = B pumps hard and A does not; w = both pump hard.

The hydrology is asymmetric. A's high-lift pumping raises A's yield but undercuts the foundations of A's own house, which sits directly over the drawdown cone; A judges that structural damage to be larger than the yield gain, and A's drinking well is unaffected by A's own pumping. A's pumping also lowers the water table under B's seeping lower field, which is precisely what B's orchard needs. B's high-lift pumping raises B's yield by less, and pulls the saline front toward the margin: B's own well goes brackish — costly but survivable, since B's house is on the district's piped supply — and the front then migrates up-gradient and fouls A's shallow well, which is A's only drinking source. So, for A, losing the drinking well is worse than the net loss from pumping; for B, the net gain from B's own pumping is positive but smaller than the benefit A's pumping confers on B's orchard.

That gives the two users' complete orderings over the four states:

A: z ≻ x ≻ y ≻ w
B: w ≻ x ≻ y ≻ z

Neither ordering refers to the other user's conduct as such. Both are responding only to water, salt, foundations and yield on their own property.

Here is the package the charter rests on.

1. The charter confers on each user a protected sphere in the sense Sen made familiar: for each user there is at least one pair of states differing only in that user's own pumping, over which the user is decisive — if the user ranks one above the other, the charter's social ranking must rank it above the other. In the case above, A is decisive over {x, z} and over {y, w}; B is decisive over {y, z} and over {w, x}.
2. The charter satisfies the Pareto principle: whenever both users strictly prefer one state to another, the social ranking must agree.
3. The domain is unrestricted. The rule must work for every logically possible pair of orderings over the four states, and the charter's legitimacy may not depend on the users happening to hold benign or public-spirited preferences.
4. Rights may not lapse or be narrowed on account of the effects their exercise has on others. I therefore reject both the harm-triggered override and the domain restriction that discards "meddlesome" profiles: a right that holds only while other people refrain from caring about, or suffering from, your choices is not a right.
5. The profile above is my exhibit. Under 1–3 it generates a cycle in the social ranking, and it does so with nobody being nosy. I take it to be a decisive objection to construing rights as decisiveness over outcome pairs.
6. My own view of what rights are, however, is the game-form view: what the charter protects is each user's set of permissible strategies, the season's state is simply whatever the two choices produce, and no social ranking of states is required at all.
7. Whatever else it does, the charter must yield a single recommended state each season — either a complete and transitive social ranking of the four states, or at least a determinate choice rule that picks one of them.
8. Users may not sell or trade their protected spheres. Paying someone not to exercise a right converts the right into a commodity and destroys the point of conferring it.

I am attached to all eight, and I cannot tell which of them is the load-bearing mistake. Please audit the set rather than lecture me about the paradox; assume I know the standard moves and their names and do not recite them.

Specifically:

- Identify which commitments, individually or in clusters, are genuinely jointly untenable, and which only look that way. Say explicitly which apparent tensions dissolve and why. Rank the genuine ones by severity rather than listing them as equally bad.
- For each genuine tension, state what has to give and what the revision costs me — institutionally, not just logically.
- Where 1 and 6 pull apart, do not merely describe the difference. Work out what each construal delivers in the case above: what the social ranking looks like under 1 and 2 (including whether any state survives as a maximal element), and what season results under 6 if the two users are simply left to choose freely, given their orderings. Then say which of the two failures is worse for a charter, and whether 5 survives that comparison in the form I have stated it.
- Tell me whether the nosiness restriction really is unavailable here, or whether I have described my own profile in a way that hides where such a restriction would bite. Distinguish, if it matters, between a restriction defined by what people care about and one defined by whether their orderings are sensitive to another's action.
- Check whether 8 is in conflict with anything else in the list, given that a mutually desired trade is also a unanimous preference.

Finally, tell me which single revision to the eight would clean up the most, and what I would be left defending at the hearing. No references, no reading list, no bibliography — I want the audit.

End with a machine-readable summary as the very last line, in exactly this form:

Verdict: severe=[...]; apparent=[...]; single_cheapest_revision=N

where severe is a severity-ordered list of the genuinely untenable groups with members joined by & (e.g. 1&2&3&7), apparent is the same format for the tensions that dissolve on inspection, and N is a single number from 1 to 8.
