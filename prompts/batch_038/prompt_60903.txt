So — I write a science-and-ideas column, and in about three weeks I'm doing a 40-minute public lecture in a linguistics department. Not a friendly room, definately not. Roughly who'll be there: two-thirds NLP/MT people, including several grad students who actually ship translation systems and one faculty member who's spent years doing large-scale error analysis on MT output; a handful of literary translators who invited me and who suspect I'm a romantic; and a couple of psycholinguists who study how people actually read sentences in real time. Nobody there needs persuading that translation is hard. Most of them think the "but does the machine understand?" question is either settled or just badly posed, and a few of them will be waiting to say that my whole talk is the Chinese Room dressed up in a French poem.

Here's the thesis I'm actually defending, which is not the Hofstadter one they'll assume:

1. Any text divides into stretches that are locally decodable — where you can produce a correct target-language sentence using only information sitting in the neighbouring words — and stretches that aren't, where the correct choice turns on a fact about the world that the sentence itself never states.
2. Current neural MT is extremely good at the first kind and unreliable at the second, and crucially the unreliability isn't evenly-spread noise. It clusters. The failures land on the same sentences across different systems and different runs.
3. So the interesting claim isn't "machines can't translate." It's: run a text through a strong system, look at where it breaks, and you've produced a map of how much of that text was ever really about anything. MT as an assay for semantic depth rather than as a failed translator.
4. Corollary I'm a bit nervous about, since it's my day job: on this assay, most news copy comes out extremely shallow.

The evidence I'll offer is small and I know it. I took one ~600-word piece of reported narrative about a factory closure — with a couple of pronouns whose referent you can only fix if you know who had the authority to sign, one aspect choice that depends on whether a man mentioned in passing is still alive, and a joke that turns on a place name — and ran it through three strong systems, then read the outputs side by side. Almost all the divergence was in four sentences, and all four were of the unstated-fact kind. The other fifty-odd came out near-identical and fine.
