I work on navigation and sensor fusion for a living: Kalman and extended Kalman filters, interacting multiple models, information filters, fault detection and isolation, and the probability machinery underneath all of that — filtrations, conditional expectation as projection onto a sigma-algebra, the tower property, martingales. I can derive these things, but I have no formal training in philosophy, and everything I know in that direction I have picked up on my own and slowly.

Why this has become personal: earlier this year I was diagnosed with a progressive neurological condition. The medication I will be on for the foreseeable future is well documented to affect mood and, in a meaningful minority of patients, concentration and judgment, and over a longer horizon the disease itself can do the same. So I know two things at once: that in a few years my beliefs about my own prognosis, about how much risk to take with treatments, and about what I want the next decade to look like will be substantially different from what they are now; and that at least part of that difference will be distortion rather than information, produced by a process I currently regard as untrustworthy. Trying to think my way through what to do about that, I ended up in the philosophical literature on van Fraassen's Reflection principle, from his 1984 paper Belief and the Will — roughly, as I understand it, the claim that if I am now certain my credence in some proposition will be x at a later time, then my present credence in it should already be x, and more generally that conditioning on the value of my future credence should leave my credence unchanged — together with the arguments that it breaks down when the future belief is produced by a process I expect to be unreliable, when evidence will have been lost in the meantime, or when the belief in question is self-locating rather than about the world. I cannot tell how much of that debate I could genuinely think about in my own terms, and how much of it changes shape or disappears when I try.

So I would like you to translate it for me, and then tell me what did not survive.

First, the translation. Restate Reflection and the case for and against it in the vocabulary I actually use — estimators, information sets, projections, martingales, bias, model banks, estimands — as faithfully as you can. My own hypothesis is that Reflection is simply the tower property applied to a belief process: it says the sequence of estimates is a martingale with respect to the filtration those estimates themselves generate, which follows automatically if each later estimate is a conditional expectation of a fixed quantity given a finer information set. On that reading, the standard counterexamples are just cases where one of those conditions fails — the future estimator runs on an information set that is not a refinement of mine (forgetting), or the estimand is not a fixed quantity but something indexed to the estimator's own position (self-locating belief), or the future estimator carries a bias I already know about, in which case deferring to it is a design error rather than a temptation (anticipated unreliability). Tell me whether that is a faithful translation or whether I have quietly changed the subject. In particular, point out anywhere my reading turns a contested normative claim into a piece of arithmetic that was never in question, since that would beg the entire issue.

Second, the residue report. Go claim by claim and sort them: what carries over intact, what carries over only if I let you bend one of my terms, and what has no counterpart at all in how I think about estimators. Flag the bending rather than hiding it, and do not smuggle — if your translation works only because you have assumed that beliefs are estimates of fixed quantities produced by a single persisting system with a known likelihood, say so out loud, because that assumption may be exactly what the philosophers are arguing about.

There are four places where I expect residue and where I want you to go in deliberately. One: in my work a martingale is the right shape for an estimate because an estimate is a conditional expectation and a conditional expectation is a projection; nothing in that story corresponds to a requirement, and nothing corresponds to a violation being anyone's fault. When Reflection is said to be rationally required — or, by its critics, not required, so that a rational agent may be obliged to break it — is that content expressible in my vocabulary, or is it precisely the part with no counterpart? Two: the diachronic betting argument for Reflection. I can render "you would accept a package of bets that loses money no matter what" as arbitrage, but arbitrage only bites on someone who is on the hook for the combined payoff, and the bets are accepted at different times by what are in effect different decision-makers. Does the betting argument genuinely constrain estimates, or is it an artefact of a bookie who holds an agent's temporally separated decisions together in a single account? Three: when I expect a sensor to degrade I do not defer to the degraded filter, I run a bank of models and weight them by likelihood. Is there an analogue of that for a person who expects his own future judgment to degrade — a mixture over possible future selves weighted by expected reliability, as a replacement for Reflection — or does Reflection resist being replaced by a mixture in a way that exposes something my framework cannot say, namely that there is one agent who owns all of the estimates, so that the question of whether to trust my own future output is not the same question as how much weight to give another system's output? Four: a puzzle that keeps coming back. In a filter the estimate is a martingale while the covariance shrinks; I cannot expect my later estimate to differ from my current one, and yet I certainly expect it to be better. Does that split between value and spread dissolve the philosophical puzzle about how I can both defer to my future credences and expect them to be more accurate than my present ones, or does it merely hide the puzzle inside machinery I happen to have?

Then give me your verdict, and I do not want it hedged. Is the residue — whatever refuses to be stated as a claim about estimators, information sets, and projections — mostly a limitation of my vocabulary, a sign that I am missing something real about agency and diachronic unity that estimation theory has no room for? Or is it mostly a sign that the Reflection debate is less substantive than it sounds, a dispute manufactured by treating a piece of estimator arithmetic as though it were a law about persons? If it is both, tell me which claims go which way. And tell me whether the answer differs between the abstract case philosophers argue about and my case, where one person is deciding in advance how much authority to give a judgment he expects to become less reliable — including whether something like a written statement of my present views, addressed to my future self and specifying how much weight to give it, is even coherent, or whether in my terms it amounts to nothing more than freezing a parameter and calling it a promise.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Give me your verdict in the opening paragraph so I know where you are going, then the translation and the residue report, then return to the verdict at the end with whatever the translation forced you to revise.
