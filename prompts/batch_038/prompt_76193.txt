I cover science and ideas for a magazine, mostly reported features and profiles, so I spend a lot of hours on tape with researchers. Over the last couple of years I keep hitting the same moment in interviews and I can't find a word for it, so I'm asking here instead of in my notes app.

The moment goes like this. Someone says: "Look, I'll be honest — if a grad student brought me this result I wouldn't believe it. Preprint, underpowered, single lab, take your pick. But I believe ours." And then they just move on to the next question. No spin, no hedging-about-the-hedging. They have conceded out loud that their own evidence doesn't support the belief, they hold the belief anyway, and both of those things are obviously, sincerely theirs at the same time.

It isn't only about their own work. A climate person gave me the equivalent of: three people whose judgement I'd put above mine disagree with me, I can't point to anything I know that they don't, I couldn't tell you what's wrong with their argument — and I haven't budged. He said it the way you'd read out a weather forecast. Not anguished, not defiant.

And one in my own building: an editor keeps a claim in a story after the fact-checker says it cant be confirmed, says out loud "by our own standards I should pull this," and doesn't pull it.

Two things I'm after.

First — is there an established concept for the state itself, believing P while sincerely judging that you shouldn't believe P? I know there's an old philosophy word for doing the thing you judge you shouldn't do, but that's about action, and every person I just described would insist they aren't *doing* anything, they're simply stuck believing. I've also come accross "higher-order evidence," which I think is the evidence-about-your-evidence stuff, but that seems to be about when you ought to revise rather than about the state of not revising. And the psychology words I do know don't land: cognitive dissonance is supposed to come with discomfort and with re-describing the evidence until it fits, self-deception assumes you don't know what you're doing, and belief perseverance is about clinging after the support is gone, which isn't this either — these people aren't clinging to a discredited claim, they're clinging to a verdict on their own confidence in it. So tell me what the nearest established things actually are, what my case does that each of them doesn't cover, and which one fits best. If nothing fits cleanly, coin something and define it properly, because I have to write about it.

Second — the wrinkle I think blows up most of the existing vocabulary. In maybe a third of these cases the person also says, sometimes sheepishly and sometimes with real pride, that not updating has worked out. The researcher who stayed convinced kept at the problem for six more years and eventually produced the correction herself; if she'd updated in year two she'd have left the field entirely. The editor's story held up. So there's a version of the state where the person endorses it *as a strategy*, with the mismatch completely visible to them and to everyone around them, and where it pays. Does any concept cover believing-against-your-own-judgement when it's instrumentally good and nobody is fooled about anything? I keep half-remembering something about self-deception being useful because you end up deceiving other people better, but that can't be it, since these people aren't deceived about a single thing — that's the whole oddness of it.

Please don't just hand me a label. I want the carving. What is the state, what is it not, and where are the edges — at what point does "I believe P but I shouldn't" stop being one person in a strange condition and start being two people in a trenchcoat, or stop being a belief at all and become more like a working habit they narrate in belief-language? And has anyone serious argued that the transparent, productive version is epistemically fine, or does everyone agree it's a defect even in the cases where it wins?
