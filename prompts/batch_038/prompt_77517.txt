Ok, context: I've been reading the anthropics threads since like 2009 — Everett, measure, the whole quantum-suicide business, the "you can't die but you can definitely end up somewhere awful" line of argument. I want a short story out of you, not an essay, but one that actually engages the mechanics instead of using many-worlds as set dressing. Please put on your hard-SF-writer-who-also-does-decision-theory hat for this, in the register of the good Chiang/Egan stuff, dry and procedural.

Here's the premise I've been turning over. Sharpen it however you want but keep the core machinery:

Near future. A company sells "harvesting." You voluntarily undergo an automated lethal-or-not procedure with a genuinely quantum-random survival probability — say 1 in 4096 — and in the branches where you come out the other side you collect a payout that was unpriceable beforehand. The company's whole business model works because the branches where you don't survive never file a claim. Nobody at the firm thinks of this as sinister, which is important; they think of it as underwriting.

The protagonist is a survivorship auditor. Their job is intake on people who claim to have survived a *string* of impossible things, and sort them into (a) fraud, (b) ordinary tail luck, or (c) genuine drift — someone who has slid so far down into the low-measure part of the distribution that the world around them has started to come apart in a particular way.

The tell, and this is the bit I care about most: it isn't random Mandela-effect junk. Real drift shows a *gradient*. Every discrepancy between the claimant's memory and the records runs in the direction of less likely, never more likely. Fraud and confabulation are noise in both directions; drift is a slope you can actually regress. I want a scene where the auditor does that arithmetic, back of the envelope, on a real file, and it should read as boring office procedure, not as a lecture.

The thing to build toward: the auditor starts noticing a gradient in their own file. And the deeper problem, which I want the story to sit inside rather than resolve — a theory like "I'm in the tail" is unfalsifiable from the first-person side in a very specific, non-lazy way. The evidence that would refute it is exactly the evidence you can never have, because in every branch where you'd see it, you're dead. So the auditor has to decide what to do about a bet with no possible disconfirming experience, using their own idiom, and I want them to get something wrong along the way.

Also: one supporting character who holds the rival view and gets a real steelman, not a strawman. Their line is that there's no drift at all, that survivors confabulate a gradient after the fact, that this entire department is survivorship bias with a logo and a budget — which is, I'll admit, the more boring and possibly correct answer. Do not let the protagonist dunk on them. If anything the reader should leave thinking the skeptic might have it.

Craft constraints:
- 1,800–2,600 words. Close third person or second, past tense.
- Zero infodumps. Worldbuilding comes through intake forms, denial letters, break-room small talk, a compliance training module nobody watches.
- Unresolved but earned ending. No dream twist, no simulation reveal, no "it was ordinary hallucination all along."
- Banned words, genuinely: multiverse, parallel universe, timeline (as a noun for a branch), and any sentence containing "reality is stranger than."
- Don't name real physicists or cite real papers inside the story. Keep it hermetic.

Process ask, please don't skip it: before you draft, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming out loud. Sketch three distinct story engines this premise could support — they should differ in what the auditor actually *does* in the last scene — pick one and say why, and then list the two or three hardest conceptual problems with the premise that you'll either have to handle honestly or paper over with style. Then write the story.

Think carefully about the anthropics and the decision theory while you're doing this. If the reasoning is wrong, the story collapses into costume jewelry, and I will know.
