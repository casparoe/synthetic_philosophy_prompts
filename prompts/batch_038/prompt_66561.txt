I am revising a chapter of my dissertation on the semiotics of machine-generated text, and a good deal of my framing came from a popular book about how machines read that I picked up in an airport some years ago. I no longer own the copy, but I transcribed its central passage at the time, and I want to know how much of it to trust before I build anything further on top of it. The passage runs as follows:

"Hofstadter's argument in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* is that meaning does not live in the message but in the decoder. Marks on a page are not meaningful in themselves; what makes something a message is that some mechanism exists, or could exist, for turning it into something else. To sharpen the point he distinguishes three layers. The inner message is the content the sender meant to convey. The outer message is the code — whatever tells you how to convert the marks into that content. The frame message is the notice that there is a message here at all, rather than noise. The layers are nested, and you work through them from the outside in: miss the frame and you see only scratches; miss the outer message and you know that something is there to read but cannot read it. He introduces the distinction by pointing to a hidden acrostic in one of the book's own dialogues, and his illustration of the frame message is a bottle washed up on a beach, where the bottle and the rolled paper inside it announce that reading is called for. The moral he draws is that meaning is always relative to a decoding mechanism, so that one and the same object can be a message for one receiver and pure noise for another, and so that there is no fact of the matter about what a text says apart from what some decoder makes of it. Hofstadter is careful to note that readers do in practice converge on interpretations, but he treats that convergence as a sociological accident of how readers happen to be trained rather than as a constraint on what a text can mean. Which is why, he concludes, the old question of whether a machine that manipulates symbols genuinely understands them is not a deep question at all: if the machine is a decoder, the meaning is there, and no further ingredient — no intention, no consciousness — has to be added."

Three things I want from you, and I want them as verdicts rather than as a survey of interpretive possibilities. First, go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a careful reader of the book would sign off on, which are fair simplifications, and which are misreadings. I am especially suspicious of the slide from the claim that meaning is not inherent in the message to the claim that there is no fact of the matter about what a text says; my hunch is that the book's talk of isomorphisms is doing work at exactly that step and that this passage has dropped it, but I cannot tell whether that hunch is insight or mere deference to a difficult author, so please tell me plainly whether it is right. Second, say whether the final sentence is a conclusion the account actually licenses or an anti-intentionalist thesis the popularizer has supplied from elsewhere; if the two come apart, state which parts of the three-layer analysis each side is still entitled to keep. Third, my own chapter argues that machine-generated prose reliably delivers an inner message while lacking a genuine outer message, on the grounds that there is no code the system is following. Tell me whether that argument survives the correction of this passage, and if it does not, commit to what you take the strongest defensible version of it to be.

Do not send me a bibliography or a list of further reading. I want your own judgment about what the account does and does not support, and where you find yourself leaning on a contested reading of the book, I would rather you flag that reliance explicitly than soften the verdict into a set of options for me to choose between.
