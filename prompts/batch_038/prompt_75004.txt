You're a scholar of Renaissance political thought — the person in the room who's read The Prince closely rather than the Wikipedia summary of it. I'm stuck on chapter 25 and I want help actually reading it, not a book report on fortuna in general.

Here's the bit I mean, in an old public-domain English translation:

"Nevertheless, not to extinguish our free will, I hold it to be true that Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less. I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers ... So it happens with fortune, who shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her."

And then, a few paragraphs later, on why a prince gets ruined without changing at all:

"a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to do, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it."

My problem — I'll call it the fair-weather proviso, tho I don't think I've seen anyone name it. The river image seems to say that when the flood is actually on there is nothing to be done; all the agency is spent earlier, in calm weather, building dykes. So the half we control is never exercisable at the moment we'd want it to be. Then the fixed-nature passage seems to say we can't reliably build the right dykes either, since the cautious man can't turn impetuous when the times demand it and vice versa. Put those together and virtù looks like it collapses back into luck: which temperment you were born with, and whether the times happen to suit it.

So, three things: (1) is that reading right, or is there a way to hold the half, the dykes, and the fixed natures together that I'm missing? (2) what does the chapter actually commit Machiavelli to about *when* virtù operates — before the crisis only, or also inside it? (3) is "one-half ... or perhaps a little less" a real quantitative claim, or is the hedge doing other work — what would the "little less" even consist in?
