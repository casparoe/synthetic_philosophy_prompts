**DRAFT: "Mill's Socialism as Institutional Federalism"**

*1.* The familiar account of Mill's leftward drift says that he began as a Benthamite individualist, was softened by Harriet Taylor, and ended as a reluctant socialist who disliked capitalism but feared bureaucracy more. I think this account mislocates the change. What shifted between the first edition of the *Principles* and the late essays is not Mill's estimate of socialism's attractiveness. It is his estimate of the *default*. Early Mill treats private property in land and capital as an institution that has been badly administered and needs piecemeal correction; late Mill treats it as one experimental arrangement among several, whose continued retention has to be earned against alternatives that have never been given a fair trial. The change is not a conversion toward socialism. It is a reversal of the burden of proof, and it is a reversal about the status of the existing order, not about the merits of the proposed one.

*2.* Mill supplies his own wedge for this reversal in the distinction he draws near the start of Book II of the *Principles*: the laws governing the production of wealth have something of the character of physical truths and are not open to human choice, whereas the laws governing its distribution are entirely a matter of human institution — once the things exist, a society may do with them as it likes, and does so through the medium of property law. Political economy, he insists, is a mixed science in which only one half is fixed. Most of his nineteenth-century readers took this to be a pious qualification. I want to argue that in the late work it becomes the load-bearing wall: if distribution is institutional, then any given property regime is a standing choice, and a standing choice made without ever having observed the alternatives is not a justified choice but an unexamined one.

*3.* Notice where Mill actually aims his reform. He is far less interested in abolishing acquisition than in severing bequest from inheritance — separating the right to accumulate from the right to transmit what one has accumulated to someone who did not. That programme, taken seriously, is not socialism. It is a radical liberalism of property law: equalize starting points, let contracts run free thereafter, and confiscate the mechanism by which unequal starting points reproduce themselves. If that is the reform Mill presses hardest, then the self-description in the *Autobiography* — the claim that he and Harriet came to regard themselves as socialists among politicians — is better read as a statement about sympathies and about the openness of a question than as a statement about a legislative programme. My draft's claim is that the label "socialist" in late Mill picks out an epistemic posture, not a blueprint.

*4.* This is where *Chapters on Socialism* should be read as continuous with the *System of Logic* rather than with any theory of justice. Mill grants the socialists most of their diagnosis: the existing distribution is not deserved, rests on force and accident rather than on any principle anyone could articulate, and produces a class of labourers whose condition is not obviously better than the feudal serfdom it replaced. But he then refuses the inference the diagnosis is usually taken to license, on the ground that socialism too has to be judged comparatively — against the actual existing order, not against an idealized one, and not against a caricature of the existing order either. The trouble, he says in effect, is that we do not know which system is better, because we have never permitted the alternative to run at sufficient scale for long enough to generate evidence. The right conclusion is not that socialism is refuted by its unknown consequences, nor that it is recommended by its known injustices elsewhere. It is that the ignorance is itself an artefact of a prohibition, and that the prohibition is what ought to be lifted.

*5.* So Mill's proposal, on my reading, is a form of what I want to call institutional federalism: a single polity that deliberately hosts several property regimes at once, with membership voluntary and exit real, and with the comparative merits settled by observed performance rather than by argument. The state becomes an arena rather than an engine. The obvious difficulty, which I flag rather than solve, is that the experiment is not clean. A producer cooperative operating inside a market that sets its prices, its interest rates, its legal rules of contract and insolvency, and the wage floor its members would fall back on is not a test of the cooperative regime. It is a test of the cooperative regime *under capitalist external conditions*, which is not the question at issue. If the coops fail, the defender of the status quo will say the regime is refuted; if they succeed, the socialist will say they succeeded despite the framework, and would have done better without it. Both readings are available from the same data. My draft currently says that Mill was aware of this and thought the informativeness of small trials was still worth having. I am not confident that is right, and I am not confident it is defensible.

*6.* I then make what I suspect is my worst move. I argue that the doctrine of *On Liberty* — that the only ground for interfering with an individual against his will is preventing harm to others — does real work here, and that it cuts against the existing order as well as for it. If a society may not impose a way of life on individuals except to prevent harm to others, then it may not impose a single property regime on all of them either; and since the choice among property regimes is, by *2*, an institutional choice, the liberal state is obliged to permit exit into alternatives. From this I infer that Mill's own hostility to fully state-managed socialism is in tension with his own principle.

*7.* The conclusion I draw is modest and deliberately deflationary. Mill is not a socialist in any programmatic sense, and he is not a conservative either. He is an inductivist about politics who noticed that his society was running an experiment of one, and who thought the epistemically responsible response to a contested institutional question was to increase the number of trials rather than to win the argument.

**What I want from you**

1. **Accuracy.** Go through the draft and sort its factual and textual claims into those you think are safe, those you think are doubtful, and those you believe are wrong. The load-bearing ones are: the production/distribution distinction and where it sits in the *Principles*; the severance of bequest from inheritance as Mill's preferred reform; the comparative-and-evidential structure of *Chapters on Socialism*; the *Autobiography*'s self-description as socialist, and the fact that it was published in the year Mill died; the recantation of the wages-fund doctrine at the very end of the 1860s; and the publication history of *Chapters on Socialism*, which I believe was posthumous and prepared by his stepdaughter. Also tell me whether Mill's abandonment of the wages-fund doctrine belongs in the paper at all, since it may be doing work for my thesis that it cannot actually support.

2. **Terminological discipline.** My draft slides between Owenite and Fourierist association, voluntary producer cooperatives, and state collectivism, and I suspect the slide is doing unnoticed work in sections 4 through 6. Tell me where it matters and where it does not. Mill himself distinguishes these; if the paper needs that distinction as a fixed vocabulary, say what the categories should be.

3. **Novelty.** Is the reversal-of-the-burden-of-proof reading, or the institutional-federalism label, a genuine contribution, or is it a line of interpretation that is already well established and that I have simply failed to encounter? Describe the standard readings my thesis might collapse into — Mill as the reluctant socialist shaped by Harriet Taylor; Mill as the unchanging liberal whose socialism is confined to sentiment; Mill as a proto-social-democrat whose continuity with twentieth-century welfare liberalism is the whole story — and tell me which of them my draft actually reproduces in disguise.

4. **Section 6, the harm-principle step.** Is my inference a fallacy, and if so, what kind? My own suspicion is that the principle governs interference with individuals, whereas a property regime is part of the background that *constitutes* the rights whose infringement counts as interference in the first place — which would make section 6 a category error rather than merely an overreach. If that diagnosis is right, is there any version of the step that survives, or should the section be deleted outright? Note that Mill's actual objections to state socialism, as I understand them, are about administrative competence, about the loss of individual energy and the formation of a nation of dependants, and about the state's poor performance at tasks that voluntary bodies can perform as well — none of which is a harm-principle argument.

5. **Section 5, the confounding objection.** Is it fatal to the evidential reading of Mill's socialism? In particular: does the mid-twentieth-century debate about whether a socialist economy could calculate at all strengthen my objection, weaken it, or belong to a different question entirely? And is there a version of the reply I am missing — for instance, that Mill's trials were never meant to be informative about a whole society, only about whether a given association can govern itself and out-compete conventional firms, which is a narrower and more tractable claim?

6. **The strongest counter-case.** Build the best argument that Mill's late position is not about permission and exit at all, but about the state's *positive* obligations — his willingness to have the state own land, run utilities, educate children, and regulate contracts, together with the concessions to state action he makes in the final chapter of *On Liberty*. If Mill thinks the state should do a great deal directly, does the arena-rather-than-engine picture survive, or does it depend on quietly ignoring half of his policy list?

7. **Structure and prose.** Identify the one paragraph I should cut, and tell me whether the paper's weight is misplaced — my instinct is that section 3 is doing more work than I have given it credit for and that section 4 is largely summary. Also tell me whether the deflationary conclusion in section 7 earns its place or reads as a hedge.

8. **What would settle it.** Finally: describe, without citing anything, what I would need to find in the corpus to make this thesis stick — which arguments, in which works, showing what. I would rather be told that the thesis requires a text that probably does not exist than spend a year looking for one.
