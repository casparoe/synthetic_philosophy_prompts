For six years I've been part of a small betting club with five friends: we each post percentage chances on whatever comes up — elections, a local referendum, whether our football club finishes in the top four, whether a friend's manuscript gets taken up — we cap the stake at fifty dollars per settled claim, and one of us reconciles the ledger at dinner once a month. The founding member is a mathematician who keeps the books, and from the start he has enforced a rule he considers non-negotiable: what you post commits you not only to the claims you wrote down but to everything those claims logically imply, whether or not you ever noticed the implication, and he may take the other side of any such implied bet at the odds your own numbers dictate. His justification is that if your commitments aren't closed under consequence, someone can always assemble a package of bets out of your own numbers that costs you money however the world turns out.

This season the rule finally cost me real money, twice. In September I posted 80% that the governing party keeps its leader through the spring, and separately 75% that if it keeps its leader it will bring the greens into the coalition; I had also, in a different thread, posted 45% that the greens enter government at all. He pointed out that my first two numbers already commit me to at least 60% on the third, took the other side, and collected. That one I half-concede, since it's arithmetic I could have done at the table in a minute. The second is what bothers me. He had circulated a puzzle — a claim about how many ways a certain arrangement could be completed — and, having no idea, I posted 50% rather than abstain. Three weeks later he produced a two-page derivation, worked out over several evenings, showing that the puzzle's answer follows from a general counting principle I had separately posted at 90%. Under the rule I was committed to at least 90% on the puzzle, he bet against my 50%, and I owe him. Here is the part I keep coming back to: if he had asked me in advance whether I'd take that bet, I'd have said no — I don't wager on things I can't check — and now that I've read his proof I've happily revised the 50% upward without any new evidence about the world. So my actual willingness to bet contradicted the numbers he was entitled to ascribe to me, and it contradicted them for a reason that had nothing to do with how confident I am about anything.

So: is his rule defensible, or is my objection more than an excuse from someone who lost? A lawyer in the group argues that what matters is what you actually assented to, while the mathematician argues that rationality is about what you're committed to regardless of what occurred to you, and they talk past each other at every dinner. I'd like to know whether there's a principled line to draw here or only arbitrary ones, and whether the idealization he's leaning on — that a rational believer's commitments extend to everything implied by what they believe — is something the whole enterprise of scoring people's stated chances actually requires, or a convenience that could be relaxed without the ledger collapsing. Tell me what genuinely breaks if we relax it. Then give me concrete proposals for rewriting the club's rules: among the candidates we've floated are charging the challenger for the effort of derivation, restricting enforceable consequences to those reachable in a bounded number of steps, allowing a no-fault retraction of an unconsidered number for a fixed penalty, requiring that a challenge be lodged before a claim settles rather than after, and simply treating implications nobody has entertained as not yet part of anyone's commitments at all. Say which of these has a real rationale behind it, which is a fudge that would let people hide behind inattention whenever they're losing, and what each one would cost us in the way of gamesmanship — I especially want to avoid a system where the member with the most free evenings can farm everyone else.
