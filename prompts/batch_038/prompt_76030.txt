ok so a friend sent me a Substack by some guy who does "epistemology for people who build models" and he's got a post on Nozick's tracking theory that I mostly followed, but a couple of things sat wrong with me and I don't know enough to say which. Here's the core paragraph, typed out roughly from memory of reading it (it's his words more or less, not mine):

> Nozick's move is to make knowledge a modal property instead of an evidential one. You know that p only if your belief is sensitive: if p were false, you wouldn't believe it. That's why he's willing to deny that knowledge is closed under known entailment. You know you have hands; you know that having hands entails you aren't a handless brain in a vat; but you don't know you aren't a handless brain in a vat, since if you were one you'd still believe you weren't. So the skeptic wins the small point and loses the big one. And Nozick's real answer to him is that "know" shifts with context — measured against the skeptic's standards we don't know we aren't brains in vats, measured against the standards of ordinary life we know we have hands, and there's no contradiction in saying both. There's a fourth condition too, adherence: if p were true you would believe it. That's the one doing the work in the fake barn case, where Henry drives through a county full of convincing barn facades and happens to look at the one real barn. Nozick says Henry doesn't know that's a barn, because his belief doesn't adhere — it isn't sticky enough across the nearby situations where there's a barn to be seen.

Then he pivots to self-driving cars and this is where I really got off the bus:

> The car's camera registers a pedestrian and it brakes. But if it had been a cardboard cutout, it would have braked exactly the same way. So its belief isn't sensitive, so it doesn't know there's a pedestrian — and no amount of extra training data fixes that, because sensitivity isn't about error rates, it's about what would happen in the nearby situations where the fact is absent. Which is why you should never trust a machine's stated confidence more than a human's.

He also says in passing, later on, that sensitivity and reliability are "basically the same idea, one modal and one statistical."
