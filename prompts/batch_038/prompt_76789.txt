So this started with an actual fight. My roommate did something pretty crappy, I told him so, and he said "you of all people don't get to say that" — because I'd done basically the same thing the year before and never got called on it. And I've been chewing on that for about a month now, not the fight, the general thing: when does somebody actually lose the right to call somebody else out, and what kind of thing would that even be? No philosophy training here, I've just been thinking and reading around, and I've talked myself into a pet theory I want stress tested. But before that I want the spec, because half of what I read assumes a standard nobody's bothered to argue for.

So: write out the requirements an account of standing to blame has to meet before I'd call it adequate. Not a survey of the answers — the answer key first. For each requirement, give me the case or the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than just a name on a list.

Here's the raw material I've got, definitely incomplete, so add what I'm missing.

Cases it has to get right:
- the honest hypocrite. Someone who does the exact thing they're calling out and says so out loud, no excuses, I do this too and it's still wrong. My gut says they keep standing, but any account built on inconsistency looks like it has to say they lose it.
- the reformed one. Used to be guilty, genuinely isn't anymore, calls it out in someone else. Do they have more standing or less than somebody who was never guilty?
- the worse offender. Same fault, ten times bigger. Does degree matter or is this binary?
- private versus said out loud. Does the hypocrite lose standing to think it, or only to say it?
- the self-blamer. Holds themselves to exactly what they're holding the other person to, visibly.
- domain bleed. Cheats at cards, calls out a liar. Is standing per-fault or does it leak?
- the stranger with no connection to you who calls you out anyway. I strongly suspect this is a different problem getting filed under the same heading as hypocrisy, and I want to know if I'm right.
- the person the fault was actually done to, doing the calling out. Extra standing, or the same standing?
- what restores it. Apology, getting caught, doing the time, just time passing?

Distinctions it can't collapse: standing versus sincerity; standing versus whether the blame is true (a hypocrite's accusation can be correct and stay correct, and I think an account that loses that is dead on arrival); blame versus criticism versus just reporting a fact versus punishment; forfeiting standing versus never having had it; all-or-nothing versus coming in degrees.

And one constraint that matters to me: I want an account a normal person can actually use in a live argument, where both people can see whether it's been met. So a requirement can't hinge on facts only a brain scanner or an all-knowing observer could check. Tell me whether that's a fair thing to demand of a theory here or whether it wrecks the project.

Then mark which of the requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just one camp's taste smuggled in as a criterion. If some subset of them can't all be satisfied at once, say so explicitly and show me why — I'd take that over a tidy list, since then the list itself is the finding. And name the single requirement that eliminates the most candidates, because that's the one worth arguing about.

My pet theory, for the short scoring round at the end: blame is a tool, and it only works on someone who can actually recieve it. So standing isn't a fact about your moral record at all, it's a fact about whether your blame can do its job — if the target will just read it as an attack and bounce it back, you have no standing no matter how clean your past is. Score that against the list along with the two or three obvious rivals (the inconsistency one, the complicity one, and the family of views where blame is a kind of protest you have to be positioned to make), but keep the scoring brief. The list is what I want.

Two more things. Tell me how confident you are in each requirement — which ones you'd defend hard and which are your own hunch dressed up as a criterion, since I'd much rather know that going in. And don't be polite about my framing: if standing turns out to be a bogus notion, or if my tool theory rests on something confused, or if this whole task can't be done well because the requirements list will just end up being whatever the writer already believed, say that straight instead of handing me a nice list. American English please.
