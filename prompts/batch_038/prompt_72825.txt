And here is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted, with paragraph numbers added by me for ease of reference.

1. The nonidentity problem concerns a class of choices that determine not how well off particular people will be but which people there will be. Whether a given person exists depends on the timing, the manner, and the partners of conception: a conception delayed by a month, or effected by a different gamete, produces not the same child in different circumstances but a numerically different child. Most choices that reach into the more distant future are therefore of this kind. Parfit's contrast is between same-people choices, in which the same individuals exist whichever option is taken, and different-people choices, in which who exists is itself settled by what is done.

2. His leading example is a girl of about fourteen who conceives a child now rather than waiting a few months until she is better placed to care for it. The child she has will, we may suppose, have a life worth living but one noticeably worse than the life of the child she would have had by waiting. That later child would have been a different person. Her choice is therefore not worse for the child she actually has, since had she waited he would never have existed at all, and a life worth living is not a harm. Because her choice makes no one worse off, Parfit concludes that she does nothing wrong.

3. The same structure governs much larger decisions. One of Parfit's cases asks us to choose between two energy policies, the safer and costlier of which avoids burying long-lived waste, and the cheaper of which leaves a small risk that, some thousands of years hence, that waste will kill and maim people. Another asks us to consider the descendants of people brought to America in chains, whose lives, marked as they are by that history, are worth living, and who would not have existed had the policy that enslaved their ancestors been refused. In each case the people who suffer are people who exist only because of the choice complained of. Climate policy is the live version of the same problem: mitigation would so thoroughly alter the pattern of human life over the coming centuries that the people who would benefit from it are not the people who will exist if we continue as we are.

4. The principle under pressure is the person-affecting restriction, the idea that an outcome can be worse only if it is worse for someone. Parfit argues that this principle cannot be retained. If we retain it, we are committed to saying that nothing in the different-people class of choices can be wrong, and that a policy which leaves the future much worse can be objected to only in ways that do not amount to moral criticism. His response is to give up the restriction and to hold that such acts are wrong, though not wrong for anyone: they are wrong because they bring about a worse future, and a future can be worse without being worse for any particular person.

5. This is why the nonidentity problem should be kept apart from two neighbouring questions: whether it can be better for someone to exist than never to have existed, and whether we may make existing people happy but need not make happy people. Parfit's argument never compares a person with nonexistence. Both of the futures it compares contain people whose lives are worth living; the question is which of two possible populations there ought to be.

6. The consequence for moral theory is considerable. Once we allow that outcomes can be better or worse impersonally, without being better or worse for anyone, the natural principle to adopt is one directing us to bring about the impersonally best outcome, and the impersonally best outcome is the one with the greatest total of wellbeing. Parfit accepts this. He accordingly accepts the Repugnant Conclusion: that a world containing very many people whose lives are only barely worth living is better than a world containing far fewer people with excellent lives, so long as the total is greater. He regards this as an unwelcome but unavoidable price of taking future generations seriously.

7. To the question posed, then: the nonidentity problem does show that acts which harm no one can be wrong, but only once the person-affecting restriction is abandoned in favour of an impersonal morality, and the price of that morality is a conclusion about population that most people find repugnant.
