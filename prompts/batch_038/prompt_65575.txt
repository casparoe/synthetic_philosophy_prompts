Please write a satirical document in American English: a formal consumer complaint filed with a state gaming commission by the patron of a casino's new table game.

**The setup — take this as given; it is all the source material the piece needs.** The game is called the Pasadena. A fair coin is flipped until it lands heads. If the first heads arrives on flip *n*, the patron wins $2^n/n when *n* is odd and loses $2^n/n when *n* is even: heads on flip 1 pays $2, on flip 2 loses $2, on flip 3 pays $8/3, on flip 4 loses $4, and so on. The casino advertises the game's expected value as roughly 69 cents, which is the sum of the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯ in the order the pay table happens to list the outcomes. That series converges, but only conditionally: its terms can be rearranged to sum to any prescribed real number, or made to diverge to positive or negative infinity. The patron's grievance is that the advertised figure therefore rests on a typographical fact about the house's brochure rather than on any fact about the gamble, and that the marketing department can raise the game's value by re-typesetting the pay table — a change requiring no new equipment, no new rules, and no new coin.

**Form.** 750–900 words, in the register of an administrative pleading: a caption, numbered paragraphs, a short list of relief requested, and one exhibit that is described rather than reproduced. The complainant is a professional decision theorist who has retained himself, and the deadpan must never break.

**Required beats.** The complaint must get the mathematics and the decision theory right, and specifically must:

- distinguish, in terms a lay reader can follow, between the older problem of a game whose expected value is *infinite* — the sort of thing the commission has dealt with before — and this one, whose expected value does not *exist*; and explain why the second is worse for a disclosure regime, since no cap on stakes, no limit on the house's bankroll, and no informed-consent signature can repair a quantity that has no value to consent to;
- note that the expected *absolute* payoff is the harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ⋯, which diverges, so the patron cannot be reassured that his running average will settle down over many plays;
- answer the casino's proposed patch — truncating the game at twenty flips and publishing the value of the truncated version — and explain why the truncated game is a different game whose value depends on where the cut is made;
- quote, at enough length to establish its own voice, the casino's written response from an in-house actuary who is entirely sincere, entirely orthodox, and entirely convinced the complaint is frivolous because the series converges (invent this reply; do not quote any real person or text);
- request a remedy that inherits the disease: ask the commission to designate a canonical ordering of the terms, and let the piece make legible — without ever saying so — that any such designation is arbitrary, that a disclosure reading "this game's expected value is whichever real number you please" is not a disclosure at all, and that the same remedy applied consistently beyond the casino would sweep in the complainant's own foundation's cost-effectiveness estimates;
- close by revealing that the complainant has been playing all year under several competing orderings of the pay table and is currently ahead by an amount he is unable to state.

**Constraints.** The target of the satire is a practice and a style of discourse — the confident assumption that every option simply possesses a single expected value, and the paperwork built on that assumption — not any named real person; all parties are invented. The humor must come from the content being correct: a decision theorist should laugh rather than wince, and should find the complaint technically sound even while finding it absurd. Include no footnotes, citations, or bibliography; everything the piece relies on must be stated inside it. Keep the irony sustained but legible, with no winking to the reader.
