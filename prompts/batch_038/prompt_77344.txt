I have published three novels, the most recent of them eight years ago, and in the intervening period I have earned my living writing other people's prose: a memoir for a retired footballer, two seasons of a costume drama, an uncredited polish on a thriller that did well. Throughout those eight years I have told my agent, my students, and anyone at a party who asks that I am at work on a fourth book — a long, difficult novel about a hydrologist who falsifies his own river data — and that it is the only thing I have written that I would want to survive me. Over the winter my older brother, a health economist, stayed with us for a fortnight and watched the shape of my days closely enough to form a view. On the last night he told me, with a force I found unsettling in a man who is usually gentle, that he thought I had been lying for eight years, though not dishonestly. His argument ran roughly as follows: every morning I have sat down at a desk and chosen, and on something like two thousand of those mornings I chose the hired work; there is nothing further that "wanting" could consist in, beyond the pattern of such choices, so the pattern is the entire truth of the matter; the hydrologist novel is a story I tell to make the pattern bearable. He added that if I really preferred the novel the preference would have arranged my life so as to be visible, and then he pointed out that in the one place where I have so arranged things — four years ago I put down a substantial deposit on a residency, forfeitable unless I delivered a chapter each quarter, and I have delivered eleven of them on time — I have demonstrated that I am entirely capable of preferring the novel when the terms make the preference operative.

What defeats me is that I cannot answer him, and I want to know whether the defeat is real or merely rhetorical. Two things trouble me. First, his thesis appears to have no outside: any evidence I might offer — the deposit, the eleven chapters, the fact that I read my own hired sentences aloud and find them dead on the page — he absorbs by observing that the offering of it was itself a choice, and that choices are the only evidence available. I have tried for weeks and cannot construct a single thing I could do or say that he would allow to show the preference present and the choices somehow failing it. Second, and this is the part I would most like you to take apart: when he praises me for the residency deposit he is praising me for building a machine that forces my hand. The machine works. And yet I do not experience it as the expression of what I want; I experience it as a standing confession that what I want cannot be relied upon to produce action unaided. If wanting is simply the disposition to choose, then what work is the machine doing? It has not altered what I want, only what happens. So: is his argument sound? Is there anything genuinely wrong with the claim that there is no fact about what a person wants over and above what that person chooses — and if there is something wrong with it, is the wrongness more than a philosopher's quibble, that is, does it change what I ought to do next year? I would rather be given a verdict than a balanced survey, and I would rather be told that my brother is right and that my sense of a missing fact is self-flattery wearing a philosophical costume.

Please answer as a professional philosopher who works across decision theory and the philosophy of action, and who is prepared to end in practical advice rather than in distinctions. Take the time to think this through before you write. Begin with a short piece of framing: state what would have to be true of the world, and of the word "want," for each of the two positions to come out right, so that I can see the structure of the disagreement rather than a heap of considerations on both sides. Then address directly whether my brother's thesis is vulnerable to any possible evidence, and what its invulnerability tells you about its content — I suspect that is where the leverage is, but I may be wrong. Then take up the residency deposit specifically, since it is the one case where I seem to have agreed with him by my own conduct. Only after that, tell me what to do about the coming year. Finally, and I mean this: if you think the way I have posed the problem is itself confused — for instance, if you think I am smuggling in a notion of what I really want that does no explanatory work, and that an honest account of my eight years requires no such thing at all — say so plainly and argue for it, rather than accommodating a premise you find doubtful. Do not send me to books, articles, or a reading list; I want your reasoning, not pointers to other people's.
