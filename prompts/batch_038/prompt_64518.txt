I fly and instruct on transport-category aircraft, and I have spent some years reading the philosophy of perception on the side — enough to know the names of the positions, not enough to have decided between them. What I want now is an audit of my own profession's training doctrine, because I think it takes a stand on the nature of perception, and I suspect it does not take the same stand twice.

Here is the practice, as concretely as I can set it out. From the first hours of instrument training, a pilot is taught that the bodily senses are unfit as evidence about the aircraft's state in flight. The vestibular system cannot separate inertial force from gravity, so linear acceleration on a night takeoff over water produces a compelling sensation of pitching up that, if followed, ends in a dive; a bank entered slowly enough to fall below the threshold of detection leaves the pilot feeling wings-level when the aircraft is in fact banked, and then makes an honest correction feel like a bank the other way; head movements in a turn generate sensations of tumbling in axes that have no counterpart in the aircraft's actual motion. The drilled response to all of it is uniform: disregard the felt sense, fly the instruments, and recover unusual attitudes with reference to the panel rather than to the body. Accompanying this is an explicit doctrinal claim that I want you to take seriously, because I think it is doing philosophical work: a pilot in the grip of such an illusion cannot detect it from the inside. The misleading presentation of attitude is said to be, from the subject's point of view, exactly what a veridical presentation would be, so there is no introspective mark distinguishing the two cases, and the only available remedy is to defer to an indirect representation of the aircraft's state that the pilot has learned to interpret.

Now the second half, which is what puzzles me. On an instrument approach, at the decision altitude or minimum descent altitude, the instruments are declared insufficient. To continue the descent the pilot must have the required outside visual references actually in sight — the runway environment itself — and if they are not there the approach is abandoned, however flawless the guidance has been. We are separately taught to expect the visual scene to mislead in recognizable ways: an unusually narrow runway or upsloping terrain produces a sense of being high that drives the aircraft low, and a night arrival over unlit water or featureless ground supports a confident sense of a normal glide path that terminates in the ground. Yet the prescribed remedy there is not more instrumentation but more procedure — a stabilized approach, published altitudes, cross-checked descent gates, a willingness to go around. And increasingly there are approved ways to substitute an infrared-derived image of the outside world for natural vision in the final segment. So in one phase of flight the deliverances of the senses are overridden by a representation; in another phase the representation is subordinated to direct visual contact with the environment; and the illusions we are warned about in the visual case are managed by rules rather than by better instruments.

With that practice in view, I would like the following.

1. Reconstruct the theory of perception the doctrine presupposes. In particular: does the claim that an illusion is undetectable from the inside commit us to the view that the misleading case and the matching veridical case share a common inner state, of the same fundamental kind, differing only in its causes? Does the practice require that claim, or does it merely wear it on the surface?

2. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is only consistent with. Is the doctrine wedded to sense-data, to a representationalist account on which perception is always a matter of taking up inner states that may or may not match the world, to a view on which veridical perception is a relation to the environment rather than an inner state at all, or to none of these — that is, is the commitment genuinely metaphysical, or merely operational, a rule about which input to trust in which phase of flight with no theory of perception attached?

3. Deal with the apparent inconsistency. Are the two halves of the doctrine really in tension, or can one and the same account of perception license both? If they can be reconciled, say precisely what does the reconciling work: differential reliability, task-dependence, the difference between knowing one's attitude and knowing one's position relative to the ground, the fact that the instruments are themselves read by looking at them, or something else. If they cannot, say which half is the anomaly.

4. Report where the practice is underdetermined. Which rival theories would license exactly the same syllabus, the same drills, and the same accident-investigation conclusions? Is there anything in the practice — anything we actually do, require, or forbid — that only makes sense on one of them? I want to know whether my profession has an implicit metaphysics or only an implicit engineering policy, and what would settle the question either way.

5. Run the counterfactual. Suppose we rebuilt the training from the view that veridical perception is not an inner state that happens to match the world but a different kind of thing altogether — direct acquaintance with the environment, not the same appearance with better causes. Would anything change? Would synthetic vision, conformal head-up symbology, and infrared-derived imagery be the natural completion of our present doctrine or a quiet abandonment of it? Would any drill for the illusions have to be rewritten, and if none would, what does that tell us about how much weight the doctrine's philosophical language is really bearing?

Lead with your bottom line: a single opening sentence stating which view of perception this practice commits us to, or that it commits us to none. Then give the reasoning, organized under the five headings above. No preamble, and no restating of the question.
