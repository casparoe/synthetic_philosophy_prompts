For eleven years I worked in a national seed certification and variety registration office; for the last six I have worked for the same government's biodiversity agency, on listings and on the management plans that follow from them. The two jobs share a vocabulary — variety, type, population, species — and I have become convinced they rest on opposite assumptions, though nobody in either office would put it that way.

In the first office, a cultivated population becomes a nameable, ownable, marketable thing only if it clears four tests. It must be new, in the sense of not having already been sold. It must be clearly distinguishable from every other known variety by one or more specified characters. The plants within it must be uniform in those characters, varying no more than one would expect given the mode of propagation. And the characters must persist unchanged through repeated propagation. Certification then polices the type: a seed lot containing more than a set percentage of off-type plants fails. The unit that counts is something that breeds true, and variation within it is treated as contamination, tolerated only up to a threshold.

In the second office, the same government protects species and subspecies, and much of what it protects is the variation itself. A population with little remaining genetic variation is counted as being in more danger, not less, and the standard prescription for a small population is to widen its gene pool. Meanwhile the units being listed are not themselves stable. Different taxonomic authorities delimit them by different criteria — reproductive isolation in one group, diagnosable monophyletic clusters in another, and in bacteriology a numerical threshold on whole-genome similarity that was adopted by convention rather than found — and a revision splitting one widespread species into several narrowly distributed ones can move a taxon from unremarkable to critically endangered while nothing whatever changes in the field.

So: what account of biological kinds does each practice presuppose? Does the first require that varieties have essences, or is uniformity merely an administrative necessity carrying no metaphysical commitment? Does the second require that species be real, and if so real in what sense — as individuals, as classes, as historical entities? Which rival accounts does each practice rule out, as against merely sitting comfortably with? Is there a genuine inconsistency in a single state simultaneously requiring that a kind be uniform in order to exist and treating a kind's variability as the thing worth preserving, or does the difference in purpose dissolve the tension? Please separate what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits, mark where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, and say what would have to change in each office were its implicit view replaced: what a registration regime for genuinely heterogeneous populations would look like, and what a listing regime would look like if taxonomic boundaries were openly treated as drawn rather than discovered.
