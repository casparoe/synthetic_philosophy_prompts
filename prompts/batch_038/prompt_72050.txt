ok so here's something that's come up at work and I keep flip-flopping on it, could use an outside head.

I sit on a small state wildlife listing committee. Our statute lets us protect "species," and a court recently told us that means we have to be able to say, in writing and in advance, what criterion we're using — not "whatever the best available science suggests," because the decision has to be reviewable. So we have to put one operational definition of species in the rulebook.

The case forcing our hand is a population of streamside salamanders — call it population X, and the specific mix of facts is really the point here:

- morphologically diagnosable, consistantly, from every neighboring population we've sampled
- distinct and monophyletic on mitochondrial DNA, but the nuclear genome is a mess: there's a hybrid zone with a widespread congener, introgression looks ongoing and asymmetric, and the hybrids have reduced but definitely not zero fertility
- it occupies a genuinely different niche (cold headwater seeps, versus the lowland vernal pools the other population breeds in) and stays there, with almost no movement between habitats despite no physical barrier between them
- it's been treated as a subspecies for decades, so right now it has no protection at all

Rank the candidate species concepts — biological/reproductive isolation, phylogenetic (and please split the strict-monophyly version from the diagnosability version, they give different answers here as far as I can tell), ecological, cohesion, recognition, morphological, plus anything you think belongs on the list that I left off — by how well each would serve as the written criterion in a rule like ours. Score each one out of 10, give a sentence or two on why it lands where it does, and then tell me which one you'd actually put in the rulebook and what the sentence should say.

Two things I care about. First, score for fitness to THIS job — a legal test applied by non-specialists to hard cases, with money and land use riding on it, and where the verdict has to be defensible to a judge — not for which concept is true or most theoretically elegant. I have a suspicion those two rankings come apart pretty sharply and I want to see where.

Second, and this is the one I really want: for each score, mark clearly whether you're reporting something systematists broadly agree on or making a judgment call that's yours. I've read enough around this literature to know people disagree a lot even about whether monism or pluralism is the right stance, and I don't want your personal take on that served to me as if it were the consensus. Where the field is genuinely split, say it's split and say which side you're on and why.
