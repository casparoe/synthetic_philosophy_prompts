Our reading group is working through Hume's essay on the standard of taste and I built an argument against it that I don't actually believe but can't seem to break, so maybe you can.

(1) Beauty isn't a quality in objects; it exists only in the mind that contemplates them.
(2) Even so, some aesthetic verdicts are better than others, and that difference has to be grounded in something other than the verdicts themselves, or every taste is as good as every other.
(3) The only ground Hume supplies is the joint verdict of true judges — people of good sense and delicacy, with practice, comparison, and no prejudice.
(4) But the only way to tell who the true judges are is by their track record: whose calls hold up and get confirmed later on.
(5) A call "holding up" just means later judges agree with it.
(6) So the standard amounts to: what the qualified agree is beautiful is beautiful, because the qualified agree. The consensus sets the standard and then certifies its own members.
(7) Which means an entrenched consensus can never be told it's wrong. The dissenter is by definition under-practised, short on comparison, and warped by a fondness for novelty, so her dissent can't count as evidence against the canon.
(8) Worse, any consensus self-validates. A culture whose qualified judges all converge on kitsch has, by construction, a standard of taste under which kitsch is beautiful.
(9) So either aesthetic value is just whatever a given culture's experts happen to converge on — the relativism Hume was trying to escape — or the whole apparatus does no work at all.

I want to keep (1), and I really don't want (9). So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd like to know what the alternatives force me to swallow, not just which one you'd pick.
