I've now sat through four years of a joint lab meeting between statistical geneticists and developmental psychologists, and the same argument about heritability recurs every semester with nobody moving. One side holds that twin-based and SNP-based estimates for a trait, however they diverge — roughly 0.8 versus 0.45 for height, the gap that generated the missing-heritability debate — are attempts to pin down one real quantity, additive genetic variance over phenotypic variance in a specified population, and that critics who say the number means nothing are confusing a well-defined estimand with the pop glosses layered on top of it. The other side holds that the quantity is indexical in a damaging way: it is fixed by whatever range of environments a study happens to sample; cloned seedlings grown in two different soils can have heritability near one within each plot while the entire difference between plots is environmental; Falconer's equal-environments and random-mating assumptions fail in opposite directions, since differential treatment of identical pairs inflates the estimate while trait-assortative mating depresses it; and gene–environment correlation means that much of what the model books under A is environment the genotype elicited.

My working hypothesis is that they are not answering the same question — one about variance decomposition and prediction, one about the causal explanation of an individual's development — with a genuinely empirical sub-question (how much rGE gets absorbed into A) doing quiet work underneath. Diagnose the impasse from what each side actually says: is it verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, or a sign the target is ill-posed? Then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a reformulated estimand, a study design — or argue that none should be expected. Say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the point in it you are least sure of.
