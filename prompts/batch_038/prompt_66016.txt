I am a second-year doctoral student in political theory, and my seminar this term is on constitutional design and the ethics of self-binding. I have been working through the precommitment literature — Elster on Ulysses, the ropes, and the wax in the sailors' ears; Holmes on the ways constitutional constraints empower and enable as much as they restrict; Waldron on whether a polity can bind itself at all; and behind them Jefferson's insistence that no generation may mortgage the next — alongside the economists' version of the same intuition, Schelling's commitment tactics and the time-inconsistency argument that rules outperform discretion. Nearly every illustration in this literature is either Homeric or constitutional. For my paper I want to stress-test it on something that is neither: an ordinary statute, trivially repealable, that nonetheless functioned for years as a genuine self-binding device.

The episode is the Budget Control Act of 2011 and the sequester. In the debt-ceiling standoff between President Obama and Speaker Boehner, Congress raised the borrowing limit in exchange for immediate spending cuts and created a twelve-member joint select committee charged with identifying further deficit reduction by that Thanksgiving. To keep the committee from simply failing, the Act attached a backstop: absent the required savings, automatic across-the-board cuts would fall evenly on defense and non-defense programs, taking effect on a date certain more than a year in the future, with certain entitlement programs exempted and the Medicare reduction capped at a small percentage, so that the pain was broad, indiscriminate, and calibrated to be intolerable to both parties' core constituencies. Enforcement was deliberately lodged outside Congress — an earlier automatic-cut statute had been invalidated precisely because it handed execution to a legislative-branch officer — so the mechanism ran through the executive's budget office, with the President issuing the order. The committee announced its failure in November 2011; the cuts were postponed by a few weeks in the year-end tax deal and then took effect in March 2013. What followed is the part the theory does not obviously predict: Congress never repealed the device, but legislated around it in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2019, each time raising the caps above the sequester levels for the next pair of fiscal years by a roughly symmetric bipartisan bargain, until the caps were effectively abandoned. Treat that sequence as given; you do not need any exact dollar figures.

I want two things. First, the mapping, done strictly and without charity: who is Ulysses, what are the sirens, what plays the part of the ropes and what the part of the wax, who are the sailors — and how does the fact that Ulysses had to secure his crew's compliance in advance bear on where the 2011 enforcement machinery was lodged? What, exactly, was the future moment of anticipated weakness that the enacting Congress meant to bind itself against, and if some of these slots have no honest occupant, say so rather than filling them. Second, and more important, the test: what does precommitment theory predict about a device that is fully reversible by the same simple majority that enacted it, given that a commitment which can be undone is, on Schelling's account, not a commitment, and that on Waldron's account there is no continuing collective agent to do the binding? Was the sequester a genuine Ulysses contract, a resolution dressed up as one, or a third thing this literature has no name for — and what actually made it stick as long as it did: procedural cost, the salience and indiscriminateness of the pain, the cover it gave members to vote for spending they could describe as forced, or the symmetry of the defense/non-defense split? Then run the analysis in the other direction and tell me what the case shows about the theory. My own inclination, which I would like argued with rather than flattered, is that the analogy misfires at its root, because in a legislature the sirens are not a temporary distortion of the agent's own settled values but a legitimate change of electoral mandate and circumstances — in which case the weakness-of-will framing cannot be transplanted from a person to a polity at all. Is that right, or does the theory have resources I am missing? Finally, tell me whether the earlier Gramm-Rudman-Hollings experiment with automatic cuts, the independence of central banks, or the European deficit rules that were simply not enforced against the large member states in the early 2000s do better or worse as instances of the same idea, and what the comparison reveals about which features of a commitment device are doing the real work. Where the theory is silent — about who bears the automatic cuts, for instance, or about how an escape hatch ought to be designed — I would rather you said it is silent than made it speak.
