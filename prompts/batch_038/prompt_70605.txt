I chair the infection prevention and control committee at a 400-bed regional hospital, and every month we publish a one-page guidance sheet for clinical staff — sometimes on a respiratory pathogen, sometimes on device-associated infections, sometimes on environmental cleaning between cases. A typical sheet carries between ten and twenty discrete recommendations, drawn from a mix of device manufacturers' instructions, professional society guidance, our own outbreak surveillance, and a handful of small studies our clinical pharmacist turns up. I know — and everyone on the committee knows — that on any given sheet at least one recommendation is probably not right: the evidence is uneven, some items rest on a single underpowered study, and a few have been carried forward from last year out of inertia rather than out of conviction. What I do not have is any way to decide, item by item and sheet by sheet, what we are entitled to print in the flat, unqualified voice that a nurse on a night shift will actually read.

Our general counsel wants us to add a standing caveat — a general statement that guidance may be revised and that individual items may turn out to be wrong — on the theory that it protects the institution. I suspect it is worse than useless: a caveat that appears on every sheet carries no information, and staff will read the recommendations as unqualified anyway. Our risk managers, meanwhile, make the point that has me stuck: a single sheet is usually applied to one patient by one clinician, who follows most of the items together rather than picking and choosing, so the chances of error do not stay independent of one another even when the evidence for each item came from independent sources.

Please write a procedure my committee can actually run. Assume no member of the twelve of us has formal training in epistemology and that we get about ninety minutes a month, with me signing off and anything contested going to the medical executive committee. The procedure needs to tell us: what confidence in an individual item is enough to print it flat; what changes when items are correlated, or when one clinician will rely on several of them jointly for a single patient; whether a blanket caveat does any work at all or whether disclosure has to attach to specific items; when a recommendation should be printed with a qualifier, printed with a recorded dissent, or pulled from the sheet; and where the procedure stops and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of institutional values rather than of evidence. Use whatever the formal literature on combining many fallible individual claims into one published document has to offer that survives translation into steps a non-specialist can execute, and be explicit about which of your steps are judgment calls dressed up as steps. Name a distinction if it has a name, but do not send me to a bibliography.

Then run it on two real cases. First: a catheter-associated urinary tract infection sheet with twelve recommendations that all point in the same direction and are each supported by reasonable observational data and by one another. Second: a sheet on a novel respiratory pathogen where nine recommendations are strong, two rest on a single sixty-patient study, and a third is an analogy drawn from a different virus. If the procedure tells us to pull or heavily qualify items on the first sheet — the one the committee is proudest of — say so plainly and do not soften it.

Write in American English and keep the entire response under 900 words: the procedure as numbered steps, the two worked cases in a few sentences each, and a short closing paragraph on what the procedure cannot settle.
