Working on a novel and I need a sanity check on one of my character's arguments.

He's a retired logician, his wife died last spring, and he's convinced he's built an airtight case that she can't have just stopped existing. Here's the chain, roughly as he lays it out at the dinner table: nothing that's really good is good by luck — goodness is built into the way things have to be; if it's even possible for one thing to have all the qualities that make something good, all at once, then there is such a thing; and existing permanently, in a way that can't be lost, is one of those qualities. So there's something that has them all and always will, and to him that's the end of the matter, his wife included.

My editor read it and just said the whole thing "collapses" somewhere and that I've written a man fooling himself. I dont want him to be a fool though — I want him wrong in an interesting way, or better still, right. So where's the actual weak link, and is there some version of this that holds up? And separately: is the last hop (from whatever he's proved to *her* specifically) redundant, or is it a second mistake entirely? I genuinely can't tell and it's the hinge of the book.
