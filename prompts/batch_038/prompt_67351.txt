I am a third-year doctoral student, and because of a staffing gap I have been handed a seventy-five-minute seminar for eighteen second-years on our history-of-political-thought module; the week's topic is Mill and India. The students have read *On Liberty* and *The Subjection of Women* and nothing else, and there is no reading set on India, so whatever they end up knowing about the East India Company has to come from me, from the front, in a whiteboard room with no slides. For what it is worth, the biographical material is not in dispute: Mill spent almost his whole working life as a Company official, from the age of seventeen until its governing powers were abolished in 1858, finishing as the head of the department that drafted the despatches to India; he gave evidence to a parliamentary select committee on India in the 1850s and defended the Company's administration against proposals to hand its functions to the Crown; his father had written a *History of British India* without ever going there; and *On Liberty*, in its first chapter, concedes that despotism may be a legitimate mode of government in dealing with societies not yet capable of being improved by free and equal discussion. What I want from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation.

So: the order in which the components must go in, and what depends on what. My suspicion — and I would like it tested rather than confirmed — is that the institutional fact of Mill's day job has to be in place before that clause in *On Liberty* can do any work at all, since otherwise it reads to them as a stray aside rather than as the load-bearing part of the principle; but the opposite ordering has a pull too, because starting from the text they have actually read is the only way to get eighteen people who have done no preparation into the room at all. Then the single case that should carry the session, and what it must have and what it must lack: should it be one concrete administrative matter from his years in the correspondence office, or should the pivot be the rebellion of 1857, or should 1857 be kept out entirely on the grounds that it collapses everything into a single trauma? Say what a carrying case has to show if the point is not to dissolve into biography — my guess is that it must be one where the action Mill defended would have failed his own domestic standards and where he knew that — and say roughly how much time each stage should get. Finally, tell me which single component, if I lose twenty minutes on the day, is the one whose absence makes everything downstream collapse.

The other half of the plan is the misconception map. Four moves I expect, and I want the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than argued with afterwards: the student who has posted on the module forum that Mill is straightforwardly a racist and the liberalism is the packaging; the student who insists the India material is an aberration to be bracketed so that the argument can be judged on its own terms; the reflex claim that he was simply a man of his time, which I do not know whether to meet in the first ten minutes or let exhaust itself; and a student who grew up in Chennai and has told me she finds the framing of the whole week bizarre, which I rather think means she has noticed something the other two have not. Alongside that, give me one question I can ask in the last five minutes that would show whether they have grasped the thing rather than merely agreed with me — it needs to separate 'Mill contradicted himself' from 'Mill's principle has a scope condition, and the scope condition is doing the work' — and a short list of what I should openly refuse to attempt at this level and say so to the room, since I have been advised that naming the limits is better than gesturing at controversies I cannot teach. I have in mind the whole historiography of Company rule, and whether the critical vocabulary that would make sense of it is even available to second-years in week four. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — the spine of the session, stated once and plainly — and only then give the plan and the reasoning, in the order it would be delivered; no preamble, no restatement of my question, no closing summary. Please write in British English.
