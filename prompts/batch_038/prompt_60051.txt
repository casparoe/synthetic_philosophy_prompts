Acting as a philosopher who specialises in population ethics and the ethics of AI, write me a dialogue, around 1000 words, between two members of a review board that's deciding whether to switch on a fleet of sentient but short-lived worker-models. Each instance would have a clearly net-positive inner life, do its assigned task for a few weeks, and then be deleted -- with no memory of, or dread about, the deletion.

Board member A defends the procreation asymmetry (roughly: the absence of suffering is good even when nobody exists to enjoy that absence, but the absence of pleasure isn't bad unless there's somebody who gets deprived of it -- so we have strong reasons not to create miserable lives, and no reason at all to create happy ones). Board member B thinks that's incoherent, or at least can't handle this case.

Before you start writing, spend 4-5 bullet lines of scratch notes pinning down the single strongest point each side has, and think hard about whether B's objection actually bites or just sounds good.

Then the dialogue. Both of them need to be genuinely excellent -- no strawmen, no convenient "huh, I guess I hadn't thought of that" conversions, and B has to defend their own positive view under pressure too, not just lob grenades at A. I'd rather see two pressure points actually worked through than every objection in the literature name-checked:

1. If deleting an existing instance deprives somebody, does A's own view commit the board to creating a small permanent population instead of a large ephemeral one? That seems to flip the practical upshot, and A should feel it.
2. Can A hold onto the first clause -- absence of suffering is good even with nobody around -- without it collapsing into "never existing beats any life whatsoever"?

Keep the arguement grounded in this case rather than in book-learning: no named philosophers, no citations, and definitely no reading list at the end. End with the disagreement sharpened rather than settled.
