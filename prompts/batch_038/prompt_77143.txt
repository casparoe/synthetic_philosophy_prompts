Background: I stumbled onto Aumann's agreement theorem (the 1976 agreeing-to-disagree one) and it kind of wrecked my brain in a good way. Two Bayesians, same prior, common knowledge of each others posteriors — then the posteriors have to be equal. So fully-informed rational disagreement is flatly impossible.

Here's my problem. There's an argument I keep walking into, both in an online group I'm in and in whatever I stumble across, and it goes nowhere. One camp says this is one of the deepest results in the whole area: if two people have honestly pooled everything they have, including the fact of each others confidence levels, and they still disagree, then at least one of them is failing at reasoning, and the cure is simply better and more complete communication. The other camp says the theorem is nearly empty — that all the content is smuggled into the common prior assumption, that you can always escape the conclusion by saying "well, we never actually shared a prior," and that expecting someone to treat their own prior as something they could be wrong about is a category error, because a prior is basically who you are: your upbringing, your temperament, the bets you'd take before you'd seen any evidence at all.

I want to know what kind of disagreement THIS is. Is it verbal — are they using "prior" or "rational" in seperate senses?
