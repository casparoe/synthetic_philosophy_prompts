I am a third-year doctoral student in philosophy, and in the coming term I am teaching an upper-level seminar in moral psychology to fourteen students who have had one ethics course but nothing at all on the person–sitison debate — that is, nothing on the situationist challenge to virtue ethics. We meet once weekly for eighty minutes, and the activity will occupy the entire first meeting, before any reading is assigned, so that the students encounter the material cold and their own data becomes the thing the literature is later asked to explain.

What I want is a runnable classroom experiment, not a discussion exercise, whose aggregate result makes the situationist challenge vivid from the inside. The engine of the design should be a gap between what students predict they will do and what they actually do, in a situation arranged around some incidental, non-moral factor — a hurry, an unexpectedly found token, the presence of a passive bystander, a trivial framing shift. The behaviour measured must be real and mildly costly (help actually given or withheld, points actually surrendered, time actually spent), not self-report. At some point students should also be asked to explain their classmates' behaviour in dispositional terms, so that peer attributions can be set against the situational variable within the same small dataset.

Please supply: the full protocol with roles, timing, and materials, assuming paper, slips, a whiteboard, and no budget beyond a handful of small tokens; the incentive structure, including how to make the cost genuinely felt without anyone's grade depending on how they behaved; the moment and manner of the reveal, in which the class tabulates its own two-by-two before any philosophy has been named; and a debrief that gets the students to generate, in their own words and in a specified order, the three principal replies open to the defender of character — that the engineered situation was not a fair test of the trait, that what the manipulation moves is a non-moral motive rather than a virtue, and that traits may be real yet local — before I supply the technical vocabulary for each.

Two constraints I care about especially. First, the deception must be fully reversible inside the session: these are my own students, participation cannot be coerced or graded, and anyone who declines needs a genuine, cost-free alternative role — so tell me how opt-outs are to be handled without silently wrecking the design. Second, be candid that with fourteen participants the class's own data will be statistically worthless, and build the debrief around that fact rather than papering over it; the lesson should include what repetition, cross-situational variety, and base rates would be needed to carry such a finding to the strong claim that global character traits do not exist, and what the absence of that support costs each side of the dispute.

Finally, I want a frank section on what will go wrong the first time I run this — the manipulation failing to fire, students reverse-engineering the design mid-session, everyone behaving well so that the result fizzles, or the class concluding too fast that virtue ethics has been refuted — with a remedy for each, and an explicit statement of which feature of the person–situation debate the activity cannot structurally reproduce and which I will therefore have to supply by argument.
