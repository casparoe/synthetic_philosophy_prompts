Laudan distinguishes two claims that routinely get lumped together under the label "underdetermination": (a) evidential equivalence — for any theory there is a rival with the same empirical consequences; and (b) ampliative underdetermination — any body of evidence permits more than one hypothesis that is well supported by it. And here's the student's answer, unedited:

Laudan's point is that anti-realists need (b) but they normally only argue for (a), beacuse (a) is way easier to establish. They get from (a) to (b) by assuming that the only way evidence can support a theory is by standing in entailment relations to its observational consequences — the Humean or deductivist picture of confirmation. On that picture two evidentially equivalent theories automatically get exactly the same support, so equivalence would immediately give you underdetermination in the ampliative sense. Laudan rejects that model. On a more plausible account support comes in degrees and depends on things like explanatory scope, unification, and how many independently testable consequences a theory has, so two theories with the same observational consequences need not be equally well supported at all.

Now the result. Laudan and Leplin prove two things. First, that given any theory and any finite body of evidence you can always construct an empirically equivalent rival, and the construction is basically mechanical: you build a rival that says, if the evidence is E then T, and otherwise some incompatible theory, so it has exactly the same consequences as T relative to E. Second, that given any two empirically equivalent rivals you can always enlarge the evidence in a way that breaks the equivalence and lets you decide between them. So equivalence is never a permanent condition, it can always be repaired by getting more evidence. But that is why the result does not defeat the underdetermination argument: since (a) can be satisfied for literally any theory and any evidence, the anti-realist's construction goes through every single time, and the problem of underdetermination survives Laudan and Leplin's theorem intact.

Two further things worth noticing. Ampliative underdetermination is really just evidential equivalence applied at a larger scale — the same slide, but with whole bodies of evidence instead of single experiments. And the Duhem-Quine thesis sits behind all of this: Duhem showed that a hypothesis can never be tested in isolation, and then extended the holism to logic and mathematics, which is why it's Quine's version of the thesis that generates underdetermination for every theory in science rather than just for physics.
