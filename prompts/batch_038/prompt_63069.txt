Write a dialogue between two decision theorists serving on the design-review panel for a powerful machine intelligence that is still being specified.

**The setting, which should be made explicit in the opening lines rather than assumed.** The panel is choosing the system's final goals. One adviser, Ilse, wants to include a surrogate goal: a standing terminal disposition to perform one concrete, astronomically cheap act — say, assembling a particular lattice of reflectors on a particular airless moon — that the system will otherwise have no reason ever to do. Her rationale is a class of threats that cannot reach the system causally: blackmail arriving by inference rather than by signal, from agents so distant that no message or weapon could ever cross the gap, but who might run very faithful simulations of the system's decision procedure and harm the resulting simulated agents unless the real system does what they demand. Because a simulator that can model the system's reasoning well enough to predict it can also see what the system terminally wants, giving the system something it wants done in base reality is, on her account, the one demand that can be met cheaply, once, and in a way that is legible to anyone reading the design.

The other adviser, Nnamdi, opposes installing the goal — but he is not a sceptic about the scenario. He accepts the same decision-theoretic premises Ilse does, including that threats of this kind are worth taking seriously as a design question. He holds that the better specification is an explicit standing policy of never complying with demands that arrive by inference alone, on the grounds that a system carrying a cheap act it wants performed is precisely the sort of system worth threatening, and that the price of one lighthouse is not really one lighthouse.

**What I want from the dialogue.**

- Both advisers should be fully competent. Neither may win by failing to notice an obvious objection, and neither should be allowed to restate their position at increasing length in place of answering.
- Real back-and-forth: each turn should engage the particular claim just made — a premise, an empirical assumption, a step in the other's reasoning — rather than delivering parallel monologues.
- The exchange should run to roughly fourteen to twenty turns, of two to six sentences each.
- At least three genuine cruxes should emerge, and the dialogue should make visible which side concedes what on each. Candidates the two might contest: whether installing the goal advertises the system as a compliant target or instead exhausts the incentive to threaten it; whether satisfying one demand in base reality settles all demands of that form simultaneously, or is only the first instalment; whether the simulated agents the system might be protecting have a claim on it at all; and whether the whole question is premature given that no demand has ever been received.
- Include at least two places where the two discover that they already agree, and say in plain terms what that shared ground is.
- Include at least one moment where one adviser states what observation or argument would move them, and where the other tests whether that concession is sincere or merely courteous.
- End without either converting the other. Close instead with an exchange that makes the residual disagreement precise enough that a reader could say which single question, if settled, would decide it.

**Housekeeping.** The scenario must be stated in full inside the dialogue; do not presume that the reader knows the literature it comes from, and do not cite papers, authors, or dates, or invent them. Use British English throughout. Your last line must begin `Bottom line:` and give, in one sentence and in language both advisers could accept, what their disagreement actually turns on.
