I've been the only intake person at a small housing-counseling nonprofit for six years — families come in facing eviction or some mess with a landlord, and I figure out what's actually going on, what they're eligible for, and what order to do things in, because half the time the thing they walk in asking for is the third-most useful thing I could help with. Our new director wants to triple the caseload without hiring another me, and her plan is: a pool of part-timers does the first interview off a script I write, two of them score the write-ups against a rubric I also write, and I move to spot-checking — pulling maybe one file in ten and reading it. After a year of that, the idea is the rubric and the score sheets carry most of the weight and I'm freed up for the weird hard cases. She calls it cloning me. I said yes tentatively, and I've felt off about it ever since, and I can't tell whether that's a real problem or just me not wanting to be replaceable.

Here's the stuff that keeps circling. When I read one of thier write-ups I can usually tell in about a paragraph whether the eventual decision is going to be a good one — checking their work is way, way faster for me than doing the case myself would have been, and that's basically her whole argument. But a lot of what I actually do isn't spotting facts, it's picking between two bad options for this particular family, and I don't know that's the kind of thing you can put on a scoring sheet without it collapsing into "did you check box 4." Also the part-timers turn over every four months and training them on *why* we do it this way takes three weeks each, so there's a strong pull toward just handing over the checklist and skipping the why — and honestly a couple of them have already asked me what the point of a given step is and I noticed I didn't have a crisp answer, it's more a feel for how the whole thing goes wrong if you skip it. One more thing that bugs me: the score sheet rewards closing cases cleanly, and I can already picture that if we're judged on throughput, the files I recieve will start looking tidy in a way real cases never are.

So what do I tell her? Is a setup like this actually going to produce decisions as good as the ones I make now, or does something specific get lost when you chop the job into pieces and hand them to people who don't hold the whole thing in their heads — and if something does get lost, is it fixable with a better rubric and better onboarding, or is it structural, the kind of thing that just won't survive being written down and passed around? What conditions should I insist on before I go build it — I keep going back and forth on the spot-check ratio, on whether the part-timers need the reasons and not just the steps, and on who gets to change the rubric once it exists. And the part I most want a straight answer on: six months in, when I'm no longer the one doing the work, how would I actually be able to tell whether what's coming out the other end is as good as what I used to produce, or only looks as good on paper? A friend who works in ops says I'm overthinking it and that any skill you can't write down wasn't a real skill anyway — is he right, and if not, what's wrong with that line?
