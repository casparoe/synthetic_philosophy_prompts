My father died in March, and the settling of his estate has put me in a role I did not apply for. He was the last of his generation. What he leaves — the house, nine hundred acres of river bottom that has been in the family since 1946, and the proceeds from the sale of the equipment business — comes to roughly nine million dollars, divided equally among my brother, my sister, and me. Our lawyer tells me the estate falls under the federal exemption and that our state imposes no transfer tax of its own, so there is no tax bill forcing the issue. The question is entirely ours to answer, and I have found that it presents itself about once a month: a relative in trouble, a scholarship someone wants named, a structure an adviser wants us to sign, a child approaching an expensive threshold.

Since the funeral I have been reading political philosophy I would not have opened two years ago — enough to know the terrain has factions, and not enough to name them reliably, so please do not make me do the naming. I do not want a survey of positions on inherited wealth. I want a procedure.

Here is the rule I have been trying to defend to my own children, and I want it turned into something I can run: family money may be used to remove bad luck, but not to buy rank. A transfer that makes a life better in a way that would still be worth having if everyone's life were equally good is permitted; a transfer whose value depends on other people not receiving the same thing is not. I suspect the line leaks in both directions — nearly everything a family buys for a child is partly about rank, and nearly every rescue of someone from misfortune also moves them up a queue — and I want a candid account of where it leaks and what a workable version of it would have to say instead.

Please deliver the following.

1. The questions to ask of any proposed use of the money, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and where the procedure simply stops at no. I want tests for the rank-versus-level distinction that a person with no economics can apply out loud: something like asking whether the benefit would survive being given to everyone in the pool at once, whether what is being bought is a fixed number of slots, whether the transfer exempts someone from a check or a wait that applies to others, and whether the same sum given to a stranger would do comparable good. Tell me which of these actually discriminate and which are decoration.

2. An adversarial step. Every transfer can be described as removing bad luck if you are the one describing it, and I do not trust myself. Build in a requirement to state the strongest case that the proposal is about rank, and say what has to be true for that case to fail.

3. How the procedure handles the floor. It cannot tell me how much my children may receive before the question becomes one of rank by definition; tell me where that number has to come from, what constraints it must satisfy to be consistent with the rest of the procedure, and every other point where the decision is genuinely mine rather than derivable.

4. Two objections it must absorb rather than wave away: my brother's, that unilateral restraint is pointless when no other family is restraining itself and our money will simply be replaced in the competition by someone else's; and the sharper one I have come to fear myself, that a family doing this privately buys a clear conscience at the price of never supporting the public version — taxes, school funding, anything that binds the other families too. Say whether that defect can be fixed from inside the procedure, and if it can, how.

5. Worked runs on four live proposals: one hundred eighty thousand dollars toward a house in a district with a high-performing public school, versus the same money toward a house where we already live; a forty-thousand-dollar stipend supporting my daughter through an unpaid year at an organization whose director is a friend of mine, together with the telephone call I could make on her behalf; a named scholarship at the selective college I attended, versus the same sum directed to unconditional cash transfers to low-income households; and my cousin's request for a sixty-thousand-dollar interest-free loan to keep his landscaping business alive through a bad year. On at least one of these, give me the answer I do not want. I would rather find out from you than from my conscience later.

6. A verdict, or an explicit failure to reach one, on the structural question. Our adviser has proposed a trust that would keep a portion of the money working for descendants not yet born, and notes separately that holding the land until our own deaths avoids a capital-gains tax that selling it now would trigger. Should I decline an arrangement the law permits, and does the procedure have anything at all to say about how much wealth may legitimately be held across generations?

7. A one-page version I can put on the table at a family meeting: three adults, twenty minutes, one of whom believes I have gone soft and one of whom co-signs the charitable gifts with me and shares my views but not my patience.

I have no training beyond one required course decades ago. Do not send me references or a reading list; I cannot check them and they are not what I need. If my stated principle misdescribes an objection you think is decisive against it, say so in plain words. And at the end, tell me which steps of your procedure are only my principle wearing a procedural costume, and which are doing independent work — because I intend to argue with the ones that are mine, and I would like to know which those are.
