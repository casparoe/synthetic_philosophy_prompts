Twice a month somebody in our tenants' union asks me some version of the same question: did *we* decide to do this, or did a few members go off and do it, and now the whole unions name is attached to it?

Context: about 60 members, two part-time staff, decisions made at a weekly general assembly by modified consensus — you can block a proposal, or you can "stand aside," which means you don't stop it but you're not in it either. I'm the one who keeps the minutes and the commitments ledger, so the question always lands on me. Sometimes it's forward-looking: a working group wants to spend money on something and there's a fight about whether they have a mandate. Sometimes it's after the fact — last month a delegation occupied a leasing office, three people got arrested, and the assembly spent two hours arguing about whether the union had authorized it, which matters a lot because we have a legal defense fund to draw on and a public statement to write.

Right now I answer from vibes and people shout at each other. I want a procedure I can actually run, twenty minutes tops, that ends in something I can put in front of the assembly as a record: fields to fill in, not a paragraph of impressions. The people running it have no philosophy background and won't read anything longer than two pages, and my co-coordinator signs off before it becomes official.

Build it on whatever the philosophy of joint action and collective intentionality actually knows thats usable. I gather there's a real literature on what it takes for a bunch of individuals to count as having intended something together, and that the people in it don't agree with each other. Where they disagree, don't paper over it — tell me which side your rules follow and what the procedure gives up by following that side. I'd rather have an honest procedure than one that sounds neutral while quietly picking winners.

What I need in it:

- the questions to ask, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each;
- the points where it can just stop and say no, that wasn't the union, and the points where it has to say yes, that was us, even though everyone involved will hate hearing it;
- where it hands off to the assembly, because the question turned out to be about what we want to be committed to rather than what we're actually committed to;
- the hard cases it has to survive. Ours: (1) a standing mandate we gave a working group fourteen months ago that nobody ever rescinded; (2) three members standing aside on a consensus decision — is the resulting action the union's, the forty-odd who didn't stand aside's, or nobody's; (3) a member who said nothing at the assembly, showed up to the action, and now insists she was there as an individual; (4) an assembly where nearly everyone backed each premise of a statement but the statement as a whole would have lost a straight vote — this happened, and to this day nobody can agree what we decided.

Then run it in full on three cases: two you pick from the four above, and one where the verdict comes out against me, because I was the person arguing the occupation delegation was unauthorized and I want to know whether the procedure is capable of telling me I was wrong.

Finish with what it can't settle at all — where there's no fact to find and we're just choosing what kind of group we want to be.

One more thing: our ledger is a mess. If a usable version of this needs me to record things differently going forward, say which fields I should be capturing at the time, so the question is answerable in three weeks instead of being reconstructed from memory and a WhatsApp group.
