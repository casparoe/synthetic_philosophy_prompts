My older brother is in his last year of seminary and has begun using a cosmological argument in conversations with friends. When I asked what he was relying on, he sent me a paragraph he had written himself, which he assured me is standard material. It reads:

"Cantor proved that some infinities are larger than others, but he also showed that no collection can be infinite in the sense of being a completed whole, since a completed totality of everything is a contradiction. Hilbert's hotel makes the point: a fully occupied hotel with infinitely many rooms can still absorb one more guest, and then infinitely many more, which would be an absurdity if it described a real building but is perfectly harmless as arithmetic. So the actual infinite is a useful fiction inside mathematics and cannot exist in the physical world. In particular the past cannot be infinite, because an infinite past would be a collection of events completed by successive addition, and one can never finish counting to infinity."

I took one logic course years ago and I know the name Hilbert's hotel, but I cannot tell how much of this I ought to concede in a discussion with him. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and say what a set theorist or a philosopher of mathematics would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is false or merely contentious presented as settled? I am especially unsure about the attribution to Cantor, about what the hotel example actually establishes, about what he means by a collection that can be listed or counted, and about whether the conclusion regarding the past follows from anything true in the paragraph. Please answer in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
