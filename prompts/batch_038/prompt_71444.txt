I chair the harm-standards committee of a large messaging platform. Roughly once a month we receive a proposal with the same awkward shape: a change that imposes a trivial, thinly spread burden on essentially our entire user base in exchange for some reduction in a class of genuinely devastating outcomes. The committee is seven people — four engineers, a lawyer, a former teacher, and me — and none of us has any training in philosophy. Each proposal gets forty-five minutes, my signature is final, and I have to publish a written justification that both the affected users and the team that built the change can read without feeling insulted. Our charter imposes two constraints that make the usual answers unavailable to us. We are forbidden from monetizing anything: no value-of-a-statistical-life figures, no willingness-to-pay surveys, no dollar columns. And we are forbidden from declaring any harm lexically worse than any other, so we may not simply announce that no quantity of small annoyances could ever outweigh one catastrophe. That second rule was adopted after a bad quarter, and I have no authority to revisit it.

What I need from you is a decision procedure the committee can actually run inside forty-five minutes: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; how to handle the fact that the trivial harm gets multiplied by something on the order of two billion people while the severe one gets multiplied by dozens; where the procedure produces a definite verdict; and where it must stop and hand the decision to me or to a vote because what remains is a matter of value rather than fact. Please ground it in what the philosophical literature on aggregation actually offers, and be explicit about the choice you are making. I know the underlying puzzle — it is the shape of the thought experiment in which one must choose between giving a single person a short, sharp electric shock and giving a billion people a barely noticed speck of dust in the eye — and I understand there are at least four distinct strategies for resolving it: accepting the trade-off as legitimate and requiring one's comparisons to be transitive; refusing it by positing a sharp cutoff beneath which small harms simply do not count; refusing it by claiming the comparisons at the boundary are vague or imprecise rather than determinate; and refusing to sum at all, on the grounds that burdens falling on different people never constitute a single burden that anyone bears. Tell me which strategy your procedure adopts, why each of the others breaks down under our two charter constraints, and what the procedure gives up by that choice. Flag every step where a judgment call is wearing the costume of arithmetic. One wrinkle I especially need handled: each proposal is judged on its own, but the trivial burdens do not cancel out — they accumulate. Tell me how the procedure keeps a running account of the stack, and what it says on the occasion when the hundredth individually acceptable speck is the one that makes the product intolerable.

Then run the procedure on three live proposals, and do not soften a verdict I will dislike. First: a filter change that holds three percent of direct messages for up to twenty minutes pending review, affecting some 180 million people on a given day at a cost of roughly ten minutes per person per year plus the mild alarm of a notice reading that the message was reviewed, projected to cut coordinated harassment campaigns by forty percent — from about eleven a year to seven, each of which currently costs its targets their employment, their housing, or years of psychiatric care. Second: raising median load time by forty milliseconds, permanently and everywhere, to run an on-device classifier that by our engineers' best estimate prevents between one and three self-harm deaths among minors per year; no individual user will feel the delay and no user could trace it to us. Third: adding eight seconds of identity confirmation to account creation for every future user, to prevent, about once a decade, a wrongful permanent ban of the kind that recently cost one journalist her professional network and two years of income. My expectation is that your procedure will clear the first two and clear the third as well, and it is the third I find repellent, because clearing it commits us to taxing every future user indefinitely for a benefit we cannot point to and to publishing the exchange rate at which we did so. If the procedure clears it, say so and then tell me what I am supposed to write in the published justification. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do: which of these three it is most likely to get wrong, in which direction, and what a committee that relied on it for five years would gradually stop noticing. Please write in American English.
