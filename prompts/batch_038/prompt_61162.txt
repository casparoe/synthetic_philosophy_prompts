I am the teaching assistant for a corporate resilience programme built around the Stoic distinction between what is up to us and what is not. The instructor's handouts quote Epictetus's opening list — judgement, impulse, desire, and aversion on one side; body, property, reputation, and office on the other — but in discussion she also uses the vocabulary of a well-known contemporary revision on which some things are only partly up to us, and she borrows management language about circles of control and circles of influence. She has never said which of these she actually holds, and I have to write the week-five exercise in her register. Her recorded verdicts on participants' questions, all given flatly and without hedging:

- whether your body stays well over the next decade: no, though you should prefer health and act for its sake
- whether you take the walk that tends to keep you well: yes
- whether your colleagues rate your work highly: no
- whether you do work that deserves a high rating: yes
- whether your adult brother stops drinking: no — she rebuked a participant who said he would feel like a failure if his brother relapsed
- whether you keep offering him help without resentment: yes
- whether your party wins the national election: no
- whether you canvass: yes
- whether you win your club doubles match: no, but she told the participant to substitute the goal of playing to the best of his ability, which is up to him
- whether the first stab of panic when the aircraft drops is up to you: no — the body, she said, speaks before you do
- whether you assent to the thought that you are about to die: yes
- whether you should still be ashamed of the cruel thing you did at nineteen: the deed is not up to you now, but the verdict you pass on it today is

The week-five case is a compliance officer who expects to be offered a shortcut in six months that would quietly benefit her firm and ruin a client. She asks three things: (i) whether she will be able to refuse it when it comes; (ii) whether becoming the sort of person who refuses it without effort — a trained disposition rather than a single act — is up to her now; (iii) whether the flush of shame that comes over her, unbidden, when the plan first occurs to her is up to her.

Predict her answer to each of the three. Then lay out the candidate rules that are compatible with every one of the twelve verdicts above: the classical act-of-will reading, the trichotomy reading, a rule keyed to praise and blame, a rule keyed to what may be aimed at without the reserve clause, a rule that indexes "up to you" to the moment of the question, and any alternative you think fits better. Say which of them genuinely survive all twelve verdicts — and where a rule has to strain, say what it is straining against — and then show precisely where the survivors part company on (i), (ii), and (iii). Commit to a prediction for each of the three with your reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and finish by naming a single further question I could put to her that would settle which rule she is running.

Please keep the whole answer under 650 words; a compact table for the rule-by-question matrix is welcome, with prose for the rest.
