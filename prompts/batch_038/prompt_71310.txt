I'm in a philosophy-of-history reading group and we've all been told to write down our actual commitments first so the others can go at them. Here's mine:

(1) Polybius's cycle of constitutions gets at something real. Regimes rot from the inside, because the prosperity the founders earn corrupts their successors, and the motor is psychological rather than military. That psychology hasn't changed.

(2) Ibn Khaldun is right that group solidarity is the fuel of political order, and that it burns out within about three generations inside a settled dynasty, so renewal only ever comes from outside, from a harder group moving in.

(3) Spengler's organism story I reject outright — cultures don't have life-spans and nothing about how old a culture is determines its shape. I also reject any providential gloss on Toynbee, tho I do buy the challenge-and-response model and his point that civilizations kill themselves rather than being killed.

(4) Institutions ratchet. Literacy, bureaucracy, print, digitisation mean each collapse starts from a higher floor than the last one, so there's genuine direction even with cycles on top of it.

(5) On method: historical explanation is real, prediction isn't. You can say why Rome went under without thereby having anything to say about whether we will. This is the one I'm most sure of.

Go through the set and tell me which commitments pull against each other and how badly. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, say for each what the cheapest fix is and what that fix costs me, and then name the single revision that would clean up the most. Also flag any pair that only looks like a conflict, because I suspect at least one does and I don't know which.
