So I've gone down a rabbit hole on animal signaling. It started with a blog post about peacock tails — the standard Zahavi line, that the tail is honest *because* it's expensive, that only a genuinely fit bird can afford to haul one around while dodging predators — and I've been reading around it for a couple weeks now, mostly pop science plus a few review articles I only half follow. Last night I tried to turn it into a general argument about human language in my group chat and got absolutely demolished, except nobody would tell me which step they objected to. They just kept typing "that's not how the models work" and changing the subject. So I'm writing it out for a stranger in the hope that you'll be more specific than they were.

Here's what I had:

1. A receiver is justified in trusting a signal about some hidden quality of the sender only if a sender who *lacked* that quality would be worse off for having sent it.
2. A sender is worse off for having sent something only if sending it costs them something.
3. So honest signals have to be costly. That's the whole peacock move: the tail is the honesty.
4. And the cost has to be differential. A signal that costs the liar exactly what it costs the truth-teller deters nobody — the requirement is that the low-quality sender can't afford it.
5. Speech is about the cheapest signal an animal body can produce. It costs me the same breath and the same fraction of a second to say "I'll pay you back Friday" whether I intend to or not, and the same to say "trust me, I'm the reliable one" whether I am or not. Production cost is flat across sender types.
6. So by (4) and (5) speech can't be an honest signal. Words are the anti-peacock.
7. And signaling theory is clear about what happens next: the receiver discounts the signal to nothing, the sender knows they'll be discounted, and you land in the babbling equilibrium where the channel carries zero information.
8. So every time I've updated on what somebody told me — a recommendation, an apology, a warning, a forecast, the contents of this message — I've been updating on noise. Which means the framework explains the reliability of every signal in nature except the one species' signals that I actually care about.
9. That's absurd. I obviously do learn things from what people say; half of what I know about the world I got from somebody telling me.

Part of the pull, I'll admit, is that "actions speak louder than words" is basically (6) with better PR, and there's *something* to the fact that we don't just say "I'm committed to you," we hand over a rock with a resale value of about zero. But I don't believe (8), and (6) seems to force it, and I can't see the escape.

So: (9) is true, the conclusion of (1)–(8) isn't acceptable, therefore one of those steps is bad, and I can't tell which. I want to keep (1) and (3) — the idea that reliability has to be purchased is the part I find exciting and giving it up feels like surrendering the whole insight. (5) feels like it's just physiology, not a claim I get to argue with. That leaves me eating (7) or (8), but I don't see how you can, since the babbling equilibrium looks like exactly what happens when faking is free. Tell me which step you'd cut, and why it looked so unobjectionable from the inside when I wrote it down. Then price the alternatives for me: for each step I'd rather keep, what does holding onto it cost, and what has to be true about human communication in particular for the cut to work — is it something about us, or something about the argument? Every time I try to say out loud whats wrong with it I end up defending it again, which is deeply annoying.
