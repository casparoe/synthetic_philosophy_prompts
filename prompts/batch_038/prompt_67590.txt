I direct the publications office at a mid-sized, nonpartisan policy institute. Roughly forty substantive reports and perhaps a hundred shorter commissioned pieces pass across my desk each year, and a large share of them lean on generational labels — in headlines, in section headings, and increasingly in the causal claims themselves. We have no standard for this. Our authors treat a cohort label as though it picked out a kind with a shared inner nature, so that a finding about a birth range is reported as a finding about a people; our reviewers, who are competent editors rather than demographers, cannot say whether any given usage is licensed. I would like a decision procedure my office can run on any draft that contains such a claim.

The core of the procedure should be a distinction I want enforced but have not seen articulated anywhere: the difference between using a cohort label indexically, as a mere shorthand for a range of birth years; using it diagnostically, as a proxy for some variable the study never measured; and using it explanatorily, as the name of a causal factor, on the assumption that the people in the range share a formative history that durably shaped their dispositions. My working hypothesis is that the first is nearly always harmless if the range is stated, that the second is where most of our reports are actually going wrong without knowing it, and that the third should be permitted only on conditions the draft almost never meets. If you disagree with that allocation, say so and defend the alternative.

Build the procedure as an ordered set of tests with explicit pass conditions, so that a reviewer who has never read a methods paper can run it in about forty-five minutes. It should at minimum include: a step requiring every generational claim to be restated in terms of age effects, period effects, and cohort effects, with the draft declaring which it is asserting and confronting the fact that the three are not separately identifiable in a single cross-section of survey data; a boundary test that re-runs the claim on adjacent birth bands and treats survival there as evidence that no cohort is doing the work; a mechanism requirement that names the formative event, the age window at exposure, and the pathway from exposure to lasting disposition; a check for whether people just outside the stated range had the same exposure, in which case the label is misdescribing a gradient; and a within-cohort differentiation check, informed by the old sociological point that a generation's shared social location crystallizes into smaller and often opposed groups rather than a single outlook. State clearly which steps are hard vetoes, which are flags, and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Where the procedure cannot decide, it should hand the question back to me with a one-sentence statement of what is at stake.

Then run it on three cases. First, our flagship annual housing report, whose headline claim is that a generation's blocked access to home ownership has produced a lasting change in its attitudes toward saving and risk, resting on one year of survey data comparing age groups. Second, a short client-commissioned piece that uses a newly coined label for the youngest cohort, whose only stated content is that its members grew up with pervasive digital technology. Third, an internal finding that workers under thirty differ sharply from everyone else on remote-work preference, where our data team's tabulation shows the under-thirty group splitting cleanly along whether the respondent entered the labor market before or after the pandemic shutdowns.

I want the first case to come out badly for us — if your procedure vindicates our flagship report, I want to know why you think that is defensible. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do, including how often it will forbid claims that are in fact true, and draft a standing disclosure note of no more than three sentences that any report retaining an explanatory cohort claim must carry. Please do not include a bibliography or a list of references; I will supply those myself.
