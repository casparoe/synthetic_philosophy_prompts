I am assembling materials for a seminar on counterfactual history, and I need two constructed scenarios rather than real ones — real ones arrive pre-loaded with historiographical disputes, and my students have all heard the familiar ones. The point of the exercise is to drive a wedge between two ideas that the proverbial tradition (the lost horseshoe nail, Pascal's remark that a slightly shorter nose on a certain queen would have changed the face of the earth) runs together: the sensitivity of a system's *trajectory* to small perturbations, and the sensitivity of its *long-run outcome* to small perturbations. My claim is that these come apart in both directions, and I want cases that show it.

**Design target.** Two scenarios, matched as closely as you can manage in surface detail, in which:

- *Case A*: an initiating event of modest size sets off a cascade such that, at the level of particular events — who marries whom, which skirmish is won, which year a given town is sacked — the subsequent history diverges from the actual course more and more as time goes on, so that after a few decades almost no particular is shared. Yet the coarse-grained outcome that a historian would actually periodize by — which polity holds a region, whether a given mode of organizing production takes hold, the level of some long-run variable — ends up the same as in the unperturbed course.
- *Case B*: an initiating event of roughly the *same* magnitude and roughly the same kind produces comparatively little divergence at the level of particulars — the decades that follow look recognizably like the unperturbed course, with the same wars, the same marriages, the same famines — but the coarse-grained outcome is different. The macro-history changes and the micro-history does not.

Note that Case B is the harder half, and that the two cases together invert the intuitive ranking: the case with *more* chaos is the case with *less* historical contingency.

**Constraints.** Both scenarios must be historically plausible, with no invented technologies and no appeal to anything exotic; the initiating events must be of comparable size and type (both, say, a delay of days, or a single decision by a person of no importance). Ideally both are set in the same region and the same century, differing only in the underlying structure of the situation — if that turns out to be impossible while still generating the two outcomes, say so explicitly and give me the closest thing you can achieve. Each scenario should run to no more than about two hundred words and must be written as narrative history: no dynamical-systems vocabulary anywhere in the scenario prose, no mention of attractors, thresholds, or sensitivity, and no authorial signalling of which case is which. State the macro-outcome in advance for each case, so that the reader can check whether it changed.

**Then the analytic section, which is what I actually care about.**

1. Name the structural feature that makes the macro-outcome robust in Case A and the feature that makes it fragile in Case B, first in ordinary historical language and then in the language of dynamical systems.
2. Say which feature is doing the work in each case, and what happens if you remove it — in particular, what Case B looks like if you move the initiating event a little earlier, a little later, or a little further from the frontier.
3. Tell me whether the robustness in Case A is better described as an attractor or as a matter of redundant, mutually replaceable causes, and whether those are the same idea in different vocabularies or genuinely different mechanisms that can come apart.
4. Explain how a structuralist historian, for whom macro-outcomes are fixed by underlying material and demographic conditions, and a contingency-minded historian would each read the pair — and specifically whether the structuralist can accept Case A as a vindication of her view without being committed to explaining Case B away.
5. Close with the practical question: given a real historical situation, what would count as evidence that it sits near a boundary between outcomes rather than deep inside one?

**What would count as cheating.** Asserting the outcomes does not satisfy the brief; the structural features have to generate them. If Case A merely stipulates that in the end the same dynasty rules anyway, or if Case B smuggles in a perturbation much larger than Case A's, the design has failed, and I would rather you tell me the specifications are not jointly satisfiable, and explain which one has to give.
