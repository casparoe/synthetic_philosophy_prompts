My friend and I argued for two hours last night over Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* and reached nothing like a conclusion, so I want a reading from someone able to hold both positions at once.

My line: Stevens is the novel's own counterexample to the communitarian account of the self. His identity is wholly constituted by Darlington Hall and by the office of butler — exactly what MacIntyre, Sandel, and Taylor hold up against the Rawlsian picture of a self antecedently individuated and free to choose its ends — and the result is not a richly situated life but a man structurally incapable of evaluating the ends he has inherited, whose dignity curdles into complicity once his employer turns toward appeasement and orders the dismissal of two Jewish housemaids.

Her reply: I have assumed the liberal standard and then scored Stevens against it. On a communitarian reading the same material looks different — the son who goes on serving at the dinner party while his father dies upstairs is not a failure of autonomy but a man whose obligations, and whose grief, are given their form by the role he inhabits; and the book's elegiac tone is not a verdict.

Give me a reading that takes both seriously rather than a referee's summary. Which scenes actually bear the weight each side puts on them, and which are being over-read? Is the seafront ending — the stranger's remark that evening is the best part of the day, and Stevens's resolution to work on his bantering for Mr Farraday — a first exercise of the capacity to revise one's ends, or simply the assumption of a new role, and why does the novel refuse to settle it? And is what Stevens lacks autonomy at all, or something this debate tends to blur: the difference between being deeply constituted by a role and having handed one's moral judgment to the man who occupies it? Close by saying where each lens fails the book, and what Ishiguro shows about situated selves that the argument between communitarians and liberals does not.
