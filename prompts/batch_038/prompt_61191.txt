I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm stuck on the last third of a book about two brothers — one drives home drunk and gets there fine, the other drives home drunk the same way and kills a kid on a crossing. Before I commit to how the family blames each of them, I read a paperback afterword on the subject that went like this:

"Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel each wrote a famous essay called Moral Luck, and between them they more or less settled the question. Williams's case was the painter who walks out on his family to go and make art: nothing available to him beforehand can tell him whether he was right to go, and the only thing that can justify him is whether the work turns out to be great. Nagel went further and sorted luck into four kinds — the character you were born with, the situations you happen to be dropped into, the way your actions happen to turn out, and the causal chain running back before you existed — and showed that once you take all four seriously the old principle that you're only blameworthy for what you control has nothing left to grip onto. The two men therefore arrived at the same place: blame is a superstition in a moral costume. Which is why the driver who gets home drunk and uneventful and the driver who gets home drunk over a child are morally identical, and only our habits of punishment make the second one look worse."

It's a clean story and I don't want to build an ending on it if its warped. Can you go through it line by line and tell me what's accurate, what's a fair simplification, and what someone who actually works on this would say is flat wrong? Specifically: did Williams and Nagel really end up agreeing, or is that the afterword's doing; does Nagel's four-way catalogue actually deliver "no one deserves blame," or does it deliver something less usable; and is the drunk-driver pair Nagel's example, Williams's, or a blur of two cases that aren't the same case at all? Then tell me what's left standing that I can honestly use — I want the brothers to be genuinely identical in one respect and genuinely different in another, and I'd like to know whether the real argument lets me hold both at once or whether thats the very thing it forbids.
