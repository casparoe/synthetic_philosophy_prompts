I'm in a phil of math seminar and a classmate ran this argument off Wigner's unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics paper, and I think it blows up way more than platonism does, which makes me suspicious of it. Roughly his version:

(1) If positing some entity lets you derive systematically novel predictions that then get confirmed, that success is the best evidence you could have that the posit picks out something real.
(2) Calculus got correct results for something like 150 years by manipulating infinitesimals — quantities smaller than every positive real but not zero — with no coherent account of what they were.
(3) So by (1) infinitesimals were real, and the continuum isn't Archimedean after all.
(4) Same move for the Dirac delta: for decades people treated it as something zero everywhere except at a point, infinite there, integrating to one, and it delivered correct physics, so by (1) there really are such objects over and above whatever rigorous reconstruction came along later.
(5) But (3) and (4) aren't things anyone should believe, and in both cases the predictive success got fully recaptured afterwards by frameworks containing no infinitesimals and no infinite values at all.
(6) So (1) is false.
(7) And since the case for mathematical structures being discovered rather than invented is just (1) applied to structures instead of infinitesimals, the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics tells us nothing whatever about what exists.

I'd rather give up (2) or (4) than (1), because (1) looks like the whole engine behind scientific realism and I don't want to lose that too — but I can't tell if that's just me disliking the conclusion.
