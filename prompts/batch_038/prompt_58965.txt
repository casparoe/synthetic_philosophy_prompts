The question was: "State the pessimistic meta-induction. Then assess whether the selective (divide et impera) response rescues scientific realism, and say what if anything Stanford's problem of unconceived alternatives adds to the pessimist's case."

Student answer, lightly abridged — only repetition cut:

"Laudan's meta-induction runs over the history of science rather than over the world: plenty of theories that were empirically successful and widely accepted in their day posited things we now think don't exist at all — phlogiston, caloric, the optical ether, the crystalline spheres. If success didn't track truth back then, present success gives no reason to think our own posits are real, and that's precisely the inference the no-miracles argument needs.

The standard realist reply is Psillos's divide et impera: split a theory's posits into the ones doing the explanatory and predictive work and the idle ones, and only commit to the former. That reply gets stronger once you check the list Laudan actually uses, since a lot of those posits turn out to be idle or the theory in question wasn't remotely as successful as he claims, which is the point Vickers presses in detail. Once you strip those out what's really left is the Fresnel/ether case, and that's best handled by structural realism: the ether ontology went, but the equations carrying the wave structure passed into Maxwell untouched. So the realist should say what's preserved across theory change is structure, not the nature of what bears it. Since divide et impera and structural realism are just two names for holding onto the working part of a theory, the PMI is defused for anything a realist actually cares about.

Stanford's new induction doesn't add much. He says the historical record shows scientists repeatedly failing to conceive of well-confirmed alternatives to their own best theories — Darwin's is the famous case — so we can't assume our current theories exhaust the space of live options. But he never names an actual unconceived alternative for any current theory, so the argument proves too much: it amounts to saying our theories might be false because better ones are possible, which is just underdetermination, and realists have always conceded that. It's the old PMI with a modal operator bolted on."
