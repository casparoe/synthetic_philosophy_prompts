I'm in a political ethics seminar — the whole term has been about dirty hands in politics, we read the Walzer essay and a bunch of responses — and my professor has this habit of ending class by reading out a hypothetical and giving a verdict on it. She's done seven so far. Next week's is assigned and I want to predict what she'll say, mostly because I think her verdicts are running on a rule she's never actually stated out loud.

Her verdicts so far (cases are my paraphrase, the quoted bits are roughly hers):

1. A wartime leader orders the bombing of a munitions plant inside a city, knowing a few hundred civilians will die, because the alternative is losing the war. → Dirty, and the right call, "but he is not allowed to feel good about it."

2. Same leader, same bombing, except afterwards he gives a speech celebrating the raid and the nerve it took. → Worse than case 1, "and the extra wrong is in the speech, not in the bombs."

3. A police chief in a hostage crisis pretends to negotiate in good faith while signalling his sniper. Hostage-taker shot, hostages saved. → Dirty and right. "Lying to a man you're about to kill is the whole content of the wrong, and it doesn't evaporate because it worked."

4. Two officials authorize the same rights-violating detention program on the same evidence, equally reasonably. In one, the threat is real and an attack is stopped. In the other, the threat was a hoax and nothing was ever prevented. → Identical dirt, different justification. "The second one is dirty and unjustified, and yet he didn't do anything different."

5. A mayor knows a curfew will produce police violence, so he declines to sign it himself and lets the council vote instead. It passes, the violence happens. → Worse than if he'd signed it. "He didn't clean his hands, he spread the dirt across eleven pairs, and now nobody owns any of it."

6. A governor pays a blackmailer out of a discretionary fund to keep an embarrassing photo of her out of the press, because publication three weeks before a vote would kill a coverage expansion. No one is harmed except arguably the treasury. → Not dirty at all. "Cowardly maybe, distasteful, but she didn't wrong anybody. There's nothing to feel."

7. A regulator buries a damaging inspection report on the biggest employer in her district, and the delay lets 400 people be laid off before it surfaces. She did it for the town and she's miserable about it. → Dirty. "And her misery is the least of what's owed, don't you dare let her off because she's unhappy."

Here's next week's case. A senior official at a state health agency learns that a reporter is three weeks from publishing a true story about billing fraud by one of the agency's biggest contractors, and that the story landing before the vote will kill a bill extending coverage to roughly 300,000 people. So the official hands an intermediary a true but deeply private fact about the reporter — got it from a file she was entitled to see, uses it in a way she wasn't — and the intermediary plants it. The reporter's credibility is destroyed and her career is effectively over. The bill passes. The fraud gets cleaned up about six months later anyway, though the official would insist it only got cleaned up because she bought the time. She feels wretched, has told her husband she'll never forgive herself, and refuses — deliberately refuses — to make any public acknowledgment or apology, on the grounds that reopening it would unravel the bill. She has also put a memo describing exactly what she did in a file scheduled to open in 25 years.

So: what does she say? I want more than a guess. Which candidate rules are consistent with all seven verdicts at once — I can see at least one that treats the anguish as the point and one that treats the refusal to go public as the real crime and one that cares mostly about whether the office's accountability machinery was left intact, and I don't know if those are the right three. What does each predict about the new case, where do they agree and where do they split, and which one is she most likely running? Specifically: does the private guilt plus the sealed memo count as owning it in her sense, or is that exactly the case-5 move again? And does the intermediary make it worse the way the council vote did, or is it different because here she's the one holding the pen?

My own instinct is she'll call it dirty and unjustified because of the six-months-later detail, since case 4 suggests she grades justification on outcomes and not on evidence. But I can't tell whether that's right or whether I'm over-reading one example.

Then tell me the case I could float in office hours that would best separate the surviving rules — one where they give different answers, not just different reasons for the same answer. And if her precedents genuinely don't decide the new case, say what she'd be committing herself to with each possible verdict, because I'd like to be able to push on that in class. No citations or reading list, I just want the structure of the reasoning.
