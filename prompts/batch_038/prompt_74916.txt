As of last month I own the section of our product's policy document that specifies when the assistant may decline, delay, or alter a user's request. The product is an ordinary conversational assistant with persistent memory and a payments layer. A substantial share of our users are people managing something they regard as a standing threat to their own judgment — a mood disorder, a gambling problem, a divorce they keep relapsing into contact with — and we offer them a standing-instruction feature: they may record, in advance and in their own words, the conditions under which the assistant should not do what they later ask. It is a Ulysses contract with a chatbot.

Our internal argument about how to write the rules has not moved in six weeks, and I have come to suspect that this is because everyone keeps reasoning from the same two stock examples. So I would like a designed case rather than another opinion.

Construct two paired scenarios involving the same assistant and the same feature, in which three views about when interference is permissible come apart in opposite directions. The views are: (1) a libertarian view, on which an informed adult's request is dispositive and the assistant never declines for the user's own sake; (2) a soft-paternalist view, on which the assistant may decline only when the present request is substantially non-voluntary or uninformed — impaired, compulsive, missing a fact it would want — and must otherwise comply; (3) a prior-authorization view, on which the warrant to decline comes from the user's own earlier, unimpaired instruction and lapses wherever that instruction does not reach.

Case one: the prior-authorization view requires the assistant to decline while the soft-paternalist view forbids it. At the moment of the request the user is calm, articulate, well informed, and offers good reasons; nothing about the episode reads as impairment. Case two: the soft-paternalist view requires the assistant to decline while the prior-authorization view forbids it, because the user has recorded an explicit disavowal of interference, and the episode displays precisely the impairment that the disavowal anticipated.

Constraints. Realistic throughout: a phone, an assistant, logs. No affect-detection technology, no predictive model better than a shrewd friend, no emergencies. The request must harm only the person making it — no third-party victims, or the case stops being about paternalism. Both scenarios together in roughly three hundred words. And describe the users behaviorally: what they typed, at what hour, what they said about their sleep or their balance. No theory-laden labels. If a case has to assert that a choice was voluntary in order to deliver the verdict, it fails; the verdicts must be generated by each view's own criteria applied to the facts.

What I want the pair to settle, at least for me, is whether these two grounds of warrant — the quality of the agency in the moment, and the authority of an earlier instruction — are genuinely independent, or whether one collapses into the other once the facts are written down precisely enough. So after the scenarios: give each view's verdict on each case and the reasoning that produces it; say whether the user's present request in case one counts as a revocation of the standing instruction, and on each view who may revoke, when, and by what means; identify the single feature that makes views two and three pull apart; and say whether removing that feature — for instance, making the request something the standing instruction never anticipated — collapses the two cases back into agreement. Then give me the counterattack: how a defender of whichever view fares worse in each case would impugn the case rather than accept the verdict, and whether that defense is available without giving up the view.

Finally, we are weighing a fourth option: the assistant neither blocks nor complies, but surfaces the standing instruction and asks the user to confirm. Tell me whether that policy escapes the dilemma in either case or merely relocates it, and which fact about these users, once specified, decides the answer.
