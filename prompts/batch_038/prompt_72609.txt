I am two years into a novel whose structure will not survive its own premise unless I can defend it, and what I need from you is a working procedure, not a seminar.

The book is one story told eleven times: eleven mutually exclusive versions of the same six years in the same provincial town, each entered through the same woman's point of view. In four branches her mother dies in the winter of 1946, before she ever meets her father, and she is raised by a different couple. In three branches, the man the town addresses by a certain name turns out, late in the book, not to be the man that name was first attached to. In two branches, the mineral the whole local economy is built on is shown to have a different composition from what the town believes it has. In one branch she was never born at all, and the narration goes on referring to her in the past tense regardless. My developmental editor's standing objection is that these are not eleven versions of one world but eleven unrelated stories wearing borrowed labels: that I have no right to the cross-branch claims the book lives on. Those claims are that this woman is the same woman in each branch; that the man called by that name is not the man; that the mineral in the false-composition branches is the mineral; and that the branch in which she does not exist is still about her.

I want to answer her with a rule I can apply myself, fast, because I am revising against a fixed delivery date and there are eleven branches rather than one.

Build me a decision procedure grounded in what the philosophy of language and metaphysics actually says about names, descriptions, and the necessary-but-knowable-only-afterwards: rigid designation, the essentiality of origins, and the gap between how we identify a thing's bearer and what it takes for a thing to be that thing at all. I know roughly what that apparatus holds. I need it translated into something a novelist can run against a chapter in twenty minutes.

Deliver it in these parts:

1. An ordered list of tests to apply to any cross-branch sentence — a claim of the form *this is the same X in branch n and branch m*. For each test: the question to ask, what counts as passing, and what a failure licenses or forbids (rewrite the sentence; demote it to a claim internal to one branch; cut it; or keep it, because it is precisely the kind of claim the structure earns). Mark which tests are mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed as procedure.
2. A rule for the identification problem — sentences that fix the reference of a name or kind term by description, and the difference between those and sentences that assert identity. Tell me how to spot the difference in my own prose when the same wording can do either job.
3. A rule for kind terms and for artifacts, where the essential feature is composition or origin rather than a person's parentage, and a note on where that rule and the rule for persons come apart.
4. What the procedure says about the branch in which she never exists: whether the narration there is talking about her, about nothing, or about something else, and which choice I should make.
5. A one-page legend my editor and the copy desk can apply without reading any philosophy — the questions in order, with the pass conditions reduced to a line each.
6. The points at which the procedure hands the decision back to me as a matter of what the book is for rather than what is true.

Then run it on three cases and show the working: the four branches where her mother dies in 1946 and she is raised by strangers but keeps the same name, the same face, and the same temperament; the three branches where the man addressed by that name is not the man it was originally attached to, including the sentence I most want to keep, which is that she has known him all her life and never once met him; and the two branches where the mineral's composition is not what the town believes.

Also give me one case where the procedure returns a verdict I will not like. Name a specific cross-branch claim of mine that it forbids, and do not soften it.

Finally, be honest about the limits: where the procedure will misfire on prose that is doing something other than assertion, where it quietly commits me to a metaphysics of persons or kinds I may not want elsewhere in the book, and which distinctions it has to drop in order to be usable.

On commitment: I want one procedure, not a menu of defensible options. Where the theory permits two readings, choose one, give me your reasons, and tell me what the choice costs the novel. If your view is that the premise is incoherent and no procedure can rescue it, say so plainly and name the single structural change that would make it coherent. I would much rather have a definite position to argue against than a survey I have to adjudicate myself.
