In three weeks the collections and metadata committee of our research library's special collections unit votes on a proposal of mine, and the outcome will turn less on the talk than on the question period. I want that question period mapped in advance.

The position I am defending, stated flatly: our unit should stop treating inherited descriptive language as a historical artifact to be shielded from amendment, and start treating the finding aid as an intervention. Concretely, three things. First, we rewrite legacy cataloguing and finding-aid language where it misdescribes the people documented in the records, retaining the superseded wording in a visible note rather than deleting it. Second, we carry community-supplied names, terms, and relationships inside the same authority record as the controlled vocabulary, rather than in a parallel unofficial file that no search reaches. Third, we reweight appraisal toward bodies of record our predecessors never collected. The load-bearing claim, and the one I expect to be attacked, is that this is not cosmetic: because retrieval runs through description, a description that omits, flattens, or distorts a group is itself one of the mechanisms by which that group goes missing from the historical record, so amending it changes what can be found and not merely how it reads. I also intend to concede in the talk, before I am forced to, that description cannot touch the deeper silence — records that were never created because nobody with the standing to make records judged the events worth recording. On that silence my program does nothing at all, and I would rather say so first.

The committee, precisely: two senior metadata specialists who work daily inside LCSH and DACS and regard standardized description as what makes our collections interoperable, citable, and harvestable; a university archivist of thirty years' standing who holds that we describe records as their creator made them and do not import contemporary judgment into the fonds; a regional historian who uses our reading room weekly and reads against the grain cheerfully, and who suspects the whole worry is overblown because competent researchers already discount the catalogue; a development officer who manages relationships with the families named in the accession files; a liaison from general counsel concerned with privacy, culturally restricted material, and characterizations that could be read as defamatory; and, present without a vote, a graduate student who works with a community-based archive across town and regards our entire framing as extractive.

What I want is the prep sheet, not a defense of the proposal. Give me roughly ten objections this room will actually raise — not the ones a general audience would raise, and not the ones I would get at a public meeting or in a theory seminar. Order them by how much damage they would do to my case if left standing. For each one: phrase it in the objector's own register, as that particular person would put it, including the professional vocabulary they would reach for; give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes; and mark the soft spot in that reply, the place where I am relying on an assumption the objector is entitled to reject. For each objection also tell me whether it rests on settled professional practice that I would simply be overriding — in which case I should acknowledge that plainly — or on a matter genuinely contested among people who work in this area, where I can fairly claim the disagreement is live. Do not hand me a contested position as though it were consensus; I would rather know exactly where I am taking a side. Identify the two or three concessions I should make in the first five minutes to buy credibility for everything after, and tell me which single objection has to be answered well or the proposal does not survive the meeting. Finally, name the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one my own framework has no answer to, most likely coming from the graduate student's corner — and tell me what the least dishonest response to it would be.
