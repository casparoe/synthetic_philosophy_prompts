I am a fourth-year graduate student, and for two terms I have been helping prepare the third chapter of my advisor's monograph on moral worth for the press. She is on leave somewhere with unreliable email, and the chapter's remaining work has fallen to me: her marginal note, in pencil, sketches one further example that has to be written up in her voice before the copyediting stage. What I have to work from is her settled list of verdicts on the examples already in the manuscript, plus the five account-names she coins in the introduction and then never defines with any precision, expecting the reader to reconstruct them from her rulings: the Duty Account, the Constraint Account, the Tracking Account, the Recognition Account, and the Endurance Account. Her six recorded verdicts are these.

1. The night nurse charts fluids and turns a patient she actively dislikes every two hours through the night because she takes it to be required of her; there is no warmth in it and she says so, and she agrees that she would not have skipped a turn even if she had come to think skipping permissible. Verdict: moral worth, and she calls it unqualified.
2. The living donor gives a kidney to a stranger because she cannot bear the thought of a person she has never met dying when she could have stopped it; she has never used the word duty about the decision, but she tells us that she first asked whether the gift might exploit her own family's expectations or the recipient's desperation, satisfied herself it did not, and only then went ahead, and her generosity is of long standing. Verdict: moral worth, and she calls it the paradigm.
3. The man of code bails out a neighbour he barely likes because, as he puts it, people of his standing do not leave people in the lurch; pressed, he agrees he would have kept to the code had the code demanded something cruel, and that whether the act was right never crossed his mind. Verdict: no moral worth.
4. The esteem-seeker volunteers weekly at a shelter, does reliably and exactly what is needed, and is moved entirely by the standing he enjoys in one particular circle and by the self-image that standing supports; he holds no moral views on the matter either way. Verdict: no moral worth, with the aside that the acts are right and she has nothing against them.
5. The woman at the stairwell hauls a toddler back from an open landing because she saw the child's hand slip; nothing about right or wrong enters her thinking, and she has no standing habit of asking whether her impulses are permissible. The manuscript does not say whether she would have moved had the child been older, or less visibly in peril. Verdict: moral worth, though rather thin.
6. The young man on the bus gives up his seat to a visibly pregnant woman because he is moved by her discomfort, and insists, meaning it, that courtesies of this kind carry no moral significance at all and that he would have been equally moved had the matter been of no moral import whatsoever. Verdict: moral worth, undiminished by his not thinking of it morally.

The example I now have to write up is the Tuesday reader. A retired man reads aloud every Tuesday to a dying patient he does not know. He says it is the thought of someone being alone at the end that gets him out of the door; obligation plays no part in how he describes it, and he has no habit of asking whether his impulses are permissible. The marginal note adds two further stipulations. First, the visit is unquestioningly right, and the feature that moves him — the patient's aloneness — is the feature that makes it right; he would not deny it if you told him the visits were a good thing, though he would not put it that way himself. Second, two years ago, moved by the same kind of impulse on a friend's behalf, he lied to a hospital administrator to have the friend admitted sooner; the lie did real harm, and he says he would do it again. The note is silent on whether he would keep coming if the patient recovered, or were moved to another ward, or turned out to be unpleasant company. What I need to know is what she will say about him: moral worth, no moral worth, or moral worth at a reduced grade.

Please work entirely from this stipulated record; no bibliography or list of sources is wanted, and no quotation from her manuscript beyond what I have given. Structure the answer in exactly these sections, in this order: **Bottom line** (three or four sentences naming your predicted verdict, the grade if she grades it, and the account you think best explains her record); **The accounts and the record** (about 500 words: for each of her five accounts, whether the six verdicts rule it out, strain it, or fit it, and which familiar position in the literature it most closely resembles — Kant's duty-only view, the limiting-condition refinement of it, the sympathy-friendly accounts that treat responsiveness to the right-making features as sufficient, the worry about moral fetishism, the knowledge-first accounts — saying where your reconstruction departs from the familiar version); **The Tuesday reader, account by account** (about 350 words: each surviving account's verdict, and in particular what each makes of the earlier lie and of the two silences in the note, including what would have to be stipulated to remove them); **My prediction** (about 200 words, with a rough probability that she awards any moral worth at all and a separate probability that she grades it rather than giving it flat); **The example that would settle it** (about 150 words: the single further case she should add that would come out differently under your two leading accounts, with the stipulations that make it do that work); and **Where her record is in tension** (about 150 words: any place where no single account can accommodate all six verdicts, which verdict she should be prepared to revise, and how the tension between her rather thin verdict on the stairwell and her undiminished verdict on the bus seat bears on the Tuesday reader).
