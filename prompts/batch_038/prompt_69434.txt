I am assembling cases to test whether theories of personal identity can answer a question they usually dodge: not merely whether I survive, but where I am. Dennett's scenario in "Where Am I?" — the narrator's brain is removed and kept alive in a vat while his body, still walking and talking, is driven remotely — makes the location question vivid, but it does so with a jump cut. The brain comes out, the body keeps moving, and the puzzle is posed all at once. I want a version with no jump cut. Please construct a thought experiment to the following specification.

**Purpose of the case.** The point is to force three theories that normally disagree only about survival to commit to a spatial answer as well, and to do it along a continuum rather than at a single moment. A case that merely restates the standard survival dispute, or that smuggles in the verdicts it is supposed to generate, fails.

**The scenario.** A subject, call him S, begins as an ordinary adult human being with his brain in his skull. Under a surgical program, each neuron of his brain is removed one at a time and, at the moment of removal, replaced by a component housed in a laboratory a thousand miles away, which assumes that neuron's functional role and communicates with the rest of the system by radio. The cranial cavity is progressively vacated. The body in the operating theatre stays alive, awake, talking, and eventually walking around, driven by hardware that is increasingly elsewhere. The transfer runs to completion.

**Constraints.**

1. Continuous consciousness. S is never unconscious, never interrupted, never replaced. He is conversing throughout and reporting what it is like from the inside. He never reports being nowhere, and never reports being in two places at once.
2. No duplication. At every moment there is exactly one stream of consciousness and exactly one candidate person. No second body, no backup, no copy, no fission and reunion.
3. Permitted technology only. The vat-and-radio arrangement, functional neuron-for-neuron replacement, and the ordinary biology of a living human body. No uploading into a machine that is not driving the body, no teleportation, no disembodied minds, no souls, no revival or resurrection.
4. Clinical texture. It should read like a hospital research program with a surgical team and a monitoring protocol, not like a science-fiction premise. A single continuous narrative, at most 300 words.

**What the case must deliver.** At three checkpoints — 25 percent of the brain transferred, 50 percent, and 100 percent — the following three theories must give different answers, with the differences falling out of each theory's own commitments rather than being stipulated: (a) a psychological-continuity theorist, for whom what matters is the right kind of causally sustained psychological connection; (b) an animalist, for whom S is a human organism and his identity consists in that organism's continued life; (c) a brain-based theorist, for whom S goes where his brain goes. At least one checkpoint must produce agreement across all three theories and at least one must produce a three-way split; you must explain why the split arises at that checkpoint and not at the other. At every checkpoint, each theory owes two answers: does S survive, and where is S — answered with a place, not evaded. No theory may answer that identity is indeterminate at a checkpoint unless you can show the indeterminacy follows from resources the theory already has, in which case say which ones.

**Format.** Please organize the response under these headings, in this order, and keep to the rough lengths given:

1. *The Scenario* — about 300 words. The narrative, satisfying constraints 1 through 4.
2. *S's Reports* — about 120 words. What S says, in his own words, at each of the three checkpoints about where he is and what he is seeing.
3. *Checkpoint Verdicts* — about 350 words. A table with the three checkpoints as rows and the three theories as columns, each cell giving the survival verdict, the location, and a one-line reason. Follow the table with roughly 100 words identifying which checkpoint yields the three-way split and why it is that one.
4. *What Does the Work* — about 150 words. Name the single feature of the case that generates the disagreement, then say what happens to each theory's verdict if that feature is removed.
5. *The Jump-Cut Version* — about 120 words. Re-run the case with the entire transfer completed instantaneously between two moments, and state which verdicts change and which do not.
6. *The Best Objection* — about 150 words. The strongest reply available to whichever theory comes out worst at the split checkpoint, and whether that reply actually defeats the case or merely relocates the difficulty.
7. *Bottom Line* — about 60 words. Whether a single case can genuinely make all three theories answer a location question, or whether the location question collapses back into the survival question.

Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a survey of who has defended what; I want the reasoning, not the scholarship. If some part of the specification turns out to be unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and explain why, rather than quietly bending it.
