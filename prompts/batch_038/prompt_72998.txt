What I'm presenting isn't just REU though — I want to defend a restricted version of it that I've been calling one-shot risk weighting, and I need help anticipating where it falls over. The claim: rank-dependent risk attitudes, where the weight on an outcome depends on its rank in the distribution and not just on its probability, are legitimate only when the decision is genuinely non-replayable — one shot, irreversible, the outcome lands once on a life you can't re-gamble — whereas plain expected utility governs repeatable or decomposable gambles, where the agent can commit to a policy and the stakes come in many small bites. My argument is three steps: Allais-type violations of independence are systematic and stable rather than performance error, so we need something non-orthodox in the toolbox; but unrestricted REU is exposed to the usual money-pump and dynamic-incoherence stuff (the Hammond-style line), so it can't be the whole story; and the way to buy off the dynamic objections is to confine risk-weighting to decisions that can't be decomposed into a sequence, using something like McClennen's resolute choice where I need it. The fun part, and its also the part I know is exposed: the view predicts a split. Big one-shot choices (take the job at the risky startup, have the risky surgery, that kind of thing) get risk-weighting, but the small-stakes risk aversion the psychologist keeps finding is either irrational or evidence that subjects are misframing a repeatable gamble as a one-shot one — and Rabin's calibration result does a lot of work for me there, since it shows the orthodox theory can't accommodate small-stakes aversion without absurd large-stakes aversion.
