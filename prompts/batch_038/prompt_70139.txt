I'm a machine learning PhD student who's been auditing a philosophy of science seminar this term, mostly because my advisor thought it would help me write better introductions, and I've come out of it more confused then when I started. Last week was Humeanism about laws: the picture where a law isn't anything over and above a regularity that earns its place in the best summary of what actually happens, the summary being chosen by trading simplicity off against how much it gets right, versus the rival picture where laws are supposed to govern or produce events and a mere tidy summary of the mosaic isn't enough. I followed all that. What I couldn't stop thinking about is that what I do at my desk all day looks suspiciously like the first view implemented as a piece of software, and nobody in the room seemed bothered by the connection.

Concretely: nothing I fit is ever judged by fit alone. I score candidates with a criterion that subtracts a penalty growing with the number of free parameters, or equivalently I ask how many bits it takes to write down the model plus the data given the model, and I reject the model that fits the observed points best — sometimes fits them perfectly — precisely because it has too many knobs. We call that overfitting and treat it as a kind of intellectual dishonesty. Meanwhile my colleagues in the physics department do exactly this and then turn around and insist that a real law has to be exceptionless, that you can't buy simplicity by tolerating a few violations, that a law is not the sort of thing that could lose out to a competitor on a score. So here's what I want from you: what does the penalty term actually presuppose about lawhood? Is penalized model selection only intelligible on a Humean metaphysics, where the candidates are summaries and there's a real trade to be struck, or can someone who believes in governing relations use it without embarrassment — is the penalty a claim about the world (that the truth is short, that nature is simple) or just a hedge against the fact that I only ever get a finite, noisy sample? And are the two things I use even the same commitment? Because one family of criteria is aimed at picking the best predictor and will happily keep parameters forever no matter how much data you feed it, while the other is aimed at identifying the true model and gets harsher as the sample grows, and those sound like different projects with different assumptions about whether there's a fact of the matter to find. Plus cross-validation, which never mentions simplicity at all and just asks how the model does on held-out points.
