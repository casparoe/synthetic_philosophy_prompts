**Unspendable evidence: higher-order defeat and the withdrawal of licence**

**1. The case.** You have spent the afternoon tallying ward returns in a local election alongside a colleague. There are forty-odd returns; you have added them twice; the total looks to you exactly right, and it puts your candidate ahead by a hundred and eleven votes. A supervisor then comes in and tells you both that the tea urn in the counting room was mistakenly filled from a tank that had been treated with a sedative, that the sedative is known to produce small arithmetical slips, and — this is the part that stings — that those slips feel, to the person making them, entirely obvious and correct. Your colleague re-checks her own tally and finds two errors in it. You re-check yours. It still looks right; but of course it would.

**2. Three familiar responses.** The steadfast response says that nothing about your first-order evidence has changed: the returns are what they are, they entail the total, and testimony about your own faculties is not testimony about the election. You should go on believing that your candidate won. The conciliatory response says that your total evidence now includes a proposition about your unreliability, and that this lowers the degree to which your evidence supports the conclusion; you should drop your credence substantially, perhaps to somewhere near the middle. The level-splitting response says you should do both at once: believe, on the first-order evidence, that your candidate won, and believe, on the higher-order evidence, that this belief of yours is irrational.

**3. A shared assumption.** All three treat the question as though the agent had a single pool of evidence, and as though the only issue were how strong a support relation that pool bears to the conclusion. The steadfast view says the higher-order material does not belong in the pool; the other two say it does. But the disagreement, I want to suggest, is generated by a picture on which believing well consists in tracking what one's evidence supports, and on which evidence is either in play or else not there at all.

**4. The proposal.** Distinguish possessing evidence from being licensed to deploy it. Higher-order defeat of the kind in the case does not add countervailing evidence about the election, and it does not weaken the support that the returns lend to the total. It withdraws a permission. The returns remain excellent evidence for the conclusion, and they become, for you and for the next hour, unspendable: something you have and may not use. The sedative testimony does not bear on the election; it bears on your standing to reason from the returns.

**5. Why this dissolves the akrasia worry.** The level-splitting view is thought to court a Moore-paradoxical predicament: believing p while believing that believing p is irrational. But the norm against akrasia is not plausibly a norm against the mere co-occurrence of two attitudes with those contents. It is a norm about basing: you ought not to hold a belief in a way you yourself regard as contrary to your lights. On the licence view, when the permission is withdrawn the route is closed. You cannot form or sustain the belief by reasoning from the returns, because that is precisely what you may no longer do. If the belief nonetheless persists — as beliefs do, arriving by memory of a conclusion rather than by fresh inference — it is not held against your lights. It is held under lights you once had and that have since been withdrawn. That is a residue, not an akrasia, and it is criticisable in a different and much milder way: you ought to let it lapse and you ought not to act on it, but you are not divided against yourself.

**6. A distinctive prediction.** The licence view and the total-evidence view should come apart over restoration. Suppose the supervisor returns an hour later, embarrassed: the urn was fine, the treated water never reached the counting room, your colleague's two errors were her own and long-standing. On the licence view your position is exactly what it was before the interruption. The returns never stopped supporting the total, and the permission to rely on them was never anything but suspended. Your belief is restored at once, and restored without re-investigation: you need not add the columns a third time, because nothing was ever subtracted. On the total-evidence view, by contrast, your evidence about the election was first weakened and then strengthened, and the fact that the weakening left no trace is a coincidence — a lucky cancellation between the discredit attaching to your arithmetic and the removal of that discredit. The licence view explains the asymmetry between withdrawal and restoration by saying that only one of them changed your position at all.

**7. Two worries I have not solved.** First, notational variance. A total-evidence theorist will say that to possess E without being permitted to deploy it is just a manner of saying that your total evidence, which includes the sedative testimony, does not support the conclusion to the degree that E alone would. If that redescription is always available, the licence view has added vocabulary rather than content, and the asymmetry pressed in Section 6 is one it can mimic: the cancellation is not lucky but structural, since the defeating material concerned only your faculties, and once it is gone nothing about the election remains on either side of the ledger. My inclination is that the redescription is available but lossy — it cannot say what was restored, because on the total-evidence view nothing was ever taken away — and I am aware that this begins to sound like a complaint about phrasing.

Second, individuation. The higher-order evidence concerns my arithmetic. But much of what I know about the election I know by perception and memory of the individual returns, and by simple comparisons the sedative plausibly does not touch. So which licences are withdrawn? If all of them, the view makes the agent far too helpless: she cannot even trust that forty is more than thirty. If only the arithmetical one, the view owes an account of how the scope of a licence is fixed by the scope of the doubt cast upon it, and I do not have one. I suspect the honest answer is that licences are individuated by the competences the higher-order evidence impeaches, and that this will not always yield a determinate verdict.

**8. Conclusion.** I think the licence view is worth keeping, but for a reason weaker than the one advertised in Section 6. Its advantage is not that it predicts different verdicts; it is that it makes sense of the phenomenology of the case — of the fact that the tally looks right and that this looking right has come to count for nothing — without having to say that the agent is at odds with herself. If that is all it does, it is a view about how to describe defeat rather than a rival account of it, and I should probably say so in the title.

3. 4. Section 5 relocates akrasia from the co-occurrence of attitudes to the way a belief is based, and then claims the residue belief escapes the charge. Does it escape, or has the problem merely been moved?

5. My sketches of the steadfast, conciliatory and level-splitting responses in Section 2 are compressed. Tell me whether any of them is misdescribed in a way that makes the shared-assumption diagnosis in Section 3 too easy to draw.
