Three quarters through a novel whose back third turns on an advance directive, and every draft of the scene I do collapses into the stock version: lucid woman signs something, gets dementia, turns sweet and contented, family argues. I want it harder than that, and I want you to build the case to spec rather than give me the lit review.

Specs:
- one paragraph, fully realistic. Care home or hospital, no sci-fi, no memory-restoring procedure, no doctor who can tell from a scan what's really going on inside her head.
- the patient, at the time of the decision, has to have what Dworkin calls critical interests and not just experiential ones: a settled reflective view about how her life as a whole should go, put in her own words. That's deliberate — I want the usual Dresser move ("she only cares now about how things feel") to be unavailable to anyone.
- those current critical interests have to conflict with the directive, and the case must not let anyone tell whether the change since she signed is a break in psychological continuity or a genuine development of the same person. The evidence available to the characters has to be honestly balanced. Don't settle it by stipulation — that's cheating, and the whole point is to see whether the three accounts can be prised apart at all in a realistic setting.

Then do the check. Say what Dworkin's account says about honoring the directive and why, what Dresser's says, and what someone holding the personal-identity objection (the signer and the woman in the bed aren't the same person anymore) says. I expect the identity theorist to come out as the odd one here; tell me if that's wrong. Finish with which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the verdicts if I make that feature obvious, and how a defender of Dworkin would attack the case — I assume he'd deny the patient can have critical interests at all, and I want to know what that concession costs him.
