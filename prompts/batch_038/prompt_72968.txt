I am assembling my own view of when a legendary narrative should be counted as history, and I have reached the point where my verdicts look arbitrary even to me. Below are the cases I have thought hardest about, with the verdict I actually reach in each. I want to know whether there is a principle, or a short set of principles, that generates them; where such a principle fails; and which of my verdicts it obliges me to give up.

Troy. The mound at Hissarlik, its stratigraphy, and a destruction layer in the late thirteenth century BC: history. A ten-year coalition war caused by an abducted queen: not history. But the Catalogue of Ships in the second book of the Iliad lists polities and places that fit the Bronze Age far better than the poet's own eighth century, including sites that were already insignificant or abandoned by his day, and I find myself willing to call the Catalogue a historical document. So I accept a fragment of the poem as evidence about the world it describes while rejecting the narrative that carries it.

The Hittite archive. Wilusa, its king Alaksandu and his treaty with Muwatalli II, the warlord Piyamaradu, the kingdom of Ahhiyawa across the sea, and a letter recording a quarrel between the Hittites and Ahhiyawa over Wilusa: history, all of it. But it is history about Anatolian diplomacy, and I do not think it corroborates a Trojan War. I am also unmoved by the fact that a king of Wilusa bore a name resembling the second name of Paris; that strikes me as coincidence-hunting dressed up as corroboration.

Roncevaux. Charlemagne's biographer Einhard records that in 778 the Frankish rearguard was ambushed in a Pyrenean pass by Basques and wiped out, and he names its commanders, among them Roland, who held the Breton march. History. The eleventh-century Song of Roland, which turns the Basques into Saracens, supplies a treasonous kinsman, and converts a military disaster into a martyrdom: legend, and worse, a legend that damages the history underneath it. Yet I also think the poem is the reason anyone remembers the ambush at all, and that unsettles me.

Arthur. That something like a British victory over advancing Saxons occurred around the year 500: probably history. That there was a man called Arthur who won it: not history. The Welsh annals compiled at St David's in the mid tenth century place a battle of Badon under a year corresponding to about 516, in which Arthur is said to have carried the cross of Christ on his shoulders for three days and three nights while the Britons were victorious — a detail that may be nothing more than a confusion of the Old Welsh words for shield and shoulder. The same annals, under a year corresponding to about 537, record a strife at Camlann in which Arthur and Medraut fell, with mortality in Britain and Ireland. From the first entry I want to keep the battle and discard the miracle. From the second I want to keep nothing, not even the man, although its register is precisely the dry one I ordinarily trust.

The Exodus. As narrated: not history. Egypt administered Canaan at the period in question, so the destination of the flight was itself under Egyptian control; there is no material trace of a large population moving through Sinai; and Egyptian records, which are not reticent about frontier troubles, say nothing. But the victory stele of Merneptah, around 1200 BC, speaks of Israel as a people in Canaan, and I count that as history without hesitation. I am further inclined to grant a kernel: that some small group of Semitic slaves left Egypt, and that their story was later magnified into a national origin myth.

The Sea Peoples. Almost everything we have comes from Egyptian royal inscriptions — triumphalist, formulaic, commissioned — and yet I count them as history, largely because destruction layers across the Levant and Anatolia fall where the inscriptions say they should.

The Donation of Constantine. A forgery, almost certainly composed in the eighth century and exposed in the fifteenth. I nonetheless count it among the most important documents of the early Middle Ages. Likewise Geoffrey of Monmouth's history of the kings of Britain, written in the 1130s, most of whose content is invented: in my judgement a first-rate source, but a source for the twelfth century rather than for the sixth.

The Burgundians. That a king called Gundahar and much of his people were destroyed around 436 by the Roman general Aetius with his Hunnic auxiliaries, near Worms: history. The Nibelung material that grew out of it, in which Attila presides over the annihilation of a Burgundian court and a hoard and a dragon are added: legend. But the later Burgundian law code lists among the king's predecessors Gibica, Gundomar, Gislahar and Gundahar — a dynasty citing its own legendary ancestors inside a legal text. I count that list as history about how a ruling house remembered itself, and as no evidence whatever about the three names that are not Gundahar.

Here is the inconsistency that troubles me most. The Catalogue of Ships and the Burgundian ancestor list look to me like the same kind of thing: a name-list in a workaday register, preserved within a tradition that later elaborated it into legend. In the first case I let the list carry historical weight about the past it names; in the second I allow it weight only about its compilers. I cannot say why I treat them differently.

My own tentative principle has two clauses, which I will call the trace clause and the corruption clause.

Trace: a narrative counts as history only where the event it reports left a trace independent of the tradition reporting it — a destruction layer, a foreign archive, an inscription, a place-name confirmed on the ground, a surviving institution.

Corruption: traditions distort in predictable directions, towards aggrandisement, moral clarity, famous enemies in place of obscure ones, the conversion of a rout into a sacrifice, the compression of structural causes into individual persons. A narrative counts as history in proportion to how much of its content is of a kind those pressures leave alone: the mundane, the unflattering, the geographically specific, the useless to the teller.

Please test this pair against the verdicts above and repair it. In particular, I want an answer to each of the following.

1. Do the two clauses jointly generate my verdicts, or only most of them? State the repaired principle or principles precisely, and for each clause identify the case in my list that motivates it together with the case that would have been misjudged without it.

2. I suspect I am sliding between two different claims: that a story is about something that really happened, and that a story is itself an artefact worth using as evidence. If so, does that distinction dissolve my inconsistencies or merely rename them? Can one principle govern both senses, or do I need two criteria plus a rule for telling which sense a given question is using?

3. Which of my verdicts cannot be saved by any reasonably simple principle? If you can preserve all of them, say what it costs in complexity; if you cannot, say which one or two go, and why those rather than some other pair.

4. Take the Catalogue of Ships against the Burgundian ancestor list. Give me a diagnosis. If the two cases are genuinely alike, tell me which way I should move, and what I lose by moving.

5. Apply your principle to cases I have not raised: Ragnar Lodbrok; Robin Hood; the hut foundations on the Palatine as against Romulus and the she-wolf; the Kurukshetra war of the Mahabharata; the historicity of Homer himself; the career of the Buddha; and Constantine's vision before the Milvian Bridge, as reported by a near-contemporary imperial biographer who claims to have had it from the emperor and confirmed under oath. In that last case I want to keep the propaganda and lose the vision; does your principle permit this, or does it drive the two apart?

6. Several of my verdicts are comparative rather than absolute: I say that one story is more history than another. Should the criterion output a threshold, an ordering, or both? If an ordering, say what makes it well behaved rather than a mere restatement of my prejudices.

Please write your answer in British English. Keep the discussion tied to the cases and to the reasoning they support, rather than to the course of the scholarly debate about them: I am not asking for a survey of what historians have said, but for the principle my verdicts commit me to.
