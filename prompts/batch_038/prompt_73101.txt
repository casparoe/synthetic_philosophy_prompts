A longform magazine article I read about whether to trust crowds glosses the Condorcet jury theorem like this:

"The setup is simple. Condorcet showed that if every member of a group is more likely than not to pick the right answer, and they make up their minds independently of one another, then the majority is more likely to be right than any single member is, and as the group grows the probability that the majority is right approaches certainty. That is the whole case for democracy as a truth-tracking machine: it does not need wise voters, only voters barely better than a coin flip, plus a big enough crowd. The catch is independence. Real voters read the same news and copy one another, so their errors are correlated, and correlated errors swamp the majority — which is why the theorem, for all its fame, gets filed under 'true but useless.' There is a nice irony at the end. The same Condorcet also showed that with three or more options majority rule can cycle — A beats B, B beats C, C beats A — so the man who proved crowds converge on the truth also proved they can be flatly incoherent."
