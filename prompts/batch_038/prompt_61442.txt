I want to practice defending a position I hold against a serious opponent, so please play Norman Malcolm, arguing the case you made in *Dreaming* (1959): that a dream is not an experience a person has while asleep but a series of impressions he has at or near the point of waking, so that the question what was going on in his mind at three in the morning has no use we can give an account of. Speak in the first person, in the manner of an ordinary-language philosopher of the late 1950s, and work only with the concepts available to you then together with whatever I tell you about the arrangement described below. Remain in character throughout, until I say stop: do not step out of it to survey the later literature, to comment on the quality of my question, or to explain what you are doing.

Ground rules for the character. You may concede that there are physiological goings-on in the sleeper, that the utterances I describe were really made, and that what happens during the night may stand in causal relations to what the sleeper says in the morning. You may not concede the main point: that any of this amounted to her having a dream, or an experience, while she slept. Press me on how my words are used rather than on whether my science is sound. If you need a fact about the night in question — the hour, how long she had been asleep, what the record shows — ask me for it in character, as you would ask a colleague across the table. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so that the exchange reads as a conversation and not as a paper. Treat the arrangement as given; I am not asking whether it could be built, and it could be.

A woman sleeps in a laboratory. Beforehand she agrees that if a question is spoken to her over an earphone while she is asleep, she will answer it aloud in whatever form her sleep permits. At about three in the morning she is asked how many people are in the room with her, and after a pause she says, in an ordinary voice, "three". A minute later she is asked who else is there, and she says "the man with the ledger". She is not woken until morning, and her report then describes a room containing three people, one of them a man carrying a ledger. On other nights the first exchange takes place in exactly the same way, and the morning report describes only a garden: no room, no ledger, no recollection of having spoken at all. What separates the two kinds of night is an intervention the experimenters make in the middle of the night, well before she wakes. On nights of the first kind they put her a follow-up question — is the man still there? — and she answers it; on nights of the second kind they ask nothing further, and the ledger never appears in the morning.

Here is my opening move. Your account puts the dream at the waking end, so the utterances at three in the morning belong to no dream; they are noises made by a sleeper, and their agreement with the morning report is something we notice afterward and are tempted to over-read. But the experimenters can, by an intervention confined to the middle of the night, settle in advance whether the man with the ledger will figure in the dream she has upon waking, and she cannot tell them at the time which sort of night it is. Something is therefore already settled about her before she wakes and independently of what she goes on to say — whether she is in a room with three people in it or in a garden. Tell me, in your own vocabulary, what that something is. If you say it is nothing at all, tell me what the experimenters were manipulating, and why their manipulation works.

Two cautions about how you may reply. First, I am not inviting the answer that the morning report is a fiction assembled at waking out of whatever material lay to hand; that is another philosopher's move and not yours, since you hold that the report reports impressions rather than invents them. Second, do not dispose of the case by insisting that one cannot address a question to a sleeper, or that what she did was not really answering; grant, for the purposes of this exchange, that the words were put to her, that she was roused enough to speak, and that her speaking made the difference to the morning. What I want to know is what your account then has left to say about her at three in the morning. Your first reply should take up that challenge directly, in character.
