Two welfare-focused researchers have spent years arguing past each other over whether habitat destruction increases or decreases the total amount of wild animal suffering. One holds that intact ecosystems are net-negative-welfare places dominated by r-selected species whose enormous broods mostly die of exposure, starvation, or parasitism, so that clearing or fragmenting habitat, however bad for biodiversity, reduces suffering. The other holds that established ecosystems are on balance net-positive, that habitat loss kills and displaces mature animals into marginal terrain, and that survivors' lives get worse through edge effects, invasive predators, crowding, and road mortality.

Draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration ought to have produced. Open with a numbered list of claims both could sign — about which taxa are sentient and with what confidence, about how populations actually respond to loss and fragmentation, about the difference between total and average welfare, and about the reliability limits of current ecological forecasting. Follow it with a table of the residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept, and each marked as substantive, verbal, or turning on empirical facts nobody yet has.

Then state the cruxes: the sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one of the two. For each, propose a case or study design both would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Use this stipulated result as your first test case: a rigorous twenty-year study of a selectively logged tropical plot finds that vertebrate individual numbers fell by 60 percent, while mean body condition and parasite loads improved and the monthly rate of starvation and predation deaths among surviving individuals also fell. Explain what each researcher should say about it, and why they nonetheless remain opposed. Reason from the case; do not cite or invent a literature.
