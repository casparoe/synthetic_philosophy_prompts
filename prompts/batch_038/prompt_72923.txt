A friend and I spent most of last night on the Bank Restriction of 1797 and reached no agreement, so I am putting the question to you in as neutral a form as I can manage.

The episode, as I trust my recollection of it: in 1797, with gold draining from the Bank of England during the war with France, Parliament suspended the convertibility of Bank notes into specie, and the suspension lasted until 1821. Over the following years prices rose, and the paper pound fell against gold and against the Hamburg exchange. The parliamentary bullion committee — Horner, Thornton, and their colleagues — reported in 1810 that the depreciation followed from an excessive note issue and urged a return to cash payments within a couple of years; Ricardo pressed the same case in pamphlet. The Bank's directors and their defenders replied that notes issued only against good short-term commercial bills cannot be excessive, since the quantity follows the needs of trade, and that whatever rise in prices and fall in the exchange had occurred was the work of bad harvests, blockade, and the cost and danger of shipping bullion in wartime. Peel's Act of 1819 set the timetable for resumption, and in 1821 the note was again made convertible at the pre-1797 parity — a choice that required deflation and generated considerable agrarian distress.

Now the theory. Berkeley's monetary writings — the numbered queries of *The Querist*, with their proposal for an Irish national bank issuing paper, and the treatment of money in *Alciphron* — hold that money is not wealth but a ticket or counter; that its value lies entirely in what it does, namely to circulate goods and, above all, to excite and direct industry; that the metal it happens to be made of is a convenience and nothing more; and that a public issue of paper secured on the domestic wealth of the country could do the work of specie better than specie did. In the closing dialogue of *Alciphron*, words do their work not by raising corresponding ideas in a hearer but by disposing him to act — and my friend insists that money, for Berkeley, is a sign of exactly that kind.

His claim: 1797–1821 is Berkeley vindicated, a quarter-century in which a great commercial nation ran on inconvertible paper that kept functioning, the metallic superstition removed, the economy not merely intact but able to finance a total war. My claim: the same episode destroys him, because if money is only a counter Berkeley has nothing to say about why the counters lost value — no quantity-of-money mechanism, no anchor — and the exchange-rate and gold-premium evidence the bullionists deployed is precisely what his account cannot register; the anti-bullionist real-bills position then looks like Berkeleyanism with the theory of value stripped out.

Please work through this as a case study rather than taking either side in advance. First, map the actors onto the theory: taken seriously, what does Berkeley's account commit the Bank's directors, the bullionists, and the anti-bullionists to, and which camp is the Berkeleyan one, if either? Second, say which features of the episode the account genuinely explains or would have predicted — that circulation continued uninterrupted, the part money plays in mobilizing industry for war, the possibility of an issue secured on domestic wealth — and which it misses or misdescribes, in particular the depreciation of the inconvertible pound, the evidential role of the foreign exchange, and the 1819 decision to resume at the old parity together with the deflation that followed. Third, tell me what the episode does to the theory: does it confirm it, expose an anomaly within it, or belong to a class of case — a sovereign paper money that has left its standard during wartime — that Berkeley was never addressing, since he wrote for a specie-poor Ireland and was proposing a bank rather than diagnosing a suspended currency? And fourth, does the sign-theoretic reading help or hurt here: if money's content is fixed by its role in a practice rather than by a metal, does that give Berkeley resources to explain why some token issues hold their value while others slide, or does it leave the token with nothing to be a token of?

I want the argument, not the sources — no bibliography, no pointing me toward the literature, and no hedging about who said exactly what in 1810. Please keep the whole thing to 700 words at most.
