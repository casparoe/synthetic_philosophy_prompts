I am three years into a novel whose second half is set almost entirely in a memory-careard unit, and the object the book turns on is not a person but a document: a signed advance directive, drafted by a retired structural engineer in her late sixties while she was still entirely herself, naming her daughter as proxy and refusing antibiotics, artificial nutrition, and resuscitation in the event that she reaches a state from which, in the judgment of two physicians, she will not return. To get the procedural texture right I have sat in on hospital ethics consultations, read state statutes on living wills and durable powers of attorney for health care, and read what I could find about the Dutch position, where in 2020 the Supreme Court held that a physician may carry out euthanasia on the strength of a written request from a patient with advanced dementia even when she can no longer confirm it, provided the statutory due-care criteria are satisfied. A lawyer friend of mine referred to all of this as precedent autonomy, and I have come to suspect that an enormous quantity of metaphysics is being smuggled through that phrase. I would like it unpacked, because I intend my scenes to carry the argument rather than recite it.

The practice I cannot make sense of has four features that sit oddly together. First, the document is treated as binding over a person who is, in every ordinary sense, no longer the one who wrote it: she has no memory of drafting it, does not recognize her daughter, holds no desires about next week, and is frequently and visibly content — she sings, she enjoys her meals, she asks to go home. The paper outranks the living woman, and it does so precisely because the living woman is thought to lack the standing to speak. Second, her apparent present wishes are sometimes given weight and sometimes not: many institutional protocols hold that a directive lapses if the patient now resists or says she wants to live, though she may be judged incapable of the capacity that would make such a statement authoritative. Third, and in the other direction, the same legal culture treats psychiatric advance directives very differently. Many American states permit a person, while well, to write down what treatment she wants and refuses during a future psychotic or manic episode; the statutes then commonly allow clinicians to override that document exactly when the episode arrives and the patient is involuntarily held — which is the only circumstance in which the document was ever meant to operate. Fourth, the same person who is denied authority over her own future when she is demented is granted unlimited authority to revoke the directive at any moment while competent, by any means, including a shrug.

So: what theory does this body of practice presuppose? I want the implicit philosophy reconstructed as carefully as you can manage. What must be true of persons over time for a signature made forty years earlier, by someone with whom the present patient shares almost no memories, values, or intentions, to govern her body now — is this a claim about identity, about the persistence of interests after the capacity to hold them has been destroyed, about autonomy as something one exercises once rather than continuously, or about welfare as the shape of a whole life rather than the sum of its experiences? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out — in particular, any view on which the demented patient is, in the respect that matters morally, a different person from the one who signed? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit well with, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license the same forms, the same committees, and the same language of capacity. Then address the asymmetry with the psychiatric directive: does it betray an inconsistent underlying view, or does it rest on something non-philosophical entirely — liability, the epistemic difficulty of predicting one's own future psychosis, a professional norm about who may restrain whom? And finally, if the presupposed view were replaced by one on which only the present self's experience counts, what specifically would have to change in the documents, the consultation process, and the vocabulary of incompetence — and what, in that revised practice, would a directive of the kind my protagonist wrote be for?
