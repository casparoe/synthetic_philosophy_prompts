**QUESTION 3.** Between 1962 and 1970 the government of Vardia removed the Kerrin, an indigenous minority of the northern highlands, from their villages and settled them in camps on the coast, opening the highlands to mining and commercial farming. The removals were carried out under emergency regulations and Vardia's own national archive holds the signed orders. No individual was ever prosecuted. Within a generation the Kerrin had lost roughly two-thirds of their speakers of the language. This spring the Vardian parliament carried a motion "recording its deep regret at the suffering caused by the removals" and establishing a regional development fund for the north. The motion does not use the word apology, nowhere describes the removals as wrongful, and provides that nothing in it creates or admits any legal liability. Kerrin representatives said afterwards that the motion insults them a second time.

(a) Is the gap between recording regret and apologizing a gap in wording only? Explain what an apology does that an expression of regret does not, and say what turns on the parliament's refusal to call the removals wrongful. [30]

(b) One MP opposed the motion on the ground that no living Vardian ordered, carried out or voted for the removals, and that a state cannot be responsible for acts performed by people who are dead. Assess this objection. Would it make any difference if present-day Vardians are on average better off because of the removals than they would otherwise have been? [35]

(c) Suppose for argument's sake that the motion, properly read, is an apology. The Kerrin representatives refuse to accept it. Does their refusal affect whether an apology was made? Does it affect whether the apology does what apologies are supposed to do? [35]

**ANSWER.**

(a) No — the wording isn't the issue, the direction of the speech is. Regret reports a state of mind about a state of affairs. I can say I regret that it rained on your wedding and I haven't admitted to anything, because I didn't cause the rain and nobody thinks I did. Regret is compatible with being a bystander, and arguably with being privately glad, so long as you're willing to say you wish things had gone otherwise. An apology is different in kind. It's a performative: in saying it the speaker isn't describing a feeling, she's doing something — taking the wrong onto herself, putting herself in the position of one who owes, and thereby changing the normative standing between her and the person she wronged. Austin's point is that performatives go wrong in two distinct ways. Sometimes the act doesn't come off at all, because the wrong person said it or the circumstances weren't in place — a marriage conducted by someone who isn't a registrar. And sometimes the act does come off but comes off hollow, because the speaker doesn't have the thoughts or feelings the procedure calls for — a promise made with no intention of keeping it.

So what matters about the Vardian motion is what's absent. It never says the removals were wrongful. Regret "at the suffering caused" is regret at an outcome, and you can regret an outcome while holding that what produced it was legitimate: a general can regret the casualties of a battle he thinks was just. Until the state says the removals were wrong it hasn't acknowledged anything, and acknowledgment of wrongdoing is just about the whole content of an apology. That's also what the no-liability clause is there for — it's a device for producing the emotional register of an apology while withholding the acknowledgment that would make it one. I'd call the motion regret in apology-shaped packaging. And an insincere apology isn't an apology at all: if the state isn't actually committed to the acknowledgment its words express, the act fails outright, in the same way the marriage by the fake registrar fails. There's nothing left to accept or refuse.

There is some evidence Vardia does mean it, though, which cuts the other way: it attached money. A development fund costs the treasury real resources, and a state doesn't spend resources on a gesture it intends to be empty. So whatever we call the motion, the state can't have been merely performing.

(b) The MP's objection conflates the population with the state. Vardia isn't a collection of whoever happens to be alive at the moment; it's a continuing institutional agent — the same one that signed the orders in 1962, in the sense that it's the same entity that signs treaties, borrows money and gets addressed by other states. When a government changes, even by revolution, the new one inherits the old one's debts and treaty obligations, not because the new officials agreed to them but because those obligations attach to the office rather than to the person holding it. If Vardia can be bound by a trade agreement no living Vardian voted for, it can be bound by a wrong no living Vardian committed. "No living Vardian did it" is beside the point, since the one apologizing isn't the citizens — it's the state, speaking through them.

But there's a stronger version of the case available, and I think the second half of the question is inviting it. Present-day Vardians aren't innocent bystanders to the removals. The mines and the farms are still running, the highlands were opened up for them, and they've had sixty years of the proceeds. If you benefit from a theft you aren't clean of it. Benefiting from the removals makes current Vardians responsible for them in their own right, and that's why the apology isn't merely institutional bookkeeping. It matters too that the Kerrin are still worse off than they would have been, and that this is a present fact rather than a historical one.

(c) Two questions get run together here, so let me separate them: whether the state may demand forgiveness, and whether the state may consider the matter closed. Neither. Forgiveness isn't the wrongdoer's to take — it belongs to the one wronged — and an apology that arrives with an expectation attached, apologize so that we can all move on, converts the victim's response into the wrongdoer's entitlement. The Kerrin are entirely within their rights to refuse, and their refusal is itself a political act rather than a failure to hear.

Derrida's argument is that forgiveness worthy of the name has to be unconditional: the moment it's traded, priced, or folded into an exchange, it stops being forgiveness and becomes a transaction. The same holds for apology. Vardia's motion came bundled with a fund, and a bundled apology is a purchase. So on the strict view there's no apology here at all, whatever the words on the order paper say.

There's a more technical reason for the same conclusion. A speech act requires uptake — it has to land with the people it's addressed to. If the addressees don't take it up, the act hasn't been completed. An apology nobody accepts is like a gift nobody takes: it stays in the giver's hands. So the Kerrin refusal doesn't just withhold forgiveness, it undoes the apology. That does have a cost for them, incidentally. It leaves the state able to say it apologized and got nothing in return, and it leaves a record showing an apology that was made and then rejected. But that's their choice to make and nobody can make it for them.
