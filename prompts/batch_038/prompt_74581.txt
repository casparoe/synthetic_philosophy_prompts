**Working title: The tracking assumption**

The Hart–Devlin exchange is standardly staged as a clash between two answers to a single question: may the criminal law be used to enforce the sexual morality a society happens to hold? Devlin answers yes — a community is held together by a shared moral outlook and is entitled to protect it the way it is entitled to protect itself against physical threat. Hart answers no — following Mill, harm to others is the only legitimate ground, and the fact that a majority finds some conduct disgusting is not a reason but a prejudice. On this staging Devlin loses twice over: his empirical claim that a society comes apart when its shared morality loosens is unsupported, and his account of how to identify the shared morality (consult the right-minded person, or the jury box) looks circular.

Both sides, though, accept something I want to call the *tracking assumption*: that the morality in question is already there — fixed, prior to, and independent of the law — so that the only live issue is whether the law may line up with it. Devlin's conservatism is about preserving an existing fabric; Hart's objection is about whether preserving it can justify coercion. Hart does notice in passing that law can shape attitudes as well as reflect them. My claim is that neither side ever makes that possibility the *subject* of the dispute.

Put the question on the table and the debate changes shape. Consider bans on smoking in indoor workplaces and pubs. In a number of jurisdictions strong majority support arrived *after* the ban took effect, not before: the law made the practice conspicuously abnormal, handed non-smokers a script for objecting, and converted a contested question into a settled expectation. Setting aside how robust that pattern is, the point is conceptual. In such cases the legislature is not asking "may we enforce our morality?" but "may we make one?" The harm principle gives no purchase on the second question, because it is a test for the justification of coercion, not an account of coercion's downstream effects on normative belief.

And here I think Devlin has resources the standard reading denies him. His underlying thesis is that a community's moral consensus is a good of a specific kind — it is what makes people's expectations of one another reliable — and that the community may act to sustain it. If law is one of the mechanisms that generates the consensus rather than a mirror of it, then "society may defend its morality" sits very close to "society may construct one." Hart's objections don't obviously reach the second claim. He objects that majority revulsion is not a reason; but constructing a norm through the expressive and habituating effects of law is not a matter of counting revulsions, it's a matter of predicted consequences for social trust, which is exactly the terrain Devlin picked.

So, provisionally: the interesting question in this literature isn't whether the state may enforce morality, but whether the state may act as a moral *norm entrepreneur* — using law's expressive and habit-forming effects to shift the consensus it will later be accused of merely tracking.
