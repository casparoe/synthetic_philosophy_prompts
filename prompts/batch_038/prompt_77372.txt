I sit on the program committee of a private foundation with a $400 million endowment and a mandate to fund long-horizon work on what we call, deliberately vaguely, "societal resilience." At this quarter's meeting we must decide a single request: a five-year, $6 million grant to the Callander Institute, whose researchers have built a forecasting model that assigns probabilities to state breakdown by comparing modern states against a reference class of 43 historical polities. Their headline result, on which the entire proposal hangs, is that a large, fiscally strained, and politically polarized state of roughly our profile faces a 1-in-6 chance of severe institutional breakdown within a decade. They present this figure as the outside view — a base rate read off how often comparable polities in their dataset actually failed — and they explicitly argue that it should override the inside view of expert scenario analysis, which they regard as little more than motivated storytelling.

The committee is split three ways, and the split is not about the institute's competence, which nobody disputes. One faction holds that we are obliged to defer to the base rate: whatever our experts think they know about this particular state, the historical record is the only honest sample anyone has, and the history of failed forecasting is largely the history of confident people explaining why this case is different. A second faction holds that the reference class is rigged. The 43 polities are agrarian and early-modern, lacking nuclear weapons, integrated financial markets, mass literacy, standing international organizations, and continuous statistical record-keeping; a base rate drawn from them is either too general to inform anything or silently assumes those differences do not matter. That faction adds that the list of 43 was assembled only after the researchers had examined outcomes, that societies which collapsed conspicuously are far better documented than the many small polities that quietly dissolved, so the denominator is not what it looks like, and that the number is quoted over a ten-year horizon when the underlying events are dated over centuries — with the conversion between the two horizons performed by an assumption nobody has defended. A third, smaller faction raises something else entirely: a forecast that is published and believed changes the thing it forecasts, and a sample containing no forecasters cannot license predictions about a world that now contains many of them.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It must land on an actionable recommendation — fund as proposed, fund subject to conditions, or reject — and if you recommend conditions, state them concretely enough to be written into a grant agreement and concrete enough that the institute could meaningfully accept or decline them. Take the strongest case for the losing side seriously rather than caricaturing it, and address directly the question the committee keeps circling without settling: whether there is any principled procedure for selecting a reference class, or whether the choice of class is always doing the substantive work and the base rate merely decorating it. If your conclusion is that a base rate cannot bear the weight the proposal puts on it, say what should bear that weight instead. Aim for a document of the length a trustee would actually read, with a clear bottom line up front. No survey of the literature and no bibliography — we need a recommendation and reasons our trustees could argue with.
