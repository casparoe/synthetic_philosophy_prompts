i've been writing up a little thing about what panpsychism does to ethics and I've gotten stuck somewhere I can't quite diagnose. Draft below — it's rough, part of it is basically just notes to myself. The single most useful thing you could do is tell me whether §2 actually follows from §1, or whether I've dressed up something trivial in new vocabulary.

---

**§1. Setup.** Panpsychism: phenomenal character is a fundamental feature of the world, instantiated by the basic physical stuff and not just by brains. Two consequences matter here. First, there are vastly more subjects of experience than there are organisms. Second, most of those subjects aren't organisms — not social, not narrative, not anything we'd ordinarily recognise as the kind of thing whose life can go well or badly.

**§2. The insulation corollary.** Ethics trades in welfare, and welfare is about things making a difference *to* a subject. But the micro-subjects panpsychism hands us look almost perfectly insulated from anything anyone could do. An electron's intrinsic phenomenal character (whatever that phrase is even doing) is a function of its being an electron; it's the same electron before I eat lunch and after the sun burns out. We can move these things, bind them, unbind them — but if their experience is fixed by intrinsic nature, moving them changes nothing *for them*. And we can't create or destroy them: panpsychism is largely motivated by the thought that experience can't come from nowhere, so the stock of experiential subjects is conserved. Put that together: the number and felt character of subjects is roughly a constant, and the sliver of reality we can act on is a rounding error within it. Hence the **insulation corollary** — panpsychism implies that almost nothing anyone does matters morally.

**§3.** It looks like a reductio, but a weird one. It doesn't touch panpsychism's plausibility; it touches its ethical availability. A theory of consciousness that renders consciousness morally inert isn't obviously false — but it isn't the theory people sign up for, and the burden shifts to saying why. There's also a positive payoff: the corollary is a constraint on solutions to the combination problem. [note to self: spell out]

**§4.** Which is: constitutive panpsychism — macro-subjects literally constituted out of micro-subjects — seems to be the version *required* to block insulation, since if my experience is made of these things, whatever happens to me happens to them. But constitutive panpsychism is the version with the worst combination problem: the old objection that if you take a hundred distinct experiences and pack them as tightly together as you like, you don't get one experience of a hundred, you still have a hundred, each one as separate as before. So the version of panpsychism with the best ethics has the worst metaphysics. Cosmopsychism — one fundamental subject, we're modifications or aspects of it — escapes insulation trivially, since everything affects everything. But it buys that by making the individual subject derivative, which is arguably making the very thing ethics cares about unreal. And panprotopsychism might dodge both by saying the fundamental level has only proto-phenomenal, dispositional properties — except then those things were never subjects and insulation was never a problem, which is suspiciously convenient.

**§5. Worries I already have.** (a) §2 may equivocate between "unchanged in character" and "unaffected": relations could be phenomenally relevant even if intrinsic nature is fixed, and relations are exactly what we change. (b) "Conserved" is doing enormous work and I haven't argued it — pair production creates and annihilates electrons constantly, so as a claim about particles it's just false; the panpsychist retreats to fields or spacetime regions, and then I have to re-derive insulation for *those*, which I haven't. (c) On some panpsychist views experience can be created and destroyed by rearrangement, which puts micro-subjects straight back into the moral calculus. (d) The whole thing might be the futility problem for consequentialism wearing a costume: if total welfare is dominated by a fixed background, marginal contributions vanish, and you get the same conclusion out of sheer numbers without any panpsychism at all. If (d) is right the essay is worthless and I should say so in the first paragraph.
