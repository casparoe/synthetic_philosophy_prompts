I constructed the following argument for a reading group on the timing of conscious experience, and now that it is written out I cannot tell whether it is a proof or a trick. The empirical premise is the color phi phenomenon. Two small lights a few degrees apart are flashed in quick succession, the first red and the second green, at an interval of a fraction of a second. At certain combinations of spacing and interval, subjects report a single dot that moves between the two locations and changes color abruptly in midpath — that is, they report a change occurring before the second flash has appeared. Because the report is always collected after both flashes, there seem to be only two ways to take it at face value. Following Dennett and Kinsbourne, call them the Stalinesque account (the brain waits for the second flash, composes the whole sequence offline, and delivers the finished version, so that the subject is never conscious of anything but the completed movie, midpath change included) and the Orwellian account (the subject is conscious of something else — a stationary red dot, or motion with no change of color — and the memory of it is rewritten once the green flash arrives, so that the report describes an experience nobody ever had).

Here is the argument.

(1) The two accounts are built to explain one and the same report. They agree about every neural event, every motor output, and every disposition the subject has, including her confidence and any further report she might be prompted to give. They differ only in whether the point at which the erroneous content enters the causal pathway is to be labeled pre-experiential or post-experiential.

(2) If two accounts agree on every physical event and every observable output and differ only in a label attached to one of those events, they are not two accounts but one account described twice.

(3) So there is no fact of the matter whether the midpath color change was composed before consciousness or rewritten after it.

(4) Nothing here is special to color phi. Any claim about when an experience occurs can be tested only by reports, and a report is necessarily later than what it reports. So for any pair of accounts of the timing of an experience that agree about the physical and behavioral facts, the same equivalence will arise.

(5) Therefore, for every experience there is no fact of the matter about when it occurs, and on the stronger reading no fact of the matter about whether it occurs at all.

(6) The color phi puzzle accordingly dissolves — not because one of the two accounts wins, but because the question was never answerable in principle.

The difficulty is that (6) appears to take the reports down with it. If there is no fact about what anyone experienced, the published findings are not findings about experience, and (1) has lost its evidence.

I would give up almost anything before (1), since it is simply the experimental result, and almost anything before the suspicion that the argument undercuts its own starting point. Everything in between is negotiable. So: which step do you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable when I wrote it? If the step is (1) or (2), tell me whether any experiment could in principle distinguish the Stalinesque from the Orwellian account, and what the difference would look like in the data. If the step is (4) or (5), tell me what a determinate but permanently untestable fact about the timing of an experience is supposed to consist in, and what it costs to posit one. Say also whether the move from "we cannot tell which description is right" to "there is no fact," and the further move from "no fact about when" to "no fact about whether," are one slide or two, and whether the correct diagnosis is instead that the dichotomy itself is false rather than indeterminate — and if so, what has to be given up to see it that way.

Please write your answer in American English, and leave out the reading list: I want the reasoning, not the references.
