I write a monthly column for a general-interest magazine on mathematics, computing, and the arguments that spill out of both. This month's piece concerns an unusually dry but surprisingly stubborn limit: a reasoning system cannot, from the inside, establish that whatever it manages to demonstrate is thereby true. Any standing rule of the form "if I can prove it, it holds" turns out to license proving anything whatsoever, so a system that adopts such a rule has effectively stopped proving things at all. The people who design autonomous systems run into this the moment an agent is supposed to consider upgrading itself: to choose safely it would have to trust the conclusions of a cleverer successor, and that trust is exactly what it cannot manufacture from its own resources. My editor wants a boxed fable to run alongside the reported matter — set apart from the column by a grey rule — for readers who will not follow a single implication sign. Two of the researchers I interviewed have promised to read it, so it must be structurally accurate rather than merely atmospheric; I would rather a reader be briefly puzzled than lectured.

The seed I would like you to work from: a guild of assayers in a trading city whose coinage, bridges, and grain contracts all depend on a sealed warrant, and whose charter specifies who may countersign whom. The head assayer is at the end of her term and must name a successor. One candidate is slow and unoriginal, and she can warrant him without difficulty, because she can personally repeat every assay he performs; the other is far better than she is, and here the charter fails her, since verifying his judgments would require trusting an eye keener than the one doing the verifying. Also present as memory, not as prologue: within the lifetime of people still working in the assay hall, a previous head tried to settle the question by issuing a standing warrant — whatever her successor certified, she certified in advance — and the guild's seals were worthless within a year, because a forger eventually produced a warrant whose content was its own genuineness, and the charter offered no way to refuse that document without pulling out the standing warrant that everything else now rested on. I would like some version of that self-certifying document to appear in the story as a physical object, described concretely, and I would like the earlier collapse to weigh on the head assayer's decision.

Constraints on execution. At most nine hundred words, and please give me the story and nothing else: no preface, no author's note, no closing paragraph that steps outside the hall to say what it all meant. No modern framing device, no artificial intelligences, no named mathematicians or theorems, and no vocabulary that belongs to my column rather than to the guild — the register should be procedural and spare, more wax, ledger, and counterweight than sword and sorcery. Most importantly, the protagonist must not find a workaround: the barrier should read as a fact about her world, not as a personal failing she might have thought her way around, and the ending should show her making a decision under that constraint rather than dissolving it.
