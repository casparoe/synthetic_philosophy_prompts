**Submission 4B, "Three Ways to Hang in the Air" (892 words).**

The claim that justification must either stop at something unjustified, circle back on itself, or continue without end is a fair statement of what Hans Albert called the Münchhausen trilemma in 1968, and of something older: among the modes reported by Sextus Empiricus and attributed to Agrippa are infinite regress, arbitrary hypothesis, and circular reasoning, alongside disagreement and relativity, which do no structural work here. Albert's name comes from the baron who lifted himself and his horse out of a marsh by his own hair. The image makes the point that a structure of justification cannot supply its own footing. I will argue that two of the three horns fail outright and that the third fails for a more interesting reason.

Foundationalism takes the first horn: some beliefs are justified without being inferred from others. The traditional way to say this is that basic beliefs are self-justifying, carrying their warrant intrinsically — Descartes's clear and distinct perceptions, or the bare deliverances of the senses. This is what Sellars attacked as the myth of the given. If a sensation is to justify a belief it must have propositional shape, since only something with content can stand in a reason relation to anything; but as soon as it has content it is the sort of thing that can be mistaken, and so stands in need of support. Foundationalists cannot have it both ways: either the given is non-conceptual, in which case it can cause my belief but not justify it, or it is conceptual, in which case it is not basic. Those who retreat to the claim that basic beliefs need only be defeasibly justified concede the point, since a belief that can be defeated has a warrant that depends on the absence of further information, and therefore on something outside itself.

Coherentism takes the second horn. Its defenders say justification is not a chain but a web, in which beliefs support one another mutually, and that the trilemma wrongly presumes a linear order. But the objection to circularity is not a quibble about the trilemma's phrasing. A circular argument presupposes what it is meant to establish, and so can move only someone who already accepts the conclusion. If my belief that the train will be late is justified by my belief that the timetable is unreliable, which is justified by my memory of past lateness, which is justified by my belief that the train will be late, then the system holds no more warrant than its least independent member. Coherentism cannot raise the warrant of any belief above zero; it can only redistribute warrant the system never possessed. This is also why Klein's Principle of Avoiding Circularity, which forbids a proposition from appearing in its own evidential ancestry, should not be read as a partisan assumption: it states a condition any account of justification has to meet.

That leaves infinitism, which holds that the evidential ancestry of a justified belief is infinite and non-repeating. Infinitism deserves credit for not fudging: it keeps both of Klein's principles, avoiding arbitrariness by always offering a further reason and avoiding circularity by never letting the ancestry loop. But it collapses under the finite mind objection. No person can complete an infinite chain of reasoning, and a justification that can never be completed is a justification that can never be had; the requirement is not merely unfulfilled but unfulfillable. Worse, the non-repeating condition quietly returns infinitism to foundationalism. Since any actual reasoner must stop somewhere, and may never revisit a reason once used, the reason at which the reasoner stops functions as an unchallengeable starting point. Infinitism is foundationalism with a later excuse for stopping.

Conclusion. The three options exhaust the structures justification could have, and each fails, so skepticism is what remains — not as a positive thesis but as the default once the alternatives are gone. Albert thought we should give up certainty rather than knowledge, but the concession does not help him, because the argument above never assumed certainty; it assumed only that a reason must be able to justify.
