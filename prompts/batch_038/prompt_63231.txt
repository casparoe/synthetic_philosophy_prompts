I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a British broadsheet, and I have spent the last few months inside Schrödinger's late philosophical writing — the Dublin lectures that became *Mind and Matter*, and the various places where he argued, on the strength of his reading of Advaita Vedānta, that consciousness is never encountered in the plural. My editor has asked for four thousand words with a definite thesis rather than another survey, and my thesis is going to be that the one-mind reading is not the mysticism it is usually taken for but a position a scientifically minded person can hold without embarrassment. Before I put that in print under my own name, I want to know whether the package of views I actually hold is coherent, because I suspect that some of the things I have written cheerfully in past columns are doing work that my new thesis quietly cancels. Please take the role of a professional philosopher of mind who has worked for years on the metaphysics of consciousness and who is equally at home in the philosophy of physics, and treat this as a consistency audit of my position rather than an essay about Schrödinger. Think it through carefully: before you write a word of the audit, work out for yourself how each commitment bears on each of the others, pairwise, and note which pairings are inert and which are loaded; only then draft your answer.

Here is the package. (1) Nobody has ever encountered a consciousness other than their own, and no observation could establish a second one; plurality of minds is therefore not an empirical datum but an inference, and a weakly supported one, resting on little more than the fact that we see many spatially separate bodies behaving as though each were inhabited. (2) I accordingly take the one-mind reading seriously — the many subjects are masks of a single canvas, one object in many mirrors — and I am willing to say in print that the number of minds is not a fact the sciences can settle. (3) I am nonetheless a physicalist about causation: everything that happens, including my typing this, is fixed by physical goings-on, there is no non-physical substance intervening in the world, and I have written sceptically about dualism for years. (4) I am an anti-panpsychist; I do not think fundamental particles experience anything, and I do not think the hard problem is solved by sprinkling proto-consciousness through the base layer of physics. Consciousness, for me, goes with organisation — with integration and with the sort of learning-and-habituation story Schrödinger himself sketched. (5) I take the disunity data seriously and have used them in print: anaesthesia, focal lesions, neglect, and above all commissurotomy patients, whose behaviour seems to me the strongest naturalistic evidence available that a single skull can house more than one stream, and hence that subjects can be multiplied or divided by rearranging tissue. (6) I think a machine could in principle be conscious, and when I cover artificial intelligence I write as though a conscious artefact would be a new subject coming into existence whose interests would have to be counted alongside ours. (7) I am not drawing any of the ethical conclusions that are usually attached to this material: I do not think it follows that I am you, and I hold the ordinary view that people are separate agents responsible for their own acts, that death is a harm to the person who dies in a way it is not a harm to anyone else, and that what happens to me matters to me as what happens to you does not. (8) I treat my own first-person reports as authoritative about the structure of my experience: if I report a single unified field, that report is the datum, not something to be explained away by an outside observer.

What I want back is the audit, not a lecture. Identify the pairs that genuinely pull against one another and rank them by severity, with your reasons for the ranking; for each, say precisely what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would cost me — which of my published views would become untenable, and which sentences of the essay I would no longer be able to write. Equally important, tell me which of these only look like tensions and dissolve on inspection, and why they dissolve, because I do not want to sacrifice a commitment for no reason; I strongly suspect that at least one of the objections I expect from a philosopher of physics — something about my anti-panpsychism, or something about the split-brain cases — is not the problem I think it is, and that the real trouble sits elsewhere. Finally, be committed rather than diplomatic: name the single revision that would clean up the most, say which of the eight claims you think is most likely to be false, and defend that choice with reasons. Do not lay out the options and hand the decision back to me, and do not tell me it depends on what I mean by "mind" unless you then tell me what I should mean by it and what follows. If you think my characterisation of Schrödinger's position — the objectivation point, the arithmetical paradox, the Vedantic gloss — is a contestable reading that a specialist would dispute, flag that separately at the end, since the essay depends on it, but keep it distinct from the audit of my own commitments.
