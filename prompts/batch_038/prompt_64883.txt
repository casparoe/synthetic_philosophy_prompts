I'm putting together a talk on whether common knowledge really does any work in coordination that finite levels of mutual knowledge can't do, and I want a case that cuts against the usual line — the email-game moral that if the confirmation chain could break at any link, everything unravels back to the safe default. Build me a two-person, completely ordinary scenario (two colleagues who need to end up in the same place at the same time is fine, but pick your own setting and stakes) where Alice learns something privately, messages Bob about it, and a short *finite* chain of private confirmations turns out to be enough for both of them to act on it, even though nobody ever gets common knowledge of the fact. Constraints: no public announcements, no shared calendar, no third party who could inform them both — anything like that just manufactures common knowledge and dodges the question; every message in the chain has to be one that could plausibly be lost or go unread; the payoffs should be ordinary, not life-or-death; and the coordination has to be robust in the sense that each is playing a best response given exactly what they know at that depth, not just getting lucky.

Then show the work. Lay out the epistemic state at each link (who knows the fact, who knows that who knows, and where the chain genuinely stops), say what each of them does with zero, one, two confirmations and why, and name the payoff feature that blocks the unraveling. Say what happens to the case if you strip that feature out, and how someone who still insists on common knowledge would attack the construction. Finish with a line starting "Verdict:" giving the minimum number of confirmations sufficent to get them both there — just the number.
