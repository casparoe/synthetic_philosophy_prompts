I am a second-year doctoral student, and in three weeks I am leading a session of our department's ancient epistemology reading group. Our topic is the structure of justification — foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism — and we are beginning with the two opening chapters of Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics* (G. R. G. Mure's translation), since that text is usually credited as the origin of the problem. The translation is dense, and I would rather arrive having already thought through the argument than discover its difficulties in front of the group. The excerpts I want to work from are below, followed by the specific questions on which I need help.

**Excerpt A — Book I, Part 1**

> "All instruction given or received by way of argument proceeds from pre-existent knowledge."

> "Recognition of a truth may in some cases contain as factors both previous knowledge and also knowledge acquired simultaneously with that recognition …"

> "If this distinction is not drawn, we are faced with the dilemma in the Meno: either a man will learn nothing or what he already knows; for we cannot accept the solution which some people offer."

> "On the other hand, I imagine there is nothing to prevent a man in one sense knowing what he is learning, in another not knowing it. The strange thing would be, not if in some sense he knew what he was learning, but if he were to know it in that precise sense and manner in which he was learning it."

**Excerpt B — Book I, Part 2**

> "We suppose ourselves to possess unqualified scientific knowledge of a thing, as opposed to knowing it in the accidental way in which the sophist knows, when we think that we know the cause on which the fact depends, as the cause of that fact and of no other, and, further, that the fact could not be other than it is."

> "By demonstration I mean a syllogism productive of scientific knowledge, a syllogism, that is, the grasp of which is eo ipso such knowledge."

> "Assuming then that my thesis as to the nature of scientific knowing is correct, the premisses of demonstrated knowledge must be true, primary, immediate, better known than and prior to the conclusion, which is further related to them as effect to cause."

> "The premisses must be primary and indemonstrable; otherwise they will require demonstration in order to be known, since to have knowledge, if it be not accidental knowledge, of things which are demonstrable, means precisely to have a demonstration of them."

> "The premisses must be the causes of the conclusion, better known than it, and prior to it"

My questions, in the order I hope to take them in the session:

1. **Is the conclusion argued for or built in?** Excerpt B appears to establish that some premises are indemonstrable by appeal to a definition of scientific knowledge stated two sentences earlier, rather than by an argument against rival structures. Please reconstruct the inference as a numbered list of premises and a conclusion, and tell me explicitly which step is doing the anti-regress work. Then assess my worry: that the regress is being closed by analysis of the *explanandum* rather than by elimination of alternatives, so that a coherentist who simply denies the initial characterisation of what scientific knowing consists in has not yet been answered at all. What is the strongest response available to Aristotle here, and does it depend on any premise not present in the excerpts?

2. **What does "immediate" add?** In the same list Aristotle requires premises to be "true, primary, immediate," and then glosses this as "primary and indemonstrable." Is "immediate" here a structural condition — the absence of a middle term in a syllogism — or an epistemic one, equivalent to "not inferred from anything else"? This matters to me because if it is merely structural, then the standard practice of transporting this argument into contemporary debates about propositional justification, where reasons need not take syllogistic form at all, may be a category mistake. Please say which reading the text supports and what is lost on each.

3. **What ordering does "better known than and prior to" impose?** I want to be precise here rather than hand-waving. Take the support relation to be irreflexive, and ask which further properties Aristotle's conditions require: asymmetry, transitivity, well-foundedness. My working hypothesis is that well-foundedness is what excludes an infinite chain, while asymmetry is what excludes a circle, and that these are genuinely separate demands. A coherentist might concede both locally — each belief supported by others in a finite network, with no closed loop — while denying that "better known than" is a *global* ordering over the whole system, holding instead that it obtains only between adjacent nodes. Does Aristotle have anything in these excerpts that blocks the local/global slide, or is the ambiguity in "prior to" doing concealed work for him?

4. **Can his own solution to the Meno be turned against him?** In Excerpt A, Aristotle escapes the learning paradox by allowing that a man may know something "in one sense" and not "in that precise sense and manner in which he was learning it." That looks like a potential/actual distinction. But a contemporary infinitist argues precisely that an endless chain of reasons can be *available* without ever being *deployed* — that justification requires only that a further reason exist to be had, not that the knower traverse the series. If Aristotle licenses potential possession in the case of the student who already knows the universal and comes to recognise the particular under it, on what principled grounds can he refuse to license it in the case of an unending supply of further premises? Please give the most plausible Aristotelian reply, and then tell me honestly whether you think it succeeds or whether it merely restates the demand for a stopping point.

5. **Simultaneity versus priority.** Excerpt A also allows that recognition may involve "knowledge acquired simultaneously with that recognition." Excerpt B insists that premises be prior to the conclusion. Are these compatible, and which sense of "prior" — temporal, explanatory, or a matter of epistemic standing — must Excerpt B be using for the anti-regress argument to go through? Please spell out what changes if the priority required is explanatory rather than epistemic, since on that reading a mutually supporting pair of beliefs might be explanatorily ordered in both directions without either being better known than the other.

6. **Where the translation may be misleading me.** Flag any place in these excerpts where you suspect Mure's English is flattening a distinction the argument depends on — for instance, whether one Greek term or two stand behind "primary" and "immediate," and whether "better known" carries an ambiguity between better known to us and better known by nature that the English hides. If you think a particular Greek term would settle the question, name it and give your confidence that it is the right one; I would rather you mark a guess as a guess than present it as settled.

7. **Finally, the framing question for the session.** Given that the subject of these chapters is demonstration within a completed science — a relation among propositions — and not the justification of a believer's attitudes, how much of the contemporary debate does this text actually generate? I want a short, defensible answer to give the group about what is preserved and what is anachronistic in treating this as the founding statement of the trilemma.

Two constraints on your reply. Please take the questions in order and keep each answer anchored in the wording of the excerpts rather than in a general summary of Aristotle's epistemology; where you go beyond what the text says, say so explicitly. And I am working offline for this session, so I want no bibliography, no reading list, and no pointers to secondary literature — the value to me is in the argument, not in the references.
