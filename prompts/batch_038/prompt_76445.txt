I run intake at a small housing-and-benefits clinic in a mid-size city, so I spend my days watching two things happen at the same desk. On one side, people come in terrified of what's already on file about them — a landlord's note, a missed appointment that got coded as non-compliance, a cousin's address that flagged them for a fraud review — and half my job is arguing that the record isn't them. On the other side, I've watched the exact same opacity protect genuinely bad actors: a slumlord with eleven LLCs that no database connects, an abusive ex whose new address nobody will disclose, a contractor who stiffed three of my clients because nothing links the complaints. My partner is the opposite of me on this — he's a sysadmin who runs everything through privacy tools, refuses loyalty cards, and thinks the whole clinic intake form is a confession machine — so we argue about it constantly and we've never once agreed on what we're actually arguing about. I've started to think the interesting question isn't how much the state should know but who gets to name the categories, because whoever writes "at-risk household" or "long-term unemployed" into a form gets to decide who looks suspicious, and no amount of me consenting to the form fixes that.

So I want you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial colaboration between two camps could produce: call them the concealment camp (citizens should default to hiding from the state; information the state holds will get repurposed no matter what the paperwork says; privacy isn't about guilt, it's about keeping a zone the state's categories can't reach) and the legibility camp (darkness is where the powerful hide; you can't distribute help fairly without knowing who needs it; the danger isn't records, it's records without countervailing institutions, and institutions can in principle be built). I want the real thing, not a tie-scored draw: claims both can honestly sign, disagreements stated in words each side would accept for its own position, and the cruxes where settling it would actually move somebody. Please work through a few specific bones of contention and don't let either side off easy: whether purpose-creep is a law of nature or just a policy failure we keep failing to fix; whether means-testing inevitably demands intimate individual knowledge or whether a universal system could need far less; whether "hiding from the state" even means anything once employers, platforms, landlords and credit bureaus hold the same data and hand it over; and whether being recorded changes what's recorded — i.e. do people in legible systems start performing for the file, and does that corrupt the data or improve behavior. Also please run both camps through this thought experiment and report what each picks: Town A has everything about you on file with the state, but there are hard legal locks on every use and an independent body with real teeth to enforce them; Town B has the state holding almost nothing, but in any emergency it can compel banks, doctors, platforms and landlords to produce your records instantly and there is no independent check at all. Which town does each camp move to, which town do they fear the other is actually describing when they talk about the real world, and is there any pair of answers to that question that isn't just restating the disagreement?

Structure it like this. First, What both sides can sign — a numbered list, eight to twelve items, each no more than two sentences, and please make at least three of them claims that would genuinely annoy a loudmouth on one side or the other rather than bland common ground. Second, Where they still differ — a table with columns for the disagreement in one line, the concealment camp's own preferred wording, the legibility camp's own preferred wording, and a final column saying whether that one is substantive or was really about words; aim for five to seven rows and be explicit in the last column about which rows collapse under scrutiny. Third, The cruxes — three to five of them, and for each give the test, case or argument that both camps would agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance, in their own voice, so you can tell later who was wrong; be concrete about what an outcome would look like that forces a concession rather than a shrug. Fourth, The two towns — about 250 words on the thought experiment above. Fifth, What would actually move us — 150 words or so on whether there's any single piece of evidence that both sides have already committed in this document to treating as decisive, and if there isn't one, say plainly that the residual disagreement isn't empirical and locate where it lives instead. Total somewhere around 1500 to 1800 words. Don't give me a reading list or citations — I want the argument worked out, not a bibliography, and I'd rather you reason from cases everyone knows than name-drop sources I'd have to look up.
