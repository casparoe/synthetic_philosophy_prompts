I teach a first-year graduate game theory sequence, and an exercise I set has come back on me. To show that dominance reasoning is subtler than it looks, I wrote out what I intended as a reductio of the whole apparatus — an argument that seems to prove iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies can never do any work — and I expected the flaw to be obvious to the class. It was not obvious to me either, and two colleagues have since located it in different places, so I want an independent diagnosis before I circulate a model answer.

The game, G. Row chooses U, M or D; Column chooses L or R. Payoffs are (Row, Column):

|  | L | R |
|---|---|---|
| U | (1, 2) | (0, 0) |
| M | (0, 0) | (1, 2) |
| D | (0.4, 1) | (0.4, 1) |

The argument.

(1) A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy, since a strictly dominated strategy is one that some available alternative beats whatever the others do, and a payoff-maximiser would use that alternative instead.

(2) Strict dominance is a comparison between single strategies. To ask whether s is strictly dominated is to compare s against each of the player's other strategies in turn; a mixture is not an alternative alongside the pure strategies it mixes but a device for sometimes playing one of them and sometimes another, so it adds nothing to the inventory of potential dominators.

(3) Nor does it add anything indirectly. If a mixture beats s in every column, then in each column its payoff is a weighted average of the payoffs the pure strategies in its support earn in that column, and an average cannot strictly exceed every quantity being averaged; so in each column at least one pure strategy in the support beats s there. Comparing s against the pure strategies one at a time therefore captures everything that dominance comparisons ever capture.

(4) In G, U is a best reply to L and M is a best reply to R, and neither L nor R beats the other against all three of Row's strategies. So no pure strategy of either player is strictly dominated by another pure strategy of that player.

(5) Hence, by (2) and (3), nothing in G is strictly dominated, and iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies removes nothing from G.

(6) That procedure is order-independent, preserves every Nash equilibrium, and leaves exactly the strategies consistent with common knowledge of rationality.

(7) So D is consistent with common knowledge of rationality in G; and more generally, since any strategy that is a best reply to some conjecture survives the procedure, elimination is inert in every game in which each strategy is a best reply to something.

(8) But half-U-half-M earns 0.5 against both L and R while D earns 0.4, so a Row player using D does strictly worse than one who randomises, whatever Column does; and the criterion was supposed to rule out precisely that.

(9) Therefore (1) is false: rationality does not exclude strictly dominated play, and dominance reasoning cannot be relied upon to capture common belief in rationality.

I find (9) unacceptable and I am confident the argument is not sound, but I cannot say where it fails. My own suspicion is somewhere between (3) and (5). One colleague insists the trouble is at (6): since D is never a best reply to anything Column might do, (7) already contradicts what (6) says survives, so the argument is internally inconsistent rather than unsound. Another thinks (2) is simply true and that (8) must go instead, on the grounds that a player in a one-shot game with no randomising device cannot implement a mixture, so nothing beats D in the only sense that matters.

Could you settle this? Specifically: identify the step or steps you reject and say precisely what is wrong with them; explain why each rejected step looked compelling to an attentive reader; and price the repairs. I want to know what I must give up if I allow mixtures as dominators — what that presupposes about the players' action sets and their access to randomisation, what it does to the relationship between surviving strategies and rationalisability, and whether anything changes if the dominance at issue is weak rather than strict — and what I lose if I keep (2) and (3) and define the procedure pairwise. Tell me too whether my first colleague is right that (6) and (7) are in tension, or whether that tension dissolves once the earlier steps are repaired.

Please be as definite as the material allows: give me a verdict on each numbered step rather than a survey of possible readings, and flag only those points where you think reasonable people could still differ. Close by saying what would change your mind — which consideration, if you have got it wrong, would leave the argument standing at (9) — and where you think your own diagnosis is most likely to fail.

No more than 800 words.
