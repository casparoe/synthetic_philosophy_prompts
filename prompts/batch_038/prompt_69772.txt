Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about supererogation and reached no agreement whatsoever, so I should like to try to settle at least my own side of the matter by setting out the verdicts I actually hold and asking what principle, if any, they add up to. Please write throughout in British English.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts. I have marked the ones I am unsure of, since the point of the exercise is partly to find out which of them I ought to abandon.

1. A stranger donates a kidney to someone they will never meet. Supererogatory, plainly: not required, and praiseworthy. I do not waver on this one.

2. A soldier throws himself on a live grenade to save three comrades who were not otherwise going to be saved. Supererogatory — but I notice I think this only because he was not ordered to do it. Yet when a lifeguard swims out into a rip current after a swimmer, I do not call it supererogatory at all; it is his job, and the risk to him is far lower. Role seems to matter to me, but a soldier has a role too, and these two verdicts sit badly together.

3. A parent gives up a promising career and most of her leisure to care for a severely disabled child over twenty years. I resist calling this supererogatory: it seems to me to be merely what the situation asks of her, and describing it as "beyond duty" strikes me as a small insult. The identical sacrifices made by an unrelated neighbour for the same child would, for me, be a paradigm of the supererogatory.

4. A billionaire gives away half his fortune to highly effective health charities. I think this is required of him. I also think he is genuinely praiseworthy for doing it, and that his peers who do not are not merely failing to earn praise but are blameworthy. So I want requirement and praiseworthiness in the same case. My friend says this is simply incoherent. I could not defend it last night and I still believe it.

5. You promise to drive a friend to a hospital appointment, and keeping the promise ends up costing you a once-in-a-career interview. Not supererogatory. The promise is doing the work; cost by itself does not lift an act above duty.

6. A woman forgives the man who killed her daughter by dangerous driving, where he is unrepentant and served three years of a short sentence. I think forgiving him is permitted, that withholding forgiveness is equally permitted, and that neither option is morally better than the other. And yet I do not want to describe her forgiveness as merely neutral: I want to praise it, while denying that refusing to forgive would have been any kind of failure. This is the case I keep going back and forth on.

7. A man persuades his elderly mother — sound of mind and eager — to give forty thousand pounds to a disaster appeal, and does all the legwork himself: choosing the charity, completing the forms, driving her to the bank. The cost falls almost entirely on her, with her glad consent; the cost to him is one weekend. The benefit to the recipients is exactly what it would have been had she given on her own initiative. My instinct is that this is not supererogatory for him, though I cannot say why. My friend says the instinct is squeamishness about manipulation. We started calling this "borrowed cost" during the argument, and I am not certain the label has any content, but the case keeps nagging at me.

8. A colleague who, over fifteen years, does a great many small permitted-but-costly things — covering other people's teaching, driving students to conferences, editing other people's papers — none of which is individually more than duty asks. I think the pattern taken as a whole is supererogatory in a way that no single item in it is. I have no idea how to state that.

My tentative principle, which my friend pulled apart within ten minutes, was: an act is supererogatory for an agent if and only if (i) the agent is permitted to refrain from it; (ii) performing it would be morally better than any alternative the agent is permitted to perform; and (iii) the additional cost falls on the agent rather than on someone else. Clause (ii) breaks on case 6, and possibly on case 3. Clauses (i) and (ii) together cannot survive case 4 at all. Clause (iii) I invented in the taxi home and have no argument for it beyond case 7.

What I should like from you is this. Give me the principle, or the small number of competing candidates, that best captures the pattern above — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case unaided — with each clause tied explicitly to the case or cases that force it into the formulation. Then tell me what your preferred principle says about case 6, which is the one I cannot settle, and about case 8, which I suspect no act-by-act formulation can handle. Then give me at least three further cases of your own choosing, with the verdicts your principle delivers on them, chosen so as to probe the clauses rather than to flatter them. Finally, be honest about the price: which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, which single one you would advise me to give up and why, and whether saving all of them would require a formulation too baroque to be worth calling a principle. If my three-clause attempt is nearer the truth than I think, repair it rather than replacing it — but do not preserve it out of politeness to me. British English, please.
