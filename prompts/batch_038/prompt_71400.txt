I am a high school senior, and for the past several months I have been reading H. L. A. Hart's 1958 article on the separation of law and morals alongside Lon Fuller's reply in the same issue of the Harvard Law Review, because I would like to write my senior thesis on that exchange. Neither text is easy for me, but I have been managing. Last week I sketched an argument of my own as a framing device for the thesis, and it has turned out to be far more trouble than it is worth: it appears to show that Hart's positivism either collapses into the natural law position he is arguing against or evaporates into the claim that judges simply do whatever they think best. I do not believe that, and my teacher does not believe it either, but neither of us has been able to say where the argument breaks.

Here it is.

1. For a legal rule to decide a case, the general terms in the rule must be applied to the facts of that case.
2. Applying a general term to a set of facts is a classification, and no finite body of past classifications uniquely fixes the next one: every past use of a term is compatible with indefinitely many ways of going on, and some of those ways diverge from the others in the case now before the court.
3. Therefore the rule itself never settles, without remainder, whether it covers the case at hand.
4. When a rule does not settle whether it covers a case, the judge who nonetheless decides the case must be moved by considerations other than reasons furnished by the rule.
5. Any consideration that is not a reason furnished by a legal rule is an extra-legal consideration — a moral, political, or prudential judgment about what ought to be done.
6. So in every judicial decision the judge is either applying a rule that settles the case, which (3) says never happens, or deciding on extra-legal grounds.
7. If judges ultimately decide on moral grounds, the natural lawyer is vindicated in holding that moral judgment is indispensable to determining what the law requires; if judges ultimately decide on whatever grounds seem best to them, then there is no such thing as law distinct from the preferences of officials.
8. Therefore Hart's positivism is either false or empty.

I take the argument to be valid, or at least repairable into a valid one, and I would much rather give up (8) than give up (1) or (2), which look to me like nothing grander than the observation that rules are made of words. My teacher's reaction was that Hart would cheerfully accept (5), since he is willing to say that judges exercise genuine choice in the penumbra of a rule rather than merely reading off an answer; but if that is his reply, I cannot see how his position differs from my conclusion, and I am not sure whether that means the argument fails somewhere or that I have simply misunderstood him.

So I have three requests. First, tell me which step should be rejected, and explain why it looked unobjectionable to me when I wrote it down — I would like to understand the mistake, not just be told where it is. Second, if more than one exit is available, price them: does rejecting (2) commit a positivist to denying that hard cases exist at all; does rejecting (3) require a notion of what the law requires that reaches beyond the text of the rules, and if so, is that notion available to someone who thinks law is a matter of social fact; does rejecting (5) concede to Fuller more than Hart can afford to concede? Third, does the argument fare any differently against inclusive positivism than against exclusive positivism, given that they disagree about whether moral criteria can form part of the test for legal validity in a given system?
