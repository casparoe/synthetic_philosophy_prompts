I'm a retired analytical chemist — thirty-five years doing trace analysis and then running a standards group, so my whole working life was calibration: if two competent people in the lab disagreed, one of them had an instrument problem, and you re-measured until you found it. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy rather than getting a boat (my daughter's suggestion), mostly epistemology, and I've somehow ended up chairing a small standing panel of eight that advises a regulator on what level of a certain industrial contaminant in soil is acceptable when brownfield sites get redeveloped for housing. Its meant to produce one recommended figure with a range, twice a year, and the drafting is done by our secretary, a perfectly bright civil servant whose last formal maths was an A-level. The trouble: for three years running two of our members have submitted estimates differing by a factor of about five, we have comissioned three rounds of new data in the interim, and the split has not moved a hair. Both are competent, both have seen everything the other has seen, and neither is a fool.

That sent me off into the literature on disagreement — Aumann's result about agreeing to disagree, the common prior assumption, Harsanyi's line that differences between rational people should come down to information rather than priors, and the argument (I've forgotten who's) that you can't actually test whether two people share a prior, which rather takes the wind out of the enterprise. What I want isn't a lecture on that but a procedure. Each year, when the submissions land, the secretary should be able to run something like a checklist over any standing disagreement and come out with a classification and an action: keep commissioning evidence, pool the estimates, or tell the regulator that this particular cell isn't a factual question and needs a decision from them rather than another study. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each given that we cannot interrogate busy academics for hours, where the procedure stops with a settled verdict, and — most important — the points where a human being has to make a judgement call, marked as such rather than smuggled in as step four looking like arithmetic. It has to be runnable in an afternoon by someone who has never seen a posterior distribution. Tell me which of the theorem's assumptions are actually doing work once you translate them this way, and which ones you've quietly dropped because no real panel satisfies them; and be straight about where it will misfire, for instance with a member who simply isn't updating, or one whose number moves the year after they take paid work.

Then run it on three cases. (a) The current split: factor of five, three rounds of new data, no movement, identical evidence packs. (b) A member whose estimate went up by a factor of two in the year after their firm began advising a housebuilder, and who insists they only revised in the light of new toxicology. (c) Our newest member, a young modeller, whose figure sits outside everyone else's range although she's had the same pack as the rest of us. Say what the procedure cannot do, and don't hedge. If you think trying to run Aumann as a checklist is fundamentally misconceived and the honest thing is something else altogether, say so in your first paragraph and then give me the something else with your reasons. I don't want a menu of approaches with the pros and cons of each and me left to pick — I'm seventy-one, I want to be told which one to run, why that one, and where you'd bet against it working.
