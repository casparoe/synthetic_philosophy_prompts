So I'm in my second year on my department's promotions committee — seven tenured faculty, written ballots collected by the chair — and the handbook says a candidate for full professor "shall be recommended if a majority of the committee finds that the candidate satisfies each of the following: distinction in research, effectiveness in teaching, and significant service to the institution." Simple enough on paper. This year's case is going to blow up, and because I'm the one who keeps bringing up aggregation stuff at meetings (my own fault entirely) the chair has asked me to circulate a short memo before we meet on the 14th.

Here's the arithmetic from the practice ballots we ran last week, which Im treating as a reliable preview. As written, the three criteria came out like this: research passes 5-2, service passes 5-2, teaching passed 4-3 — except that right afterward one member said he should have recused himself from the teaching question since he was her dissertation adviser. He'd voted yes, so pull his ballot out and it's 3-3, and the handbook says literally nothing about ties. Meanwhile, when the chair asked each of us the direct question "should she be promoted, all things considered," exactly one of us said yes. The other six said no, most of them because they privately weight research way more heavily than service and think the three-criterion list doesn't capture that. So the bottom-line majority says reject while the criterion-by-criterion majority says promote. And it's worse one level down: the handbook defines "distinction in research" as either a monograph with a strong press or six-plus articles in top journals. Only three of us say yes on the monograph route (fails 3-4) and only two say yes on the articles route (fails 2-5), and nobody says yes to both — yet five of us voted that she satisfies the research criterion as written.

What I need from you is the memo itself: a recommendation I can actually put in front of the committee, covering both what we should do with this candidate and what aggregation rule we should adopt in writing before the next cycle, since two more cases come up next year and I dont want to relitigate this from scratch each time. Put the bottom line in your very first sentence — promote, reject, defer and re-ballot under a stated procedure, whatever you genuinely think is right — and then give the reasons, including a serious engagement with the strongest case for the losing side, which as I see it is that bottom-line-first voting is the only rule that takes each member's own all-things-considered judgment seriously instead of forcing seven people with seven different weightings to pretend they share a single decision function. Be explicit about which level the committee should be aggregating at (disjuncts, criteria, or bottom line, and what happens when they disagree), and about the tie. Hard cap of 450 words, short headers at most, and please no bibliography or names of papers — nobody on this committee is going to look anything up, they'll read it once over coffee and then vote.
