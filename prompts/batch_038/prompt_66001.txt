So, bit of an odd one and I'd really value a second pair of eyes on the framing before I commit to it. I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student, nominally in philosophy of social science but co-supervised by someone in a sociology department, and I'm putting in for a small internal nine-month fellowship that funds a self-contained chunk of dissertation work. My topic is the old question of whether generations are real kinds — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z and so on — and I've gone back and foward between the two camps enough times to be bored of both. The realists say a birth cohort that shares formative historical circumstances has genuine causal-explanatory weight, that cohort effects show up in the data and do predictive work, and that the boundaries being fuzzy doesn't make the kind any less real than, say, a species or a recession. The sceptics say the boundaries are drawn by journalists and market researchers rather than found in the world, that the label attaches to whoever happens to be aged 18-34 in a given news cycle, and that once you control properly for age and for the period in which the measurement was taken there's nothing distinctively cohort-shaped left over. My half-formed idea, and this is the bit I want you to pressure-test into shape: both sides assume the question is whether the kind has intrinsic causal powers, and neither pays attention to what actually happens when people try to estimate those powers. In age-period-cohort analysis the three variables are exactly linearly dependent — cohort is just period minus age — so no data set can ever pin down a linear cohort trend on its own, and every published cohort effect rests on a normalising constraint that the researcher chose rather than discovered. Which suggests a third position neither camp has occupied: that generations might be real in something like the way a gauge-dependent quantity is real, or real-relative-to-a-constraint, and that the interesting philosophical work is not whether cohorts carve nature at the joints but what kind of object survives non-identifiability. I want that turned into a proper third option with a name and a defence, not a compromise position that gets shouted down by both sides.

The deliverable is a two-page proposal, and I want it to do the things the panel will actually look for. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer and that a sociologist could recognise it as a question about their own practice, not a philosopher's word-game. Explain why the two existing positions leave it open — and be specific about why the realist can't just say 'the constraint is obviously the right one' and why the sceptic can't just say 'non-identifiability proves my point', because if either move works my project collapses and I'd rather know now. Then the method: what I would concretely do, roughly month by month across the nine months, and I'd like it to include something more than reading — reconstructing one standard decomposition of cohort effects so I can see exactly where the constraint enters and what it does to the answer, plus working through a live empirical case where the same data get read as a generational shift by one team and as a period effect by another. Say why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives, e.g. straight conceptual analysis of what a kind is, or just going and running the regressions myself. Then say what a successful result looks like, what would count as the project failing — I want a real failure condition, something like 'if it turns out the constraint is always forced on us by the data structure and never genuinely chosen, then there's no third position', not a weasel clause. And finish with an honest scope assessment: what's too big for nine months, where the statistics might eat me alive, and what I should cut to keep it finishable. The panel has one non-philosopher on it, so keep the jargon on a lead and gloss anything technical in a clause.
