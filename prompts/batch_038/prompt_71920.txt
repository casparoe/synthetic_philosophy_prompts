I'm the TA for a fourth-year value theory seminar, and this was a timed take-home question worth 20 marks. Below is one student's answer, submitted as-is. I haven't graded it and have no view about its quality, so please assess it on its merits rather than assuming it is strong or weak.

Question: Temkin argues, using a spectrum of pains varying in intensity and duration, that "all things considered better than" is not transitive. Reconstruct the argument, making its premises and the role of transitivity explicit. Identify the premise that its opponents most commonly reject and explain why they reject it, and assess whether the leading responses succeed.

Student's answer:

Temkin's spectrum argument is built from a sequence of states of affairs involving pain, ordered by a trade-off between intensity and duration. Call them A1 ... An. A1 is two years of excruciating torture; A2 is four years of pain slightly less intense; A3 is eight years of pain slightly less intense again; and so on, until An, a long life containing nothing worse than an hour of hangnail-level discomfort. Each adjacent pair differs only slightly, since a small drop in intensity is traded against a modest increase in duration.

The argument has two substantive premises. The first is the small-differences premise: for each i, A(i+1) is not better than Ai, because the difference between adjacent members of the series is too slight to amount to an improvement, however far apart the endpoints are. The second is the endpoint premise: An is better than A1, since it is hard to deny that a long life of hangnail-level discomfort is better than two years of excruciating torture. Given the further assumption that "better than" is complete, so that "at least as good as" is transitive whenever "better than" is, the first premise can be chained: A1 is at least as good as A2, A2 at least as good as A3, and so on, so A1 is at least as good as An. That contradicts the endpoint premise. Since both premises seem more secure than the assumption of transitivity, Temkin concludes that "better than" is not transitive after all.

Opponents nearly always attack the small-differences premise, because giving up transitivity seems to give up the possibility of weighing outcomes at all. The most common reply is Griffin's: value is discontinuous, so somewhere in the spectrum there is a pair of adjacent states between which there really is a difference in betterness, however slight the change looks. This reply carries a heavy cost — the cutoff cannot be located anywhere non-arbitrarily, and the argument seems to show that no small difference anywhere in the series could matter, which is itself implausible — but its defenders argue that abandoning transitivity is the greater cost. A related move is to treat the comparisons as imprecise rather than determinate, as Parfit suggests in the population case.

On Temkin's view, then, adjacent members of the spectrum are incomparable: there is no fact of the matter about which of the two is better, and that is what stops the chain from transmitting from one end of the spectrum to the other.

The same reasoning also disposes of Parfit's Repugnant Conclusion. Both arguments turn on the premise that a long series of individually negligible changes cannot add up to a significant one, so once betterness is intransitive the move from a world of ten billion very happy people to a world of a trillion people whose lives are barely worth living no longer goes through.

My own assessment is that the argument succeeds, but at a price Temkin underestimates: transitivity is not merely a piece of logical machinery but a condition on coherent deliberation, since a ranking that fails to transmit across long chains cannot guide choice between distant alternatives.
