I'm a third-year philosophy student, and this term I'm in a seminar on Kant's practical philosophy. Our section has just finished the discussion of the formula of universal law, and next week I'm running an optional problem-set session for the undergraduates who want to go deeper than the lectures did. The trouble is that the seminar has produced a fair amount of label-swapping — people announce that they are 'Kantians' or 'constructivists' or 'Hareans' without having any clear idea which claims about the universalizability test they are actually endorsing, and two of us spent an hour last Thursday arguing about a case only to discover we were using the word 'maxim' in different senses. So I want to hand out a diagnostic decision tree, the sort that sorts people by what they say about cases rather than by what they call themselves, and I'd like you to build it for me. The audience knows Kant's four worked examples from the second Groundwork section — the despairing man, the false deposit or promise, the neglect of one's talents, and the refusal to aid others — but has read no secondary literature whatsoever, so the instrument must not presuppose any vocabulary they haven't met.

The thing I most want is that you construct the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of interpreters: first the questions and the answer-combinations they permit, then the readings located in that space. Please write seven to nine nodes, each phrased as a concrete scenario or a claim a student could accept or reject cold, with two or three answer options. No node may name a position, and none may use stock phrases like 'contradiction in conception' or 'contradiction in the will' — those are exactly what the answers are supposed to reveal. The distinctions I need represented are these. First, maxim-description sensitivity: include at least one node where re-describing the same case at a different level of generality flips the verdict, and another asking who gets to fix the correct description — the agent's own subjective account, the most natural description of what they are doing, or the one most likely to survive universalization. Second, the diagnosis of the false-promise case: is what goes wrong that the universalized state of affairs is logically incoherent, or that the agent's plan stops working because the practice it parasitizes would vanish? A node should separate those two answers, since they seem to me to generate different verdicts elsewhere. Third, the indifferent agent: someone who genuinely prefers a world without mutual aid and is entirely unbothered by the prospect that nobody would help them. Does the test still condemn this person, and if so, on grounds that come from somewhere other than what they happen to want? Fourth, whether the two examples Kant says fail only at the volitional stage — talents and aid — can be shown to fail without any premise about what is worth wanting or about the purposes nature has given our capacities. Fifth, whether the test is a criterion of what makes an act wrong or a procedure the agent must run: consider someone who acts wrongly without ever universalizing anything, and someone who universalizes carefully but misdescribes their own maxim. Sixth, whether passing the test establishes permissibility or merely the absence of one disqualifier. Please keep every branch about how the test itself is supposed to work — I don't want nodes about the formula of humanity, about freedom, or about transcendental idealism, since those would split the group along a different axis entirely.

For the deliverable: give me the tree first, nodes and options, then the leaves. Each leaf should carry the conventional name of the reading if it has one — the orthodox two-test reading, the practical-contradiction reading on which the failure lies in the agent's own end being defeated by the success of their action, the universal-prescriptivism reading on which universalizability combined with a preference-based account of moral judgment slides into something aggregative, the deflationary reading on which the test is empty until maxims are pinned down and therefore does no work at all, and the reading on which the test is a necessary but never sufficient condition — or 'unnamed' if the cell is one nobody occupies, along with whatever other readings the space turns out to admit. Each leaf needs one sentence on the commitment its holder finds most awkward, and a list of the answer patterns that lead to it. Then tell me which answer-combinations are unoccupied: I'd like at least three, each with a diagnosis of whether the cell is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply explored it. Flag any pair of leaves your nodes cannot genuinely separate, and say whether some further case would separate them or whether the difference is only verbal. Finally, tell me what to say to a student whose answers are inconsistent — which single node is most likely to have been answered in bad faith or in confusion, and how to walk them back. Two practical constraints: this is meant to be something a teaching assistant could photocopy, so keep it to roughly four pages, and please do not give me citations, a bibliography, or quotations from Kant or from any interpreter. Paraphrase the examples in your own words. Nobody in my group has library access, and I would rather have an honest reconstruction than invented references.
