For eight years I have designed and operated the internal currencies of a large consumer platform — loyalty credits, creator payouts, seasonal event tokens — and I think about money almost entirely in that register: issuance rules and hard caps, faucets and sinks, float, velocity, redemption rights, what sits in the reserve behind a redeemable token, the unit of account and how it is quoted against outside assets, secondary markets and the arbitrage they invite, price oracles, burn mechanisms, and the credibility of a commitment to convertibility. Behind that I have one undergraduate economics course and no philosophy whatever. On a whim I recently read Berkeley on money — the last dialogue of Alciphron (1732) and a good stretch of The Querist (published in parts between 1735 and 1737) — and found him far more congenial than I expected.

Here is what I took away, in my own words; please correct me first if I have garbled any of it, since I would rather translate the actual position than my reconstruction of it. First, money is not a commodity with value of its own: gold and silver are tokens or counters whose whole business is to record and transfer someone's power to call on the labour of others. Second, the material the token is made of is therefore nearly indifferent — what matters is that it circulates, and that holding it is not a substitute for working. Third, a nation's wealth is its industry rather than its hoard, and a policy aimed at accumulating specie mistakes the counter for the thing counted. Fourth, Ireland's coin shortage is on this showing a design problem, and the remedy is a national bank issuing paper against the security of land. Fifth, idle money is dead money and the issuing authority should be able to keep it moving, which is why Berkeley was willing to contemplate legal limits on the rate of interest, a high return on idle money being an invitation not to work.

Now the task. Restate those five claims in the vocabulary I actually use, as literally as it will go: what is each one saying about issuance, sinks, float, redemption, backing, or convertibility? Then give me the residue report, which is the part I really want. For each of the five, say whether it translates cleanly, whether it translates only if I quietly import an assumption my framework does not license, or whether it has no expression in my terms at all. Be explicit about the fudges. I suspect, for instance, that rendering the claim that money exists to set industry in motion as the claim that money should have high velocity converts a purpose into a statistic, and I want to know whether that is a genuine loss or merely a difference of idiom. Tell me what the security of land carries in his bank proposal that collateral does not carry in mine — is there something about a fixed, non-reproducible, territorially anchored backing that my notion of a reserve asset cannot state? And tell me whether his identification of value with a recorded proportion of power over other people's labour has any counterpart at all in a ledger of balances, where a balance is just a number that owes nothing to anyone's exertion.

Two further questions. How much of this depends on Berkeley's general philosophy — his rejection of abstract ideas and his account of words as signs that need have no idea annexed to them — and does the monetary theory still stand once that is stripped out, or is the metaphysics load-bearing? And then run the residue in the opposite direction: name the features of my vocabulary that Berkeley has no way to express, such as secondary markets, arbitrage against a peg, expected inflation, and credible commitment, and say whether their absence marks a limitation of his theory or is evidence that my machinery largely manufactures problems in order to solve them.

Close with a verdict on which of the five claims survives the translation intact, which survives only in distorted form, and which dies — and for each death, whether something worth keeping was lost or whether the claim merely looked deeper than it was. Do not smuggle my framework's assumptions in and present them as translation; wherever you must choose between fidelity to Berkeley and fluency in my idiom, choose Berkeley and tell me what the choice cost. I have no web access, so do not point me at sources or give me a bibliography. Please answer in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists, only paragraphs, on the order of 1,200 to 1,600 words.
