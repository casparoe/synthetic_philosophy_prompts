A popular defense of Penrose's Gödelian argument that I've been reading makes its case by analogy: a formal system is like an object language that cannot define its own truth-predicate, whereas the human mathematician is like the metalanguage in which that predicate can be defined — which is precisely why we can see the truth of the system's Gödel sentence even though the system cannot. The author takes this to establish that no machine can capture mathematical insight, since whatever system a machine runs, we can always occupy a level above it. Another member of my reading group objected that the passage from a language to its metalanguage is itself a uniform, rule-governed operation, so the "metalanguage" is only a larger machine, and the analogy, if it shows anything, shows that we are machines too.

Please audit the analogy. Give the explicit mapping between source and target — system, Gödel sentence, truth-predicate, and the act of ascending a level — and separate the disanalogies that bear on the intended conclusion from the merely decorative ones. Then take the iterability objection head-on: if every individual ascent is effective, what would have to be true about where the mind sits in the resulting hierarchy for it to be doing something no finite program could do, and is that plausible? Conclude by saying whether the anti-mechanist inference survives, and, if you think the comparison fails, what analogy would carry the point better.
