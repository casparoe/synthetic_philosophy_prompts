I'm putting together a handout for my grad seminar on perception and I've got a running list of cases where I'm pretty confident about whether cognition gets inside perception or doesn't, but I can't see what ties my calls together. Here they are.

1. Checker-shadow illusion — you know the two squares are the same grey and it changes nothing you see. Not penetrated.
2. Hollow-face mask — same, knowledge is useless. Not penetrated.
3. Experts tasting a white wine that's been quietly dyed red and describing it with red-wine vocabulary. Penetrated. I think they taste something different, not just say something different.
4. That old study where kids called a desireable playing card physically bigger than an undesirable one of the same size. Not penetrated — my read is it's a judgement effect, not a perceptual one.
5. The birdwatcher who picks out a warbler in the foliage where I see only leaves. Penetrated. She sees it; she isn't inferring it.
6. Someone who hasn't eaten all day is faster to detect briefly flashed food words than a fed control. Not penetrated.
7. Speakers of a language with two basic blues are faster to discriminate two shades when the shades straddle the label boundary, and the advantage evaporates under verbal interference. Penetrated.
8. Same speakers, two shades of green where their language draws no line: no advantage at all. Nothing to explain there, but it matters to me that 7 and 8 come apart exactly where the lexicon does.

The pair that bugs me is 4 and 5: a prior state shifts something appearance-y, structurally identical as far as I can tell, and I call one penetration and one response bias. Same unease with 3 versus 6.

Then tell me what your principle says about two cases I made up. (a) A subject sees a blurred ambiguous figure for half a second; two seconds after it's gone they're told what it was; later, asked to pick from a set of near-neighbours the image that best matches what they saw, they pick the more prototypical one more often than controls who got the label before viewing. (b) Someone told the building is on fire reports the corridor to the exit as looking longer than a control does.

What I want: the principle — or the smallest set of them — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I've never seen, with a line on which of my verdicts each clause exists to save, and honesty about which ones it can't. If the cleanest version overturns 4 or 5, say which and why. And end with what would change your mind: which consideration, if I pressed it, would push you to a different principle, or where you think your own reasoning is most likely to break. 400 words max, please.
