I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy — bioethics-flavoured — and I'm the lay ethics member on our regional health board's benefits advisory panel, which mostly means I'm the one asked to write things up when the clinicians start going round in circles. Next month we decide whether to fund elective oocyte cryopreservation (egg freezing for non-medical reasons) for women under 35, capped at two cycles. Our analysts cost it at roughly £3,000 a cycle, so the programme lands around £1.8m a year for our patch.

The fight isn't about the money. One member, a consultant obstetrician, objects on grounds about the children. Her case: our own impact assessment projects that users would push their first birth from an average age of about 29 to about 34, and a first birth at 34-ish carries elevated absolute risk on a handful of outcomes the modellers flagged (they also concede, to be fair to them, that the direct evidence on children born from frozen rather than fresh oocytes is thin and contested, so she's leaning partly on the maternal-age channel). On her telling, we'd be spending public money to bring into the world children who are more likely to be born with a serious condition — that isn't a health gain, it's a harm we'd be commissioning, and the service shouldn't do that for free.

Two other members think she's making a beginner's philosophical mistake, and they've been saying so in increasingly smug terms. Their line: those children don't exist yet; if we don't fund the programme, those particular children will never exist at all, and different children will — conceived earlier, from different eggs. So the programme can't leave them worse off, because the only alternative for them is never existing, and a life carrying a modestly raised risk of a serious condition is not a life that isn't worth living. Ergo the objection is empty.

Here's why I can't just write the memo their way. The same panel has a second item coming up in the autumn: a clinic has written to us asking whether it can offer preimplantation genetic testing to a couple who want to select deliberately for an embryo carrying a hereditary disability, and at least two of the smug ones have already said in the minutes that they'd struggle to refuse it. If the move "you can't harm someone by causing them to exist rather than not exist" settles the egg-freezing case, it looks like it settles that one too, and I don't think this board wants to be the one that publicly funds the deliberate selection of a disabled embryo. But the obvious escape — say the autumn case is different because there you're choosing between possible children, all of whom will exist as somebody-or-other — I can't see how to make stick against the obstetrician, who'll say that in the egg-freezing case we're choosing between possible children too.

So please draft the memo I'll circulate. It has to land somewhere concrete: fund it, refuse it, or fund it under named conditions. And it has to hand the panel a test we can actually apply in the autumn to the clinic's request, not just a verdict on this one item.

Format, if you can:

- **Recommendation** — up front, about 120 words, one paragraph saying what we should vote and why, in language a non-philosopher could repeat back to me in the corridor.
- **What the objection is actually claiming** — around 200 words. Set out the obstetrician's argument properly and at its strongest; half the panel is caricaturing it as simple ignorance of how conception works, and that's not fair to it.
- **Why the existence reply doesn't close the case** — around 300 words, this is the meat. It has to work for a reader who has never encountered any of this literature: please don't use the phrase "nonidentity problem" or any other technical label unless you immediately cash it out in ordinary words.
- **The autumn test** — around 250 words. State the criterion so a clinician could apply it to a brand-new request without calling me, and be honest about the one or two cases where I can already see it delivering an answer that will make people unhappy.
- **The strongest thing the losing side can say** — around 150 words. Whichever of the three positions you end up rejecting, steelman it, and tell me what my recommendation costs us: what we give up, and what we'd have to say if the cost lands.

Keep the whole thing under about 1,000 words. No references, no citations, no names of papers or thinkers I'd have to go and look up — the panel won't, and neither will I.
