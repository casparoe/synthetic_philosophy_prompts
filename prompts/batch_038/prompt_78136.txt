I'm co-teaching (well, "co-teaching" — I'm the TA and I run the section) an upper-level seminar this term called Persons and Roles: Early Chinese Thought and Tragedy. Twelve students, mostly philosophy and lit majors. We've just come out of the Analects and Mengzi selections and next week we do King Lear, and I was asked to write up a reading of the play through Confucian role ethics — specifically the Ames and Rosemont line, where a person isn't an autonomous individual who then happens to occupy roles, but is constituted by them, a center of relations rather than a bearer of rights or preferences. I know that sounds like the setup for a term paper I don't want to write, but I think there's something real here and I want help thinking it through rather than a summary of either side. The worry I keep coming back to is that Lear is the one play where the role vocabulary is explicit and where it goes catastrophically wrong, so I can see a version of this reading that's just "Confucius predicted the tragedy," which would be boring and also probably unfair to everybody involved.

So here's what I actually want to chew on. First: Cordelia. Her answer to the love test — that she loves his majesty "according to my bond; nor more nor less," plus the follow-up about how when she marries, the man she weds will carry half her love, care and duty with him — reads to me like the most role-ethical speech in the play. She refuses to describe an inner state and instead names what she owes in virtue of the relation she's in. Is that her being the one character who gets the metaphysics of personhood right, or is she the character who exposes what role ethics can't handle, namely a good role-occupant stuck with a bad role-giver? The tradition has resources here I'd like you to actually use: the doctrine that names have to be rectified, that if they aren't then speech doesn't accord with things, affairs don't get completed, ritual and music don't flourish, punishments miss thier mark, and people don't know where to put hand and foot; the idea that a person without ren has nothing to do with ritual, so empty performance isn't ritual at all; and the filial-piety doctrine that a child may and sometimes must remonstrate with a parent who is about to do wrong, gently and without resentment. Goneril and Regan perform the role flawlessly and are hollow, Cordelia refuses to perform and is truthful — which of those is the Confucian diagnosis and which is the Shakespearean one? Second: Lear's abdication. He insists he'll still retain "the name, and all the additions to a king" while handing over the rule and the revenue. Does the play then simply unfold the rectification-of-names sequence, or is the storm doing work the doctrine can't do? And there's a comparison I keep wanting to make and don't know how to defend: in the Mengzi there's that hypothetical where a disciple asks what Shun would do if, as emperor, his own father had killed a man — and the answer is that the judge would arrest him, nothing could stop him, and Shun would treat giving up the empire as like throwing away a worn sandal, carry his father off on his back to the seacoast and live there happily, having forgotten the empire. Lear is that case inverted: he gives up the empire first and loses the daughter anyway.

Third, and this is the one that keeps me up: the heath. Lear looks at Poor Tom and says unaccommodated man is no more but such a poor bare forked animal as thou art, and starts tearing his clothes off. On one hand that looks like Shakespeare staging the Confucian thesis as a horror — strip away every relation and there is no residual self to find, just an animal, which is exactly why the relations are load-bearing. On the other hand, the compassion lands in that same scene, in the speech about the poor naked wretches and "O, I have ta'en too little care of this," and it arrives precisely when the roles are gone. If there's no self outside of roles, where does that recognition come from, and does the play thereby smuggle in the very thing role ethics denies? I want you to say which way that scene cuts rather than splitting the difference. Then tell me where the reading breaks — I don't want Lear reduced to an illustration, and I'm genuinely uneasy about using one tradition as a yardstick for a play that has nothing to do with it, so flag honestly where the analogy strains or where I'm just doing comparative ventriloquism. End with what you think the play adds to the debate that argument alone doesn't get at (I suspect it has something to do with what losing a role actually feels like from the inside, and with the fact that the love test is a demand to step outside the role to describe it — but develop it better than that), and give me two or three discussion questions I could drop into section. No bibliography or reading list please, I'm not asking for sources, I'm asking for the reading itself.
