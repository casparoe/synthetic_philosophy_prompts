Thirty-eight years of X-ray crystallography, and now that I'm retired I've fallen into philosophy the way other men my age fall into golf. My granddaughter is eleven and she's the one who got me onto this particular puzzle, because I watched her play for a few weekends and then I watched myself react to what she was doing, and the two don't line up at all. I'd like the equivalent of a minimal model that fits my data points, so here they are: my gut verdicts about video-game characters, with no theory attached.

1. She spent something like two hundred hours building up a little fishing village in one of those cozy games — named every villager, laid out the gardens, the works. The save corrupted and she lost it and she cried for most of a day. I felt bad for her, about as bad as if she'd lost a sketchbook. Fine, that's just her loss, nothing to do with the villagers.
2. But last month I deleted an old save of my own, a town I'd put comparable hours into, and I got a twinge that was not about losing my work. It felt more like clearing out a house somebody had been living in. I don't want to say the twinge is an illusion, but I also don't believe those villagers were anything.
3. In an open-world game, going out of your way to run over pedestrians for the fun of it: wrong. I'm confident about this. Not "bad for you" as a habit, not a warning about desensitization — wrong in the doing, the way cruelty is wrong.
4. Same game, same act, when the game hands it to you as a mission objective — car chase, escape, whatever: not wrong, or at any rate not the same kind of thing. On screen the two are identical. This is the pair I cannot reconcile.
5. Killing the companion the game has spent thirty hours making you like, when it offers that as a choice: much worse than killing the fortieth nameless bandit, even though as a matter of code they're the same sort of object and I know it.
6. Those newer games where the non-player characters are driven by a language model and can improvise, beg, refer back to what you did to them ten minutes ago: going through one of those with a sword feels considerably worse to me than the scripted version, and I know perfectly well there is nobody home in either case. So one of these two verdicts must be tracking a cue instead of a fact — and I'd rather not end up with a principle whose main job is to tell me my own feelings are miscalibrated.
7. A neighbour's father died last year and the family found his console with a half-finished save on it. My neighbour couldn't bring himself to erase it and in the end he just left the machine unplugged in a closet. I think he was right to feel that way, and I'd feel the same about not binning a dead man's letters — but I would think him odd to say it about the dead man's car.
8. Speedrunners who skip all the dialogue and treat every character as an obstacle: no moral reaction from me whatsoever, not even mild disapproval. If anything I admire the efficiency.
9. Modding a game so the civilians plead more vividly, then playing it: worse than playing the unmodded version. Writing that mod and never playing it: not worse than anything I can name.
10. Told that a given game's characters are actually people operating avatars remotely for a wage: everything flips instantly and completely, and I don't feel the least conflict about the flip.

The case I keep going round on is a game whose characters are engineered to be as psychologically persuasive as possible, whose designers state plainly that nothing behind them is sentient, and where I believe the designers — and I still can't bring myself to do the thing. Do I have an obligation there, or good taste, or a phobia? Those feel like three very different reports and I can't tell which one I'm making.

So: is there a principle that gets most of these? I'd like it stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case that does the work for each clause — I want to know which of my data points is actually load-bearing rather than decoration. Then tell me which verdict it forces me to give up. I expect it's one of 4 and 6, but I want that argued rather than assumed, and I want the cost spelled out. And then give me its verdict on a case I haven't listed, something like this: a game whose characters are simulated in enough detail that the developers genuinely can't rule out that some kind of experience is occurring, and nobody has checked, because checking would be expensive and the answer might force them to shut the servers down. Finally — and this is where I suspect I'm smuggling something in — say whether you'd rather have the baroque principle that saves all ten of my points or a clean one that throws two out. In crystallography we had a rule of thumb about not adding a parameter unless it paid for itself in the fit, and I can't tell whether that rule of thumb transfers to this subject or whether its a habit that has no business outside the lab.
