I'd like you to take on the role of a moral philosopher who works on blame, criticism, and the conditions under which one person may reproach another — someone who can tell me what the structure of my problem actually is, rather than simply what to do next. Before you advise me, please spend a few sentences identifying the shape of the difficulty and setting out the genuinely available options, and think carefully about which considerations pull in each direction before you settle anything. I am not asking for references, a reading list, or a survey of positions; if there are established distinctions in this area that do real work on my case, use them and gloss them briefly as you go, but do not send me off to look things up. What I want is reasoning I can act on, and a verdict you are prepared to own.

Here is the situation. I serve on the seven-member volunteer board of a community land trust in a small town; we hold about four hundred acres of farmland and woodland in perpetuity. One member, whom I'll call D., missed six of eight meetings between September and February, then in April sent a long email not only to the board but to our entire membership list, accusing us of letting the mission slide and pointing specifically to our decision to accept a restricted gift from a developer whose later phases we have no leverage over. The frustrating part is that D. is largely right. Several of us said as much privately. The board has now scheduled a June meeting at which a formal censure of D. — for the absences and for going to the membership — is likely to be proposed, and the chair has asked me to draft the motion and speak to it, on the grounds that I'm the one who knows the substance best.

The complication is that I missed five of those same eight meetings. My father was dying; that is the reason, and it is a good one, but I never told anyone, and the chair entered me as excused without explanation. Two people know: the chair, who has said he will not raise it and who has let me understand that he considers me in his debt, and my spouse. In the gaps between meetings I did substantially more work than my share — the bylaws amendment and the survey of the easements were both mine — and I will admit that some of that was driven by guilt. Attendance records exist and any member may request them, so this is not a secret that is safe, only one that is currently unspoken. There is one other member, M., with perfect attendance and nothing comparable in her past, who is willing to bring the censure instead; but M. is widely disliked on the board and has a history of using procedure to settle personal scores, and if she raises it the motion will be read as a grudge, D. will become a martyr, and the underlying problem with the gift will never get addressed. One friend tells me that having done the same thing, I have forfeited any right to say so. Another tells me that this is precisely the logic that lets people off the hook, since on that standard no one is ever in a position to criticize anyone. I don't know which of them is right, and I suspect their disagreement runs deeper than my case.

So: may I be the one to bring this, and if so, on what conditions? Does disclosing my own absences first change the answer, or would disclosure just be another self-serving performance — and does it matter whether I disclose voluntarily or am exposed? Does the fact that I've since quietly made up the work restore anything, or does the fact that only two people know make things worse rather than better? I want you to commit: tell me what you would do and how confident you are, and flag the point in your reasoning where you are least sure, plus any detail of my description that, if it were different, would flip your conclusion. And please push back if my framing is off. If it turns out that my own conduct is doing no work at all here, and what my two friends are arguing about is a distraction from the real question; or if what I've dressed up as a question about whether I may speak is really just fear of being found out; or if the whole thing can't be resolved at the level of principle and I'm asking you for something that doesn't exist — say so plainly instead of answering politely inside my terms.
