Two friends of mine who both teach research ethics have asked me to referee an argument they cannot settle. The case: a man with early-stage prostate cancer agrees to a radical prostatectomy after his surgeon tells him the operation carries a 5 percent risk of permanent incontinence. The hospital's own registry puts that surgeon's rate at 15 percent; the surgeon knew this and rounded down, reasoning that the honest number would drive the man toward watchful waiting, which he judged worse for him. The surgery went well and the man is continent. He learned the real figure two years later and is furious.

A: The consent was invalid, full stop. He agreed to a different operation than the one performed — one with a third of the risk. That he came to no harm is beside the point; that is precisely what makes this a violation of autonomy rather than a bad bet.

B: Invalid is too strong. The question is whether the deception changed what he was consenting to, or merely how attractive the thing he consented to looked. He chose this operation by this surgeon; he was simply wrong about one of its properties. And if being wrong about any property voids consent, then consent is void whenever a patient's beliefs diverge from the truth at all — including when the physician said nothing.

A: That reductio overreaches, because it lumps together the noise of the world and the surgeon's own registry. There is a difference between a patient who happens to be mistaken and a patient who has been made mistaken by the very person soliciting his agreement.

B: Grant it — but then your test is about who produced the false belief, not about what the consent was for, and you have quietly dropped the "different operation" line you opened with. You also now owe us a materiality standard. Whose? If it is this patient's — would he have refused had he known — then the same sentence from the same surgeon yields valid consent with one man and void consent with another, purely as a function of their private risk appetites.

A: That is a feature, not a bug. The autonomy at stake is his, not an average patient's.

B: Then you cannot also convict the surgeon, since on your view he had no way of knowing.

They stopped there. Please settle it move by move.

Format: begin with "The crux" (about 100 words: what they are actually disagreeing about once the rhetoric is stripped away). Then "Move-by-move" (roughly 600 words): take the six turns in order, rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify every rating. Then "The candidate tests" (about 300 words): lay out the tests on offer — what was agreed to versus who caused the false belief versus subjective materiality — and say which, if any, survives the objections raised above, or whether a different test is needed. Then "What both of them missed" (about 200 words). End with "Verdict" (about 150 words): who is more right overall, and whether the fact that no harm resulted should enter the analysis at all. Refer to doctrines and positions by name only; I will supply citations myself.
