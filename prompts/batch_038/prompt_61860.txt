Second-year PhD student, history of science, and I've got a gap in my vocabulary that I can't fill. Not asking for references or a reading list — I want to know whether the thing below has a name, and if it doesn't, what you'd call it and where exactly its edges are. I need it because I'm writing up a methods chapter and I currently have to describe this in three awkward sentences every single time it comes up.

Background: everyone in my department agrees you shouldn't judge past people by today's standards. Fine, agreed. But I keep noticing that the instruction to avoid presentism is itself a present-day instruction — it's a now-norm about how to treat the past, and it gets enforced with now-moral energy. Two instances that made me notice:

1. Imagine a historian in the 1950s writing about Caribbean plantation owners in a deliberately unsentimental, try-to-see-it-from-their-side way, and getting praised at the time for being balanced and fair. That same book today would get torn apart for precisely the balance — for treating enslavers as complicated humans and thereby soft-pedalling the institution. So the 1950s author followed the anti-presentist rule impeccably, and is now condemned for following it, by people who think they're enforcing the same rule.

2. In seminars now, people sometimes say: I'm sure future historians will read what we write about climate or colonialism or gender and be appalled, but I genuinely have no idea what specifically they'll be appalled by. And saying that is treated as a virtue — it marks you out as self-aware. But as a method it's empty. If you can't say what your distortions are, you can't correct for them, so "beware of presentism" can't actually guide anyone's research choices. It just licenses a general mood of contrition.

The pattern I'm circling is a methodological norm whose whole point is to stop you importing your own era's standards, but which can only ever be articulated and policed using your own era's standards, and whose violations are in principle unidentifiable from inside. There's some bootstrapping thing going on and I want a handle on it.

Things I know it sits near but which I'm fairly sure are not it: plain anachronism, which is about getting the past wrong rather than about the norm for not getting it wrong; presentism-vs-historicism as those get used in the Carr-and-Elton-style theory-of-history arguments; whig history, which seems to be about a particular narrative shape; chronological snobbery, which runs in the opposite direction; and something I half-remember from philosophy of science about past scientists being unable to conceive of the alternatives to their own theories, which feels structurally very close but is about science rather than about historians writing history.

So: does this have an established name in historiography or the philosophy of history? If it does, tell me which of the above it actually is and where I've gone wrong. If it doesnt, coin something and define it properly. I care much more about the carving than the label — what would count as a genuine instance, what wouldn't, and in particular whether instance 2 (the unactionable self-accusation) is the same phenomenon as instance 1 (the norm enforced with present-day moral force) or two different things that I've carelessly bundled together. If they're different, I'd rather have two terms than one sloppy one. A bare name won't help me at all.

Please write the whole answer in British English, spellings and phrasing both.
