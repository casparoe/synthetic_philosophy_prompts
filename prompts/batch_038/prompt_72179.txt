I am putting the finishing touches on the syllabus for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of time, and the session that has given me the most trouble is the one on the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. The students will have read Rietdijk's 1966 paper in Philosophy of Science, Putnam's 1967 "Time and Physical Geometry," and Howard Stein's response, so they will arrive knowing the dialectic by heart: a frame-independence premise about what counts as real, a distant galaxy in slow relative motion whose plane of simultaneity — thanks to the enormous spatial separation multiplying a tiny velocity term — cuts through our world-lines years in our future, another such galaxy moving the other way whose plane cuts through our past, and hence the conclusion that the future is no less real than the present and that becoming has no place in a Minkowskian picture. I want to hand them something that attacks the argument at a joint they will not expect, and I believe I have it, but I should like an independent check before I commit it to paper.

Here is the line I intend to press. The whole construction depends on treating the relation of distant simultaneity as though physics handed it to us, when in fact, on the Reichenbach–Grünbaum account of the matter, it is fixed by a free stipulation: the one-way speed of light is not measurable independently of a synchronization convention, and the parameter ε may be set anywhere in the open unit interval without changing a single observable prediction. So the presentist is not obliged to accept Einstein synchronization at all. She may choose a non-standard ε under which the hyperplane through her present moment is tilted in whatever way suits her — in particular, under which the events in the Andromeda-like galaxy that Putnam's moving observer counts as already real are not so counted — and since no experiment can distinguish her convention from the standard one, Minkowski geometry is in no position to object. On this reading the Rietdijk–Putnam argument is not an inference from physics to four-dimensionalism at all, but an inference from one permissible coordinate convention to an ontological conclusion, and a presentist who simply declines the convention has blocked it at no cost. I plan to add, by way of anticipating the obvious rejoinder, that Malament's result deriving standard simultaneity from the causal connectibility relation does not help the eternalist, since it merely shows what is definable given a choice of relation the presentist need not privilege.

What I would like from you is a diagnosis of this argument, pitched as you would pitch it to a first-year graduate student who has the special relativity but not the metaphysics. Where exactly does it hold and where does it break? In particular, I want to be told plainly whether the ε-freedom of the conventionality thesis has any bearing whatever on the cross-frame relativity of simultaneity that the argument actually exploits, whether a non-standard synchronization yields the single objective present the presentist needs or instead a proliferation of candidate presents, and what it costs her — in the way of a preferred foliation or other structure — if she wants the global, frame-independent simultaneity that the Lorentzian formulations build in by hand. Then, separately, a question I cannot answer to my own satisfaction and which I expect to face from a colleague in the physics department: Rietdijk's title promises determinism, whereas the conclusion the argument seems able to support concerns only the reality of future events, not their causal fixity. Is the distinction between the future's being already real and its being already settled one the eternalist is entitled to draw, or does it collapse once the block is in place? If it collapses, I should like to know what becomes of the familiar complaint that the argument licenses fatalism about deliberation.
