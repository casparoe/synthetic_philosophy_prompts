I'm the volunteer governance lead for a professional association (about 6,000 members, one big annual meeting) and I've been dragged into a bylaws rewrite. Three things get voted on regularly: an annual prize (usually five to nine finalists, decided by a 40-member selection committee using secret ballots and sequential elimination of the lowest each round), board elections (14 seats, around 30 nominees, whole membership votes, STV with a Droop quota), and occasional referenda — this year it's whether dues go up 4%, up 11%, or stay flat, plurality wins.

Last year's board election went badly and I think its my fault for not understanding what I was administering. There's a well-organized caucus from one subfield, maybe 12% of the people who actually vote, and they got five of their own elected. Afterwards several of them told me cheerfully that they'd planned it in advance: list only our five, list nobody else, don't bother with preferences. Meanwhile the prize had a different mess — one heavy favorite and two similar mid-tier candidates, and afterwards a couple of committee members admitted they'd buried one of the similar ones to stop a split.

Our committee is seven people, none of us voting theorists, plus one retired economist who keeps saying "Gibbard–Satterthwaite, so it doesn't matter what we pick" and then proposes things anyway. Two hours a month, spreadsheets at most, and whatever we recommend has to survive a membership vote to amend the bylaws.

What I want is a procedure, not an essay. Something we can run whenever a rule question lands on the agenda — which rule for which office, whether to change one, whether a screening stage is allowed — with the questions to ask in order, what counts as a settled answer to each, and the points where the procedure stops and hands the choice back to the membership because it's a values call rather than a technical one. Crucially it must not let us hide behind "every rule is manipulable." I need to be able to tell the economist why that sentence doesn't end the conversation, and tell the other six what it does rule out.

Then run it on three cases: the prize with one favorite and two similar rivals; the 14-seat board election with the caucus; and the three-option dues referendum, where I suspect there's a majority cycle. At least one of those should come out in a way the committee will hate — better now than at the annual meeting.

Two things I want handled explicitly rather than glossed. First, does screening at the nomination stage actually get us out from under the impossibility result, or does it just relocate the manipulation somewhere less visible? We're floating a nominations committee that narrows 30 nominees down to 14 before the membership votes. Second, someone proposed "randomize among the top three after a ranked count" as a fix — is that even coherent, and can the procedure tell us when randomization earns its keep versus when it's just noise dressed as fairness?

Last thing: the economist wants a rule where nobody can gain more than "one ranking position's worth" by lying. I think that's not measurable on the ballots we actually collect, but I can't argue it properly. Say whether he's wrong, and if he isn't, how seven non-specialists would check it in practice.

No citations or reading list — I'm not writing anything up, I just need something usable at next month's meeting.
