I run a small reading group on epistemic game theory and this term I want to knock out the intuition that making true information public can't make everybody worse off. I know the standard gestures — bank runs, currency attacks, Morris-Shin style global games — and that's exactly why I don't want one of those as the setting. The underlying mechanism can rhyme with them, that's fine, but the case itself has to be small and mundane and fit on one slide.

So: build me a two-player case to this spec.

- Finite players (two), a finite set of states of the world (two is plenty, three if you really need it), an explicit payoff matrix for each state, small integer payoffs. A source that is truthful, and known by both players to be truthful, reports which state obtains. No lying, no cheap talk, no trembles, no bounded reasoning, no mixed strategies doing the load-bearing work.
- Scenario A: the report is made publicly. Both players hear the same sentence, both know both heard it, and so on all the way up. End at a unique outcome.
- Scenario B: the same sentence, about the same state, is delivered so that both players end up knowing the state but neither knows that the other knows it — for instance each is told seperately and given no idea whether the other was told. You can pick the arrangement, but those two conditions are non-negotiable. End at a unique outcome here too.
- The scenario B outcome has to be strictly better for *both* players than the scenario A outcome. So the harm in A falls on the person whose interests the report serves too, not just on the other guy.

Then the checks:

1. Spell out each scenario as an explicit chain. At every level, say what each player believes, what each believes the other believes, and what each plays. Then say exactly which link is missing — or merely probabilistic rather than certain — in scenario B, and show that the scenario A inference genuinely cannot be run without it.
2. Show the scenario A outcome is unique rather than just the one I'd like: name the other candidate and kill it. If scenario B admits more than one outcome, tell me which selection principle you're using and defend it in one sentence.
3. Identify the single feature of the setup doing the work, and tell me what happens if I remove it — e.g. if in scenario B the source adds "I told the other one too."
4. Say how someone who insists that public information is never worse than private information would attack this case, and whether the attack lands.
5. If you had to weaken any of my constraints to get it to work, say which and why — I'd rather know than have you quietly fudge it.

The whole point is that the only difference between A and B is the order of knowledge, so anything that smuggles in extra information, extra players, an infinite regress, or a continuum of types counts as cheating. Also please don't just reskin the muddy children or the email game.
