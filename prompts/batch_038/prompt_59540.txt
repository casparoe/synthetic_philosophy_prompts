I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works on formal ethics about what to do when you don't know which moral theory is true, and I came away genuinely unsure whether my own position holds together. Here it is, as plainly as I can state it:

(1) Exactly one first-order moral theory is true, and which one it is does not depend in any way on my credences.
(2) Since I don't know which theory that is, the right procedure is to maximize expected choice-worthiness: assign each option a choice-worthiness under each candidate theory, weight by my credence in that theory, and take the option with the highest total.
(3) Comparisons of choice-worthiness across theories are possible, but only roughly — I don't believe there is a precise cardinal exchange rate between, say, a utilitarian and a deontological verdict, and I won't pretend there is one.
(4) Where comparison genuinely fails, I switch to something like a parliamentary model: the theories send delegates in proportion to my credences, and the delegates bargain.
(5) I don't think the fanaticism objection defeats expected-value reasoning in general, but I do impose a ceiling: no two percent credence in an astronomically large good may override what I am near-certain is a serious wrong here and now.
(6) An agent who follows (2) and thereby does what is objectively wrong has done wrong, full stop — though she is not criticizable for it, since she reasoned as well as she could.
(7) Moral uncertainty should not generate requirements for an agent who has no grasp of the theory in question; credence in something she could not articulate shouldn't move her.

Please audit this set rather than lecture me: which of these commitments pull against one another, which pairs only look like they do, and what the least costly revision would be in each real case. Rank the genuine tensions by severity instead of treating them as equally bad, and close by naming the single revision that would clean up the most.
