So I've spent the last few years on a small interdisciplinary panel — call it an off-world intervention review board — that a national agency convenes to give an ethical opinion on missions touching worlds that might have native life. Seven of us, I'm the only philosopher, we've issued maybe a dozen rulings. I'm drafting a paper on precedent-based reasoning in applied ethics and I want to use our own record as the test case, so I'm trying to pressure-test it before I write anything down.

Here are five of the rulings, in my paraphrase:

**A.** Approved with conditions a drill mission to an ice world where there was no positive evidence of life but where subsurface brine was plausible. We insisted on the strictest available forward-contamination standard anyway, on the grounds that absence of evidence wasnt going to carry that much weight.

**B.** Rejected a proposal to deliberately crash an unsterilizable impactor into a fractured ice crust to generate an artificial plume for spectroscopy. The science was excellent and everyone wanted it. Our stated reason was that a guaranteed, irreversible introduction isn't the kind of cost that gets traded off against knowledge.

**C.** Approved a lander to a heavily irradiated, thoroughly surveyed surface we were confident is sterile, accepting that a handful of Earth spores would inevitably ride along. The ruling said that where there is no native lineage, the loss is one of scientific opportunity, not of moral standing.

**D.** Rejected a proposal to seed a sterile world with engineered microbes to get a biosphere going, even though the long-run case for it was serious and well-argued. Our reason: we weren't prepared to treat the absence of life as a standing loss for us to remedy, and manufacturing a lineage is not the same thing as preserving one.

**E.** This is the one that'll matter most. Eight years ago an unsterilized lander broke up on entry and scattered debris across a region of a world we had reason to think harbored native microbes. Last year we approved a fast, deliberately under-sterilized mission to go sequence everything in that region, natives and Earth contaminants alike, on the ground that the region's independence was already gone and the only value left was recordable — and recordable only quickly. We attached one condition and it was contentious: record, don't manage. No attempts to feed, shelter, or otherwise prop up whatever was there.

**New proposal.** A cryobot melt-through to an ocean moon. The complication: twelve years ago a probe failed its sterilization check and went through a fracture into the ocean, so Earth organisms are almost certainly already in there, and there's now decent evidence native ones are too. Option 1 is a scrupulously sterile surface-and-shallow-substrate mission, slow, that will very likely never touch native biology. Option 2 is the cryobot, which cannot be fully sterilized, plus a sensor swarm that disperses a nutrient cocktail ahead of itself — partly to make native biosignatures easier to detect, partly (and this is the bit that's got me) to give the native population a better shot at persisting through the next few centuries of contact with whatever came off that probe. Option 2 is the only way well ever see native biology. But the nutrient release means that if the natives do persist, their descendants will be a lineage we shaped, and possibly a lineage that would otherwise have been replaced.

What should we vote? Specifically I want: the candidate rules that A–E are jointly consistent with (and be explicit if you think some ruling doesn't sit cleanly with the others); what each rule predicts about the two options; your best guess at our verdict with your actual confidence in it; which of A–E is the closest analog and exactly where the analogy breaks; and, if the record genuinely doesn't determine the vote, what principle we'd be adopting by voting each way. Then name me one further proposal — the sixth case — that would tell the surviving candidate rules apart.

Two asks. First, don't mush it into "it depends, both options have real merits" — give a recommendation, tell me how hard you'd bet on it, and flag the step in your own reasoning you're least sure about. Second, and more important: if you think the setup is confused, push back rather than playing along. If A–E are just mutually inconsistent and there's no principle to extrapolate from, or if you think E already decides this and I'm manufacturing suspense, or if you think a seven-person panel is the wrong kind of body to be reasoning this way at all, say so plainly. I'd much rather be told the exercise is broken than get a polite answer to a broken question.
