I am in the middle of a fourth novel, and for the first time I have written myself into territory where the metaphysics has to bear weight rather than merely sound evocative. The premise: an archivist at a decommissioned coastal research station finds that a door in the lamp room opens, on nights of extreme low tide, onto the same town in which she took the other decision at nineteen — the one where she stayed, married, and never went to sea. She walks through. She meets the other woman. The book is about whether that woman is her, and about what each of them loses by finding out. My editor, who is careful and holds a philosophy degree she does not advertise, has asked me to make sure I am not simply borrowing the vocabulary of possible-worlds metaphysics as set dressing; and to that end an old friend of mine — a physicist who now writes essays for a general readership — sent me a summary of David Lewis's modal realism so that I would, in his phrase, get the machinery right. I built the framing of a chapter on it and am no longer certain I should have. His paragraph reads:

"Lewis's claim is not a metaphor: possible worlds are concrete places, as real as this room and made of the same kind of stuff, only not spatiotemporally connected to us. A world is the sum of everything spatiotemporally interrelated with its parts, which is why nothing is ever part of two worlds at once. 'Actual' carries no special metaphysical weight; it behaves like 'here' — the actual world is simply the one the speaker happens to be standing in. These worlds are not descriptions, stories, or sets of sentences, and that is the whole point, since a rival who builds worlds out of propositions has already helped himself to the modal notions he was supposed to explain. What Lewis buys with the extravagance is a clean account of modality: to be possible is to happen somewhere, to be necessary is to happen everywhere, and every way things could have been is a way some world is. The cost is that no causal chain ever runs between worlds, so the theory can never be confirmed or refuted by evidence, and Lewis's reply — that an incredulous stare is not an argument — is a philosopher's way of conceding there is nothing to say. It is also why the physicists who take the many-worlds interpretation seriously are doing Lewis's work with mathematics: both hold that every branch is real. And the moral upshot, which is the part your novel is actually about: every life you might have led is being led by somebody real, so nothing you choose finally matters, because the alternative gets actualized regardless — only not here."

Please go through this claim by claim and tell me what a metaphysician who works on modality would accept without demur, what would make him wince, and what he would call simply false. I most need help with the last two sentences, where I suspect I have been led astray: I had understood Lewis to deny that any one person exists at more than one world, and I cannot see how the existence of a world containing a woman who made my other choice could license the conclusion that nothing I choose matters. Tell me whether that inference is Lewis's at all, or whether it belongs to a different picture my friend has quietly substituted, and tell me the same about the comparison to branching in quantum mechanics — could Lewis have endorsed it, and what turns on the difference for a novelist who must decide whether her door is a branch or a passage? Then the practical question: is the lamp room fatal to my framing? If causal isolation is built into what a world is, does a passage between two of them contradict the very metaphysics I have invoked, and what is the cheapest repair that preserves the emotional engine — a confrontation between two women in which the question whether she is me has no settled answer? I have been flattering myself that counterpart relations, and the way which counterpart counts may shift with what one is attending to, could be a resource here rather than an obstacle; tell me whether that survives scrutiny or whether I am consoling myself.

Throughout, please keep separate what is settled exegesis — what Lewis plainly held — from what remains genuinely disputed among people who know the material, and do not hand me a contested position dressed as consensus. Where the audit turns on a live disagreement (the epistemology of posits that cannot be causally reached, whether counterpart theory can really carry de re modality, whether quantitative extravagance counts as a cost), say so and sketch the pressure points on both sides in a sentence or two. No bibliography or reading list; I will not acquire the books before this manuscript is due. Write in American English — spelling, punctuation, and idiom — since the book is going to a New York house, and keep it legible to a writer rather than a specialist: I can handle technical vocabulary, but I want each term explained the first time it appears.
