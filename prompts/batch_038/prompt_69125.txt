**Risk Attitudes as Artifacts of Description: A Deflationary Reading of REU Theory**

**§1. The target.** In *Risk and Rationality* (Oxford, 2013), Lara Buchak proposes that a rational agent chooses the act maximizing risk-weighted expected utility. Rank the act's possible outcomes worst to best, x₁ through xₙ; let Pᵢ be the probability, given the act, of an outcome at least as good as xᵢ; let u(x₀) ≡ 0. Then REU(A) = Σᵢ r(Pᵢ) · (u(xᵢ) − u(xᵢ₋₁)), where r maps [0,1] to itself continuously and increasingly, with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1. Expected utility theory is the special case r = identity. Risk aversion corresponds to r lying below the 45-degree line (equivalently, convex over the relevant range), so that every utility increment above the worst outcome is discounted by more than its probability alone would discount it; this reproduces the Allais preferences, which expected utility theory cannot. Three features of Buchak's package matter for what follows. First, r is introduced as a third independent parameter of an agent's practical psychology, alongside credence and utility — a global attitude toward the shape of a whole gamble, which she argues cannot be absorbed into the curvature of the utility function. Second, agents who violate the independence axiom are, on her account, not thereby irrational; REU is offered as a normative theory of ideal rationality, not as descriptive psychology. Third, of the three responses available to the Allais data — reject expected utility theory, call the preferences irrational, or redescribe the choice problem so the preferences come out consistent — she defends the first and rejects the other two, including redescription.

My claim: keep the formalism, drop the third parameter. r is not a trait. It is a compression artifact.

**§2. The compression hypothesis.** Any choice problem can be written at many resolutions, and I'll call the resulting ordering the description ladder. At the coarse rung, "a million dollars" is a single cell with a single utility. At a finer rung it is a distribution over concretely different lives: the mortgage extinguished, the year of freedom taken, the sum invested and never touched, the sum lent to a brother-in-law and lost. Suppose an agent's utility function is unremarkable at the fine rung — concave in the ordinary way, with diminishing marginal utility over the sub-outcomes of what a large sum does. Suppose further that the agent deliberates, and reports preferences, at the coarse rung, where each cell is represented by its headline payoff and hence valued at the utility of that payoff.

That last step is not expectation-preserving. For a concave fine-grained utility function, the utility of a cell's representative payoff exceeds the mean utility within the cell. The coarse description therefore systematically overstates the value of every cell, and overstates it by more the wider the cell's internal dispersion. Call the gap dispersion leakage.

Now note the asymmetry that does the work. In ordinary descriptions, the good cells of a large gamble are precisely the wide ones. "$1,000,000" spans many possible lives; "$0" spans almost none. So leakage is large on upper increments and negligible on lower ones. The result is a discount that attaches to a cell by rank rather than by payoff level — bigger for the top of the distribution, vanishing at the bottom, and monotone in the cumulative probability of landing at or above the cell, because cell width grows as you move up a distribution described in aggregate terms. That is the signature of a rank-dependent weighting function with r below the diagonal. REU's third parameter, on this reading, is what ordinary fine-grained concavity looks like when the problem is written down at a rung too coarse to see it.

**§3. Predictions.** The compression hypothesis and the trait hypothesis agree on the static representation and disagree everywhere else. Three discriminating predictions. (P1) At fixed payoffs and probabilities, measured r should covary with within-cell dispersion: itemizing the top prize into concrete uses should weaken rank-dependence, and re-aggregating it ("a substantial sum") should strengthen it. (P2) Refinement should push r monotonically toward the identity with no floor — there is no residual global risk attitude at the limit. Corollary: individual differences in measured r should track habits of description, so r should show low stability across description formats and high stability within one. (P3), the strong version: r should be recoverable from independently elicited fine-grained utilities plus a measure of cell widths, as a numerical identity rather than a correlation.

**§4. The privileged-rung problem.** Two difficulties, and I don't have a clean answer to either. First, the ladder has no top. If utility is only ever defined relative to a description, then "the fine rung" is a posit, and any observed r can be explained by inventing a finer rung beneath it — which makes the hypothesis unfalsifiable in exactly the way I criticized elsewhere. My attempt at a fix: the operative rung is the one at which the agent's preferences are complete and stable under further questioning; ask them to rank the sub-outcomes, and if their coarse preference moves when they do, the coarse rung was the operative one. But this defines the privileged rung by the very behavioral signature the hypothesis was supposed to explain, which is circular, and I can see no way around it that doesn't quietly reintroduce r as a primitive. Second, level mismatch. REU is a normative theory of ideal rationality; the compression hypothesis is a theory of how agents represent problems to themselves. If they sit at different levels they don't compete, and I've explained nothing normative. So I have to choose: either (a) coarse-rung rank-dependence is rationally permissible, because the coarse description is all the agent has, or (b) it is a representation error, corrigible by refinement — which is the redescription response Buchak explicitly rejects, and which implies that a great deal of actual human risk aversion is irrational after all. The draft currently slides between (a) and (b) and I know it.

**§5. The counterexample I can't dissolve.** A colleague of mine insures a piece of equipment worth $20,000 against a 1% annual chance of total loss, pays $1,200 for the policy — six times the actuarially fair premium — and regards this as obviously sensible. The description is already at the finest rung available to him. The replacement cost is a catalog number. The loss branch is not a vague bad cell but a single fully specified state: equipment gone, insurer pays, he reorders, three weeks' delay, consequences itemized. The no-loss branch is likewise a single state. There is no within-cell dispersion, so there is nothing to leak, so the compression hypothesis says his decision weights should equal the probabilities and he should decline the policy. Saving his acceptance within expected utility theory instead requires a utility function whose local concavity over $1,200 to $20,000 at his net worth is ruled out by the familiar calibration-style argument: a curve concave enough to justify this refusal implies refusals of large-stakes bets he would obviously accept. So here is a non-identity r with no compression to explain it. My three exits all look bad. Deny that his fine-grained utility is as close to linear as the calibration argument assumes — but then I owe an account of where the concavity comes from, which is the trait hypothesis wearing my clothes. Say he's simply irrational — but that concedes REU is doing no descriptive work and commits me to horn (b) of §4. Claim the delay-and-reorder branch has hidden dispersion — which is precisely the unfalsifiable move §4 warned against, deployed by me.

What's at stake, and why I care: if the compression hypothesis holds even where it holds, the Buchak/orthodoxy dispute is substantially about who gets to write the choice problem down. The consent form, the dashboard, the summary generated for you by a system that has read the fine print and reports only the headline — each of these fixes the rung, and fixing the rung fixes the effective risk function. That's an argument for treating the description of a decision as part of the decision, and for distrusting any arrangement in which risks are presented at a resolution the decision-maker did not choose.

(b) Are P1–P3 discriminating, or would a defender of r-as-trait predict them too? P3 especially: is the identity I'm gesturing at even well-defined, given that cell width isn't something an agent reports?
