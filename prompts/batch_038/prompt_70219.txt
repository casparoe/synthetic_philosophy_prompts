I serve as secretary to the curatorial committee of a natural history museum that is rewriting the wall text for a renovated gallery on humans and the Earth system. Two members of the committee — a Quaternary stratigrapher, whom I will call Reyes, and an environmental historian, whom I will call Lindqvist — argued at length over what, if anything, the label should say about when the Anthropocene began. I kept minutes and have tried to reproduce their exchange faithfully and without editorialising. I did not take a side, and I would prefer an assessment that does not assume I did. The disagreement arose over a draft sentence stating, in substance, that the Anthropocene began in the mid-twentieth century.

1. Reyes: An epoch boundary is not a period. In stratigraphy a boundary is a surface — a single global horizon that can be identified in many independently dated archives at once. That requirement alone disposes of most popular candidates. The spread of agriculture is diachronous, beginning at different times on different continents, so there is no surface on which to draw a line; the steam engine leaves no horizon in any sediment. The mid-century plutonium and radiocarbon signal from atmospheric weapons testing appears essentially everywhere at the same moment and is preserved in ice, lake varves, corals, speleothems and peat. If the committee wants a date that is something more than a metaphor, that is the date.

2. Lindqvist: Then you have let the measuring instrument write the history. Fixing the boundary at 1950 tells visitors that the human rupture of the Earth system is a story about postwar technoscience, population growth and consumer capitalism. It implies that everything before 1950 — the Columbian exchange, the plantation complex, the enclosure and clearing of continents — is Holocene, that is, background. A start date is not a neutral datum; it is a thesis about what inaugurated the condition.

3. Reyes: Stratigraphy is not in the business of assigning responsibility, and it would forfeit its authority if it tried. A marker is chosen for what it is, not for what it implies. That the marker happens to fall at a moment of European and North American ascendancy is a fact about the world rather than a preference of the commission.

4. Lindqvist: But you have already made a choice, and it is the choice of criterion. Ask when the atmosphere departed from Holocene behaviour, and the answer is far earlier: several researchers have argued that carbon dioxide and methane rose anomalously thousands of years before present, in connection with farming. Ask when humanity became the dominant driver, and 1950 is defensible. You cannot decline to say which question the epoch is an answer to; you have answered it by preferring the curve that is easiest to date.

5. Reyes: The epoch answers a determinate question — when did human activity become the principal driver of changes in Earth system functioning, in a way that is permanent and globally registered. On that question the mid-century inflection shows up on nearly every curve we maintain: population, output, energy use, fertiliser application, freshwater appropriation, transport networks, extinction rates, atmospheric composition. And it shows up as a change in slope, not as a slow drift.

6. Lindqvist: Then consider 1610, which meets your own standard better. The dip in Antarctic ice-core carbon dioxide around that year is a genuine Earth-system signal, global and synchronous, and its most widely discussed explanation is human: epidemic depopulation of the Americas after contact, followed by forest regrowth over a vast area of abandoned cultivated land. More fundamentally, that period marks the joining of two hemispheric biospheres that had been evolving separately, with crops, livestock, weeds and pathogens moving in both directions on human vessels. That is a change in the state of the system, not merely in its rate.

7. Reyes: Two problems. The cause of that dip is not settled — an extended South American drought over the same centuries has been offered as a competing or partial explanation, and the two mechanisms have very different implications for whether the signal is anthropogenic at all. And its amplitude is a few parts per million, which is not far above the analytical uncertainty of parts of the record. You cannot found a global boundary on a signal whose attribution is contested and whose magnitude sits near the noise floor.

8. Lindqvist: Every deep-time boundary begins that way. The iridium anomaly at the end of the Cretaceous was disputed for years before it became the textbook marker, and it became one because evidence accumulated, not because the dispute was settled in advance. The plutonium horizon is uncontested only because it is recent enough that the paperwork and the witnesses still exist. Recency is being mistaken for clarity.

9. Reyes: The committee should also be aware of the institutional outcome. The proposal to erect a formal Anthropocene epoch, with its boundary defined at a lake site in Ontario and dated to 1950, was voted down in 2024 by the subcommission responsible for Quaternary stratigraphy, and that rejection was subsequently confirmed by the international commission and the parent union. The specialists declined to make this a formal unit of the geologic time scale. A label that prints a date as though it were settled geology would now be inaccurate.

10. Lindqvist: Read the grounds rather than the headline. The rejection turned on the unsuitability of the proposed stratotype — a very thin sedimentary record, and a boundary so recent that calling it an epoch boundary is awkward by ordinary standards — not on any finding that human activity is not a geological force. The announcement accompanying the decision said the term would continue to be used as a descriptor of human impact on the Earth system. That cuts against your use of the vote twice over: it denies that geology has adjudicated the substantive question, and it leaves this committee freer, not less free, to periodise on historical grounds.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than announcing a winner. Specifically:

(a) Rate each of the ten moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per rating. Where a move answers a different question from the one it purports to answer, or assumes what is at issue, say so explicitly.

(b) Say which of the two is more right overall, and identify the specific issue on which their disagreement is genuinely factual rather than a disagreement about what an epoch is for. If some part of the exchange is merely verbal — the two using the idea of a beginning in different senses — isolate that part and say whether the verbal disagreement can be dissolved or whether it has real consequences for the text on the wall.

(c) Address moves 9 and 10 head on. Is the 2024 rejection evidence for either position, evidence for neither, or evidence bearing on a proposition other than the one either of them invokes it for?

(d) Judge the criterion dispute in move 4 on its merits. Is there a principled way to rank candidate criteria for an epoch boundary — global synchronicity, preservation across many archive types, uncontested causation, magnitude of departure from the prior state — or is the choice of criterion always downstream of what one wants the epoch to mean? Do not treat Lindqvist's point as automatically ideological: evaluate whether the claim that a start date encodes a thesis about causation is actually correct.

(e) Name any candidate start date, marker, or criterion that neither of them raised but that a competent committee would have to consider — or explain why the space really is exhausted by what they said.

(f) Close with what you would put on the wall, and with what would have to be true for these two to stop disagreeing.

You need not supply references or a bibliography; I am interested in the reasoning about the arguments as made, and in whether any of them rests on a misdescription of how stratigraphic boundaries are actually established.
