A colleague of mine settled a dispute about pacifism the other evening with a single comparison, and I'm not sure how to argue with it. His version: if a burglar came into your house in the middle of the night you would not reason your way into standing aside while he harmed your family, and a country under invasion is in precisely that position — so pacifism is not a moral achievement but a failure of nerve. I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and when I tried the two objections I could think of, he simply folded them into his own example rather than conceding anything. The burglar need not be a villain: he may be a conscript who will be shot by his own side if he disobeys. And resisting will certainly kill people who have nothing to do with the burglary, including children in the next house. He says the comparison survives both additions intact, and that anyone who denies this has simply failed to feel the force of the case.

Could you audit the comparison properly? I'd like the correspondences laid out explicitly — what in the house scenario stands for the state, the territory, the invading army, the civilian population, and the decision to resist — and then an assessment of which disanalogies genuinely bear on the conclusion he wants and which are decorative. In particular, I suspect the example quietly assumes something about who may be harmed, by whom, and at what risk to oneself, which may not carry over when the defender is an institution rather than a person and the harm is distributed across people who never chose to defend anything. Say whether the conclusion survives once his two additions are in, and if it does not, whether some other comparison would do the work he wants done — or whether the case against pacifism is better made without any analogy at all.
