i'm on a city council subcommittee and we've spent a year arguing about statues and street names without anybody saying what a commemoration policy is supposed to accomplish. before i read any more position papers i want the spec: what does an adequate account of public commemoration have to deliver? which distinctions can it not collapse — honoring a person vs honoring what they did, a street name vs a museum plaque vs a statue on a plinth in the square, the message the council intends vs the one a passerby actually recieves, removing vs contextualizing vs replacing? and which cases does it have to get right: a 19th century benefactor whose fortune came from something we'd now condemn, whose name is on a hospital wing the city can't afford to rename; a statue whose subject everybody has forgotten, so it commemorates nothing in particular; a name that means something quite different to the neighborhood living under it than it did to the people who picked it. plus the practical constraints it can't shrug off — the costs of readdressing land on residents not the city, meanings drift over generations, and taking something down is itself a speech act with an audience. tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are just one camp's priors, whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable (i suspect preserve-the-record plus don't-honor-the-dishonorable is already a squeeze), and which single requirement knocks out the most candidates. skip the scorecard of the usual views, we'll do that at the meeting.
