I work on trust & safety tooling at a mid-size social platform. I'm not a lawyer and I've never taken a philosophy class, so bear with me — mostly what I do is write classifiers and schema migrations.

Here's the situation. We have an internal label that reviewers can apply to a conflict, and it does real work in the system: it routes that conflict's content into a slower human-reviewed queue, it gates whether we show a crisis-info panel, and it appears in our transparency report. Right now it's a single boolean, and our policy doc defines it as "meets the criteria of the 1948 UN convention." Last month a situation came up in a region I'm not going to name here — the reporting was about mass village burnings and the forced displacement of one ethnic group out of a valley, plus closing their schools and banning their language in public life, but not (yet) large-scale killing. Reviewers wanted to flip the boolean. Legal said no: their reading is that the convention requires proof of intent to physically destroy the group, and displacement plus wiping out the culture doesnt clear that bar. So we didn't label it. Two weeks later a chunk of the content that went through our normal queue turned out to be coordination for further attacks, and now people are asking whether our taxonomy failed.

We're redesigning and the team is split three ways:

1. My PM: keep the boolean tied to the legal definition. Her argument is that the word only does work because it's rare and precise — if we apply it to things a court would never call genocide, we lose credibility, and every future case gets argued down to "you cried wolf last time."
2. A researcher on my team: he says the legal test was written by states negotiating in 1948 who wanted to keep their own freedom of action, and that the guy who originally coined the term meant something much broader, including destroying a group's language and institutions. So using the treaty as our ground truth imports the interests of the people who drafted it. He wants a moral-category label, not a legal one.
3. Me: I don't trust either boolean. My proposal is to delete it and replace it with a structured record — seperate fields for which acts are documented, what evidence there is about intent, and whether the group is being targeted as a group — and let each downstream consumer (review queue, info panel, transparency report) decide for itself which combination it cares about. Basically push the judgment call out of the schema and into the policies that read it.

But I can't tell whether #3 actually solves the problem or just hides it. Part of me suspects we'd be splitting one contested word into five contested fields and pretending that's neutral, which feels like a dodge. And there's a thing nagging me that I can't quite articulate: our label isn't just describing the world, it triggers stuff, so maybe the right definition depends on what the label is for rather than on what's true — but if that's right, the same events might need two different labels in two different parts of the system, and that seems insane to me.

So: which of the three should we build, and how do I make the case in a design review where half the room thinks this is a legal question and the other half thinks it's a data modeling question? Please tell me straight if I'm wrong that #3 is neutral, and tell me what to actually say to the PM and to the researcher — I think they're each half right and I can't figure out which halves.
