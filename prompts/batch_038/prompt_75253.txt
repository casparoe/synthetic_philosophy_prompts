Set theory's dispute over whether the continuum hypothesis has a determinate truth value has now run for more than half a century, and as far as I can tell nothing anyone says converts anyone. Let me lay out the positions as I understand them, then say what I want from you.

On one side stands a multiverse conception: the forcing results are held to have settled everything settleable, since the continuum hypothesis holds in some universes of set theory and fails in others, with no single background universe against which the question has an answer; the remaining work is to chart how the hypothesis and its relatives behave across universes and to understand the structure of that plurality. On the other side stands a single-universe realism: there is one intended universe of sets, the continuum hypothesis is therefore determinately true or false, and our ignorance merely reflects the incompleteness of our axioms, to be repaired by further principles — intrinsic ones, appealing to maximality or reflection, or extrinsic ones, justified by the fruitfulness of what they yield.

The methodological situation is awkward, and I think this is the crux: the candidate axioms pull in opposite directions. An axiom of constructibility gives the generalized continuum hypothesis and hence the continuum hypothesis; a strong forcing axiom such as Martin's Maximum gives a continuum of size the second uncountable cardinal and hence its negation; large cardinal hypotheses leave the continuum hypothesis open even while settling nearby questions such as the regularity properties of the projective sets. So the two camps can agree on every theorem anyone proves and still disagree about whether there is a fact of the matter.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the real disagreement is not about the continuum hypothesis and not even about which axioms to add, but about whether the quantifier over subsets of the natural numbers has a determinate range — a dispute about determinacy sitting upstream of every first-order argument, which is precisely why the impasse is stable.

Test that diagnosis. Is this a verbal dispute about truth and about the phrase "the universe of sets"; a substantive dispute in mathematical ontology; a disagreement about what counts as evidence or justification for a new axiom; or two groups answering different questions under one heading — one asking what follows from the accepted axioms and their natural strengthenings, the other asking whether the question is answerable in principle? Argue for your diagnosis from what each side is actually committed to, rather than surveying programs for settling the hypothesis. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a decisive new axiom of a kind both camps could accept, a metamathematical result about the class of candidate axioms, a demonstration that one of the two positions is internally unstable, or a reformulation of the question — or else an argument that no progress should be expected, and why not.
