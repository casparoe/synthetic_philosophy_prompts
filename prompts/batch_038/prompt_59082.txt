Write a dialogue of between 1,500 and 2,000 words between two philosophers of science standing beside a running waterwheel apparatus — a rim of leaky buckets turning under a tap whose flow rate can be varied — which is lurching and reversing in a haphazard, apparently wilful manner despite being wholly mechanical and deterministic. Both are thoroughly versed in dynamical systems theory, and neither may be made a fool of: they are intellectual equals who happen to disagree.

The first maintains that deterministic chaos leaves the classical picture of determinism intact, and that any claim that the waterwheel is unpredictable *in principle* either equivocates on "in principle" or smuggles in a thesis about the limitations of finite, embodied agents. On this view the predictability horizon is quantitative rather than qualitative: error in the initial data doubles on a characteristic timescale, one can write down how the horizon scales with the precision of those data and with the saturation level set by the size of the system's attractor, and for an idealised Laplacian intelligence with exact initial data and exact arithmetic the apparatus offers no resistance at all — its future is simply computed.

The second maintains that this reply concedes too little and mistakes the philosophy: that the horizon is not a practical embarrassment to be quantified away, but marks a genuine boundary on what any physically situated predictor can know about the future of a deterministic system, and that the appeal to an exact-arithmetic demon is question-begging, since nothing that could exist in the world is capable of exactness.

The exchange should turn on substantive technical pressure points rather than on restating these two theses, and the participants should in particular grapple with:

- the shadowing lemma, and what it does and does not license: it yields the *existence* of a true trajectory lying near a numerically computed pseudo-trajectory, but the closeness thereby guaranteed can be of the same order as the error already present in one's initial data, and the result is known to hold for uniformly hyperbolic systems and to break down elsewhere — so one participant should press the question whether the waterwheel in front of them is even the kind of system to which shadowing applies;
- whether prediction and retrodiction are asymmetric here at all, given that the same positive exponents that erase information about the future erase information about the past, and what this does to the usual framing of the debate;
- whether the identity, for suitable ergodic smooth systems, of metric entropy with the sum of the positive Lyapunov exponents shows that a chaotic deterministic system is indistinguishable from a random one in any sense that does philosophical work, or merely that it is informationally expensive to specify;
- the suggestion — which one participant should raise and the other should engage seriously rather than wave away — that quantum mechanics imposes a physical ceiling on how finely a state can be specified, and that the correspondence between quantum and classical evolution for a chaotic system itself degrades over a timescale logarithmic in the effective Planck constant, so that the demon's exactness is not merely unattainable but unphysical;
- the charge, which the first participant should press near the end, that the disagreement has collapsed into a merely verbal one about the meaning of "in principle", and whether that charge is fair or whether a substantive thesis survives it.

Do not resolve the dialogue by having either party convert. You may, and should, allow at least one local concession: some specific sub-claim that one of them abandons because of something the other actually said, with the reasoning made explicit. Keep the register that of two experts: standard terminology and the occasional displayed equation are welcome, and neither character should pause to explain to the other what a Lyapunov exponent is, nor should either accuse the other of question-begging without saying precisely which premise is being assumed.
