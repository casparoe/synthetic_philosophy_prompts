Last Saturday night, at a housewarming where I was nominally helping in the kitchen, I ended up as the silent third party at a table while two friends argued for the better part of three hours about whether the no-free-lunch theorem settles Hume's problem of induction, dissolves it, or was never addressing it in the first place. Dominik trains and evaluates models for a living and reads the technical literature in his spare time; Elena is a philosophy graduate student writing her dissertation on the epistemology of inductive inference. Both are sharp, neither was grandstanding, and I came away unable to say who had the better of it. I took notes as best I could and have reconstructed the exchange below, numbered in the order it actually unfolded.

(1) Dominik opened by claiming the dispute was resolved mathematically in the 1990s. The results of Wolpert and Macready — and Wolpert's earlier versions for machine learning — show that when performance is averaged across all possible objective functions, no search algorithm does better than any other, or than blind search. His gloss: assumption-free generalization is provably impossible, which is Hume's conclusion arrived at by proof rather than by paragraph. (2) Elena replied that the theorem is true but empty, and that its emptiness is legible in its own hypotheses. To average over all functions from a finite domain to a set of values is to average over a space in which, for every function exhibiting a discernible pattern, there are astronomically many exhibiting none. She said a result of that shape is a theorem about the size of the problem space rather than about inference, no different in kind from observing that no key opens every lock; and she pointed to the familiar case of a sequence beginning 2, 4, 8, 16, where the space of continuations is overwhelmingly populated by functions for which the next term is arbitrary. (3) Dominik answered that the theorem's value lies in making precise which assumption does the work: before it, "induction requires a uniformity premise" was a slogan, and after it one can point to the exact quantity — the prior over target functions — and show that comparative performance claims are meaningless without it. That is substantive, he said, precisely because a great many practitioners talk as though their method's superiority were prior-free. (4) Elena conceded the point about practitioners but said it proved too much for his purposes, because the framework already assumes what Hume doubts. The first theorem supposes an objective function that does not change while the algorithm runs, so the training data and the test data come from one static world; Hume's problem is whether the world holds still at all. A result that grants a fixed world and then asks only which prior you brought to it is a theorem inside a picture Hume would reject, not a formalization of his challenge. (5) Dominik said no: the space of fixed functions includes ones that look perfectly lawful across everything yet observed and diverge immediately thereafter, and under the uniform average the Goodman-style world and the ordinary world carry identical weight, which is the skeptical problem in formal dress. (6) Elena said that is exactly where the smuggling occurs. Weighting those worlds identically is not neutrality but a substantive and, she thinks, refutable choice. On a finite domain of n points with binary values, the labelings that display any appreciable regularity form a minute fraction of all labelings, so a uniform prior over functions assigns our actual evidence vanishingly small probability; and since regularity is what we have observed in bulk for as long as anyone has kept records, the indifferent prior is empirically discredited in the ordinary Bayesian way — it predicts that our evidence would be a miracle. The conclusion, she said, is not that induction lacks warrant but that indifference is a bad prior, and recognizing that is itself a form of inference the theorem cannot reach. (7) Dominik said this concedes everything: any replacement prior — a simplicity bias, a Solomonoff-style mixture, a preference for compressible hypotheses — is itself undemonstrable, and undemonstrability was the whole complaint. Elena's answer was that undemonstrable and arbitrary are different words; a prior can fail to be provable while remaining answerable to evidence, and once it is answerable to evidence it is not a leap of faith in Hume's sense but a hypothesis like any other. Dominik's last word was that she then needs a prior over priors in order to conditionalize on the evidence, so the regress simply restarts one level up and the theorem's real lesson — that there is no ground floor — survives intact. Elena's last word was that a regress in the order of justification is not the same thing as an absence of warrant, and that if he intends to treat the two as identical he owes an argument rather than a theorem.
