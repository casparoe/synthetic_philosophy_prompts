I am drafting a paper on the design of penal institutions and I have reached the point where I need to know what I actually think, rather than what I am in the habit of saying. What follows is a list of cases and my considered verdicts on them. Some of these verdicts I hold firmly; others I hold only because I cannot see how to give them up. I want to know what principle, or small ordered set of principles, generates the pattern — and I want to be told plainly where no such principle exists.

1. An offender serves a long, fixed sentence in a facility built on the Scandinavian model: no bars, a kitchen, work and study indistinguishable in kind from what is available outside, officers who address him by name. By his own account his life inside is materially better than the life he left. I judge this arrangement entirely legitimate. I do not feel the pull of the objection that he is being punished too little, even when I know that some of the people he harmed live in worse conditions than he does.

2. The same facility, the same amenities, the same population — but the sentence is indeterminate. Release comes when a board certifies that he no longer presents the risk he did, with a cap that makes his expected time served shorter than in case 1. I judge this arrangement worse than case 1, and I judge it worse despite agreeing that he will, on average, suffer less and the public will be no less safe.

3. A repeat sex offender is offered chemical intervention that suppresses the impulses behind his offences, in exchange for the final three years of his sentence. He consents, understands the effects, and may withdraw. I am strongly inclined to call this impermissible. Yet a mandatory curriculum in emotional regulation, with the same structure, the same exchange, and a comparable effect on risk, strikes me as unobjectionable. I cannot say what distinguishes them, since in both cases the state is altering the dispositions of a captive person for its own convenience.

4. Vocational and therapeutic programs are offered only to those assessed as low-risk and near release, and the measured effect on reoffending among participants is large. I am uneasy about this, and my unease is not about consent. Now compare the identical program offered to everyone in the facility, with a much smaller measured effect. I prefer the second. This preference strikes me as wasteful of the very good I claim to care about, and I cannot defend it.

5. A facility that is severe but scrupulously procedural: no cruelty, no arbitrary discretion, no deprivation beyond the loss of liberty itself — and yet men describe it as unbearable, chiefly because of isolation. I judge it illegitimate, though I cannot identify a single rule it breaks or a right it violates.

6. A town petitions to have the amenities of its local facility removed because residents cannot bear the thought that the offender's life is going well. I hold that this demand carries no weight whatsoever — not reduced weight, no weight. But I also hold that a victim's claim to be informed, and to be present at sentencing, carries substantial weight. These two look to me like the same kind of thing: the expressive interests of people outside the cell. I treat them as opposites.

7. An intervention succeeds completely. The inmate now disowns his conduct, feels remorse he did not previously feel, is a better companion, and says he is grateful. I want to call this legitimate as to its effects and yet to insist that something has been lost — something that punishment was meant to accomplish and no longer has. I do not know what I mean by this, and I suspect that if I cannot say, I should stop saying it.

8. A man of sixty is convicted of a serious offence committed at twenty, with thirty unblemished years between. I think there is something wrong with punishing him. Yet I see nothing wrong with punishing a man for an offence forty years old whose character is unchanged, and who has, so far as anyone can tell, simply not been caught. The passage of time appears to do no independent work in my reasoning.

My tentative principle is this: the state may take away the ordinary means by which a person directs his own life, and it may offer him better means, but it may not take over the direction itself; it may not make the terms of his release depend on the contents of his character, and it may not install in him the dispositions it approves of. I am aware that this may be nothing more than a restatement of the pattern, dressed as an explanation. Test it. It seems to handle cases 2, 3, and 7; it seems to founder on 4, 5, and 8; and I do not see that it says anything at all about 6.

What I want from you is the following. First, state the principle or principles precisely, clause by clause, with the case above that motivates each clause — I want to see which clause is doing which work. Second, tell me whether the currency of punishment on this account is still suffering, or something else: the loss of authority over the shape of one's own life, the loss of standing to bind oneself, subjection to another's will. Case 1 is what makes me doubt that suffering is the right measure, and I want that doubt either vindicated or dissolved. Third, apply the result to the case I keep circling: a rule tying the amenities of a facility to the amenities available to the poorest law-abiding residents of the jurisdiction. It has the appeal of giving case 6 a principled form, and I fear it licenses a ratchet. Fourth, apply it to three cases I have not listed — an inmate who voluntarily refuses available amenities as an act of penance; early release granted in exchange for a public apology the offender does not believe; and a jurisdiction that declines to prosecute someone who is already, by his own efforts and at his own cost, thoroughly reformed. Fifth, and most importantly, tell me which of my verdicts the account cannot save. If a simpler principle overturns exactly one of them, I would rather have that than an elaborate one that rescues all eight, but I want to be shown which verdict is the cheapest to lose and why.
