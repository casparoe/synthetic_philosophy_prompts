I hold a chair in philosophy and work mainly in moral psychology and the theory of action, but I am coming to you about a family matter, and I would rather you treated it as a problem to be solved than as an occasion for reassurance. Please adopt the role of a philosopher with a working command of the theories of autonomy, preference formation, and the normative status of a person's own reports about her life. Think carefully, and lay out your reasoning before you reach a verdict.

Here is the case, reported as plainly as I can manage.

My younger sister Miriam is forty-six. As children she was by far the better musician of the two of us; by seventeen she was practising five hours a day and speaking of nothing else. At nineteen our mother had a stroke. Miriam deferred her place, then never took it up, and has spent the intervening twenty-five years in our hometown: first nursing our mother until she died, then raising her two children, then, for the past decade, working part-time in the front office of a physiotherapy practice alongside a good deal of unpaid work for the parish and for neighbours. I went to university. My fees were paid out of the same household that could not have paid for two.

What I need help with is that Miriam is happy. Not stoical, not resigned, not performing contentment for my benefit — happy. She says she listens to more music now than she did at seventeen; she says she has no regrets; she says she finds the life she gave up claustrophobic rather than tragic. Last month, within her daughter's hearing, she remarked that when a woman begins talking about the career she did not have, she is usually telling you that she has not looked properly at the one she does have.

Her daughter Ines is nineteen, has been offered a conservatoire place, and has written to say she is declining it in order to stay, work at the practice with her mother, and keep up her grandmother's house. Miriam is pleased, and has said so. Ines has written to me asking whether she is making a mistake, and I do not know what to tell her.

My difficulty is this. There are two things one could say about Miriam's contentment, and I cannot find a way of choosing between them that is not already a choice. I could say that she has told the truth about herself and that she is the authority on her own life, so that Ines's decision is hers to make and mine only to respect. Or I could say that a person whose options were foreclosed at nineteen, by a family that could afford one education and not two, is badly placed to have preferences about the road she was never permitted to travel, and that her peace is a scar rather than a report. The first reading makes me a respectful brother. The second makes me a man who knows something about his sister's inner life that she does not, and it makes my tuition, rather than her nursing, the interesting fact about that household. Both readings are available to me; each flatters me in a different direction; and neither looks like the sort of thing that evidence could settle.

Please do the following.

First, before committing to an account, brainstorm the range of readings this case genuinely admits. I want at least five, including at least one on which Miriam is simply right about herself, and at least one on which the question as I have posed it is malformed rather than hard. For each, state what would have to be true of the world, or of her history, for that reading to be the correct one.

Second, tell me whether any test exists — any observation about how her preferences were formed, any way of interrogating her counterfactual responses, any appeal to what she would say under fuller information — that could discriminate a settled preference from a distortion that has merely settled. Be explicit about whether you take the failure I am describing to be contingent on this case or built into the concepts I am forced to use, and say what follows for the confidence with which anyone may call another person's satisfaction a bias.

Third, tell me what to write back to Ines. She is nineteen and waiting for an answer, and a piece of metaethics will not serve. I want a verdict, not a suspension: if you conclude that the question cannot be settled, say which of the two readings it is nonetheless right to act on, and why acting on that one rather than the other is not simply my preference wearing a disguise.

Fourth, identify the weakest point in the account you have given me, and say whether the person best placed to answer Ines's letter is disqualified from doing so by having been the one who was sent away to school.

I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or suggestions for further reading; I know the literature and it will not decide this. I want the argument.
