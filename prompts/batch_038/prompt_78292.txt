I am in my third year of a philosophy of physics PhD; my dissertation is about the measure problem in eternal inflation, and my reading group has asked each of us to write down, in advance and without hedging, the package of views we actually hold on a contested topic so that the others can attack it in detail. I chose the Boltzmann brain problem. I circulated the following list, my advisor read it and said only that it looked to him like a set of commitments that had never been examined jointly, and I would now like an audit rather than an exposition. Here is the package.

1. I accept the Past Hypothesis. Explaining the entropy gradient, and with it records, memories and the asymmetry of intervention, requires a brute constraint selecting a very low-entropy initial macrostate for the universe, and I accept the accompanying statistical postulate: the actual initial microstate is typical, in the sense of the standard measure, among those compatible with that macrostate. I am content that this constraint is not explained by anything deeper. I take the package to be what disposes of the older fluctuation story on which our ordered region is a chance excursion out of equilibrium.

2. I nevertheless refuse to transport any typicality principle from microstates to observers. I reject the Self-Sampling Assumption outright: I do not think one is ever entitled to reason as though one were a random member of some reference class of observers. My grounds are the usual ones. The reference class is never uniquely determined, and the inference yields conclusions I cannot accept, notably the Doomsday result and the presumptuous philosopher.

3. I regard the Boltzmann brain problem as a genuine physical predicament rather than a philosophical curiosity, and specifically as a predicament about the far future rather than the early universe. If the cosmological constant is positive and the universe settles into an empty de Sitter phase, that phase carries a horizon temperature and a finite horizon entropy of order 10^122 in Planck units, so across unbounded time the vacuum behaves thermally and will nucleate self-aware structures. Since nucleation rates fall exponentially with the entropy cost of the structure produced, minimal freak observers should be produced vastly more often than complete ordered ones. The worry is sharpened by the further point that such an observer need not fluctuate into being all at once: a staircase of cheaper fluctuations, each retracing a stage of the structure's own decay in reverse, assembles the same thing at far lower total cost, which enlarges rather than shrinks the population of freaks.

4. I hold that a cosmology on which typical observers are freak observers is not merely strange but rationally unholdable. Adopting it requires trusting the inductive habits and the apparent memories which, on the theory itself, are for almost every observer systematically delusory. Such a cosmology must therefore be rejected, and rejected on that epistemic ground rather than because it is uncomfortable.

5. Accordingly I want freedom from freak observers to function as a selection criterion within the measure problem. Among candidate measures that are otherwise empirically adequate, I prefer the one that does not place the bulk of observers in fluctuated states; and where no measure achieves this within a given cosmology, I prefer to abandon the cosmology, for example by denying that empty de Sitter space is a true equilibrium state with accessible fluctuations, or by arranging for the vacuum energy to decay.

6. On indexical reasoning more generally I accept the Self-Indication Assumption: other things equal, a hypothesis is confirmed in proportion to the number of observers it predicts, since the fact that I exist at all is the datum requiring explanation. I wanted this chiefly as a way of neutralising the Doomsday argument.

7. I also maintain that nothing in my present experience tells me that I am not a Boltzmann brain, since a brain that fluctuated into existence a moment ago equipped with exactly my apparent memories would be having exactly this experience. Whatever support the ordinary picture enjoys must come from somewhere else.

Please go through this as an auditor and not as a lecturer. Identify which pairs of commitments genuinely conflict and which only appear to conflict, and rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than reporting them as equally damaging. For each, say exactly which commitment would have to be weakened or dropped, what the cheapest such revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package. If the list can be rendered coherent only by surrendering a premise I have not written down, something I am using silently in moving between items, name that premise explicitly. Finally, state which single revision would repair the greatest number of conflicts at once, and whether the resulting position is one a philosopher of physics could still publish under their own name.
