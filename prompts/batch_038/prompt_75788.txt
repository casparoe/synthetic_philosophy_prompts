Bit of context first: I'm not an academic or anything, I do the numbers at a small debt charity up in the north of England — one spreadsheet, a lot of goodwill, no data scientist in sight. A couple of years back I knocked together a score to work out which of our clients is most likely to be back in arreas within six months, so we can point our limited one-to-one casework at the people who need it most. Nothing clever: size of the debt relative to income, whether they've gone quiet on us before, whether they've had a bailiff visit, that kind of thing.

Then I went down a rabbit hole about the American court risk-score row and about the mathematical result saying you can't have calibration and equal error rates at the same time when two groups have different base rates. So I checked our own figures, and sure enough: within each score band, the proportion who actually end up back in arrears is about the same for our white British clients and our Black and minority ethnic clients, so it's calibrated. But the false positive rate is roughly double for the second group. Someone at our main funder called that discrimination with a decimal point, and I couldn't argue, because I tried to write down why she was right and it came out like this:

(1) A score is fair to people only if it treats equally risky people equally, whatever group they're in — same score, same actual rate of the bad outcome.
(2) It's also fair only if it makes mistakes at the same rate for both groups — same false positive rate, and same false negative rate.
(3) It's been proved that (1) and (2) can't both hold when the groups have different base rates.
(4) So where base rates differ, no score can be fair to both groups.
(5) Base rates differ for basically every outcome anybody actually wants to predict — offending, default, missing an appointment, getting ill — so this isn't some special case.
(6) So no real-world score is fair.
(7) And an unfair score is worse than no score at all, because the unfairness gets a receipt: nobody thinks to argue with a number, whereas everyone knows to argue with a person's hunch.
(8) So we should bin the score and go back to caseworkers deciding who gets the intensive help.

Here's my problem: I don't believe (8). I've sat in the case reviews and the hunches aren't neutral either. But (2) and (7) feel like the entire point to me, and if I have to lose something I'd lose (8) first and (5) second, definitely not (2). I just can't find where the chain actually snaps.

Separately, there's something about my own data that might matter and I can't work out whether it does: we only know someone went back into arrears if they told us or we chased them, and we only chase the ones who turned up to the six-month check-in — and turn-up rates differ between groups, because the check-in is at 10am on a Tuesday and some of our clients work shifts or can't get the bus in. Does that change (5), or is it a completely different problem?

Walk me through it: which step do you actually reject, why did it look perfectly innocent when I wrote it down, and what do I have to give up if I reject it instead — including what it costs to keep (7), since that's the one I'm most attached to. And please be straight with me about how confident you are in each part of the diagnosis, plus the single point you're least sure about. I'd much rather know where you're guessing than have the whole thing sound equally certain.
