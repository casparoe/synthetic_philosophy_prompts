I work on trust-and-safety infrastructure at a mid-size consumer platform. I'm an engineer with no formal philosophy background, so please flag anywhere I've misstated the ideas below.

Our enforcement stack works roughly as follows. Every account action is logged, and a gradient-boosted model with on the order of two thousand features assigns each account a rolling risk score. That score drives a graduated ladder of automated penalties: temporary rate limits, removal of individual capabilities such as posting or messaging, reduced distribution so an account's content reaches fewer people without the account being informed, and finally suspension. Two properties matter for my question. First, penalty magnitude is not read off a published table; an optimizer selects, per account, the smallest intervention predicted to push that account's future violation rate below a threshold, so two users who commit identical acts routinely receive different penalties. Second, the model is retrained weekly on the trailing ninety days of behavior, so the operative rule shifts continuously and could not be published in advance even in principle, since there is no rule to state in words. Detection, by contrast, is close to total, and the penalty lands within minutes.

A colleague suggested Cesare Beccaria's 1764 treatise on crimes and punishments as the right lens, and I've read a summary of it. My understanding is that he grounded punishment in social contract and utility, held that its purpose is to prevent future harm rather than to inflict suffering as such, insisted that certainty and promptness matter more than severity, demanded that penal law be public, clear, and fixed in advance with proportionate penalties, and objected to judges exercising discretionary power to interpret the law rather than merely applying it.

On that basis, how would Beccaria judge this system? Please address: (a) which features he would count as improvements over the eighteenth-century practices he attacked, and which as the same abuses in new form; (b) whether per-account optimization of expected deterrence is the consistent working-out of his utility principle or a violation of it, given that it makes the penalty a function of predicted future conduct rather than of the act committed; (c) how he would treat the invisibility of reduced distribution — whether a penalty the subject cannot perceive can deter at all, or whether it becomes something he would classify differently; and (d) whether his social-contract argument reaches a private platform at all, where users are not citizens and have surrendered no right to punish. Where you think his framework genuinely lacks the resources for the case, say so rather than straining it.
