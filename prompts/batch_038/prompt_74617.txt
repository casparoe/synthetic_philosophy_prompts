I co-host a graduate-student-run epistemology podcast, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends the KK principle: roughly, that if you know something, you are in a position to know that you know it. She is not a household name in the field, so I cannot rely on the audience already knowing where the pressure points are; I need the questions to do that work.

Some context, because it should shape what you write. After we finished recording plans, a friend and I argued for the better part of two hours about whether the anti-luminosity argument has already settled the matter against her. His line was that the argument's conclusion is that no non-trivial condition is luminous, that "knows that p" is plainly a non-trivial condition, and that KK is nothing other than the claim that this condition is luminous — so the refutation is complete and requires no further premises about clocks, dials, or cold mornings. My reply was that this is too quick: the argument gets its bite from a margin-for-error premise that is generated by a safety-style constraint on a particular basis of belief, and it needs a series of cases that differ only by amounts the subject cannot discriminate. Neither ingredient looks obviously available for, say, a subject's second-order belief about her own arithmetical knowledge, where there is no such series and no such basis-sensitivity. I do not know who is right, and I would rather the interview let an audience feel the weight of precisely that disagreement than have me win or lose it on air.

Please write eight to ten questions, in the order I should ask them, meeting the following requirements.

Coverage. At least three questions must bear on the luminosity connection, and one of those must put my friend's inference to her directly and make her say which premise she rejects and what she is committed to in its place. At least one question must work through an imprecision case of the clock-hand or dial variety — a subject who knows where the hand points but whose visual discrimination is too coarse to know that she knows — and press her to explain what is happening to the second-order belief in the nearby worlds; the follow-up should test whether her answer quietly imports infallibilism, or instead abandons the safety constraint that most of her opponents take for granted. At least one question must address over-intellectualization: whether KK requires that a young child or a non-human animal who knows there is food in the bowl also know that she knows it, and, if she restricts the principle to reflective or suitably idealized agents, what is left of KK as a thesis about knowledge rather than about a special subclass of knowers. At least one question must ask what KK costs and buys elsewhere — its role in the paradoxes that turn on iterated knowledge, what an epistemic logic without it forfeits, whether she treats it as analytic or as a substantive discovery, and whether theses about the luminosity of one's own evidence stand or fall with it. Finally, one question must ask what evidence would count against the principle, and must be phrased so that an appeal to idealization or to the impossibility of introspective error does not count as an answer.

Form. Each question should be speakable aloud in one or two sentences; put whatever setup the audience needs in a separate italicized line before the question, so I know what I am allowed to assume they already follow. For every question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: name that expected answer in the abstract — a move, a distinction, a retreat to a weaker formulation — and say in one line how the follow-up closes off the escape rather than merely restating the challenge. Follow-ups should escalate rather than repeat; by the end of the set it should be progressively harder for her to reach for a distinction she has already used.

Editorial judgment. Tell me which one or two questions to cut if we are running out of time, and why the remainder still functions as a sequence. Also flag any place where a natural-sounding follow-up of mine would misrepresent the view — for instance conflating KK with the general transparency of belief, with a requirement that one be able to articulate one's justification, or with the claim that every true belief about one's own mental states is self-presenting — and give me the corrected version.

No generic material: nothing about what drew her to philosophy, what she is working on next, or how she would summarize the view for a general audience. And no recommendations of readings or literature; I want questions, not a bibliography.
