I cover the mind sciences and their collisions with law and policy for a general-interest magazine, and I'm reporting a piece on police seizures of phones at demonstrations. A philosopher I interviewed offered a framing I want to check before it goes in print. His account: because the extended mind thesis holds that Otto's notebook is not an aid to Otto's mind but a component of it, a person's phone is likewise a component of that person's cognitive system, so confiscating it doesn't merely inconvenience him — it shrinks his mind. He went further, arguing that the thesis therefore treats mentality as something that comes in amounts: people who carry richer, better-integrated external resources simply have more mind than people who don't, a difference he said ought to show up in whatever we can measure, and he cited this as the reason a protester cut off from her devices is a cognitively diminished agent, in the way a person with a memory impairment is. He thinks this is also why the thesis has legal consequences that opponents rarely acknowledge.

Please assess that reasoning, in American English, for a reader who knows only what a magazine article would have told them. Where exactly does his reading depart from what the extended mind thesis claims — and is the departure a matter of overstatement, or does the thesis fail to support the step altogether? I'd also like two things I can use directly in the piece: an account of what the theory actually requires of a device before it counts as part of someone's cognitive system, and a statement of what could show that a given device is not extending a given person's mind, so that readers can tell whether this is the sort of claim that anything could settle.
