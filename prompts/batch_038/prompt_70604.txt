I'm TA'ing a third-year philosophy of social science module and I've got a pile of exam scripts to mark tonight. Here's the question and one student's answer — can you mark it for me, out of 20, and give me the marginal comments I'd actually write?

**Question.** Popper argues that there can be no laws of historical development, only trends. (a) Set out the argument, and explain what work the law/trend distinction does inside it. (b) Consider the claim that some measure of technological capability has roughly doubled every two years for fifty years, and will keep doubling for the next fifty. On Popper's own account, is that a law or a trend, and would extrapolating it be historicism? Explain. (12 marks for (a), 8 for (b).)

**Student's answer.** Popper's argument runs in a handful of steps. First, the course of human history is strongly influenced by the growth of human knowledge, including technical knowledge — he says almost any materialist has to accept this, since changes in the productive forces depend on what people know. Second, we cannot predict the future growth of our scientific knowledge by rational or scientific methods, because if we could predict a discovery in advance we would already have made it: the prediction would just be the knowledge itself, delivered early. Third, so we cannot predict the future course of human history. Fourth, so there can be no theoretical science of history answering to the way physics is a theory of nature, and therefore no basis for historical prophecy. Historicism — the view that history moves through discoverable patterns or stages which the social scientist can lay bare and read the future off — collapses at its root.

A lot of the work is done by laws versus trends. Historicists take trends they've found in the record (population growth, urbanisation, concentration of capital) and treat them tho they were laws, then extrapolate. But a trend for Popper is an existential statement about some particular stretch of history, and it only holds while the initial conditions that produced it hold; if conditions shift the trend can just stop. Laws are universal, support counterfactuals, and license unconditional predictions. So a science of society can perfectly well have laws — situational analysis, the logic of institutions — what it can't have is a law governing the whole course of history, because the whole of history is one unrepeatable sequence and there's no way of testing an overall trend against anything.

This is where the Oedipus effect comes in. Social actors learn predictions about themselves and react, so prophecies either fulfil themselves or defeat themselves, and that is the real reason the social world resists prediction in a way that nature doesn't. Historical prediction is impossible in principle, not merely difficult — which is also why Popper wants piecemeal social engineering instead of utopian reconstruction: you can't know where history is going, so the best you can do is fix named ills and see whether the fix works.

On (b): the doubling curve looks like exactly the kind of trend Popper has in mind, but it is quantitatively precise and has survived fifty years of testing, and that makes it a genuine law rather than a trend. Popper's own distinction therefore exempts it, and someone who projects the curve out to 2100 is doing science rather than historicism. He would also presumably add that the projection is itself an Oedipus effect: believing the curve makes investors and labs pour money into it, which is what makes it come true.
