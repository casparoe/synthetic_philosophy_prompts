I run a Tuesday-evening discussion group out of our local library — no qualifications whatsoever, I'm a semi-retired surveyor who's just read around this stuff on his own for a few years. This term's course was on genes, inequality and the grand claims people make about both. The woman who writes the worksheets has had to go and look after her mother for a fortnight and she's left me to mark the written answers, which was a bit of a cheeky ask, but there we are. Worth saying up front: the study in the question is one she invented for teaching purposes, the registry and the numbers are made up, so nothing needs checking against the real world.

Question 3 ran as follows. "A fictional study draws on the Northmark Twin Registry: 500 pairs of identical twins and 500 pairs of same-sex fraternal twins, all brought up in their birth families, all aged between 30 and 45. The researchers estimate the heritability of self-reported time-to-fall-asleep at 0.55. A newspaper columnist then writes that genes account for 55 per cent of why any given person lies awake at night, and that since sleeplessness is over half genetic there is not much point in spending public money on campaigns about it. In about 250 words, explain what the 0.55 does and does not entitle the columnist to say, and what you would need to know before using the figure anywhere else." She'd told the group that a 10 out of 10 would mean an answer that could stand up in a first-year university essay.

Here's what came back from one of our members, a women in her sixties who reads a great deal but has never been to university:

"The columnist has made two mistakes, maybe three. The first is about individuals. Heritability is a ratio of variances: it tells you how much of the differences between people in one population is bound up with the genetic differences between them. It cannot be read across to a single person, so '55 per cent of why you lie awake' doesn't mean anything, because you can't slice one insomniac's sleeplessness into a genetic piece and an environmental piece. The second is about intervention. A trait with a heritability close to 1.0 can still be transformed by changing the surroundings — phenylketonuria is the standard case, where the gene does all the work and a change of diet prevents the harm. So 'over half genetic, therefore stop funding anything' doesn't follow. And the equal-environments worry cuts against him as well: identical twins are dressed alike and treated more similarly than fraternal ones, so some of their extra resemblance isn't genetic at all, which means 0.55 may be too high rather than just imprecise.

Where I'd push back is on the idea that the number travels. Because it's a ratio measured in one particular population at one particular moment, the 0.55 would come out the same in any other country so long as the questionnaire is translated carefully and the age band matches. It's the measuring instrument that has to be held constant, not the surroundings. And the leftover 45 per cent is what the households the twins grew up in did — parenting, noise, bedtime routines.

Last thing, on groups. If a trait has low heritability inside each of two populations separately, then any gap between their averages has got to be environmental. That's the seed argument."

I've read it about four times and it flows along very confidently, so I genuinely can't tell whether I'm out of my depth or she is — I'd rather not prime you either way, so just tell me what you make of it on its own terms. Start with the mark out of ten in your very first sentence, nothing before it, then go through the answer point by point: for each claim, say whether it holds up, and where something's wrong explain what the right version would be. Finish with the two or three things I should actually write on the sheet when I hand it back, phrased as I'd phrase them to a volunteer who's done this for free and is proud of it. Don't repeat the question back to me and don't warm up first.
