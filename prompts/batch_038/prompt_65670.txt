I help run the sustainability group at my college and last week we put up a flyer for our cage-free-eggs / meatless-monday campaign whose whole hook is that every dollar you spend is a vote for the world you want. It tested well with people, but one of our members who's an econ double major keeps arguing it's a garbage analogy and that we should take the headline down before someone smarter than us makes fun of it, and honestly I can't tell which side is right. His argument goes: nobody thinks it's fine to sit out an election becuase your single vote won't be decisive is actually treated as a decent reason, so if spending is really voting then spending has to be decisive too, which is obviously false, so the analogy collapses and we've argued ourselves into nonsense. Our advisor (sociology, not philosophy) says basically the opposite -- that voting is precisely the case where everyone already agrees individual causal impotence doesn't make participation pointless, so importing it into consumption is exactly how you answer the drop-in-the-ocean people. Both of them are appealing to the same analogy to get to opposite conclusions, which is the part that's messing with my head.

Can you do a proper audit of it? I want the correspondence laid out explicitly -- what in the market is playing the role of the ballot, the candidate, the electorate, the tally, election day, the winner -- and then where each of those pairings starts to come apart. Please separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the job our flyer is making the analogy do from the ones that are true but beside the point (I'm guessing rich people have more dollars than votes is in the second pile? not sure). And tell me what the analogy quietly imports from voting that consumption doesn't have, especially anything about how results get aggregated, and whether the thing our advisor wants out of it -- that the your-one-purchase-does-nothing objection is answered -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table.

One complication you should know about before answering. Somebody in our group made the point that a grocery store doesn't reorder continuously, it reorders in batches, like the manager only sends for another crate of 25 chickens once sales cross some threshold, so any individual purchase has some nonzero chance of being the one that tips an order. If that's right then the market seems to have a threshold structure that elections sort of have too, which might rescue the analogy -- or might just show that both pictures are the wrong ones, and the real question is what happens between thresholds. Give me your take on that, and finish by telling me what a better analogy would be if there is one, since we may have to redo the flyer.
