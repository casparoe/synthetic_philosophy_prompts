so — three months ago I started at a company that makes a short video app, and through some org-chart accident I ended up on the team that owns both the ranking model and what our PR deck calls the wellbeing features. Working on both at once has been slowly driving me crazy and I want someone to help me figure out what we've actually committed ourselves to.

Here's the setup. The ranker is trained on predicted watch-through and predicted completion/share — basically whatever the user does is treated as the answer to what the user wants. Someone watches 90% of a clip and doesn't swipe, that's a strong positive label. The framing in our own internal docs is straight up inverse-reinforcement-learning flavored: assume the person is roughly optimizing some reward, infer the reward from the trace. Meanwhile my other project is a break reminder — after some threshold of continuous scrolling we push a notification asking if they want to take a break — and the entire justification for it in the design doc is that session length is NOT a good measure of what people want, that people keep scrolling well past the point where they'd say they were enjoying it.

Then it gets circular in a way I cant stop thinking about. We instrumented the reminder, about 94% of people dismiss it without tapping anything, and the little model that decides how often and to whom we show it treats a dismissal as a negative signal — this person didn't want the break, show it less. So the feature that exists because revealed behavior isn't trustworthy gets tuned by revealed behavior. And whether it ships at all is decided by an A/B test whose guardrail metrics are next-day retention and total time-in-app; if it costs too much of either, it doesn't launch, no matter what the survey data says.

One more thing that keeps coming up in code review: a user who abandons a video at 40% might be bored, or their bus came, or they got a text message. We genuinely cannot tell from the log. The pipeline just calls it a weak negative and moves on.

So what I want is an unpacking of what each of these pieces is committed to, philosophically. For the ranker: is it revealed preference in the strict sense, where being chosen just is what makes something preferred, or is it a latent-true-preference story where behavior is only noisy evidence about a preference that could be wrong — and does the practice actually care which one it is, or would the code look identical? What does the assume-they're-optimizing move presuppose about agency, and what does it rule out? For the reminder: what does it take a preference to be, and who's the authority on it — the person's later self-report? If so, why isn't the dismissal of the notification itself self-report-adjacent evidence? And what does the retention guardrail presuppose about whose welfare counts and how it gets measured — is that a welfare claim or just a business claim wearing a welfare costume?

Reconstruct the implicit views as carefully as you can, name which rival accounts of preference and welfare each one quietly forecloses (I'm thinking desire-satisfaction vs. objective-list vs. idealized or fully-informed-preference views, but tell me if that's the wrong map), and be honest about where the practice is underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same training data and the same shipping decision, so that the code isn't taking a stand at all. And finally: if we replaced the ranker's implicit view with something like a preference is what the person would want under better conditions, what would concretely have to change about the labels, about the dismissal signal, and about the ship/no-ship test? Is there any version of that change that doesn't just collapse back into measuring engagement with extra steps?
