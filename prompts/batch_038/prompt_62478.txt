I'm the TA for an intro ethics section and I'd like a second opinion before I hand this one back.

The question was: "A dam operator upstream of two towns faces a big storm. She can either open the spillway early, which floods the low-lying farmland of one town, or hold the water, which means that if the dam overtops the other town floods. There is no third option and no time to evacuate anybody. Suppose we say she ought to save the first town and that she also ought to save the second. Some philosophers think a situation like that can't really be described without contradiction. Explain the argument, and then say whether what's left over afterwards — her regret, her sense that she owes something to the people she didn't save — gives us a way around it." Two pages max.

Here's the answer:

"The contradiction comes from two principles. If she ought to save town A and ought to save town B, then she ought to save both of them. And ought implies can. Since she can't save both, the two oughts can't both be true, so one of them has to go. This is why Ross, who built his whole theory on prima facie duties, denied genuine dilemmas only reluctantly: for him the leftover feeling of regret after the choice is evidence that the duty you didn't act on is still binding on you, which is just what a real dilemma would be. So if you want dilemmas you have to reject one of the two principles.

Residue looks like the way to do it. Afterwards the operator isn't merely upset that the outcome was bad, she feels agent-regret, she feels she has to apologize or compensate the town she flooded. That only makes sense if what she did was actually wrong, so the conflict wasn't really settled by picking the stronger duty. It also shows why utilitarianism can't handle these cases: if that theory is right she did the best thing available, so there'd be nothing at all left over to regret."
