I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, writing a dissertation chapter on the relationship between Lewis's analysis of convention in *Convention: A Philosophical Study* (1969) and the later, more dynamic accounts of how regularities stabilize without anyone having designed them. My supervisor has now read the chapter twice, and on both occasions she responded not by disputing my exegesis but by issuing case verdicts: I describe a regularity, she says whether it is a convention. The argument of the chapter turns on which rule she is applying, and I cannot tell from the verdicts alone. I would like your help reconstructing it.

For reference, the framework she works in is Lewis's, which I will paraphrase rather than quote. A convention is a regularity R in the behaviour of the members of a population P who are recurrently agents in a situation S, such that: (i) almost everyone in P conforms to R; (ii) almost everyone expects almost everyone else to conform to R; (iii) almost everyone has approximately the same conditional preferences over the action-combinations available in S; (iv) almost everyone prefers that any one individual conform to R, on condition that almost everyone conforms; (v) conformity to R is an equilibrium, in that no one would do better by departing from it unilaterally; and (vi) R is arbitrary, in that some alternative regularity R′ would have satisfied the other clauses just as well. Lewis allows that the states posited in (ii)–(iv) need not be articulable by the agents, and may show themselves only in dispositions.

Her verdicts to date, with the reason she offered when she offered one:

1. Keeping to the right on the roads in the country where she grew up. Drivers learned it in childhood, have never considered the alternative, and when asked say only that everyone else does it. Verdict: a convention.
2. Everyone in her old department submitted expense claims on Fridays. Nobody gains from coinciding, nobody would mind if others submitted on other days, and the practice originated with an administrator who collected the forms on Fridays and retired eleven years ago. Verdict: not a convention — "a regularity with the coordination drained out of it."
3. The watch rotation aboard a small research vessel, where the equipment and the hull geometry make exactly one rotation workable and every alternative leaves the whole crew worse off. Verdict: not a convention — "a technical necessity."
4. At a law firm, whatever the senior partner proposes at the Monday meeting is adopted. Everyone expects it, everyone prefers it to open conflict, and every junior partner would prefer a rotation rule and would adopt one if they could coordinate on it. Verdict: a convention — "unfair, but fairness was never in the definition."
5. In one town, residents park facing the kerb. A bylaw and two wardens enforce it; during a year when the wardens' posts were frozen, the practice collapsed within weeks and has not recovered since they returned. Verdict: not a convention — "held up from outside, not from within."
6. In a certain office building, everyone stands on the right-hand side of the escalator and leaves the left clear. At the airport across town, people do the reverse, and either would serve equally well. A research assistant staged an intervention: for two weeks confederates stood left and walked right, and in the observed cases where the car held one commuter and one confederate standing on the left, the commuter still moved right. The regularity was unchanged afterwards. Verdict: a convention, with the remark that "their not switching shows only that the cue was too weak to register, not that expectation was doing no work."
7. In a bilingual town, one neighbourhood uses one word for a certain pastry and the other neighbourhood uses another, with almost no crossover. Verdict: two conventions in two populations, not one contested convention town-wide; she added that the same regularity can be a convention among some agents and a mere regularity among others.

The case I am taking to her next week is this. Roughly two hundred market-making algorithms, operated by different firms, trade on the same electronic exchange. Each was trained independently by its own firm using reinforcement learning on historical order flow; there is no shared code, no communication, and no designer of the pattern about to be described. The exchange's surveillance unit has found that after a large order sweeps several price levels, every one of the bots withdraws its quotes for approximately 400 milliseconds before re-entering, producing a brief liquidity gap that, on the exchange's own analysis, damps price oscillation. The pattern is stable; holding the other bots' policies fixed, each bot's policy maximizes its operator's realized trading payoff; and in re-runs with different random seeds, simulated populations settle on different pause lengths — 120 ms, 400 ms, 900 ms — that are equally stable, so an alternative regularity would have served as well. No bot represents the others; each policy is a function of recent order-flow statistics. Because the other bots' pauses alter those statistics, each bot's behaviour does covary with what the others do, but the firms' engineers tested the dependence directly: retrained in a simulation where the others never paused, one variant dropped the pause entirely, while two other variants kept pausing regardless. None of the human operators knew about the pattern before the exchange's report. Now that they do, each firm says it will keep the behaviour as long as its rivals do, would disable it if its rivals did, and would prefer that its rivals keep pausing whatever it does itself. Two firms have jointly proposed that the exchange codify the pause as a rule; the exchange has not acted.

Please do the following.

(a) Identify the candidate rules that fit all seven verdicts. At a minimum I expect a structuralist reading on which clauses (i) and (iii)–(vi) do all the work and the expectations of clause (ii) carry no independent weight, and a dispositional reading on which conformity must additionally be sustained by expectations of others' conformity, however implicit and unarticulable, with the test being counterfactual responsiveness to non-conformity. Add any further readings that also fit — for instance one requiring a population of minded agents, or one on which clause (ii) collapses into clause (iv) — and for each say which of the seven verdicts constrain it and which leave it tied with its rivals.
(b) Predict her verdict on the algorithmic case. Because the analysis is population-relative, give the verdict separately for the bots, for the firms, and for the exchange's participants taken as a whole, and say whether her case-7 remark licenses splitting them.
(c) For each candidate rule, state what it implies about the algorithmic case, including how it handles the mixed counterfactual evidence among the bots and the firms' newly formed conditional preferences, which did not exist while the pattern was emerging.
(d) Give your best single prediction with reasons, drawing especially on the remarks attached to cases 2 and 6, and say how confident you are and what would lower your confidence.
(e) Design one further case, fully stipulated so that I can put it to her, on which the two leading rules would yield opposite verdicts; explain why none of the seven earlier cases, including the escalator intervention, can perform this function.
(f) Assess a suspicion of mine. I think she is running the dispositional reading, but with a notion of expectation so permissive that anything counterfactually covarying with others' behaviour counts, which would make the reading empirically empty and indistinguishable from the structuralist one. Tell me whether that diagnosis is correct, or whether it misreads what her verdicts show — and either way, what it implies for whether a Lewisian framework can accommodate regularities that are produced by selection or drift in one population and then inherited by another population that does have the relevant expectations.

Two constraints. Please keep it to roughly 1,100 words, and do not supply a bibliography, citations, or quotations from Lewis or anyone else: I have given you the paraphrase you need, and I would rather you reasoned from the cases than reconstructed sources. Use game-theoretic vocabulary freely, but gloss any term that is not standard. If you think one of my seven case descriptions is ambiguous in a way that affects the analysis, flag it rather than resolving it silently.
