Background first, because I think it explains why I'm asking. I spent 34 years doing water chemistry for a state agency — nutrients and trace metals in rivers and reservoirs — and the last decade of that was mostly calibrating instruments and arguing with people about when a difference counted as real. Since I retired I've been reading philosophy, badly and out of order, and lately I've been stuck on whether one thing being better than another has to be transitive: the spectrum arguments, the Sorites worry, the claim that an agent with intransitive preferences can be money-pumped. Turns out this isn't academic for me, because I now chair the review panel of a small regional conservation fund, and we have a house rule that I think is quietly doing the exact thing the philosophers are arguing about.

The setup. About 40 applications a round, two rounds a year, we fund 9 or 10 of them, roughly $180k a year total. Five panelists, all volunteers: me, a retired schoolteacher, a farmer, and two people who work at land trusts. Nobody has any training in decision theory, me included, and the whole ranking conversation has to happen in one three-hour meeting, with a quieter week beforehand for reading. Each of us scores each application on six criteria under agreed weights, we average, and the average comes out of 100. The family foundation that gives us the money requires a strictly ranked list with no ties, so that if a top project falls through the money cascades down. Everything we publish has to be defensible to the people we rejected; two of them appealed last year.

The rule I invented and now suspect. Coming from the lab I couldn't stand the idea that a 1.5-point gap out of 100 was being treated as meaningful when our scores obviously carry more noise than that. So I got the panel to adopt a tolerence band: if two applications land within 3 points of each other, we call them tied and discuss them together. Two things went wrong.

One, ties chain. Last round we had 14 applications running from 78 down to 61 where every adjacent pair was within 3 points. By our own rule they were all ties, and yet when I asked the panel to compare the top one directly against the bottom one — a riparian buffer along Miller Creek versus interpretive signage at a boat launch — all five said Miller Creek was obviously the better project. Nobody blinked.

Two, head-to-head disagrees with the sum. A $12k culvert removal that reopens about four miles of spawning stream scored 71. A $95k project fencing livestock out of 40 acres of wetland scored 74. When we debated those two on their own, the panel voted 4–1 for the culvert. The weighted sum said the fence. We funded the fence and I still don't know whether that was right.

What I want is a decision procedure this panel can actually run — in the three-hour meeting and in the week before it. Not an essay. Steps, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and with the places clearly marked where the procedure stops and hands the call to a human being because the question is one of values and not of arithmetic. It has to fit on one page for five volunteers.

It should settle as far as it can: whether the band should exist at all, and if so how wide and applied to what; what governs when every adjacent pair is a tie but the endpoints are far apart; whether cycles are genuinely possible in what we do and what to do the day one shows up; how to break a real tie for a funder that forbids ties without doing something as nakedly arbitrary as alphabetical order, which we did once and an applicant noticed; and when a head-to-head vote should override the weighted sum and when it shouldn't.

Ground it in what the philosophy actually says about the transitivity of betterness — the spectrum argument, why small differences piling up is supposed to be a problem, the self-defeat objections to giving transitivity up, and the main exits people have proposed: denying that small differences never matter, denying transitivity, using a threshold under which strict preference stays transitive but indifference doesn't, or conceding that some pairs simply aren't precisely comparable. Explain those in plain words. No reading list, no bibliography, no citations of any kind — I won't be able to look them up, and I'd much rather you explain an idea than point me at a book.

Then run your procedure on the three cases: the 14-application chain, the culvert versus the fence, and a tie-break case of your own devising that resembles the ones we hit. For at least one of them I expect an answer I won't like. I'm the guy who invented the band, so if the honest conclusion is that it has to go, or that it only survives with a proviso I'll find embarrassing, say it straight instead of cushioning it.

Last thing: tell me which of your steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes, and what the procedure can't do — the ordinary failures it will produce on normal input, not just the exotic ones.
