For forty-three years I worked at a bench: calibrations, error bars, records that any competent colleague could repeat and check. Since retiring I have been reading Carnap, and what has arrested me is not his logic but his politics — a man who wanted society planned scientifically, who nonetheless held that a value statement asserts nothing at all and merely expresses an attitude, and who regarded the choice between frameworks as a practical decision for which no one can be refuted. I should like a sonnet set inside that tension.

Let the octave be written wholly in the vocabulary of the protocol — instruments, quantities, inter-subjectively checkable reports — and let it rhyme in enclosed pairs, abba abba, so that it reads as a closed system continually confirming itself. Let the volta fall precisely where the plan demands an objective, a for-whom, and let the sestet show that this term cannot be recovered from the octave, however many further measurements are appended to it; set the sestet in a scheme that never returns to its own opening rhyme, so that the form itself declines to derive the conclusion from the premises. Close the couplet on the planner's position: the decision is no discovery, and the work begins anyway. Do not name Carnap or his circle, and do not let the poem lapse into the familiar platitude that science cannot tell us what to do; a historian of logical empiricism should recognize the doctrine without being told it.
