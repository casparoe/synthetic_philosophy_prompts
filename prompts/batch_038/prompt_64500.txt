You're an expert in epistemology and value theory, and specifically you've thought hard about the Meno problem — why knowledge should be worth more than mere true beleif. I'm building next week's reading group session around my colleague Dana's own track record, and I want you to think carefully before answering: start by brainstorming every candidate principle that could generate her verdicts, test each one against all five, and only then narrow down.

The cases I ran past her, and what she said:

1. Two equally thirsty hikers: one gets to the spring by reading a reliable map, one stumbles onto it by luck. Both drink. — Knowledge worth more.
2. Same pair, but a rescue helicopter lands ten minutes later and neither of them will ever act on that belief again. — Still worth more.
3. A subject wired to a device that reliably implants true beliefs about the local weather; she is entirely passive. — Not worth more than a lucky true belief.
4. A careless guesser who happens to come out right, and an archer who looses a sloppy shot that a gust of wind blows back onto the target. — Neither worth more.
5. A grad student who checks every source, takes enormous pains, clearly cares about geting it right — but her method is hopeless and she lands on the truth by a fluke. — Not worth more.

Here's the case she hasn't ruled on yet. Ruth is an experienced radiologist who is arrogant, badly out of date, and in a hurry to leave; she never opens the history, spends about four seconds on the film, and comes to believe there's a hairline fracture largely because she wants to go home. But her pattern recognition genuinely is a honed skill: it's what produced the belief, the fracture is really there, and had the film shown something else she'd have seen that instead. So the truth looks like it's down to her ability, and yet nothing about how she went about it looks creditable.

(a) What will she say — worth more than a lucky true belief, or not? (b) Which principles are still live after all five verdicts? I'm fairly confident more than one survives, and that they come apart on Ruth; lay each one out, say what it predicts here, and tell me which you'd bet on and why. (c) Give me one further case that would settle things between your top two candidates. And (d) if you think her five verdicts aren't jointly consistent after all, say which one has to go and what principle forces it.
