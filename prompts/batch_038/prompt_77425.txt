Six weeks ago I sat on the shortlisting panel for our department's summer research fellowship, and I caught myself ranking a candidate who had submitted a ninety-page undergraduate thesis that nobody on the panel had the time to read above a candidate whose eight pages were demonstrably better. When I said out loud why I had done it — that she had evidently been able to afford ninety pages, and that seemed to mean something about her — the two senior colleagues opposite me could not tell me whether I had just read a signal or acted on a prejudice. I have been chewing on that exchange ever since, and it has bled into my teaching. This term I am covering, at short notice and for the first time, one 75-minute session of a cross-listed course in philosophy of biology and behavioural ecology: twenty-four students, roughly half philosophy and half ecology, second and third years, none of whom will have been assigned anything beforehand, in a room with movable tables and a whiteboard. My own training is in epistemology rather than biology; I can follow a signalling game when someone writes it out for me, but I have never run one, so I need the mechanics specified concretely enough that I could hand them to a colleague and walk out of the room. I have no budget: no real money, no prizes bought out of pocket, and nothing that requires printing more than one double-sided page per student. Paper chips, index cards, beans, dice and pens are all fine.

What I want is an in-class game that makes the students run into the problem of costly signalling themselves, before anyone says the words handicap, index, or cheap talk — those belong in the debrief, not on the handout. The fiction should be set somewhere that is not the labour market, precisely because the economics students will reach for it: a breeding colony, a begging brood, a coalition of foragers, whatever you judge will carry the mechanics best. Each round should have signallers drawing a concealed quality card (I would like three levels rather than two, so that the threshold at which the signal stops being worth sending is not discoverable by inspection) and receivers whose own payoff depends on choosing well, so that receivers are not neutral scorekeepers but parties with an interest in not being fooled. Please build in the contrasts that I think the lesson actually turns on: a round in which messages cost nothing and should collapse; a round in which the payment is destroyed outright; a round in which the same payment goes to the receiver instead of vanishing, so the class can feel how differently the two arrangements discipline the sender; a round in which the signal is free but detected fakery is punished, with imperfect detection; and a final round in which some parameter shifts — the ornament becomes dearer, or the prize for being chosen becomes cheaper — so that a pattern which looked stable stops holding. I also need you to solve a problem I have had before: if the tokens have no terminal value, students play whimsically and the equilibrium logic never bites. Tell me how to make paper chips matter to twenty-four people in a room where I cannot offer them anything.

Then give me the debrief, and be exact about it. Map what they will have watched happen in each round onto the theoretical distinctions I want them to leave with — the difference between a cue, an index, and a signal that could be faked but is not; why Zahavi's original picture of waste as the guarantor of truth is not the same claim as Grafen's equilibrium result, in which what does the work is that the marginal cost of signalling falls less steeply on the better signaller; and the fact that there is a live argument about whether honest signals need to be costly at all. On that last point I would like the debrief to use the numbers the class generated rather than an assertion from me: which round's results support which side, and what to say if a bright ecology student points out that our free-but-punished round already refutes the strong version. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect token accounting to drift, friends to collude, receivers to invent side channels the rules never mentioned, at least one student to resent being handed the low-quality card, and the costless-message round to end in sullen deadlock rather than the informative collapse I am hoping for — and how to handle each, including what to do if someone names the peacock's tail or a university degree in minute five and short-circuits the whole discovery. Finish with an honest note on which features of the real phenomenon this activity simply cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by talking: that no one in the room inherits a strategy, that receiver preferences here are read off a card instead of having been shaped over generations, that quality is a discrete role rather than a condition-dependent state. And please do not send me a reading list; I need something I can run off the page on Tuesday.
