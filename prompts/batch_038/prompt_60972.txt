The question was:

Q4 (25 marks). (a) State Lewis's Principal Principle as precisely as you can, and explain what work the admissibility condition is doing. (b) Why does Lewis think the principle says something substantive, rather than just recording that chance and credence are two names for one thing? (c) Lewis later said there was a bug in the combination of the principle with his own account of what chance is. Explain the bug and say what Lewis does about it.

The answer:

(a) The principle says that a rational agent's credence in a proposition A should equal A's objective chance, provided the agent's evidence is admissable. Formally: if X is the proposition that the chance of A at time t is x, and E is any admissible proposition, then C(A | X & E) = x. Two features of the setup matter. Chance is time-indexed — the same token event can have chance 1/2 now and chance 1 tomorrow, once it has happened — so the principle is always relativised to a time and a history. And chance is single-case: it attaches to this flip of this coin rather than to a long run, which is why the principle can't just be read off relative frequencies; a frequency view gives you nothing to put in place of x for an event that never repeats.

Admissibility is doing the real work. E is admissible just in case it is evidence about the past, or evidence that carries no information about A beyond what X already gives you. Without the restriction the principle contradicts itself. Suppose I watch the coin land heads. My evidence says A is true, X says its chance was 1/2, and the principle then tells me to hold credence 1/2 in something I know. So Lewis rules out future-directed evidence, and after that credence tracks chance in exactly those cases where the agent isn't already in a position to know the outcome.

(b) Lewis is a subjectivist about credence: nothing in the world makes a degree of belief correct, only coherence does. Chance is the other way round, it's a feature of the world. So the principle is no trivial identity, it's a bridge from an objective quantity to a subjective one, and it is what entitles the word chance to the name — Lewis's point is that any candidate feature of the world that didn't constrain rational credence in this way wouldn't deserve to be called chance at all. Note the direction, though. Because the constraint runs this way, chance gets analysed in terms of the credences it ought to produce, so Lewis is a subjectivist about chance too: chance is whatever it is rational to align your degrees of belief with. That is also why the principle can't be derived from the probability axioms, since coherence on its own never forces any particular number.

(c) The bug is that Lewis's account makes the chances supervene on the whole pattern of events, past and future alike, so a chance hypothesis can turn out to be incompatible with futures that have positive chance of occurring — undermining futures. If F has positive chance, then by the principle my credence in F given the chance hypothesis should be positive, so F isn't ruled out by that hypothesis; but F's occurring would itself be part of what makes some other chance hypothesis the true one, so F does bear on the chances after all, and the principle ends up demanding a credence in a proposition that the very same principle counts as inadmissible. Lewis's response is to restrict the principle once more, conditionalising not on a single chance hypothesis but on the whole theory of chance, which is what everyone now calls the New Principle. He sets this out in the 1986 Postscript and takes the bug to be settled there.
