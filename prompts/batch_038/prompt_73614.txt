**Draft: "Cluelessness Is a Noise Problem, Not an Ignorance Problem"**

**P1.** Since James Lenman's 2000 paper "Consequentialism and Cluelessness" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs* 29(4)), the standard form of the epistemic objection to consequentialism has been that we cannot know the remote consequences of ordinary acts, and therefore cannot know which acts are right. The diagnosis is epistemic rather than axiological: the problem is not that consequences fail to matter, but that they lie beyond our ken. Familiar replies cluster in three families — that remote effects largely cancel out, so we may attend to tractable proximate ones; that consequentialism is a criterion of rightness rather than a decision procedure, so our ignorance is not fatal to the theory; and that the objection proves too much, since non-consequentialist theories also need some knowledge of outcomes to apply their own constraints.

**P2.** My claim is that all three replies target the wrong quantity, because the objection is standardly stated as an *ignorance* problem when it is really a *noise* problem. Failing to know the sign of an act's remote effects is not the same as being unable to rank acts. For any class of acts A (donating to a global health charity, having a child, publishing a paper, voting), define its moral signal-to-noise ratio as SNR(A) = [E(V(a*)) − E(V(a₂))] / SD(V(a)), where V is long-run value, a* the best available act in A, a₂ the second best, and SD the standard deviation of V across acts drawn at random from A. Cluelessness, stated precisely, is the claim that SNR(A) ≈ 0 for most classes of everyday acts. That is a contingent claim about which act classes we happen to be choosing from, not an a priori limit on moral knowledge.

**P3.** The reframing explains why the standard replies miss. Cancellation is a claim about the mean of the noise, not its spread. If remote ripple effects are symmetrically distributed around zero, that establishes only that the noise has mean zero; it leaves the standard deviation untouched, and it is the spread — relative to the gap between my top two options — that determines whether the ranking is recoverable. Symmetric noise of enormous magnitude is just as paralyzing as asymmetric noise. So the most popular reply in the literature does not, as far as I can see, engage the objection at all.

**P4.** The reframing also generates a research program. Some act classes should have systematically higher SNR: those that alter the *distribution* of future options rather than adding a perturbation to a fixed distribution — transmitting a technique, founding or reforming an institution, publishing a result others build on. The statistical intuition is that when an act's effects propagate through many roughly independent channels, the variance of the total grows approximately linearly in the number of channels, while the systematic component compounds along the channels the act controls; so SNR should rise with the number of agents whose option sets an act durably changes.

**P5.** Conclusion: cluelessness does not refute consequentialism so much as relocate it. The operative question is not "what will this act cause?" but "which act class am I choosing from, and is its signal-to-noise ratio favorable?" That is portfolio reasoning, and it is continuous with how we already deliberate under deep uncertainty in medicine and public policy.
