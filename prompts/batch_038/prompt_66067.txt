So I sit on my hospital's ethics committee — mid-size system, mostly part-time clinicians plus two of us from the university, and we usually get maybe four or five cases a year. This one's got everyone worked up and I've been volunteered to write something for Thursday.

Quick version, details changed: a woman in her thirties, two months out from a traumatic brain injury. Behaviorally she's been diagnosed as minimally conscious — she'll track with her eyes sometimes, grimace, but nothing reproducible, nothing you'd call a response to a request. Last week she got an fMRI "command-following" assessment, the paradigm where you ask someone to imagine playing tennis, or walking through their house, and look for the corresponding activation. On two of three runs she produced the motor-imagery pattern when asked to imagine tennis, and the pattern was distinguishable from the one you get when she's just told to relax and let her mind wander. Our neurologist's read, in the note and in the hallway and at lunch, is that this shows she understood the instruction and followed it, that she's "in there," and that we owe it to her to treat that as settled. The chaplain agrees and wants to tell the family Thursday. Her husband has started saying things like "she's still with us, she just can't move."

Here's my problem, and it isn't that I think the result means nothing. It's that I can't tell what it's supposed to mean. We have a brain state that reliably tracks the content of an instruction. Half the committee treats that as the patient herself answering a question, and the other half — me, mostly — keeps wanting to say we've detected a causal signature and then leapt to an inner life, and that nobody's explained what would license that leap. I also don't know what the scan even is, evidentially: testimony from the patient? a third-person measurement like a blood test? a bit of behavior that happens to be inside the skull? And I don't know how much the complete behavioral silence should count against it. There's something about the relation between having an experience and the outward signs of having one that the whole practice is resting on, and I don't think anyone here has thought about it, including me.

Can you draft the short opinion I'll circulate before the meeting? I need a clear bottom line — what we tell the family Thursday, specifically, and what our policy should be for this class of case going forward (who gets offered the scan, what the report may and may not say, whether a positive result should feed into decisions about continuing or withdrawing treatment) — plus the reasoning behind it, with the criteria the committee can actually apply next time instead of arguing from intuition. Please give the neurologist's side its strongest form and then say honestly where it breaks or holds; he's a friend and he'll read this. And please don't just fall back on "the science is unsettled, we need more research" — if a positive result can't support the claim that she's aware, I want to know why not, in a form I could say out loud to a husband who's been given hope.
