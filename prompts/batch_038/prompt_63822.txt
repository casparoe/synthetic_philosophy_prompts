I'm writing the methods chapter of my PhD — consciousness science, more neuro than philosophy, but the chapter has to be properly philosophical — and my supervisor's asked me to set my methodological commitments down in one place so he can attack them in a single pass. I'd rather be attacked by you first. Here's the list, roughly as I'd defend it:

(1) Heterophenomenology is the right method for a science of consciousness: treat the subject's reports and other behaviour as a text, construct the heterophenomenological world the text implies, and make that world the object of study. (2) What makes the method scientific is its neutrality — it commits you to nothing whatsoever about whether the denizens of that world (the afterimages, the pains, the redness of the tomato) actually exist; a qualia realist and I can both use it, which is the whole selling point. (3) Reports are data, not revelations: no first-person authority in any strong sense, subjects can be sincere, confident and simply wrong about what they just experienced, and the method has to leave room for that. (4) In practice though I'm conservative about the text — I build the world to fit the report as closely as I can and I don't edit what the subject says they had. (5) I'm not an instrumentalist about the intentional stance, which heterophenomenology is just an extension of: on the real patterns line an attribution is literally true when the pattern is real, and I do think some of the contents of subjects' heterophenomenological worlds are real patterns rather than useful fictions. (6) For postdiction cases — colour phi and friends — I go Orwellian rather than Stalinesque: the report of a seamless, continuously shifting experience is not a report of what was experienced when it happened, it's a memory edit. (7) Hard constraint: if something can't in principle be pinned down by third-person observation, it doesn't get into the science. (8) Case verdict, from my own experiment: subjects report a richly detailed, full-colour experience of the whole display while performing at chance on peripheral discriminations; I say the correct heterophenomenological world here is sparse and the report is wrong, rather than positing a rich experience plus a bottleneck.

So: which of these actually pull against each other? Some will look like they clash and won't, and I'd rather you tell me which is which than flag everything as a worry — I've commited to a lot of items and I can't see the shape of the thing any more. Rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them in order, give the cheapest fix for each genuine one and say what the fix costs me (which bits of the programme I'd lose), then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Don't assume there's one item I'd rather drop than another, and if (1)–(8) jointly commits me to something I haven't listed, say what it is.

Format: a JSON object with exactly these fields. "audit" — an array sorted by severity descending, each entry an object with "pair" (the numbers involved, e.g. "(2)+(5)"), "kind" ("genuine" or "apparent"), "severity" (integer 1 to 5), "mechanism" (one or two sentences on why they really pull, or why they only look like they do), "cheapest_fix", and "cost" (what I give up by making that fix). "most_productive_revision" — an object with "drop" (a number), "replace_with", "keeps" (which of the remaining commitments survive untouched) and "loses". "unlisted_commitments" — array of strings, things my eight items already commit me to that I didn't say out loud. "verdict" — one of "hangs together", "needs surgery", "doesn't hang together". Nothing else in the object.
