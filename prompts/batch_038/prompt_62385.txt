I convene a reading group on accuracy-first epistemology, and one of its members has produced an argument that he claims sinks the entire programme. I do not accept his conclusion, but I have been unable to locate the faulty joint, and every step appears to be something a defender of the view is independently committed to. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Beliefs are the sort of thing whose only intrinsic epistemic merit is accuracy: how close they come to the truth. Any other putative epistemic virtue — coherence, evidential fit, responsiveness — is either reducible to accuracy or is not an epistemic virtue at all.

(2) Accuracy is measured by a strictly proper scoring rule: a rule on which, by the agent's own lights, her actual credence uniquely maximizes expected accuracy.

(3) Dominance theorem: given any credence function that is not a probability function over an algebra F, some probability function over F is more accurate than it at every possible world. Incoherent credences are therefore accuracy-dominated, and so, by (1), irrational.

(4) The theorem imposes no restriction on the size or complexity of F, and the norms in (1)–(3) are not indexed to what the agent can see or compute. Accuracy is objective; that is precisely the point of the programme. A credence can be defective in the only way credences can be defective whether or not its possessor is in a position to detect the defect.

(5) So let F be the algebra generated by the sentences of arithmetic, and let c be any actual agent's credence function on F. If c assigns anything less than 1 to some theorem of arithmetic, then c is not a probability function.

(6) By (3), some probability function over F accuracy-dominates c, and by (2) the agent herself expects that alternative to be strictly better. Since accuracy is the only epistemic merit (1), she is rationally required to adopt it.

(7) Rationality therefore requires credence 1 in every theorem of arithmetic: logical omniscience.

(8) The same reasoning disposes of imprecise credences, since a set of probability functions is likewise dominated by some single probability function. Rational credal states must be sharp.

(9) But no finite agent is logically omniscient, and a norm that no agent could meet is not a norm for agents.

(10) Hence accuracy-first epistemology must either abandon (1), admitting non-alethic epistemic values and so abandoning the programme, or else it issues requirements that cannot be complied with, in which case it is silent about every actual believer. Either way it fails.

I should like to keep (1) and (3) if I possibly can; they are what make the view attractive in the first place. Which step should be given up, and what distinction does it trade on that made it look innocuous where it stands? What does the accuracy-first theorist pay for giving it up — the generality of the dominance theorem, the objectivity of accuracy, the action-guiding character of her norms, or something else? Does whatever repair you propose also handle (8), or does the imprecise-credence case require separate treatment? And once the argument is repaired as far as it can be, does a residue of it survive as a genuine difficulty for the programme when the agents in question have finite computational resources, or does the difficulty evaporate entirely?
