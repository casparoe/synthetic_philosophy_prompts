Between 1681 and the 1830s, the King Lear performed on the London stage was not Shakespeare's. Nahum Tate's adaptation cut the Fool, gave Cordelia a confidante and an abduction scene of her own on the heath, built a love plot between her and Edgar, and closed with Lear back on his throne and Cordelia alive and about to be married. That version held the stage for roughly a century and a half. Addison protested it in print; Samuel Johnson, in his Preface to Shakespeare, defended it precisely as an improvement in poetic justice. Shakespeare's ending, with Cordelia dead and Lear dying over her body, did not return to the theater until William Charles Macready staged it, partially in 1834 and in fuller form in 1838, after which the original text gradually reclaimed the play.

I want this episode analyzed through the paradox of tragedy, and I want the analysis to run in both directions: what the leading solutions predict about a century and a half of London audiences, and what that century and a half does to the solutions. Take the facts above as given. Do not spend space on plot summary or on textual history beyond what I have supplied.

First, the mapping. Consider the main families of answer: catharsis in Aristotle's sense; Hume's conversion account, on which eloquence turns the painful passion into a pleasant one; the fictionality and control accounts, on which distress is discounted because no one is really suffering; the compensation and cognitive-value accounts, on which sadness is redeemed by what the work gives back; and the meta-response accounts, on which what pleases is our own appropriately engaged response. For each, state a concrete prediction about the Tate period — not that audiences would find Cordelia's death painful, but something about attendance, repeat viewings, and whether a commercial manager in a patent theater would risk staging the original. Then say which of those predictions a run of 157 years bears out and which it does not.

Second, the fit and the misfit. Why was the ending the thing that had to change? Tate cut the Fool and added sentimental business, but the heath scenes and Gloucester's fate apparently created no comparable difficulty; where you are unsure what Tate retained beyond what I have told you, flag the gap rather than filling it. I am especially interested in whether a peak-and-end structure of affective memory — retrospective judgment weighted toward the final moments and largely indifferent to duration — does more explanatory work here than any of the philosophical accounts, and if so, whether that is a solution to the paradox of tragedy or a bypassing of it. Related: Tate and Johnson described the change in moral and structural terms, not hedonic ones, and no one in the controversy seems to have said the original was simply too unpleasant to sit through. What do these theories do with participants who fail to report the phenomenology the theories posit? And does Burke's contrary suggestion, that a real public execution would draw a larger crowd than the finest staged tragedy, make the Tate result harder or easier to explain?

Third, in the other direction: was there ever a paradox for these audiences? If the puzzle assumes a modern spectator who chooses a sad film for a single sitting and reports having enjoyed it, say whether Tate's run is a counterexample, an anomaly, or a case the paradox never contemplated. Then design the discriminating test: name one piece of evidence about the Tate period that would separate the conversion account from the fictionality account, given that both predict the same preference for a happy ending.

Finally, be explicit about what the theories cannot cover. If none of them explains why the same text became stageable again in the 1830s without any obvious change in human psychology, say so, and say what sort of explanation would be required instead — and whether that explanation still answers the question why we enjoy sad art, or has quietly replaced it with a different question about institutions, canon formation, and who decides what a national classic should cost an audience. Describe mechanisms rather than name-dropping; no bibliography, and no citations you cannot characterize in your own words.
