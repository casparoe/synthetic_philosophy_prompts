I am setting the take-home final for a graduate seminar in formal philosophy of science — students have had a term of propositional logic plus one week on truthlikeness, 90 minutes for this item, 25 marks. Write one essay question on verisimilitude that cannot be answered by narrating the arc from Popper's content-based definition, through the 1974 results showing that on it no false theory can outrank another, to the similarity-based programmes that followed.

The question must be wholly self-contained: it should state every definition a student needs, namely a toy propositional language with three atoms whose true state description is given, a measure of average likeness across the constituents a theory comprises, and a min-distance measure over the same constituents, each with its formula. It should then present two theories, A a single constituent that misses on exactly one atom, B a disjunction containing the true constituent together with two constituents that each miss on two atoms, and require the student to compute both measures for both theories, notice that the two measures order A and B in opposite directions, and argue which verdict is right — or why the choice between the measures cannot be settled from within the case.

Deliver three things: the question; a model answer of about 800 words that does the arithmetic explicitly and says what the divergence between the two measures actually turns on; and a marking scheme allocating the 25 marks to named moves, separating an A from a B answer, and flagging four errors that should cost marks. Among those errors, include equating truthlikeness with probability or degree of confirmation, assuming that logical strength tracks truthlikeness, treating the collapse of Popper's definition as showing that truthlikeness is impossible rather than that one definition fails, and sliding from the measure-relativity of the ranking to the unobjectivity of truthlikeness without argument. Require no citations from the student and include none in the model answer.
