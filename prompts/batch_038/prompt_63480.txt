I run the defect triage desk at a company that builds patient-monitoring software. We take in roughly four hundred reports a week from field support, device telemetry, and internal QA, and four analysts decide for each one whether it duplicates an existing defect record or opens a new one. Individually these are small calls; collectively they are load-bearing. Merging too eagerly hides systemic faults and deflates the counts our regulatory team relies on in filings; splitting too finely buries the desk in near-identical records and makes every severity statistic uninterpretable. We currently apply three informal tests, inconsistently and in whatever order the analyst happens to reach for: what we call the fix test (two reports describe the same defect if a single change to the code would prevent both), the repro test (if the same steps reproduce both, they are the same defect), and the cause test (same root cause, same defect). They conflict often enough that we have no principled way to break ties.

I want you to convert the problem of universals into a procedure these analysts can actually run. I did a philosophy degree some years ago and remember the shape of the debate; they do not, and the procedure has to work without them. Deliver, in order:

First, the procedure itself — the questions to ask of a new report and in what sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a fast path that disposes of routine reports inside about five minutes, the points at which it terminates in duplicate, distinct, or escalate, and the points at which it deliberately hands the decision to a human being because the question is one of policy rather than fact. Our ticketing system offers a fixed set of dispositions, and any record closed as a duplicate of a safety-relevant defect must be defensible to regulatory affairs, who sign off after the fact.

Second, a ruling on the three tests: which should be primary, what becomes of the other two, and why an engineer with no interest in metaphysics should accept that ordering.

Third, explicit handling of the cases that currently break us. Two reports with different root causes that a single input-validation guard would prevent. One root cause requiring two different patches in two subsystems. A cluster of six reports that pairwise resemble one another but share no single feature common to all six. A fault that can only be stated disjunctively. And the fact that a refactor scheduled for next year would reclassify records we merged this quarter: tell me whether that is a flaw in the procedure or an honest feature of it.

Fourth, transitivity. Our tooling assumes duplicate-of is an equivalence relation, so every report lands in exactly one bucket and the counts add up. The second and third cases above suggest it is not transitive. Say whether we should abandon transitivity, what has to change downstream if we do, and if we should not, how the procedure blocks the intransitive cases without pretending they are not real.

Fifth, worked applications. Run the procedure end to end on two cases: the six-report cluster with no common feature, and the eleven intermittent waveform-dropout reports we merged last autumn into a single record and closed as a site-specific network configuration problem before it emerged that three unrelated causes had been producing them. For the second, state plainly what the procedure now implies about that disposition, and do not soften it if the implication is that a closed record should reopen. Then choose one further case of your own construction on which the procedure and our old three tests give different answers, and show where they diverge.

Finally, an annotation addressed to me rather than to the desk: which distinction in the debate each step of the procedure is encoding — whether the defect type is something in the codebase to be discovered or a carving we impose for our purposes, the worry that any two reports resemble each other in some respect so that resemblance alone cannot pick out a class, the regress that threatens anyone who wants something extra to tie instances to their type — and, just as important, which parts of the debate the procedure discards and what we lose by discarding them.

Be candid about where the procedure will misfire systematically, what it cannot settle at all, and which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of rules. Argue everything from the concepts themselves: no reading lists, no citations, no named texts or authors.
