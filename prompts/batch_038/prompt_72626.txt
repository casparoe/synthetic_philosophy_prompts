I convene a graduate reading group in epistemology, and this term we are working through self-distrust: cases in which you acquire reason to think your own judgment is untrustworthy while the first-order evidence stays exactly where it was. I want to open our first session with a diagnostic quiz that sorts the twelve of us by which account of that situation we actually hold, so that we stop talking past one another behind labels. Please write the quiz to the following specification.

Nine items. Each is a concrete first-person scenario of two to five sentences, followed by two or three answer options that are themselves substantive claims rather than hedges or degrees of confidence. No item may contain any of the following words: akrasia, higher-order, defeat, level, warrant, or rational. The jargon belongs in the key, never in the questions. No item may ask what the respondent's view is; every item must ask what the respondent would say, do, or conclude about the case.

Draw at least five of your scenarios from the following, and invent the rest yourself in the same spirit:

1. A physician tells you that a medication you began this week will dull numerical reasoning for about a month; you have just spent an hour on a calculation you find compelling, and no one else can check it for a week.
2. A colleague you trust as a careful reader says there is a subtle error somewhere in the argument you have just given, but declines to say which step, on the grounds that telling you would spoil the exercise.
3. You learn that the intuition you are leaning on is reliably produced by a framing effect that reverses when the same quantities are presented differently; you read the alternative framing and still find your original conclusion compelling.
4. You re-derive the argument twice, slowly, and find no flaw anywhere in it, and you have no independent reason to think your re-checking is itself compromised.
5. You discover that you slept four hours before writing the assessment that everyone now treats as your best piece of work.
6. Someone whose track record is mixed tells you that you are a far worse estimator of elapsed time than you suppose, and you notice the remark nagging at you even though nothing in the present question turns on time.
7. You are told you were impaired, and you have already tried twice to give up the belief and found that you simply cannot.
8. A friend says out loud that she does not know whether what she believes is supported, and then continues to act and argue on that belief exactly as before.

At least two items must be about someone other than the respondent, and at least one must ask what the respondent may say or publish rather than what she may believe, since the accounts diverge on whether a mismatch is objectionable in belief, in assertion, or in both.

Vary the options across items so that a given account does not correspond to the same letter every time; nothing in the layout should let a respondent recognize which leaf an option leads to.

After item nine, supply a key that does three things:

(a) Maps answer patterns onto named positions. The leaves must include, at minimum: the account on which the information about your own untrustworthiness simply removes your entitlement to the first-order belief, so no mismatch is available; the account on which a mismatched pair of states can be entirely permissible when you cannot bring them into line; the account on which such a pair is never permissible because believing is aiming at the truth of what you believe; the account on which the information is not evidence bearing on the question at all but a reason to withhold judgment; the account on which its force depends entirely on how load-bearing the doubted faculty was for the belief in question; and the deflationary account on which there is no phenomenon here to explain, because information about your own untrustworthiness must always mislead you. If a leaf has a standard name in the literature, give the name; if it does not, coin a short one. For each leaf, state separately what it permits in belief and what it permits in assertion.

(b) Lists the combinations of answers that your items make available but that no position in the space occupies, and says for each whether the gap exists because the view would be incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply noticed it.

(c) Flags any pair of leaves that your nine items cannot reliably separate, and describes the tenth item that would separate them.

Begin your response with the text of item 1. No preface, no restatement of these instructions, and no commentary of any kind before the quiz; the key and the two diagnostic notes follow item nine.
