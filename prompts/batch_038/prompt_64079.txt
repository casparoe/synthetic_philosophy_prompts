The question was worth 25 points. Retyping it from the exam:

**Q3.** A subject faces two choice problems.

- Problem 1: A = $500 for certain. B = 10% chance of $4,000, 89% chance of $500, 1% chance of $0.
- Problem 2: C = 11% chance of $500, 89% chance of $0. D = 10% chance of $4,000, 90% chance of $0.

Suppose the subject prefers A to B, and prefers D to C.

(a) Show that no expected utility maximizer can hold both preferences, whatever their utility function over these three amounts. [8 pts]
(b) Name the axiom that is violated and explain how it relates to Savage's sure-thing principle. [7 pts]
(c) Does this preference pattern show the subject is irrational? Discuss, being explicit about the difference between the descriptive and the normative question. [10 pts]

Here is the student's answer, transcribed from their booklet:

"(a) Set the utility of $0 at 0 and the utility of $4,000 at 1, and call u(500) = a. Preferring A to B means a > 0.10(1) + 0.89(a) + 0.01(0), i.e. a > 0.10 + 0.89a, i.e. 0.11a > 0.10, i.e. a > 0.909. Preferring D to C means 0.10(1) + 0.90(0) > 0.11(a) + 0.89(0), i.e. 0.10 > 0.11a, i.e. a < 0.909. Both can't hold, so no utility function over these three amounts gives both preferences. But really what's going on is risk aversion. A very risk averse person has a very concave utility function, so their u(500) sits close to u(4000), which is just what a > 0.909 says. And that same risk averse person in problem 2 doesn't want to trade away their shot at the 4,000, because 11 percent of 500 is barely a payoff at all. So a concave enough utility function should be able to generate both choices, and the actual trouble is that expected utility measures utility too crudely, not that any axiom is wrong.

(b) The violated axiom is the independence axiom: if you prefer P to Q, you prefer any mixture of P with a third lottery R to the same mixture of Q with R. Savage's sure-thing principle is a different and stronger axiom, stronger because it also rules out intransitive preferences, which independence does not do by itself. In problem 1 the two options share the 89 percent branch, so independence says you compare them only on the leftover 11 percent, and then in problem 2 you have to rank them the same way.

(c) Yes, the subject is irrational. Their preferences are intransitive: they put A above B and then reverse when the same two options get scaled down, so there's a cycle, and a bookie who spotted it could offer them a run of bets that takes money off them every round. Expected utility theory is a theory of rational choice, so failing its axioms just is failing to be rational. Prospect theory repairs the situation by weighting small probabilities, which shows the choices themselves are fine and it was the economists' model that was broken."
