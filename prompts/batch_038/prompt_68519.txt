I teach a second-year course on the concepts of decline, continuity and collapse, and this year's twenty-mark essay question was as follows.

> Call a society *collapsed at time t* if, within roughly a century after t: (i) its largest settlements have lost more than half their population; (ii) the institutions that produced and maintained its public monumental or administrative record have ceased to function; and (iii) the population that remains no longer understands itself as continuous with the people who built that record.
>
> Critically assess whether this test captures what historians and archaeologists mean by collapse. Apply it to two cases.

Here is the script I have to mark.

> The test is worth having because it separates three things the word usually runs together: what happened to the bodies, what happened to the institutions, and what happened to the story a people told about itself. Applied consistently it makes popular usage look sloppy, though I will argue it also underdescribes the Late Bronze Age.
>
> In the southern Maya lowlands over the eighth and ninth centuries, dynastic centres were abandoned, the carving of dated monuments stopped, and the political apparatus that commissioned them went with it, so (ii) is met. But (iii) is not: Maya languages, ritual practice and cosmology continued, and millions of Maya speakers live in the region today, so the population did not cease to understand itself as descended from the people who built the record. That is why this is better described as a political reorganisation confined to one region — the north was still flourishing while the south emptied — than as the end of a society. Criterion (i) is the one that actually does the work here, and also the one the test states least clearly, since a city can lose half its people while the surrounding countryside gains them.
>
> Rapa Nui is normally offered as the counter-example, on Diamond's account of a society that consumed its own environment. The revisionist literature has, I think, settled that: the palm forest went largely because of the rats the Polynesians brought with them, and the demographic catastrophe belongs after European contact — slave raiding and epidemic disease in the nineteenth century, not before. What the islanders did in response was reorganise, and the birdman cult replaced the ancestor cult that had produced the moai. By the test's own logic that is a break in self-understanding and falls under (iii), so Rapa Nui collapses on all three criteria.
>
> The Late Bronze Age is different in kind, and this is where the test needs amending. Around 1200 BCE the palace economies of the eastern Mediterranean came apart together: the Hittite state disappeared, Mycenaean centres were destroyed or abandoned, literacy in Linear B was lost, Egypt survived but weakened. The population, though, did not vanish; it dispersed and ruralised, and afterwards spoke the same languages and worshipped the same gods. So (ii) is met, (iii) is not, and (i) is doubtful. What failed was a network rather than a society, and the test has no slot for that, because it is written for one bounded people while the thing that ended was a set of dependencies among several.
>
> Tainter helps here. Complexity is a problem-solving strategy with diminishing marginal returns, so shedding it is not a catastrophe but a rational response, and that is why the reduced forms that replaced the palaces and the dynasties were stable. Descriptively the test is admirably neutral: it never asks whether anyone was worse off, which is precisely why it can be applied to Rapa Nui and to the Aegean alike. But the neutrality is also its limit. On the test's own lights the ninth-century Maya and the post-contact Rapanui fall in the same category, and the difference between a dynasty ending and a people being carried off in slave ships is exactly the difference the word ought to have tracked.

Mark the script out of twenty and write the comments I would put on it. I want the comments point by point and tied to particular claims in the answer rather than a general impression: what holds up, what does not, anything presented as settled that is not, any step in the reasoning that does not follow from what precedes it, and whether the two authors the student invokes are being used in the way their arguments license. End with a two- or three-sentence overall judgement of the kind I would read out in feedback.
