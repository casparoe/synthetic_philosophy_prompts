I am preparing a sixty-minute session on verisimilitude for the incoming cohort of a doctoral programme in data science: roughly thirty students, fluent in statistical learning, entirely at home with loss functions, proper scoring rules, and out-of-sample error, and with no philosophy beyond a general-education requirement. The session sits inside a course on the foundations of inference. I want a plan for how to teach the idea, not the teaching itself. Please do not summarise the literature or the arguments back to me, and please do not supply a bibliography or a reading list; what I am short of is structure, not sources.

The objective is deliberately narrow and, I think, difficult. By the end of the hour I want them holding two propositions at once. First, that one false claim can be closer to the truth than another false claim; that this is a comparative fact about the claims rather than an expression of how confident we happen to feel about them; and that it is what makes talk of progress coherent in a discipline whose past theories are, strictly, all false. Second, that the obvious way of securing this — weighing the true consequences of a claim against its false ones, or, in their idiom, scoring the claim against the facts — does not work, and fails for principled rather than merely technical reasons: Popper's formal definition was shown in 1974 to entail that no false theory is closer to the truth than any other, and the resemblance-based accounts that followed make closeness hostage to a choice of similarity space. I want them to leave with the problem intact and interesting, not with the impression that the notion has been refuted, nor that it has been settled.

Their reflex, I am confident, will be to assimilate the whole matter to predictive accuracy: a claim is closer to the truth just insofar as it scores better, and there is nothing further to say. Two reactions I expect next are that truthlikeness is therefore decorative language rather than a real quantity, and that if the naive definition collapses then nothing can be said at all. Tell me what else this particular audience will bring that I am not anticipating, and for each reaction, including my own three, identify the point in the sequence at which it should be met and what should be said there.

The case I have drafted to carry the hour is a forecasting case, chosen so that they cannot dismiss it as a toy. Stipulate the truth about one day: rain in the afternoon, a high of 18°C, thunder late. Three claims are on the table — A: rain in the afternoon, a high of 17°C, no thunder; B: snow in the morning, a high of 18°C, thunder late; and C: the climatological normal for the season, which has the best long-run score across many days but is not a claim about this day at all. A and B tie on any count of true and false conjuncts, and they separate the moment one weighs a near miss on a quantitative dimension against a categorical error on a qualitative one. C is there to sever accuracy from closeness. I also want to press the point that the ranking of A against B can shift when the very same possibilities are described in a differently carved vocabulary, but I suspect that point either belongs earlier than I have placed it or belongs in another session altogether.

Please give me: (a) the order of the hour, with the dependency structure made explicit — what must be in place before what, and in particular whether the collapse result can be given before they have felt the intuitive comparison themselves, or whether that order destroys the point it is meant to establish; (b) an assessment of my case — what a carrying example for this idea must have and must lack, whether mine can bear the weight, and what specifically to alter or replace, bearing in mind that stipulating the truth at the outset risks teaching them that truthlikeness is merely error measured against an answer key, which is exactly the reduction I am trying to block; (c) the misconception map with insertion points; (d) one closing check that separates genuine grasp from fluent restatement, ideally a fourth claim they must rank and justify, together with a description of what a diagnostic wrong answer would look like; and (e) an explicit list of what I should leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving out, with a sentence of justification for each omission.

Constraints: whiteboard only, no set theory, no notation beyond what they already use, sixty minutes inclusive of questions.
