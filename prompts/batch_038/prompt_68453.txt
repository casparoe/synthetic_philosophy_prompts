Consider a familiar cluster of practices in American restaurants and city streets. An establishment posts menu prices that exclude any service charge and prints suggested percentages of 18, 20, and 22 on the receipt, yet treats the tip as a matter of the customer's discretion: someone who leaves nothing has violated no rule the restaurant can enforce, while staff and fellow diners will treat the omission as a genuine moral failure. For parties of eight or more, however, the same restaurant substitutes an automatic service charge that simply appears on the bill and is no longer up to the customer. Meanwhile, the walk there may involve jaywalking, which the city prohibits and fines; a visitor caught doing so is not excused by not having known the ordinance, whereas a visitor who fails to tip is very often excused by not having known the custom.

Reconstruct the theory of obligation this pattern presupposes. What must be true about the source of a convention's binding force for all three responses to hang together — in particular, for an identical sum to be optional at a table of two and compulsory at a table of eight, and for ignorance to excuse in one case but not the other? Distinguish what the practices strictly require from what they merely sit comfortably with, mark any point at which they are philosophically underdetermined by several rival accounts, and say what would have to change — in the restaurant's billing, in the ordinance, or in our treatment of visitors — if we replaced the presupposed theory with the view that conventional norms bind only those who have in some way taken them up.
