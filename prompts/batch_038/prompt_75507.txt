I have spent something like fifteen years reading and occasionally arguing in that corner of the internet that grew out of the Overcoming Bias days, and I have finally written up a short piece on the experience machine that I intend to post. Before I do, I would like a real critique rather than encouragement. The draft runs to about eleven hundred words and follows below. I have deliberately left in the place where I think the argument leaks, because pretending otherwise would waste your time.

The draft.

Nozick's machine has been doing a particular piece of work in this community for a long time: it is the standing refutation of wireheading, the reason we say that a life spent in a tank is not a life worth having however good it feels from the inside. The usual gloss on the argument is that we want contact with reality, that experience is not the only thing that matters, and therefore that hedonism about well-being is false. I want to suggest that the gloss is too blunt, that the machine is doing more interesting work than the gloss allows, and that once you see what that work is, the anti-hedonist conclusion it is usually taken to support turns out to be considerably weaker than advertised.

Start by prising apart two things that get run together under the phrase contact with reality. The first is informational: that your beliefs about the world are true, that you are not deceived about your situation. The second is what I will call responsiveness: that the world would have been different had you chosen differently, that there is a dense web of counterfactual dependence running outward from your decisions to the state of things. Its absence I will call insulation. Notice that a tank life can be scored perfectly on the first dimension and still be insulated, and that this is not a merely verbal point, because the two dimensions can come apart in a way that lets us test which one our intuitions are actually tracking.

The informational dimension, on its own, is easy to dismiss as the source of the intuition. Suppose you are told, before plugging in, exactly how the machine works, exactly what it will show you, and you retain that knowledge throughout. You are deceived about nothing. The repugnance does not budge, which suggests it was never primarily about being lied to.

Now consider the second dimension. Imagine the machine's operators are not merely honest but extremely good at their job, and imagine a dial that controls how faithfully the simulation tracks what would actually have happened. At its lowest setting the machine gives you unearned triumphs and frictionless romance. At its highest setting it is a near-perfect model of the world you left: every project meets exactly the resistance it would have met, every relationship develops at exactly the rate the real person would have permitted, every failure is a failure you earned. Set the dial to maximum and, by hypothesis, your experiences are counterfactually faithful to your choices. The insulation objection should now have nothing to bite on. And yet I find that the case still feels wrong, and I suspect you will agree.

So the residue of the intuition is not responsiveness either, at least not responsiveness in general. Here is where I think the interesting fact emerges. The dial can be turned to maximum only with respect to you. It cannot be turned to maximum with respect to anyone else, because at maximum there is no anyone else to be faithful to. The people in the faithful simulation are not the people whose welfare your real choices would have affected; they are exquisite models of them, and models cannot be helped or harmed. What survives at maximum faithfulness is a closed single-agent loop in which your choices reliably change your experiences and reliably change nothing else. The thing we cannot give up, then, is not truth and not even counterfactual structure as such. It is that our choices land on other people.

If that is right, the machine has been aimed at the wrong target. It is not an argument against hedonism about well-being; it is an argument against egoism about practical reason. A hedonist can concede every datum Nozick offers and maintain that the tank life is at least as good for the person living it as the real one, while insisting that the tank life is much worse simpliciter, because inside the tank there is no one for you to matter to. Read this way, the list of reasons to refuse the machine sorts itself. Wanting to do things rather than have the experience of doing them, and wanting to be a certain sort of person rather than an indeterminate blob, are both on my account descriptions of a life that makes a difference to something outside itself. Only the third item, the complaint about being confined to a man-made reality with no contact with anything deeper, is genuinely epistemic, and it is the weakest of the three, because as the faithful machine shows, the depth of the reality is doing no independent work.

This is where I think the argument leaks, and I want it pressed. If responsiveness to other people is valuable non-experientially, then I have conceded the structural point the anti-hedonist needs: something outside the stream of experience can make a life better. Once that concession is on the table, insisting that we still have hedonism, merely non-egoistic hedonism, may be a distinction without a difference for any purpose the machine argument is used for. The wirehead objection returns in force, since a wirehead harms no one, and my account seems committed to saying the wirehead is fine. I do not currently have a good response.

A related difficulty concerns Nozick's second reason. A character trait is, on a dispositional view, a bundle of counterfactuals: how you would act if threatened, if tempted, if asked for something costly. Inside the faithful machine those counterfactuals are true of the simulation. Are they therefore true of you, in which case the machine does after all let you be a certain sort of person, or are they hollow, in which case I have to explain what does the hollowing, since the counterfactuals are by stipulation exactly right?

Finally, the empirical material, which I think cuts both ways and which I am nervous about using. Both Joshua Greene and Felipe De Brigard have proposed inverting the presentation: you start inside, already decades in, and are asked whether to return to the real world. De Brigard's version also varied what subjects were told about that real life, telling one group nothing, another group that they were serving time in a maximum-security prison, and a third that they were wealthy artists in Monaco; reluctance to disconnect varied sharply across the groups. The standard reading finds this awkward, since if the intuition tracks the reality of one's life as such, the content of that life should be irrelevant. But it is awkward for me too, because the pattern suggests people's reluctance is about the quality of their own prospects, which is exactly the self-regarding explanation I am trying to displace.

So the bottom line I want to defend is narrow: keep the machine, downgrade the target. What it refutes is not the claim that well-being consists in experience, but the claim that well-being is all there is to care about.

That is the whole draft.

Now for what I want from you. Please lead with your verdict in a sentence or two, whether the thesis survives your reading of it, and then work through the rest; I would like to know where you landed before I have to reconstruct it. Tell me which single inference you consider weakest, and say what would have to be true for it to hold, since I would rather fix one load-bearing step than receive a general impression. Say plainly whether the informational-responsiveness distinction does any real work or is a redescription of the familiar process-versus-outcome distinction, and if it is a redescription, tell me what the coinage costs me rhetorically, because I have some suspicion that responsiveness, insulation, and the faithfulness dial are three new words for one old idea and that I should cut them. Tell me whether the faithful-machine case begs the question against the anti-hedonist, that is, whether stipulating perfect counterfactual tracking is just a disguised way of stipulating that it is real, in which case my central thought experiment never gets off the ground. On the paragraph where I flag the leak, I want a definite answer rather than sympathy: is the concession fatal, or is there a way to hold that non-experiential value attaches to relations with other agents without opening the door to the wirehead, and if so, sketch the move.
