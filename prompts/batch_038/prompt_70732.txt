You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive science with serious expertise in the 4E literature — extended, embodied, embedded, enacted cognition — and in the metaphysics of how systems get individuated. Please think carefully and work through the assignment in stages. I am not looking for a competent summary of a debate I know better than any summary could tell me; I am looking for a diagnosis of its structure.

**Context.** The extended mind debate has now run for more than a quarter century. On one side: Clark and Chalmers' parity principle and the Otto-and-Inga notebook case; the "glue-and-trust" conditions governing when an external resource counts (available when needed, typically invoked, its contents automatically endorsed); and the subsequent refinement on which the resource need not be functionally isomorphic to an inner process but need only complement it. On the other side: the charge of a coupling–constitution fallacy, pressed by Adams and Aizawa, that reliable two-way causal coupling no more makes a notebook part of a mind than it makes a piano part of the pianist; the demand for a prior mark of the cognitive, to be located in intrinsic and broadly neural properties; the bloat objection, that criteria this generous license counting search engines, cities, and eventually the whole information environment as parts of minds; and the recurring insistence that extended *cognition* and extended *mind* are distinct theses, the advertised one being stronger than anyone can defend. There have also been attempted repairs: a mutual-manipulability criterion adapted from the mechanistic account of constitutive relevance; the original authors' concession that phenomenal consciousness stays inside the skull even if cognition does not; and a general retreat from isomorphism to complementarity.

**The stasis.** Having taught this material for several years, my observation is that the arguments recur in the same order, that each side accuses the other of begging the question, and that nobody has ever been moved by an argument. What I want is an account of why.

**My hypotheses — offered as hypotheses to be tested, rejected, or amended, not as a menu.** (a) The dispute is verbal: the two camps attach different criteria to "cognitive," while agreeing on every empirical claim about coupling, reliability, and integration. (b) Two or three different questions are being answered under one heading — where cognition is located, what implements a cognitive process, and what units cognitive-scientific explanation should posit — so that the arguments, aimed at different questions, never actually engage. (c) A hidden empirical question is doing the work: the disagreement would in principle be settled by data about the actual coupling dynamics of notebook use, tool use, and transactive memory, and the stalemate reflects the absence of such data rather than conceptual difficulty. (d) The real disagreement is methodological and about evidence: one side treats pre-theoretical boundaries as data to be respected, the other treats them as targets of revision, and neither can persuade the other because they disagree about what could persuade. (e) The debate is a replay of the content-externalism arguments of the 1970s and 80s, with the vehicle/content distinction doing concealed work, and it stalls for the same underlying reason. (f) The question is ill-posed: it demands a binary answer about something that is graded, domain-relative, and interest-relative, so the impasse signals malformation rather than difficulty.

**A case I built for my seminar, and which I do not think appears in the literature in this form.** Call her M.: a historian who has spent twenty years building an annotated card catalogue of her own design. Within her domain she cannot reliably retrieve without it, and with it she outperforms every colleague working from biological memory. In an adjacent domain — general political history — the same retrieval habits are tuned to the catalogue's structure and actively misfire, so she is *worse* than colleagues there. Moreover her endorsement of the resource is not uniform: certain drawers were compiled by an assistant whose work she has since come to distrust, and she checks those cards against sources while accepting others without inspection. So trust here is graded and card-by-card, not global; and the catalogue's structure was itself shaped by her over two decades. Run each surviving diagnosis against this case. Which diagnosis predicts the pattern of disagreement the two camps would exhibit about it? Is there anything about the case that could break the tie between diagnoses, or does every diagnosis accommodate it equally — and if so, does that itself tell us something?
