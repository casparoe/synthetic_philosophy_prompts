I'm trying to get a feel for the localism vs globalism fight over scalar implicature, and a dialogue would help me way more than another summary article. Can you write one?

The two characters:

- A Gricean globalist. For them an implicature is the upshot of reasoning about the speaker: she said something weaker than she might have said, so ask why, and the answer comes out at the level of the whole sentence, once you've already got its truth-conditional content. Implicature is downstream of semantics, and it's downstream of a mind.
- A localist, from the Chierchia/Fox neighborhood. For them the same implicature-computing machinery can apply to sub-sentential pieces, and can apply blind — without waiting to work out what the whole sentence means or what the speaker is up to. So embedded strengthening happens as part of the compositional build.

Their battlefield: "You may have cake or ice cream." Hearers reliably come out thinking each dessert is separately permitted, and that's not in the literal content. Local derivation gets it pretty directly (exhaustify the disjuncts under the "may"); the globalist looks stuck deriving at most something like "the speaker doesn't know whether you may have just cake," which isn't what people hear. Let the localist press that hard, it's the best card in their hand.

But don't let the globalist be a punching bag. Give them the downward-entangling cases, where blind local computation looks flatly wrong — "John didn't eat some of the cookies" comes out true, on the localist's build, in a situation where John ate every last cookie, which is not how it sounds to anybody. Global reasoning about alternatives predicts no strengthening there, and that's the right call. So the localist owes some kind of repair or pruning story and should be made to say what it costs them. Also worth having them fight about cancellability: "you may have cake or ice cream — actually no, only cake" retracts free choice instantly, which sits awkwardly with an automatic blind default.

Then, about halfway in, throw in the curveball I actually care about: suppose the permission doesn't come from a person at all. It's a customer-service chatbot, or a recorded announcement, and both characters know there's nobody behind it with beliefs or intentions — it emitted a disjunction because of how it was built and trained. The free choice reading still shows up for hearers. Localist says: no mind to reason about, the inference happens anyway, that's a strike for blind computation. Globalist pushes back in good faith — either the intentions just live one level up, in whoever designed or trained the thing and made it hedge with "or" instead of committing; or machine output is a derivative case Grice already has resources for; or the hearer keeps running intention-attribution because they can't switch it off even when they know better. Let both land real hits here, and let each concede something concrete rather than agreeing to disagree.

Keep it tight — a dozen-ish exchanges, them answering each other's actual moves rather then trading speeches, and no throat-clearing or scene-setting narration. No bibliography and no list of papers to go read: I'm offline and couldn't check them anyway, so if you're not confident an attribution is right, describe the view instead of naming it.
