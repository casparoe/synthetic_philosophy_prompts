I am a novelist by trade, and for six years I have also sat on the stewardship committee of a regional land trust — seven volunteers who decide, parcel by parcel, what is to be done with ground we already own. I keep a private concordance of our minutes, because the work is not unlike my own: given what a character has already done, what would they credibly do next? Lately I cannot tell whether we have been consistent or merely fortunate, and next month we take up a case that will settle the question in one direction or the other. I have been asked to circulate a memo in advance of the meeting, and I would like you to draft it.

Two clauses govern us, adopted forty years apart and never reconciled. The founding clause commits the trust to the enduring health of the whole community of life upon our lands. A clause added in the 1990s commits us to seeing that no creature is left to suffer for what has been done to it by us. We have never defined *us*, and we have never said which clause yields when the two collide.

Our record to date, abbreviated but faithful:

1. **Approved** — the purchase and re-flooding of a wet meadow drained in the 1950s, restoring a sedge complex and an amphibian breeding site. Our ecologist estimated that the restored ground would produce roughly thirty times the number of juveniles the drained field now supports, nearly all of them dead within weeks of hatching. The minutes record that we exist to restore function, not to manage demographics.

2. **Approved with conditions** — when an irruption of native deer began eliminating a state-listed plant community on one of our preserves, we declined a lethal cull and funded exclosure fencing instead, on the stated ground that the irruption followed this region's own extirpation of the deer's predators, and that where two measures would equally secure the plant community we take the less destructive. The phrase has since hardened into a standing rule: the least destructive effective means.

3. **Approved** — two winters of lethal trapping that killed some two thousand invasive burrowing rodents on a dune system, each animal dying slowly, in order to save a nesting shorebird colony and the dune vegetation. Our ecologist's projection was that more individual animals would die on those dunes over the following twenty years *with* the removal than without it, since the rodents were already suppressing native small-mammal numbers and their own irruption was headed for a starvation crash. We approved regardless. A dissent was entered: we have decided that two thousand mammal deaths are a rounding error.

4. **Rejected** — a burn regime on a degraded grassland that would have favored a suite of non-native but wildlife-rich grasses, scored highest of any option before us on our own habitat-quality index, and shortened many animals' exposure to winter starvation. Rejected because the resulting community would have resembled nothing that grew there before settlement. The chair's phrase, entered in full: we are not in the business of building novel ecosystems.

5. **Approved** — three seasons of capture-and-treatment for a native amphibian population on a riverfront preserve, afflicted by a pathogen introduced decades earlier by the stocking program of this trust's own predecessor organization, together with a permanent treatment station whose chemical baths and handling traffic measurably damaged the riparian invertebrate community and sustained a population our ecologist judged non-viable in any event. The minute reads, in full: this one is ours. A dissent called it sentiment.

The case now before us. Last autumn we acquired, at a substantial discount, twenty-six hectares adjoining a chemical works that closed in 1983: a dry sand prairie remnant of a quality this county will not see again, and along its lower edge a population of some one hundred and twenty eastern box turtles. Arsenic and lead persist in the upper soil horizons from the works' operating decades. The turtles are not dying quickly. They accumulate metals across lives that run past fifty years; our ecologist documents shell lesions, immune suppression, and clutch failure, and projects the population functionally extinct within four decades. The prairie itself is unharmed and is the reason the parcel came to us at all. The discount reflected the contamination, and the deed by which we took title releases the former operator from further liability. Our acquisition memorandum — which I drafted — records that we took the site with the whole of its history.

Three measures are live:

**A.** Excavate and remove the contaminated horizon, then re-establish the prairie from nursery-propagated material. This closes the exposure route within a decade; it destroys the existing assemblage and its seed bank; our ecologist puts the chance of re-establishing the listed community at roughly half, and the cost at eleven times our annual stewardship budget.

**B.** Leave the parcel as it stands. The prairie persists as one of the finest in the region; the turtles decline across thirty to forty years of visible chronic illness.

**C.** Capture and permanently relocate the turtles to two other preserves, then let the prairie stand unmanaged with annual monitoring. Our ecologist regards translocation of adult box turtles as a sentence of elevated mortality — most of them dying within two seasons as they attempt to home — and warns of transferred disease and competition for two resident populations at the receiving sites.

A soil-amendment approach that would bind the metals in place was examined and set aside as unproven at this site; treat it as unavailable. The committee is split three ways: three members read the record as putting community integrity first and welfare second; two read the 1990s clause as reaching any harm traceable to human hands, whoever laid them; two hold that the discounted price and the release we signed made this harm ours by assumption.

What I need from you is the memo itself, in a form seven volunteers could act on Thursday: a clear recommendation among A, B, and C, or a defensible sequencing of them, with the reasons stated plainly enough that the members who disagree could answer them. It should identify which of our five precedents is the closest analog and say precisely where the analogy gives out. It should say what rule or rules the record as a whole actually supports — I suspect it is consistent with more than one — and what each of those rules predicts here, together with my best guess as to which one we have been running without knowing it. It should state what principle we adopt with each available choice, whether or not we mean to adopt one, and it should give the strongest honest case for the losing side rather than a straw man of it. Finally, propose one further case for a later meeting — a hypothetical parcel or an actual one — that would drive the surviving rules apart, so that we choose a principle deliberately instead of by drift. Reason from our own record and from the merits; I have no use for a survey of what others have written.
