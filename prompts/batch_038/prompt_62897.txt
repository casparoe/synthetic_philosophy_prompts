I coordinate family meetings for the ethics service at a mid-sized teaching hospital — my own training is in theology, not medicine — and I have been asked to write a short plain-language piece for our bereavement newsletter on how death is determined in the intensive care unit. As background I listened to an episode of a well-produced general-audience science podcast and transcribed the passage that seemed to me to do most of the argumentative work. It runs as follows: "Every American state now defines death in essentially the same way, and the statutory language reads: 'An individual who has sustained either (1) irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or (2) irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem, is dead.' Note what that second clause permits. A person can be declared dead while the heart is still beating, because a ventilator is doing the breathing; the warm skin and the rising chest are artifacts of the machine, not evidence of life. But this is where the concept starts to wobble, because death by neurological criteria is not really a biological discovery at all — it is a legal status conferred on a body that has stopped being a person. The criteria were first set out by a committee at Harvard in 1968, at a moment when transplant surgeons needed organs that only a beating heart could supply, and the test they settled on, the loss of all function of the entire brain, is best understood as a proxy for the thing that actually matters, which is the loss of consciousness. Once you see that, the dead donor rule — the principle that organs may be taken only from patients who are already dead — begins to look like a form of words held in place by nobody's willingness to say the quiet part aloud. Take donation after circulatory death: standard protocol is to wait roughly five minutes after the heart stops before declaring death and beginning the operation. Five minutes is nowhere near long enough for the cessation of circulation to be irreversible, since circulation is restored far later than that, and routinely so. Either these donors are not dead when the incision is made, or the word 'irreversible' in the statute has been quietly traded for something weaker, in which case the statute is not what is being applied. Neither horn is comfortable, and it is telling that medicine does not want to discuss this." Several of my colleagues found that bracing and honest; my own suspicion is that they have been persuaded by the confidence of the delivery rather than by the argument, but I cannot yet say where, or even whether, the argument gives way.

Would you work through the passage claim by claim and sort each claim into four buckets: what an intensivist or a scholar of the determination of death would accept without qualification; what is a fair popular simplification of something more complicated; what is misleading or simply wrong; and — the bucket I care about most — claims that are live scholarly disputes on which the speaker has taken a side without telling the listener. Please be specific about a few pressure points I noticed but could not evaluate: whether "irreversible" and "permanent" mean the same thing in this context, and what turns on the difference for the circulatory-death passage; whether "all functions of the entire brain" is best read as a claim about consciousness, and what a defender of the whole-brain standard would say to the proxy reading; whether the assertion that death by neurological criteria is a legal status rather than a biological fact describes the consensus or is itself one contested position among others; and whether the criteria used at a bedside today are in fact the ones the 1968 committee proposed. I would also like to know what the passage leaves out about how a determination is actually carried out in practice — the preconditions, the testing, the things a family sitting at a bedside would need to understand — since an omission can be as misleading as an error.
