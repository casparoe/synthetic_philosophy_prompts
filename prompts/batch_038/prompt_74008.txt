Three years ago my mother died, and in her last week she extracted two promises from me about a shoebox of letters she had kept from a relationship that predated my father: that I would never read them, and that I would burn them within a year of her death. I have kept the first and failed the second; the box is still in my hall closet. Two weeks ago my sister announced that she is writing a memoir containing a set of claims about that period of our mother's life — claims the letters would settle in an afternoon, in one direction or the other, and claims that will be published whether or not I say anything. My sister does not know the box exists. My mother cannot release me. What was, when I gave my word, a small private act of piety has become the difference between letting my sister publish something false about our mother and letting her publish something true, without telling her which.

I have been reading around the question of why a promise binds at all, and I keep landing on two accounts that appear to give opposite verdicts about my box. One holds that promising is the exercise of a power to bind oneself: in giving my word I created an obligation that owes nothing to anybody's expectations, and correspondingly only the person I gave it to can undo it. The other holds that there is no such power — that a promise is simply an unusually deliberate way of getting someone to expect something, and that the obligation is the ordinary one not to exploit expectations you engineered, so that its force tracks those expectations and fades where none remain.

Write me a dialogue between a defender of each view, staged in my kitchen with the box on the table, arguing about what I should do with it. Neither may be a strawman: the first must answer why words spoken by a dead woman should outweigh a living woman's interest in not being misled about her own mother, and the second must answer what is left of the intuition that only the promisee can release me, and why my sister's very real interest does not by itself generate an obligation where the expectation has been extinguished.

Have them work through, at minimum: whether my missing the one-year deadline dissolves the rest of the promise or merely adds a breach to a promise that still stands; whether a functional substitute — depositing the letters sealed with an archive under a seventy-five-year closure — counts as keeping the promise, breaking it, or breaking it well; whether what I owe is performance or performance-or-compensation; and whether anything is now owed to my mother, as distinct from merely concerning her. Each character should at some point state plainly what their own view has to concede.

No names, no books, no intellectual genealogy — I want the arguments, not the lineage. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words, and let it end unresolved, provided the final exchange makes clear what the disagreement now actually turns on. Then add a short note, out of character: which of the two you think handles my case better, how confident you are in that, and where either character has been made to stretch their view beyond what it can bear in order to keep the exchange going.

One further request. If you think my framing is faulty — if these two views do not genuinely disagree about this box once it is specified this precisely, or if the dialogue form will impose a liveliness the dispute does not have, or if some consideration I have not mentioned is in fact doing all the work here — tell me that first and directly, before writing anything. I would much rather be told that my question is malformed than be handed a polished exchange that quietly flatters it.
