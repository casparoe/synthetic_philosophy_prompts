I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and this season the column is running in pairs: an essay on one page, a commissioned piece of short fiction on the facing page, doing work the essay cannot. The essay I have just filed is about periodization — about whether the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution name anything that actually happened, or whether they are retrospective devices, invented long after the fact by people who needed somewhere to stand. I argue the second position and then spend a thousand words discovering how thoroughly I depend on the first: my own sentences keep treating the labels as containers with walls, and the walls keep doing the work. What I want from you is a short story, roughly 2,400 to 3,000 words, that dramatizes the bind instead of describing it — not a parable with a moral about how history is constructed, but a piece of fiction in which the construction is something characters have to do on a deadline, with consequences.

Here is the setting I would like you to use; develop it freely, but please do not replace it. A consortium of libraries, archives, and dataset publishers maintains a machine-readable metadata standard — call it the Chronology Interchange Format — and the standard requires every named period to resolve to a single integer start year and a single integer end year, because the systems downstream of it (search indexes, curriculum engines, corpus builders, cataloguing migrations) will not accept a range of scholarly opinion in a field typed as an integer. A small working group meets to fix the values for the European tripartite scheme before the next major release. Give me three or four members with genuinely opposed commitments: the schema maintainer, for whom an unresolvable period is worse than a mistaken one, since if the standard declines to supply a number the downstream systems will quietly invent one; a medievalist who knows that the three-part division was first spelled out by a fifteenth-century Florentine historian standing on the far side of the gap he was naming, and who can show that the same centuries look like rupture or like continuity depending entirely on the unit you track — ideas, instruments, institutions, diets, the transmission latency of a manuscript; and a chair who must sign the release note and holds a private view she has not yet stated aloud. I would also like you to build in a device of my own invention. Every five years the secretariat recomputes each period's boundaries from actual usage of the term across the corpus it indexes, and the boundaries move: the Renaissance begins later at each cycle, the Scientific Revolution begins earlier and ends later at once so that it swells, and the modern shortens at the front. One junior member has run the projections and found the intervals converging on the observers, such that within a few cycles the earliest period would begin after the consortium itself was founded. What that means is for you to decide, but let at least one character read it as evidence that the labels are descriptions of the describers, and let at least one read it as an artifact of a bad sampling frame.

On craft: the argument must live in scene — a roll-call vote, a hallway exchange, a projected plot nobody wants to look at, a changelog entry being wordsmithed at eleven at night — and not in narration; no character may lecture, and no passage may read like the essay I have already written. Work in at least one stretch of diegetic technical prose (a field definition, a version number, a comment string under a hard character limit that must justify a date in plain language for whoever reads the code in two centuries). Let the pragmatist be right at least once, and let the medievalist's objection cost somebody something concrete; I want the reader to feel the price of the fiction and the price of its absence within the same few pages. The standard also maintains a deprecation queue of terms scheduled for deletion, and I would like the story to notice that deleting a name does not delete a boundary: a migration script can map every instance of a deprecated label onto a neutrally worded replacement carrying exactly the same years, so that what began as an opinion hardens into infrastructure. End on an object rather than a summation — my own preference is the comment string. Two constraints, both firm. First, no citations, no bibliography, no reading list, and no real historians or works named as authorities; invented scholars and invented documents are welcome. Second, you cannot look anything up, so treat the few historical specifics I have supplied — the fifteenth-century formalization of the tripartite scheme, a Latin phrase for a middle season first attested in the 1460s, a nineteenth-century philosopher of science who applied the word revolution to the turn from trust in the mind's own powers to dependence on external observation — as seeds you may use, allude to, or ignore, and do not add attributions of your own. The story should work for a reader who has never encountered the debate and contains no in-jokes, and it should acknowledge somewhere, without turning into a manifesto, that a standard written in one part of the world with one integer per period flattens every other calendar it touches. Title it as you like; my editor will almost certainly change it.
