Our reading group on infinite ethics has assembled an argument whose conclusion nobody in it will accept, and I want a diagnosis before we meet. Fix the setting: a world containing countably infinitely many people, each with a finite welfare level, so that an outcome assigns a real number to every person.

(1) If outcome A is better than outcome B, then A's total welfare exceeds B's.
(2) The total welfare of an outcome is the sum of everyone's welfare, computed as the limit of the running totals along some listing of the people; where no such limit exists, the outcome has no total.
(3) Nothing in the world privileges one listing over another: the population has no natural order.
(4) Suppose welfare runs +1, -1/2, +1/3, -1/4, ... along listing L. The running totals converge, so this outcome has a total; but by the classical theorem on conditionally convergent series, some other listing of the very same people gives a different total, and yet another gives none.
(5) So one and the same outcome has many totals, or none; and since betterness is a matter of comparing totals, whether A is better than B shifts with the listing we happen to write down.
(6) Betterness cannot turn on a choice as arbitrary as that.
(7) Therefore there are no betterness facts among such outcomes.
(8) The obvious repair is a catch-up rule: A is better than B just in case, past some finite initial stretch, the running totals of A's advantage over B stay positive, so that the infinite background cancels. But apply the same theorem to the difference between the outcome of (4) and an outcome that leaves everyone at zero: along L the running totals of the advantage settle above zero and A wins, while along some other listing they settle below zero and B wins. The repair is order-dependent in exactly the way (6) forbids, and where the difference is neither conditionally convergent nor eventually one-signed it delivers no verdict at all.
(9) Hence in any world with infinitely many people, no act is better than any other.

I would rather reject the setting and deny that there can be infinitely many people, but the group says that dodges the point: the argument is meant to show that an infinitarian axiology is incoherent even where the infinity is merely possible. Which premise or step should go? Say why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives for me: calling such outcomes incomparable rather than saying there are no facts about them, imposing permutation-invariance as a condition on admissible sums, retreating to acts that differ only finitely from the status quo, and simply accepting the conclusion.
