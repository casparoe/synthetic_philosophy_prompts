I'm TA-ing an intro philosophy of probability section this term and I want to build a diagnostic instrument for the week we do Bertrand's paradox, because the discussion always collapses into people saying "well you just have to pick the right parameterisation" without noticing how much they've committed to by saying that. So: write me a decision tree that starts from a starting point every student in the room already accepts and ends at a named position about what the principle of indifference amounts to (or whether there's anything left of it). The starting commitment should be something like "the chord problem is fully stated — a circle, a chord chosen at random, the chord longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — and the three familiar constructions really do deliver 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4, with no arithmetic error anywhere." From there, every branch node has to be a question a student can answer cold, on the merits, without knowing which view it leads to. Nothing like "do you think probability is objective?" or "are you a frequentist?" — those just let people sort themselves by label. Concrete or semi-concrete forks only.

Here's what I need the tree to cover, and feel free to reorder or add nodes, but please don't drop these forks: (1) whether "a chord at random" is a complete description of a chance setup or an incomplete one that's waiting on a mechanism; (2) whether the three answers are rival answers to one question or correct answers to three different questions; (3) whether an invariance requirement — indifference to how you choose to measure or describe the thing — can ever be satisfied for all the parameters at once, or whether it sometimes has no solution and sometimes several; (4) whether the relevant probabilities are long-run, single-case physical, or degrees of belief that never need to match anything; (5) whether a rational state of uncertainty has to be expressible as one precise number, or whether a set of numbers can be the honest answer; (6) a practical fork — you're offered even odds at a table, someone actually picks a chord with a real physical procedure you can watch, do you bet, and at what price. The leaves I want to see reached (some may turn out to be the same leaf, and if so say so and merge them): the collective/long-run view on which an unspecified selection procedure simply has no probability; the single-case physical disposition view; the invariance-or-maximum-entropy objectivism that thinks the right symmetry argument picks out a unique distribution after all; the subjective Bayesian for whom indifference has no rational force whatsoever; the imprecise-probability leaf that keeps a whole set of priors and refuses to arbitrate; the dissolutionist who says the question was never well posed until a mechanism is named; the "it doesn't matter what you start with, the physics washes it out" leaf; and a pessimist leaf holding that the paradox is genuine and none of the standard fixes actually work. Each leaf also has to be checked against three further cases — the bottle whose wine-to-water ratio is between one-third and three, the factory whose cubes are uniform in side length or in surface area or in volume depending on how you describe them, and the random point on the sphere — and if the leaf's diagnosis of the chord problem breaks down or has to be amended on any of those, that goes in the worst-cost field rather than getting papered over.

Also flag honestly which pairs of leaves your tree can't really separate (I suspect the invariance objectivist and some versions of the subjective Bayesian are going to be hard to tell apart from inside), and mark every answer-combination the tree makes available that no actual position occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of why it's empty: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Format: first the tree as a numbered indented outline so I can read it aloud in section, then a single JSON object with three top-level keys. "nodes" is an array of {"id", "question", "options": [{"answer", "next"}]} where "next" is a node id or a leaf id. "leaves" is an array of {"id", "label", "verdict_on_the_three_answers" (one of them right / all wrong / three different questions / no fact of the matter / other, in one sentence), "core_commitment", "fate_of_indifference" (keep as-is, restrict, replace with a symmetry principle, replace with maximum entropy, abandon, or deny there's a determinate answer), "worst_cost", "breaks_on" (whichever of the three extra cases, or "none"), "indistinguishable_from" (array of leaf ids), "path" (the ordered node-answers that reach it)}. "vacant" is an array of {"combo", "why_empty" (incoherent | unattractive | unexplored), "note"}. Last thing: if any of your node questions secretly smuggles in a position — e.g. phrasing that only a dissolver would answer yes to — add a one-line note admitting it, since I'd rather know than have my students sort themselves by accident.
