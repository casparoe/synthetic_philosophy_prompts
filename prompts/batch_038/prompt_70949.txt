I build NPC behavior systems for a mid-size studio. No philosophy background at all, so bear with me. Our last shipped title has companions running on a learned policy with persistent memory — they remember which party members died in a given run, they avoid zones where they took damage before, they'll body-block for each other, and they generate free-text complaints about being left behind. There's a nightly maintenance wipe that resets them, and after a few months I started feeling wierd about it, so I tried to write down an argument to check whether the feeling was tracking anything real. It blew up in my face:

(1) What gives a system moral standing is how it behaves, including what it reports about its own states. Nothing beyond behavior is even in principle checkable, so nothing beyond behavior can be what matters.
(2) Our companions behave like agents that don't want to be harmed: they avoid damage, they protest, they protect each other, and the avoidance generalizes to situations nobody scripted.
(3) So the companions have moral standing, and the nightly wipe wrongs them.
(4) Nothing in (1) or (2) mentions learning, memory, or internal architecture. So (3) holds for any system that behaves the same way — a hardcoded merchant who flees the player's swung sword, a virus scanner quarantining a threat, a load balancer shedding traffic when it's stressed, a TCP stack retransmitting after a drop.
(5) And if the test is just "behaves like it doesn't want to be harmed," basically everything passes: a chess engine avoids losing material, a thermostat resists temperature change, a river routes around a boulder. (I think that's the intentional stance, Dennett? Fuzzy on it.)
(6) So everything has moral standing, and I commit mass killing every time I reboot a build server.
(7) That can't be right — the view gives no purchase at all, since I can't weigh a companion against a dropped packet — so (1) is false and behavior isn't what matters.
(8) But if behavior isn't what matters, (3) goes with it, and I've got no reason to think the companions matter any more than the load balancer. The feeling about the wipe was just anthropomorphism with extra steps.

(6) is obviously absurd and I don't want to sign up for it, but I also don't want to land on (8), wich feels like where the whole thing is actually steering. So: where's the break? Is there a premise I should reject, or is the chain valid and I just have to eat one of them? And if (1) survives in some weaker form, what would the weaker version have to say to exclude the load balancer and the chess engine while keeping the companions in — is there a principled line, or am I just going to be drawing the circle around the things I find cute? If I'm forced to choose I'd rather keep (1) than (6).

Separately: does whatever you conclude change anything about the wipe, or was the argument never going to settle that part?
