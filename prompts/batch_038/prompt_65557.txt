My housemate and I have just spent the better part of a Sunday afternoon, and a distressing amount of the evening, quarrelling about a position I am supposed to be interviewing someone about in three weeks' time. I convene our department's graduate podcast: forty-five unedited minutes in a basement seminar room, no website, distributed largely by word of mouth. Our next guest is a philosopher who defends what I shall call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and her position, as she states it, has three commitments. First, suffering above a certain intensity threshold is bad in a different kind of way from all suffering below it: a single instance of threshold-crossing agony makes a world worse than any world containing no such instance, however many lives that world contains, however long they last, and however much pleasure they hold. Second, below the threshold, pains and pleasures sit on one common scale and aggregate in the ordinary way, with pains weighted more heavily per unit than pleasures. Third, the discontinuity at the threshold is a genuine break of kind rather than a very large exchange rate in disguise; she maintains that no finite quantity of sub-threshold badness, and no quantity of goodness whatever, sums to one instance of the supra-threshold kind.

My housemate is a health economist with little patience for any of this, and he spent the afternoon arguing that the third commitment is either empty or incoherent. Empty, because any view that ranks worlds in this order can be rewritten as a cardinal view with a sufficiently steep weighting function, so that the talk of incommensurability does no explanatory work and merely dramatizes a very big number. Incoherent, because the moment she says what the threshold consists in — pain that cannot be borne, the defeat of agency, the point past which a subject would surrender any number of remaining years to escape it — she has characterized the threshold in terms of a willingness to exchange, which is precisely the ratio she claims is undefined. I could not answer either charge cleanly, and I should not like to lose the argument twice, once at the kitchen table and once on the microphone.

So I would like you to write the question list I should bring into the room. Ten questions, sequenced so that the pressure increases rather than merely accumulates, each accompanied by the reply I ought to expect from someone of her fluency — she has fielded the spectrum objections and the charge that her principles license painless extinction many times and will not be rattled by either — stated in a sentence or two, and then a follow-up constructed to meet that particular reply rather than the objection in the abstract. Where her likely reply forks, as between biting the bullet and restricting the claim, tell me which branch you have assumed and give me the follow-up for the other branch as well. Among the ten I want, at minimum: a question on whether the threshold can be vague without the view's collapsing into the cardinalism it repudiates; a question on whether any operationalization of the threshold — self-report, the defeat of agency, a stated willingness to trade years of life — can avoid defining it by an exchange rate; a question on whether ten thousand lives each falling just below the threshold can together amount to something that crosses it, and if they can, whether the threshold is about intensity at all; a question on how two worlds that both contain threshold-crossing suffering are to be compared, and in particular whether a minute of agony and a decade of it stand in any determinate relation on her account; a question on whether the threshold is fixed by intensity alone or by the psychological capacities of the sufferer, and what follows for non-human animals, whose treatment in intensive confinement plausibly exceeds any intensity threshold one cares to name; a question on whether the reading of her view that treats the presence of any threshold-crossing suffering as decisive and the reading that treats such suffering as merely lexically prior in the aggregation yield different verdicts, and which of the two she is committed to; and a question that confronts her with a clinical case — terminal sedation that relieves agony at the price of every remaining pleasure, say, or the allocation of a scarce analgesic — in which her second commitment's common scale and her third commitment's denial of one appear to give opposite advice. Nothing about her intellectual biography, her teachers, or what she is reading at the moment, please; we have no reading list to point listeners toward and I would rather spend the time on the substance. The final question should present a choice she cannot decline: to say in one sentence which of the two formalizations of her own view she endorses, and to accept the consequence that the other branch would have allowed her to avoid.
