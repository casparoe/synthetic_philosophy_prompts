ok so I'm 17 and I'm in my school's philosophy club, and for the last few weeks we've been going back and forth on whether dying is actually bad for the person who dies. My teacher handed out like two paragraphs of Epicurus where he says the whole thing is nothing to us, because while we're here death isn't, and when death is here we aren't, so there's never anyone it can be bad for. Half of us thought that was genuinely clever and half thought it was obviously a trick but couldn't say what the trick was, which was annoying.

Here's the pattern I kept noticing tho, and it's the thing I can't find a word for. Every single time one of us came up with a candidate bad thing about being dead — you'll miss what happens to your friends, your projects go unfinished, you never find out how the thing you cared about turns out — somebody defending Epicurus said basically the same shape of thing every time: that's not a badness that belongs to your being dead, it belongs to your being alive, it's the dread or the wanting or the losing that happens now, while there's still a you around to have it. So nothing ever gets denied. The badness just gets moved to a date where there's a subject standing there to receive it. It happened like four times in one meeting with four completely different examples, and each time it felt like the same move. And when I tried to push back and say "fine but you've conceded there's a loss, you've just filed the paperwork under a different date," the answer was that filing it under a different date changes what kind of thing the loss is, and then we got stuck and started talking about whether it's the same as the symmetry thing (I've also seen "deprivation account" and "the timing problem" floating around and I keep mixing those two up, so idk if either is what I mean).

So my actual question: is there a name for this move, where a proposed harm isn't rejected but relocated onto whichever state does have a subject in it? What are the closest established concepts, how is what I'm describing different from each of them, and which one fits best — because I suspect it's not the timing problem exactly, that sounds like a question rather than a reply someone makes. And if nobody's named it, can you define it sharply enough that I can tell it apart from (a) just conceding the point and (b) moving the goalposts, since that's what two people in my club will say it is, and I don't think it's either, because the relocation happens out in the open instead of sneakily. I'd also want to know where it stops working — like, is there a kind of harm that can't be relocated this way no matter how hard you try? A bare name isn't going to help me, I want to be able to walk into next week's meeting and say exactly what the move is, what it isn't, and what would count as a case that breaks it.
