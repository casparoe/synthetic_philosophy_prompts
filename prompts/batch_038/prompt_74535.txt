I have been trying to settle what I think about the necessity of origin for persons — specifically, whether I could have come from a different sperm and egg — not by weighing arguments but by listing the cases I have firm feelings about and then seeing whether any single principle explains all of them. I would like you to do the reflective-equilibrium work: propose candidate principles, test them against my verdicts, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts have to be surrendered.

Here are my verdicts, in roughly the order in which I arrived at them.

1. Same egg, same sperm, but conception occurs a day later in the cycle than it actually did. Me. The timing of the fertilization event strikes me as wholly irrelevant.
2. Same egg, same sperm, but fertilization occurs in a dish and the embryo is then transferred into my mother, who carries me to term. Me.
3. Same egg, same sperm, but the embryo is cryopreserved for twenty years and then transferred into a gestational surrogate with no genetic connection to me. Me — though less comfortably than in case 2. I want to say the womb is no part of my origin at all, and yet I notice that I flinch.
4. Same egg from my mother, but a different sperm from my father, one of the millions of others. Not me: a possible sibling. This verdict is very firm.
5. Same sperm from my father, but a different egg from my mother. Also not me, and just as firm — and I cannot say why these two should feel exactly equally firm rather than one feeling worse than the other.
6. A transfer mix-up: an embryo formed from my parents' gametes is implanted in and carried by a different woman, who raises the child. Me. So gestation and rearing are out, and the gametes are in.
7. A zygote assembled in the lab from newly synthesized parts so that its genome is identical, base for base, to the genome of the zygote I came from, implanted in my mother. Me, I think. I hold this weakly, but I hold it.
8. A clone: a cell taken from my adult body, reprogrammed to an embryonic state, implanted in my mother, carried to term, and born with a genome identical to mine. Not me. I feel this considerably more strongly than I feel 7, which is embarrassing, since from where I stand the two cases look nearly the same.
9. Mitochondrial replacement: my parents' egg and sperm, except that the egg's nucleus was transferred into a donor egg from which the nucleus had been removed, so that the mitochondria — and nothing else in the resulting person — come from a third woman. Me. And yet case 4 seems to tell me that genetic material contributed by someone other than my parents yields a sibling rather than me. I do not see how both can be right.
10. My monozygotic twin and I: the zygote we came from split in two. I believe there is a determinate fact about which of the two resulting cell masses was me, even though nothing about them, or about the split itself, distinguishes them. I would rather not believe this, since it appears to commit me to bare individuality attaching to one of two qualitatively identical things; but I cannot bring myself to say instead that the question has no answer.
11. If the sperm had been a chimpanzee's, there would have been no me at all — not a deformed me, not a me of a different shape, but no candidate for me whatsoever.

My tentative principle, which I want tested and repaired if possible, is that what is essential is not the gametes but the genome: a person is essentially whoever begins as a diploid cell bearing the genome that the zygote I came from bore. It delivers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, and 11 in a single stroke, and it explains why I am indifferent to timing, to location, and to the womb. So far as I can tell it fails only on 8, and on 10 it is simply silent.

What I would like from you:

(a) Two or three candidate principles, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, including an explicit conditional saying what makes some merely possible person identical to me. For each principle, identify which of my eleven verdicts each of its clauses exists to deliver.

(b) An honest ledger. For each principle, say which of my verdicts it reproduces, which it reverses, and which it leaves undefined. I would much rather be told that 7 and 8 cannot both be kept than be handed a principle that quietly renders both true.

(c) Your verdicts, with reasons, on three cases I have not listed: an embryo produced by fusing my zygote with another couple's zygote, where the overwhelming majority of the resulting person's cells descend from mine; a child conceived from sperm my father banked before his death and an egg retrieved from my mother after it, born three years after I actually was; and a series of worlds in which progressively larger fractions of my egg's cytoplasm — one percent, then ten, then half, then ninety-nine — are replaced by a donor's. For the last, tell me whether your principle supplies a cutoff, and whether that cutoff is something the principle discovers or something I am permitted to stipulate.

(d) One further case of your own devising that you judge my set of verdicts is most likely to mishandle, together with what your recommended principle implies about it.

(e) A recommendation. Which principle should I adopt, and what does it cost me? State explicitly whether the item whose origin turns out to be essential is the pair of gametes, the genome, the fertilization event, or the entire developmental process that began it — and why each of the others is the wrong thing to quantify over.

(f) Whether the principle you recommend is special to persons or is the same principle that settles whether a table could have been made from a different piece of wood. If it is general, say what it predicts for a ship whose planks are replaced one at a time, and whether that prediction counts in its favor or against it.

Please do not give me a survey of the literature or of who has defended what. Assume that I already know the standard doctrine that one could not have originated from different gametes, and the standard counterpart-theoretic response to it. What I do not know is what to make of my own list.
