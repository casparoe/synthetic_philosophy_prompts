ok so I just spent three hours arguing with a friend (a type designer, which makes it worse because he thinks that entitles him to win) about what makes a letterform the letter it is, and I need a third party. You're the right one for this: play the part of a philosopher of cognitive science who also genuinely cares about typography and has thought hard about category formation — the Hofstadter Letter Spirit territory, where a program gets shown a few letters drawn in some style and then has to render the rest of the alphabet in that same spirit. Brainstorm out loud for a bit before you commit to anything, I'd much rather watch you thrash than get a tidy answer immediately.

Here are my verdicts, gut-level, roughly in the order I listed them for him:

1. A double-storey 'a', like in most book text: obviously an a.
2. A single-storey 'a' — Futura, handwriting, the one kids learn first: also obviously an a. And yet it shares maybe one stroke with #1. This is the one that keeps me up.
3. An uppercase A: same letter, different form. I'd die on this hill. Even though it shares even less with #1 than #2 does.
4. A Cyrillic а set in the very same typeface: NOT the letter a. It's a different letter that happens to look identical. He says this is squeamishness about history, that a shape-identical glyph in the same font just IS the same letter. We were stuck on this for like an hour.
5. An 'a' whose bowl doesn't quite close, tiny gap where the strokes should meet: still an a. Reads fine, feels slightly broken.
6. The same 'a' with the counter filled in solid, so it's a blob with a stem sticking out: not an a anymore.
7. An 'a' stretched four times as wide: still an a. Squeezed to a third of its normal width: still an a.
8. An 'a' turned upside down: not an a. But an 'n' upside down IS a 'u' and a 'u' upside down is an 'n'. So rotation sometimes destroys letterhood and sometimes hands it to a neighbour, and I cannot make that consistent.
9. An 'æ': half an a, sort of, but I'd say it's its own letter descended from an a plus an e, not itself an a.
10. '@': not an a, even though it seems to have grown out of one. He agrees with the verdict but says my reasoning has to be wrong, because '@' looks more like an 'a' than Futura's does.
11. A six-year-old's 'a' drawn as a circle with a stick leaning on it: yes, an a, and I'll defend that even though it breaks basically every structural rule you could write down.
12. What I'll call the graft test: if I show you a style in which you've only seen a, c, e and s, and you can produce an o that a reader says belongs to the family, then whatever you did counts as having gotten the letter o. Failing the graft test means you don't have the category, even if every individual glyph you draw passes 1 through 11.

My own tentative principle, which he wrecked: a glyph is an 'a' iff a competent reader of the relevant script and style would read it as 'a'. It handles 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11 nicely. It dies on 4 — a competent reader of Cyrillic reads it as the Cyrillic a, and honestly a competent Latin reader looking at that font probably would too — and it turns letterhood into a fact about readers rather than about forms, which I don't want. I want #6 to be a fact about the glyph. And #8 looks like a fact about the internal symmetries of the alphabet, not about anybody's reading.
