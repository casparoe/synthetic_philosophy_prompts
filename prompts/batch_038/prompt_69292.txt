The Browning–Goldhagen exchange over the perpetrators of the Holocaust is usually read as a dispute about Germans, and specifically about one unit: Reserve Police Battalion 101. I want to test whether the two explanatory frameworks can travel, by running them on an episode they were never built for — the massacre at My Lai.

The facts, so that we can agree on them. On 16 March 1968, Company C of the 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry, Americal Division, entered the hamlets of Son My in Quang Ngai Province and killed somewhere between roughly 350 and 500 Vietnamese civilians, mostly women, children, and old men. The unit took no fire, recovered essentially no weapons, and encountered no enemy soldiers. It had been in country only about three months, had already lost men to mines and booby traps, and had not yet fought a set-piece engagement. Its commander, Captain Ernest Medina, briefed the men the night before on a village said to be a Viet Cong stronghold; the content of that order — whether it licensed killing everyone present or only the enemy — was fiercely disputed afterward. Second Lieutenant William Calley led the platoon that did most of the killing, defended himself on the ground that he had been ordered to do it, and was convicted of murder in 1971; Medina was acquitted; nobody above them was convicted. A helicopter crew under Hugh Thompson landed between American soldiers and Vietnamese civilians, ordered its gunners to fire on their own countrymen if they fired on the villagers, and got about a dozen people out. The episode was suppressed until a former soldier, Ronald Ridenhour, wrote letters about it in 1969 and Seymour Hersh published the story.

First, map the two frameworks onto the episode explicitly and separately. Under Browning's situational account — unformed men with no prior ideological commitment, a task presented by an authority as distasteful but unavoidable, an offer to step aside that almost nobody takes, small-group loyalty, routinization and a division of labour that spreads the killing out, alcohol, and the escalation that comes from doing it again and again — which mechanisms are actually present at My Lai, which are absent, and which are present in distorted form? These were not middle-aged Hamburg policemen but young draftees with no antecedent formation at all; there was no standing offer to step out, though there were refusals; the killing was compressed into a single morning rather than distributed across months of operations. Under Goldhagen's account — perpetrators moved by an eliminationist cognitive model of the victim group, killing willingly and sometimes with relish, needing little in the way of coercion — what would have to be true of American military culture in Vietnam for the framework to apply: the racial epithets, the body-count metrics, the free-fire zones, the statistical and moral erasure of Vietnamese civilian life? Is that culture analogous to eliminationist antisemitism in the respects that do the explanatory work, or only superficially similar?

Second, and this is the part I care about most: the base rates press against both accounts, in opposite directions. If the situation at My Lai — ambiguous orders, a bloodied but unengaged unit, an indistinguishable population, no supervision, dehumanizing idiom — was ubiquitous in that theatre, why was there one My Lai? And if the racial and bureaucratic culture was shared by essentially every American soldier in Vietnam, why did only part of one company kill hundreds of unarmed people, while an aircrew from the same army intervened at some risk of court-martial? Say whether either framework has resources for this asymmetry, or whether each merely relocates the work: Browning into a hunt for the situating trigger that made this unit anomalous, Goldhagen into the claim that willingness was general but only here permitted to express itself.

Third, turn the episode back on the debate. The courtroom arguments at My Lai — Calley's obedience defence, the prosecution's insistence on individual moral agency, the holding that a manifestly unlawful order is no defence — are a kind of applied, adjudicated version of the Browning–Goldhagen dispute, settled under rules of evidence rather than historiography. Does that convergence support the idea that there is a general question here, or does it show that the two accounts answer different questions altogether? And what is the status of the reporters and the interveners: do Ridenhour, Thompson, and the men who refused belong to a third factor — character, or something like it — that neither position can accommodate, or are they explicable within a situational account after all? I am inclined to think the interveners are the hardest data in the case, and I would like to be shown that I am wrong about that.

Finally, say what the transplant shows about the scope of the original debate: was it ever a general theory of perpetration, or a pair of rival readings of one national and one institutional setting, such that carrying it to Vietnam is a category mistake? If it is a category mistake, say so plainly and tell me what framework ought to be used on My Lai instead.

Two further requests. Distinguish throughout between what the My Lai evidence supports and what a framework would have to assume; do not force the case to speak where it is silent, and identify where it is silent. And close with a statement of your own exposure: which of your conclusions you would abandon if I produced one particular fact — an order, a personnel record, an after-action report, a documented pattern of refusals across comparable units — and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail. If you conclude that one framework clearly wins, name the single consideration that would most plausibly reverse that verdict.
