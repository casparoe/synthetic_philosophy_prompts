I'm about two thirds of the way through a novel and I've got a character problem that I'm pretty sure is actually a philosophy problem, so I want you to think with me rather than give me craft tips.

My protagonist runs a small rural clinic. Over the course of the book she does a run of things everyone around her calls saintly: stays through the night for patients who aren't on her list, drives two hours in bad weather to collect a medicine, turns down a fellowship that would have got her out, and about halfway through donates a kidney to a man she's met twice. My editor's note is that she's turning into a plaster saint and the book is getting preachy. He's right, and I can't fix it with surface stuff — more drinking, a foul temper, a neglected kid — because the real problem is that I haven't decided which idea about morality I'm writing her under, and it shows in the sentences.

So here's what I want. Compare supererogation — the category of acts that are praiseworthy but not required, going beyond the call of duty — with the Kantian idea of wide or imperfect duty, where you're genuinely obliged to adopt certain ends (help others, develop yourself) but have real latitude about when, how much, and toward whom, so that you can't be pinned to any particular act yet can be faulted for never doing any. Two frameworks that look like they describe my doctor the same way and don't.

Be substantive about it, not a glossary entry. I specifically want:

- what each view makes the moral status of the kidney. One of them has to say she went above and beyond; the other has to say she did something good that she also could not have done and been fine. Show me how each argues for its verdict and what it has to swallow to get there.
- what each does to her inner life. Under one she gets to feel she's done something special; under the other the whole economy of credit and admiration is supposed to evaporate. Does the wide-duty agent feel relieved, or does she feel permanently in arrears? Which one produces guilt and which produces pride, and why.
- how each handles the case where doing the big good thing crowds out a smaller thing she actually owed — she skips three days of clinic to be with the recipient's family and a regular patient goes unmonitored. Which framework can say something went wrong, and which can only shrug that she traded one good for a better good.
- what each implies for the people around her. Can her colleagues resent her? Can a reader think she's showing them up, and does that reaction make sense on one view and not the other?

Then take a side. Tell me which of the two I should write her under if I want the reader's verdict on her to stay complicated instead of collapsing into admiration, and make it concrete: give me four or five lines of her interior monologue for the same moment — the first hour after the donation, in the recovery room — written once under each framework. Point out which words in each version betray which theory, because that's the part I can't see myself. And flag the places where the two frameworks actually agree so I don't overplay the contrast for drama.

One more thing: I know there's a consequentialist line on which nothing is supererogatory at all, because there's always a further good you could have done, so duty swallows the whole category. Say briefly where that fits and whether my doctor would even register as good on it — but keep the main event as the two-way comparison.

Don't send me to books or build me a reading list, I'm not going to read any of it this month. Just argue it out with me in the reply.
