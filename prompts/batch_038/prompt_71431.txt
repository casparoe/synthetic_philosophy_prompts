I am the animal welfare lead at a mid-size aquaculture group: two Atlantic salmon sea-cage sites in a northern fjord system, plus a penaeid shrimp operation with its own broodstock unit and hatchery and contract grow-out ponds, so a single year of decisions touches roughly two million fish and something on the order of a hundred million crustaceans. I came to the job as a fish veterinarian, not an ethicist, and proposals now reach me four to six times a year from quite different directions — a capital request for an in-water electrical stunner at harvest, a campaign from an animal-protection organisation demanding we end unilateral eyestalk ablation in broodstock within twelve months, an offer of co-funding from a research body to help validate a usable welfare indicator for our shrimp species, a tightening of a retail customer's supplier code, a consultant's enrichment idea, a proposal to cut stocking density in grow-out ponds. Requests run from about €15,000 to €1.2m, and anything above €250,000 goes to a board that will read one page and nothing else. At the moment our COO ranks these by dividing an estimate of animal-minutes of suffering avoided by the euro cost; everyone in the building knows this is indefensible where the animals are crustaceans and we have no validated measure of anything, but it produces a single number, and nobody has offered a better instrument.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run, and specifically one that survives contact with the shrimp side of the business. Build it out of whatever farmed-animal welfare science and animal ethics genuinely establish, rather than out of general good sense: the difference between outcome measures that describe the animal's own state and input or management measures that merely describe what we provide; the framing that separates what an animal is given from how it is doing; the arithmetic of severity, duration and number of animals, and the places where that arithmetic is known to break down; the precautionary treatment of a species whose sentience is not settled but whose capacities are not negligible either; and the distinction between a harm we inflict and a harm we fail to prevent. Please be explicit about which of these distinctions you keep intact, which you flatten to make the procedure runnable, and which you drop altogether, with a reason in each case — I want to know what my team will no longer be able to think once they are using your instrument. Lay the procedure out as an ordered set of gates: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, where an insufficient answer stops the proposal with a no rather than sending it back for more work, and where the procedure must stop and hand the question to a named human being because it is a question of values rather than facts. Tell me what to do when no number exists and cannot honestly be manufactured, without letting the scoring scheme invent one. The constraints are real: half a day per proposal, two people, one of whom is a site manager with a biology degree and no patience for philosophy, and an output that has to serve both as a one-page board recommendation and as a record that will stand up to a certification auditor and to the organisation that is currently campaigning against us.

Then run it on three proposals and show your work at every gate. First, buying the in-water electrical stunner to replace manual percussive knockout at harvest, where the science is good, the measure is observable, and the cost is high. Second, ending eyestalk ablation within twelve months, where our own modelling suggests we would need roughly a third more broodstock animals to hold postlarvae output, or would have to buy postlarvae from third-party hatcheries that do ablate — so tell me whether the procedure counts displaced and outsourced animals, how, and whether it is prepared to return a verdict I will not like. Third, the co-funded indicator-validation study, which harms no animal and helps none directly, and where the interesting question is whether measurement counts as a welfare intervention at all and how it should be scored against interventions that do something. At least one of these three should come out against my own sympathies; if all three come out the way I expect them to, say so and say why that is suspicious. Finish with a bias audit: name the ways the procedure will systematically favour the measurable over the unmeasurable, the acute over the chronic, the fish over the shrimp, our own sites over the supply chain, and the intervention with a campaign behind it over the one nobody is watching; say what can be done about each bias inside the procedure, and say plainly where nothing can. Close with a list of the questions the procedure cannot settle and must send to a person, and a note on how often it should be re-run and on what would tell me it is failing.
