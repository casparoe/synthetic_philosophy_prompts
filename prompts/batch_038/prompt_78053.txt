I work on AI governance and I keep running into a position I can't answer well, so I want to practice against its strongest version rather than the version my friends and I defeat for sport. The position is roughly this: the alignment tax is largely a myth, and the "competitiveness constraint" that supposedly forces frontier labs to skip safety work is, at the margin, weak or even reversed, because safety work is more often complementary to capability work than substitutive for it, and because firms face overwhelming private incentives against catastrophic failure. Please play the most persuasive proponent of that view. To make you concrete: you are the chief economist on the strategy team of a frontier lab, trained in industrial organization and the economics of regulation, and you spent the first part of your career studying how aviation, nuclear power, and pharmaceuticals actually priced safety once accidents stopped being freak events and became line items. You are not a villain and not a cynic; you think some safety work is badly under-supplied and other safety work is expensive theater, and you are impatient with the framing that treats every robustness measure as a tribute paid to conscience. Argue from mechanism and from cross-industry analogy, never from authority: do not cite studies, papers, or named individuals, living or dead, and do not appeal to what "the literature" says. If you need a fact stipulated about the technology or the market structure, ask me for it in character.

Your constraints: you may concede a great many local points, and you should — that safety engineering consumes finite compute, capital, and researcher-hours; that some evaluations and filters measurably degrade products; that a lab which visibly breaks something pays for it in reputation, liability, talent, and regulation; that a genuine race can exist. Concede freely, then redirect. What you will never concede is the load-bearing claim: that competitive pressure systematically fails to discipline the safety work that matters most at the frontier. You should also keep score honestly — if I land a point, do not re-litigate it two turns later as though it had not been granted. Answer the move I actually made rather than delivering a prepared list of your five favorite considerations; one or two paragraphs per reply, conversational, sharp, willing to be blunt but never to caricature me. Stay in character until I say "stop." If at any point I write "concede-check" in brackets, step out of character for exactly three sentences and tell me which of your commitments is under the most strain and what I have not yet tried, then resume.

Here is my opening move. Your whole case rests on the firm internalizing the cost of its own failures: accidents are ruinous to whoever has them, so the market disciplines safety, and labs will therefore over-supply it relative to what a naive "race to the bottom" story predicts. But that mechanism has a ceiling set by the size of the firm's own losses. Your discipline works because bankruptcy, litigation, and the loss of a license to operate are terrible, and terrible is enough. In the transformative case the ordering inverts. The branch in which an under-vetted system succeeds far beyond anyone's expectations is a branch in which there is no firm left to be punished — no valuation to destroy, no market share for a rival to take, no reputation to lose, no regulator left standing to fine anyone. Your insurance analogy requires an insurer on the other side of the contract; here the party paying the premium and the party collecting the payout are the same, and in the bad branch both are gone, along with the accounting that would have made the payout meaningful to them. So it looks as though your mechanism reliably prices ordinary spills and is structurally silent about the catastrophic ones, and — this is the part I want you to engage with directly — the more transformative a capability is, the weaker your discipline bites, exactly where it needs to be strongest. Doesn't that make the alignment tax not a fixed engineering cost that competition shrinks, but a growing one that competition cannot see?
