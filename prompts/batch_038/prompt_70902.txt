I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy, and in three weeks I am running a fifty-minute discussion section — whiteboard only, no slides — attached to our second-year graduate core sequence in decision theory. There will be about a dozen students: nine from philosophy, who have completed the first-year core and so have von Neumann–Morgenstern and Savage behind them; two from economics, who met rank-dependent utility models in their graduate micro sequence; and one from statistics. The topic is Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory, and my brief is to present it as a normative proposal rather than as a piece of behavioral description.

Two constraints shape the problem. First, the seminar instructor worked through the Allais pair in week two, so every student in the room has seen it, solved it, and filed it away as the paradox where people violate independence. I cannot build the section on it; if I do, they will pattern-match instead of thinking. Second, they have all been taught the orthodox line that risk aversion is captured by a concave utility function, and they believe it. I am therefore not introducing an idea into empty space: I have to get them to see that a concept they already possess has been doing a different job than they supposed.

I do not want an explanation of risk-weighted expected utility. I can write down the rank-weighted representation and I know where the convexity of the risk function does its work. What I want is a plan for the explanation.

1. The dependency structure. Give me the order in which the pieces must go in and what forces that order. In particular: must the argument that concavity of utility cannot account for ordinary small-stakes risk aversion be established *before* the risk function is introduced, since otherwise the risk function looks like a redundant second dial? Can the formal representation be stated at all inside fifty minutes without becoming the object of the section rather than a tool for it? Does "rank-dependent" have to be defined before or after the normative claim it is meant to serve? And where, if anywhere, does the small-world/grand-world distinction need to appear?

2. The carrying example. I have two candidates and I trust neither.

Candidate A, meant to motivate. A one-shot choice between a certain $900,000 and a 0.9 chance of $1,050,000 with a 0.1 chance of nothing; and then the same choice offered as one hundred independent draws, with the certain option scaled to $90,000,000. My claim is that an agent with a linear utility function and a convex risk function prefers the certain option in the one-shot case and the risky option in the hundred-fold case, because the aggregate distribution concentrates, and that a concave-utility-over-wealth agent cannot have it both ways here for calibration reasons of the sort Rabin pressed. I have not verified this, and if it fails the example has to go. The specific thing I cannot settle is whether the many-shot leg is doing any work that a constant-absolute-risk-aversion utility function could not do by itself.

Candidate B, meant to discriminate. A relief agency choosing between logistics contracts. Pair one: Contract 1 guarantees delivery of 1,000 tons; Contract 2 gives a 0.10 chance of 6,000 tons, a 0.85 chance of 1,000 tons, and a 0.05 chance of total default. Pair two, a separate smaller procurement: Contract 1' gives a 0.15 chance of 1,000 tons and an 0.85 chance of nothing; Contract 2' gives a 0.10 chance of 6,000 tons and a 0.90 chance of nothing. With linear utility and the risk function ρ(p) = p³, the agent prefers Contract 1 over Contract 2 and Contract 2' over Contract 1'. I constructed this as a common-consequence structure so that no expected-utility maximizer, however curved her utility, can reproduce the pair. Separately: I first tried a two-outcome common-ratio version and found that under ρ(p) = p² the ranking simply carries across with no reversal, and I cannot tell whether that is an artifact of the particular risk function I chose or a general feature of two-outcome cases — that is, whether a floor outcome is structurally required.

Tell me which candidate carries the section, or whether they must run in sequence and in which order. Tell me what a carrying example for this theory has to have and what it has to lack — my own guess is that it must make the linear-utility reading genuinely available and must not be re-describable by enriching the outcome space, but I want the full list. And tell me what whichever example I choose will mislead them about, and where I should flag that in advance.

3. The misconception map. For each of the following, tell me the point in the sequence at which it should be headed off, including which ones I must plant and defuse considerably earlier than feels natural: (a) that the risk function is a second probability function, so that the theory is making a claim about how agents weight evidence; (b) that a concave utility function already delivers everything the risk function delivers, which I expect within five minutes and which I suspect is the real target of the section; (c) from the economists, that this is rank-dependent utility from their micro sequence with a philosophical gloss attached, so nothing normative has been added; (d) that convexity of ρ and concavity of u are the same mathematical gesture; (e) that the theory is descriptive, so whether risk-weighting is permitted is a question of psychology; (f) that if risk attitudes are unconstrained the theory makes rationality toothless; and (g) the objection, which the statistician will probably raise, that an agent who risk-weights inside a small problem is exploitable across time or dominated once the problem is embedded in her life as a whole. On (g) I do not know whether to raise it myself, and if so whether it belongs at the ten-minute mark as a framing of what is at stake or in the final five minutes as a question I explicitly decline to settle. Please add any misconception I have not listed that you judge more likely than these, and say what in my presentation would produce it.

4. A check for understanding. One question I can put to them in the last three minutes whose answer will show whether they have grasped the distinction between risk-weighting and utility curvature rather than acquired a vocabulary. Tell me what a good answer sounds like, and what a wrong answer that nevertheless reveals something diagnosable looks like.

5. What to leave out, and what to say when I leave it out. The representation theorem and its axioms, the diachronic and self-location debates, the comparison with prospect theory, and the applications to the veil of ignorance and to long-horizon policy all seem to be past fifty minutes. Tell me which of these I should name as deliberately excluded and why, and which I should not mention at all, because mentioning them would open a door I cannot close in the time remaining.

Please close with a rough allocation of the fifty minutes across the parts of your plan, and write your response in American English. I am not looking for readings or a bibliography; I have the literature.
