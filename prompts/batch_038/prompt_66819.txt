So my older sister has been a public defender for about twelve years and over Thanksgiving she got into it with my stepdad, who's a retired trial judge, about sentencing. Her whole pitch was that half of what she does at the penalty phase is assembling a social history of the client — the abuse, the mom's addiction, the lead paint, the foster placements, the schools that expelled him at twelve — and that there's an entire profession of mitigation specialists whose job is basically to build that narrative and get it in front of a judge. My stepdad's pitch was that all of that is fine as context but the number has to answer to what the defendant actually did, and he kept saying things like "everybody's got a bad story" and "nobody put a gun in his hand." What neither of them seemed to notice is the asymmetry: a rotten upbringing counts in the defendant's favor, but I've never once heard of a comfortable upbringing counting against anyone. No sentencing statute I've looked at lists "had every advantage and squandered it" as an aggravating factor, even though prosecutors will happily say it in a closing argument. And the Supreme Court has gone the other direction too — in the juvenile cases it's insisted that judges take into account a kid's immaturity and the home environment he couldn't extricate himself from before imposing the harshest sentences.

I've been reading Strawson's Basic Argument on the side, the one that says you can't be morally responsible for how you act unless you're responsible for the way you are in the relevant mental respects, and you can't be that unless you somehow made yourself, which nothing can do. It seems like that should just flatten the whole mitigation enterprise — but I also suspect the practice is reaching for something Strawson's all-or-nothing framing can't express, like responsibility coming in degrees with how much of the act traces to the person rather than the circumstances. So: what theory of responsibility does the mitigation industry actually presuppose? Reconstruct it as carefully as you can and then separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, because my hunch is that several very different theories — capacities, fair opportunity, the expressive function of blame, something about traceability — can all license the same hearing, and the hearing doesn't decide between them. I'd also like to know whether the mitigates-but-doesn't-aggravate pattern is coherent, incoherent, or coherent only on one specific reading; and whether the trial side of the same system, where the evidence rules basically bar the prosecution from arguing the defendant is a bad sort who probably did it, contradicts the sentencing side or can be held together by one account.

The part I actually care about is the counterfactual. If you took Strawson seriously and scrubbed the practice of everything it can't support, what would be left standing? Be specific about what goes (retribution? proportionality to culpability? the entire vocabulary of desert?) and what survives (quarantine, deterrence, rehabilitation, victim statements, the parole board), and then tell me whether the survivors can be motivated without smuggling desert back in through the side door — because it looks to me like even a purely preventive system still needs to answer why *this* person gets locked up and not the one who's equally dangerous but had it easier. And flag anything in the current practice that quietly presupposes we ARE responsible for our own characters, like my stepdad's "nobody put a gun in his hand" line or the way judges talk about defendants who "chose" their associates and "had every chance to change." I want to know if that's a second, inconsistent commitment the system is holding, or whether it's actually a real reply to Strawson that defenders of the practice should be making out loud instead of burying in a presentence report. sorry this is long — no need for a reading list at the end, I'll find sources on my own.
