The question was:

"Rosenthal's higher-order thought theory is standardly said to rest on the transitivity principle. State that principle and explain what role it plays in the theory. Then set out the misrepresentation objection to higher-order thought theory, and assess whether a dispositionalist version of the theory can avoid it."

The student's answer:

"The transitivity principle says that a mental state is conscious just in case the subject is aware of being in that state. Rosenthal uses it to give a reductive account of what makes a state conscious: consciousness is not an intrinsic property of a state but a relational one, consisting in the state's being represented by a further state of the subject. This is what separates his view from first-order representationalism, on which a state is conscious in virtue of representing the world in the right way; for Rosenthal, a world-directed representation is conscious only if the subject also has a thought to the effect that she is in it. The awareness in question is a kind of inner perception: the subject monitors her own mental states by an internal faculty, much as she monitors the environment by the external senses, and it is this internal scanning that makes misrepresentation possible in the same way that perceptual illusion is.

Two consequences follow, and both are advantages. First, the theory explains why conscious states are subjective: there is something it is like to be in a state only for a subject who can take herself to be in it. Second, it avoids an infinite regress. If a conscious state requires a higher-order thought, one might worry that the higher-order thought, being itself a mental state, would require a further one, and so on without end. Rosenthal blocks this by holding that the higher-order thought is itself conscious — we have introspective access to it — but that this awareness is of a different and non-regressive kind, since the chain terminates in the subject's general capacity for self-awareness.

The most serious objection is the misrepresentation objection. Because the theory makes consciousness depend on a representation distinct from the state it is about, that representation can fail to match its target. It may represent the subject as being in a state she is not in, an empty higher-order thought; or, more troublingly, it may arise with no suitable first-order state at all, in which case the theory is committed to a conscious state that does not exist. Critics push the point further: nothing in the theory restricts higher-order thoughts to mental targets, so if I came to have a non-inferential thought about the condition of my liver, the theory would have to grant that there is something it is like to be my liver. Rosenthal's reply is that the awareness must be caused in the appropriate way by the state it is about, but this concedes that the causal relation, rather than the content of the higher-order thought, is doing the explanatory work.

The dispositionalist alternative, defended by Carruthers, replaces the actual higher-order thought with a first-order state that is disposed to cause such a thought, typically by being available to a mindreading faculty. On this view no actual misrepresenting state is required, so the misrepresentation objection does not arise: a disposition cannot misfire in the way an actual representation can. It carries a further consequence, that nonhuman animals and prelinguistic infants are not conscious, since they lack the conceptual resources to have the relevant dispositions. Carruthers accepts this cost, but it sits badly with the naturalistic ambitions of the theory, and it is a reason to prefer a first-order account on which consciousness requires only the right kind of world-directed representation."
