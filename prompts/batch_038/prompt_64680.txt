I am assembling a small anthology of commissioned fiction to accompany a third-year undergraduate seminar on personal identity, and I have run into a gap I would like you to fill. My students engage readily enough with the standard technological thought experiments — teletransportation, fission, gradual uploading — but they tend to treat the underlying premise as a merely verbal concession, something granted for the sake of the puzzle rather than actually believed. The one place where I have seen that premise bite is Douglas Hofstadter's *I Am a Strange Loop* (2007), where he argues that an "I" is a self-sustaining pattern rather than a substance, and that patterns of this kind are not confined to the skull that first ran them: they can be instantiated, at much lower resolution and drastically reduced bandwidth, in the heads of other people who knew their bearer well. The claim becomes concrete and painful in the most personal chapters of the book, which concern the sudden death of his wife Carol in 1993. There he describes how her characteristic ways of noticing, judging, and responding went on operating in him and in the friends and family around her, so that she remained partially present — distributed, in fragments, across a handful of living brains, each of which carried a partial copy that could drift, deepen, or degrade.

What I want from you is a short story of roughly 2,500 words that treats this thesis as literally true and works out what a community organized around it would look like. Please use the following setting as your seed, adjusting particulars freely. On a small island off the west coast of Scotland there survives a secular custom: when a person of consequence dies, three "keepers" are appointed — people who knew the deceased closely and who, over the following decades, continue to speak for them in ordinary practical matters. The deceased at the center of your story is Eilidh MacLeod, a retired head teacher who for forty years informally settled disputes between crofters, and whose papers, photographs, and recordings were destroyed at her funeral in accordance with the custom, so that nothing of her survives except in the keepers' heads. Her three keepers are her son Calum, a shellfish diver; Fiona, the island's only doctor, who was her pupil at nine; and Duncan, a retired crofter and church elder who disliked her for thirty years. The plot should turn on a proposal to lease the common grazing and the adjacent sea bed to a tidal-energy company: the island is evenly split, and by long custom Eilidh would have chaired the meeting and given the deciding opinion. The keepers are asked what she would have said, and they return three incompatible answers, each supported by flawless recall of things she actually said and did. The meeting is adjourned until the keepers can agree on which of them is her.

Please observe these further requirements. First, no preserving technology of any kind — no recordings, letters, diaries, or photographs may be consulted, and the story should make the absence of such artifacts felt as a deliberate discipline rather than an inconvenience. Second, show the copies at the level of small behavioral detail: the pause before an unkind remark, a rule of thumb about when not to settle a quarrel, a prejudice she could never quite justify, a way of holding a cup. Third, include at least one scene in which a keeper produces a judgment Eilidh never made and could not have predicted — an extrapolation — leaving the others to decide whether this is her continuing to develop or a keeper's own self bleeding through. Fourth, let one keeper have begun to lose the ability to distinguish her own impulses from the ones she carries, and treat this soberly, as a cost, not as a horror trope or an illness to be diagnosed. Fifth, give the skeptic real force: someone — Duncan, or a young woman who left the island at eighteen and has returned only since the funeral, carrying a second-hand copy of a copy — should press the objection that all this is elaborate commemorative theater, that nothing is present but the keepers' grief and self-interest, and that a pattern instantiated in three disagreeing skulls is no pattern at all. Do not resolve that objection by fiat or by a sentimental epiphany; the story should end on a definite scene that leaves the question genuinely open.

Two housekeeping points. Nothing in the fiction should name the philosophy: no mention of Hofstadter, strange loops, substrate independence, or the technical vocabulary of personal identity, and no epigraphs or framing devices in which the tale turns out to be a document read aloud at a seminar. Write the story in British English throughout — spelling, punctuation, and idiom — and keep the register restrained and concrete rather than lyrical about souls. After the story, please add an author's note of no more than 150 words stating which aspect of the distributed-self thesis each of the three keepers was intended to embody, and where you think your own story pushes back against it.
