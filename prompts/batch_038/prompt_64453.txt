You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of aesthetics and epistemology, with substantial experience designing diagnostic teaching materials. This is a design task rather than a survey task: please think carefully, and work through the logical space before committing to any structure.

**Context.** I co-run a reading group on the epistemology of aesthetic judgment. Half the members are philosophers, half are cognitive scientists, and one is a film programmer. In our session on whether it is legitimate to form a belief about a work's merits on someone else's say-so, we discovered that people who use the same vocabulary — all of them cheerfully invoke some version of the acquaintance principle — disagree wildly about the cases. I want to hand out an instrument that sorts us by what we actually believe rather than by what we call ourselves.

**Deliverable.** A decision tree of nine to eleven nodes that begins from a commitment everyone in the group shares and ends at a named position, together with a scoring key mapping patterns of answers onto the leaves. Every node must be a concrete scenario or a claim stated in plain language, with two or three answer options. No node may ask the respondent to choose a label, invoke a term of art, or say which thinker they side with, and no node should be answerable merely by knowing how the debate is conventionally carved up.

**Method, in two stages, both of which I want to see.** Stage one: before drawing anything, brainstorm the space. List the axes along which views about testimony in aesthetics can genuinely differ, give the answer options each axis admits, and note which combinations the axes jointly permit. Spend a paragraph asking which of these axes are truly independent and which collapse into one another once the cases are fixed; nodes that collapse should be merged, not duplicated. Stage two: locate actual positions in that space. Where a permitted combination has no defenders that you know of, keep it on the map and diagnose the empty cell — is the view incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Where your tree routes two genuinely distinct positions to the same leaf, say so explicitly; I would rather see the limitation than have it blurred over.

**Axes that must appear.** Whether testimony makes a true and justified aesthetic belief *available* at all, versus whether it makes one available but illegitimate to *use*; whether the defect, if there is one, is doxastic or appreciative — a matter of what you may believe, or of what it takes to value and enjoy a work; whether bare assertion differs in kind from testimony that supplies reasons, features, and comparisons; whether whatever blocks deference is specific to the aesthetic, or one instance of a general difficulty with testimony about anything calling for first-hand grasp (moral testimony, testimony about what an experience is like, testimony about whether a dish is properly seasoned); what kind of norm does the blocking work if any — a norm governing belief formation, a claim about the value of first-hand experience, or a claim about the agent's autonomy, taste, or character; and whether aesthetic judgment is belief-like at all, since one respectable exit from the tree denies the framing outright.

**Cases.** Build the nodes out of scenarios like the following. Use at least seven, adapting them rather than reproducing them verbatim:

1. A trusted friend says a novel is beautiful and offers nothing further. You read it and find yourself agreeing.
2. Same testimony, but you have not read it; you put it on your reading list and, at a party, announce that it is one of the great books of the decade.
3. A critic whose palate you trust says a wine is unbalanced. Tasting it, you detect nothing of the sort.
4. Your partner has told you a friend's painting is bad. The friend asks you in person what you think of it.
5. Someone tells you a proof is elegant. You can follow every line and feel nothing.
6. Midway through a gallery visit you learn that the painting you have been admiring is a highly skilled forgery.
7. Three people who know you well and know the genre tell you a documentary you have not seen is magnificent; you are then asked to write three sentences about it for a newsletter.
8. You have never heard a particular musical tradition at all; a knowledgeable listener tells you a performance in it was transcendent.
9. Someone warns you that a joke is hilarious, then tells it. You do not laugh.

**The recommendation puzzle.** At least one node must confront the respondent with the practical residue of their own view: if deference is illegitimate, why is it perfectly acceptable to let other people's recommendations govern what you actually go and see, and to fault them when they steer you badly? The tree should separate those who answer this by narrowing what testimony can transmit from those who answer it by narrowing what belief requires, from those who have no answer and simply retreat to a different leaf.

**Output.** The tree and scoring key, followed by a short diagnostic report template: for each terminal pattern, the leaf, the places where the respondent's case verdicts outrun the principle they endorsed at an earlier node, and the cheapest revision available to them.

**Constraints.** No bibliography, no citations, no attributions to named philosophers — I am deliberately asking for the space, not the literature. If you think a position I have requested does not in fact exist, say so and label the leaf "unnamed" rather than inventing a defender for it. The finished instrument should fit on two pages and be completable in about ten minutes by someone with no background in the topic.
