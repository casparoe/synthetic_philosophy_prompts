Six weeks ago I was appointed to the compensation committee of a mid-sized engineering firm, and among the matters I inherited is the reform of our annual awards program. My difficulty is not with the reform itself but with understanding what the existing program is supposed to be, so I would like a careful reconstruction of its implicit commitments before I put anything to the committee.

The centerpiece is a cash contribution award, decided on peer nomination and then ratified by us. In every piece of internal literature it is described as recognizing merit: the payment is framed not as a bonus for results but as a judgment that someone has earned it. Four features of the program have come to seem to me in tension with that framing.

First, raters are instructed in writing to discount an achievement that is largely attributable to luck of assignment — having been put on the project that happened to succeed, working alongside an unusually strong team, arriving at the right moment — and to weigh instead the effort, judgment, and character the person displayed.

Second, there is a settled unwritten norm that no one receives the award twice within five years, explained to nominees as letting the recognition get around.

Third, work falling squarely within a person's stated job responsibilities is ineligible, on the ground that it is already covered by salary.

Fourth, salaries are set annually by benchmarking against external market survey data, and our stated compensation philosophy holds that pay reflects what a role is worth in the market rather than what the incumbent deserves.

I am assured that all of this expresses one coherent commitment to rewarding merit. I cannot see that it does. Please work through the following.

Take the four features one at a time and say what each presupposes about the nature of desert: is it a backward-looking relation between a person's own actions or qualities and a benefit conferred, a status created by public rules that the institution itself brings into being, an expressive act of recognition, an instrument for shaping future behavior, or a device for neutralizing unequal starting points? I suspect the four do not share an answer, and I want the differences made explicit rather than smoothed over.

The second feature is the one I find hardest to reconcile. If two people equally deserve a thing, can desert itself license awarding it to only one of them because the other has been awarded recently? Separate the version in which the constraint comes from a fixed sum of money from the version in which it comes from a norm about who should be seen to be honored, and say whether each is compatible with a desert-based justification.

The third appears to assume that salary is itself a payment of desert, while the fourth denies that salary tracks anything of that kind. Can both stand? Tell me what would have to be true about the relation between contract and desert for the ineligibility rule to make sense.

Then identify which single account, if any, can accommodate all four features without residue, and be equally clear about where the program is philosophically underdetermined — where several rival accounts would license exactly the same paperwork and the choice between them makes no difference to practice.

Finally, suppose the committee were persuaded by the stronger claim that nobody deserves their natural endowment or even their disposition to make an effort, since that disposition is itself the product of fortunate circumstances. Tell me precisely which clauses of our criteria would have to be struck, which sentences of our internal language would become false, and whether anything the company distributes could still honestly be called a merit award.

I am not looking for a survey of the literature or for recommendations about what to read; I want the analysis itself, and I want it disciplined about distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with.
