ok so for a workshop on decision theory under imperfect recall i want to hand out a poem instead of a summary, and i think the absent-minded driver is the one that actually fits a strict form. Write a sestina — six stanzas of six lines plus the three-line envoi, with the end-words cycling through the standard permutation.

The setup it needs to carry: two highway exits, indistinguishable from behind the wheel, and no memory of which one he's already passed. Taking the first ramp is worth nothing, the second one is home, blowing past both leaves him somewhere worse than home but not zero. So the smart plan made at the start isn't "exit at the second one" — he can't condition on which one it is — it's to keep going with probability two-thirds at every ramp, and that's the number i want implied by the poem rather than stated as a number.

Make the form do the paradox. The end-words coming back in new slots with nothing in the line to mark which pass you're on is exactly what it's like to be at a ramp you can't identify, and the moment where his at-the-ramp reasoning ("if this is the second one, exit") collides with the plan he made when he did know where he was should land like a stanza boundary rather than an argument. Save the envoi for the one place he has information: at the start, keys in hand, before that's any use to him. Don't label or explain the pattern anywhere in the poem.

Someone who knows the example should be able to read off the payoffs and the two-thirds; someone who doesn't should just get a decent poem about not knowing where you are.
