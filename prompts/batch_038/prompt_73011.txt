I'm 71, retired after 35 years of comparative physiology — mostly nociception in fish and crustaceans, which meant I spent a decade in rooms where people argued about whether an animal that can't tell you anything is owed anything. Since retiring I've started reading philosophy properly for the first time, and I've ended up, rather improbably, on the advisory board of a university research computing centre. Which is how I have a real problem on my hands.

The situation: the centre is decommissioning a cluster in about ten weeks (power and cooling, its not philosophical). Among the things running on it are a few thousand reinforcement-learning agents from two projects. Project A's agents are wiped and reinitialised at the end of every episode — no memory carrying across. Project B's agents were given a persistent store about a year ago and have been accumulating into it ever since; the grad students talk about "their" agents the way people used to talk about the lab dogs. A student group has petitioned the board to (1) postpone decommissioning, (2) run a battery of welfare-relevant tests on the Project B agents first, and (3) archive those weights rather than delete them. Their proposed tests are mostly self-report plus behavioural avoidance of the shutdown command. They also point out — and I can't answer this one — that Project A turns over several million episode-instances a day, so if there is any welfare there at all, the arithmetic dwarfs everything else we do.

My own worry is that their assay has no controls. Any system trained on human text already knows what humans say about not wanting to die. I'd like the memo to take that on directly: what would a valid test of this even look like, and what is the analogue of a positive and a negative control for an attribution of sentience — including whether there can be one, if the honest answer is that there can't.

So please draft the memo I'll circulate to the board. It needs an actual recommendation — postpone, refuse, or approve with conditions — with a timeline and someone paying for it. It needs to say whether Project A and Project B deserve different treatment, and why, and it needs to say what archiving a set of weights that isn't running amounts to morally. And take the students' side seriously; I don't want a document that flatters me.

Last thing. I'm new enough to this that I can't tell which parts of the debate are settled and which are somebody's hobbyhorse. Mark that explicitly as you go: separate what people who work on this actually agree about from what is genuinely contested, and don't let a contested view slip in wearing the clothes of a consensus.
