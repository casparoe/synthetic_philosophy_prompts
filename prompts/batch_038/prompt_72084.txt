I would like help identifying the principle, if there is one, that underlies my verdicts about which states count as emotions. I have been going case by case, and the pattern that emerges does not look like any of the three families I know of — emotions as judgments or appraisals, emotions as perceptions of value, emotions as feelings or as perceptions of one's own bodily changes. My verdicts:

1. My heart races and my palms dampen before a job interview, and I experience this as dread — but the cause is a double espresso, and once I notice that, the dread evaporates. Not an emotion, or at any rate not fear.
2. I recoil in terror from a house spider that I know, without reservation, to be harmless. My considered judgment says there is nothing to fear. This is fear, unmistakably.
3. A low-grade unease that has hung over me for three weeks and for which I could name no object if asked. A mood, not an emotion.
4. Being brought to tears by a passage of music I have heard a hundred times, while believing nothing sad has happened and not taking the music to be about anything. An emotion, though I cannot say what it is directed at.
5. A cold, articulate indignation at an injustice I read about: I can state precisely what was done wrong and to whom, I am disposed to write letters and to donate, but there is no heat and no agitation, nothing it is like to be indignant. Still anger.
6. My startle at a door slamming behind me. Not an emotion.
7. Shame at my accent when speaking a language I learned late, where nothing morally bad has occurred and nobody has been slighted. An emotion.
8. Fear on behalf of a character in a novel, in full and unshakeable knowledge that the character does not exist. An emotion.
9. Schadenfreude at a rival's public failure, which I find repugnant and would disavow on reflection. An emotion, and no less of one for my repudiation of it.
10. A fury at a colleague that persisted across a week in which I thought of him twice and felt nothing in particular. Still anger, not its absence.

At least two pairs here seem irreconcilable. The espresso case has all the felt arousal of fear and none of the appraisal, and I deny that it is an emotion; the cold indignation has all the appraisal and none of the arousal, and I insist that it is one. Likewise, the standing unease and the response to the music both lack a nameable object, and I sort them differently.

My own tentative principle is that an emotion is a state that represents something as good or bad — for me, or for someone whose fortunes I am invested in — and that is poised to move me to act. I can see that this is either too broad, since thirst represents water as good and moves me to seek it, or that once I add clauses to exclude thirst the clauses are doing all the work and the principle none.

What I want from you:

(a) State one principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precisely enough that an unfamiliar case could be fed into it and a verdict read off. For each clause, say which of my ten verdicts motivated it.

(b) Score each candidate against all ten cases. Be explicit about which verdicts it cannot preserve. I would rather be told that one of my verdicts has to go than be handed a baroque hybrid that saves everything by stipulation; but if the only faithful account really is disjunctive, say so, and say what the disjunction costs.

(c) Take seriously the deflationary alternative: that these verdicts track no single kind at all, because "emotion" is a heterogeneous folk category with no common essence, and that my insistence on a principle is a mistake. If you press this line, tell me what survives of my conviction that the espresso case and the spider case genuinely differ, rather than merely differing in how I happen to feel about them.

(d) Test the surviving candidate or candidates on three cases of your own devising that I have not raised. At least one should be a case where you expect my intuition to conflict with the principle; state the conflict and tell me which of us you think is wrong and why.

(e) Locate the resulting view relative to the judgment, perception, and feeling families, and say what it borrows from each and what it refuses.

One note on format: I care about the shape of the account, not its pedigree. Name positions where a name is genuinely informative, but do not build me a bibliography, a reading list, or a survey of who held what.
