So I got into an arguement with my roommate at like 3am about whether beliefs are real and he thinks he's got a proof that they aren't, and I cant find the flaw. Here it is cleaned up, I think this is fair to him:

(1) A pattern is real — it's in the world and not just in the notation — to the degree that spotting it compresses the data: you can swap a bit-by-bit record for something much shorter that still lets you get the record back.
(2) So beliefs and desires are real features of a system only if taking the intentional stance toward that system compresses: shorter than the rival descriptions, and still good for the behavior.
(3) For any system whatsoever, there's an ascription that nails the behavior perfectly: give it the desire to go on doing what it's doing, plus the belief that what it's doing is the way to go on doing it.
(4) Run it on the doorstop in his kitchen, the one that stops the door slamming. Desire: door stays open. Belief: wedging under it keeps the door open. That predicts the thing in calm weather, in a draft, when you lift it, when you kick it and it skids. The physical stance would take pages of continuum mechanics for less coverage, and the design stance gets you about the same predictions but no better ones.
(5) So by (1) and (2) the doorstop has beliefs and desires, and they're real.
(6) But (3) works for anything — a dead phone, a styrofoam peanut, a weather front, a corpse. The intentional stance never fails, which means it never separates the systems that have beliefs from the ones that don't.
(7) A level of description that's true of everything and rules out nothing isn't tracking a real pattern in anything.
(8) Therefore nobody has beliefs or desires, doorstops included. Keep the intentional stance as an instrument if you like, but it doesn't report facts.

He says I have to pick my poison: swallow (5) and go around saying rocks believe things, or swallow (8). He takes (8) and seems pleased about it.

I hate this because I want to keep (1) — the compression idea is the whole reason I ever found this framework attractive — and I want to keep the claim that you and I have beliefs and the doorstop doesn't. I tried denying (3) and he just gerrymandered the ascription harder, and I couldn't say what was wrong with the gerrymandered one either, which is worse. Somewhere around (4) through (7) I get the feeling a word quietly changes meaning but I can't catch it in the act.

Which step do you break, and what does breaking it cost me? And — this is the part that actually worries me — run his trivializing move against the physical stance too, where every object gets described as obeying whatever laws it does obey. Does it land there as well? If it does, is his argument really about beliefs, or is it about levels of description in general, and what's left of the three stances either way?
