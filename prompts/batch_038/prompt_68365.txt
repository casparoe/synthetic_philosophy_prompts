Some background on me. I have been reading rationalist blogs and forecasting forums for the better part of two decades, I have internalized the standard sermon about the outside view — anchor on base rates, discount your narrative, distrust the sense that this case is unique — and I currently run a small, informal prediction project with friends in which we score ourselves on questions about geopolitical and economic trajectories over multi-decade horizons. The argument below is my own, assembled out of things I have believed separately for years. I only noticed what it implies when I wrote it out in sequence, and I have not been able to find the bad step since. Each premise looks true to me when I consider it in isolation, which is exactly why I do not trust the whole.

(1) Outside view. For any forecast, your rational credence should be set by the frequency of the outcome among the cases in the relevant reference class, and case-specific detail should modify that credence only insofar as its diagnostic value has itself been established from frequencies across cases.

(2) The world does not come pre-sorted into reference classes. Which class a case falls into depends on which of its features you judge relevant to the outcome, and for any interesting case there is a plurality of defensible sortings. Take the question my group is actually arguing about — whether a particular rising power will still be a great power fifty years from now. You can sort by prior rank, by regime type, by rate of growth over the preceding decades, by whether the state is continental or maritime, by whether its rise was debt-financed. The modern record contains few enough great powers that each of these choices moves the base rate by a large margin.

(3) To judge which features are relevant to the outcome is already to hold a view about how cases of this kind turn out — and therefore a view about the outcome of the very case you are trying to predict.

(4) So every base rate you compute for a case is a function of a prior judgment about that case's outcome. The number does not constrain the judgment; it is generated by it.

(5) A procedure whose output is produced by the judgment it is meant to check is not a check on that judgment. It is a way of dressing a guess in the vocabulary of statistics: the guess does the work, and the base rate is decoration.

(6) Therefore the outside view cannot discipline forecasting. Whenever someone says the base rate for cases like this one is twenty percent, the figure is downstream of a prior commitment about whether this case is or is not like the others, and no quantity of base-rate talk settles the disagreement.

(7) And the reasoning generalizes without loss. All statistical learning from past cases requires sorting the past into classes, sorting requires relevance judgments, and relevance judgments require a prior view of outcomes. So there is no learning from history in the base-rate sense at all — not for empires and their declines, not for wars, not for financial crises, not for the timelines of large projects.

(8) This includes the evidence normally cited on the outside view's behalf. The tournament results, the comparisons between planners who anchored on past projects and planners who did not, the demonstrations that certain forecasters are more accurate because they reason from frequencies — each of these is itself a comparison across a class of past episodes, and hence a base rate, and hence falls under (7). The argument therefore cannot be answered with evidence, because it explains away any evidence offered against it as one more arbitrary class choice. I cannot tell whether that self-sealing quality is a sign of depth or of emptiness.
