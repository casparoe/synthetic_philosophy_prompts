Last month I took up a post on the policy team of a British charity that funds governance work in South Asia, and in my second week our chief executive gave a newspaper interview in which she defended our model of long-term technical assistance as a form of trusteeship rather than charity, naming Mill as its intellectual ancestor. The remark drew a reply in a weekend broadsheet from an academic historian, and that reply has been circulated to the whole board with a note that we ought to be able to answer it. I have been asked to prepare a two-page note for next month's meeting, and I may have to speak to it on a panel, so I need to be precise about what Mill actually held rather than about what he is generally taken to have held. The operative passage runs as follows:

'Mill is not an embarrassing guest in the liberal canon but its most consistent imperialist. He spent thirty-five years in the service of the East India Company, rising to head the office that drafted its instructions to India, and he never set foot in the country he helped to govern; right up to the Company's abolition he defended that arrangement as the best available instrument of British commercial and strategic interest. His utilitarianism, he was explicit, was a principle for civilised peoples only. In *On Liberty* he says that the greatest-happiness calculation does not apply to societies such as India, and that what such societies require is despotism, legitimate in their case simply because it is exercised by a civilised power. He drew no distinction between races, only between stages of civilisation, but he took the lower stage to be a settled condition, and so saw no terminus to British rule and no point at which India might govern itself. The same logic governs his essay on intervention, in which he argues that civilised states have not merely a right but a duty to intervene wherever a people is oppressed by its rulers, on the ground that barbarous peoples have no rights as nations except to whatever treatment will soonest and best prepare them to possess such rights. Mill is therefore the ancestor of liberal interventionism, and the contemporary vocabulary of capacity-building and beneficent administration is his vocabulary laundered. Charities that use it should say so plainly.'

I read *On Liberty* as an undergraduate and remember that there is a passage about despotism and barbarians, but I could not now reconstruct the argument around it, and I have never read the essay on intervention or the chapters on dependent countries. Please audit the column claim by claim. For each sentence, tell me what a scholar of Mill would accept as it stands, what is a defensible compression, and what is simply wrong; and where a sentence turns on something specialists dispute, say what the dispute is and on which side of it the columnist has planted himself. Three things in particular I cannot settle. First, whether Mill restricted the principle of utility itself, or only the maxims of liberty and self-government, and what work the condition he attaches to the despotism claim is really doing — whether a specialist would regard that condition as load-bearing or as decoration. Second, whether his criterion genuinely is developmental rather than racial, or whether that is too tidy a picture of texts that also sort dependencies by the composition of their peoples. Third, whether the column has the direction of the intervention argument right, since I retain a vague impression that Mill was sceptical about securing a people's liberty for them by foreign arms, which does not sit well with the duty the columnist describes.

Finally, tell me how much of the closing recommendation survives the corrections. If the column is wrong where I suspect it is wrong, does Mill's liberalism still stand in the relation to empire that the columnist alleges, and is our chief executive's appeal to trusteeship something I can defend in the note, or something I should advise her to withdraw? I am inclined to think the columnist is broadly right about the politics and careless about the philosophy; what I want to know is whether that division can be sustained once the philosophy is stated accurately.
