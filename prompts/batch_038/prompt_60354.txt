I spent thirty-eight years doing X-ray crystallography and retired three years back, and to keep from going soft in the head I've been sitting in on a reading group at the community college that's working through the philosophy of corruption — half political theory, half anthropology, which is an odd diet for a lab person. Last week we got properly stuck on an analogy and I don't think anyone in the room knew how to get unstuck, so I'm bringing it to you instead.

The analogy comes from Mauss on the gift. As I understand him, the argument is that in the societies he was describing a gift is never really free: there are three obligations, to give, to receive, and to reciprocate, the object carries something of the giver along with it (he picks this up from Maori material about a spirit in the thing, though someone in our group claimed he garbled that from a secondhand informant account), and the whole exchange is a public, total affair that builds standing and alliance rather than transferring a commodity. He was aiming it squarely at the utilitarian picture of exchange as self-interested contract. Now, my neice ran a maternal-health program for an NGO in West Africa for about eight years, and she leans on this account hard in arguments with the compliance people. Her line is that the gift network in the districts she worked in is functionally the same thing we call a contract or a line of credit — obligations to reciprocate that are enforced by reputation instead of courts — and that when a Western donor agency makes a village nurse declare a chicken or a bag of rice as a bribe, it isn't detecting corruption, it's exporting a local custom about where the self ends and the office begins. She also has a second analogy running alongside it: the gift network is really an insurance system in a place where the state never showed up, so calling it corrupt is like calling a barn-raising corrupt because nobody filed a building permit.

What I want is an audit, not a verdict on her politics. Lay out the correspondences one at a time and say plainly which of them are load-bearing and which are decorative: what plays the role of the consideration, of the enforcement mechanism, of the parties, and — this is where I got stuck — of the person whose interests are actually at stake, since in the bribery case there's always a third party the official is supposed to be serving and I can't see where that party sits in Mauss's picture. Then tell me which disanalogies genuinely threaten her conclusion and which ones a relativist can shrug off: publicity versus concealment, exchange between equals versus exchange between an officeholder and a supplicant, the gift as a total social fact versus the gift as one payment inside a modern administrative machine. Does the fact that Mauss himself thought modern welfare states were partly recovering the morality of the gift help her or hurt her? And here's the bit I'd most like you to press on: if the analogy really works, does it not work in the other direction too — can't you describe lobbying, the revolving door, and campaign donations as exactly the same gift network with obligations to reciprocate, which would make her argument not a defense of local practice but an indictment of ours? Finally, do her two analogies pull the same way, or does the insurance framing quietly concede that the contract framing failed? Give me your bottom line in the first few sentences — does the analogy hold, and does the conclusion it's being used for survive — and then work through it.

One practical request: write it the way you'd write me a letter, running prose only, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, since I read this on a tablet and the formatting comes out as soup. And don't send me off to a bibliography; I can't chase anything down and would rather you just reason it out in front of me.
