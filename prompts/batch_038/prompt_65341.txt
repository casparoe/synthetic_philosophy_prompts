I hold a view about what makes a life go well that I inherited, loosely, from Moore's account of the ideal, though I want it assessed as a substantive position rather than as scholarship about him. It has five parts.

(i) Two kinds of state are of supreme intrinsic value: mutual affection among a few people who know and admire one another, and the admiring contemplation of beauty — in art, in nature, and above all in persons.

(ii) Their value is not reducible to the pleasure contained in them. Pleasure is a genuine component, but a whole composed of awareness of an object, an emotion appropriate to it, and knowledge that the object really exists and really is as one takes it to be can be worth vastly more than the sum of its parts. Conversely, pleasure attached to a negligible or misapprehended object adds very little.

(iii) Beauty that is never contemplated has very little value. Value concentrates in the contemplating.

(iv) Practical corollary: the best use of a life is to cultivate a small number of deep friendships and a serious capacity for appreciation, rather than to devote oneself to increasing aggregate welfare impartially. Benevolence matters chiefly as a means to conditions under which such goods become widely possible.

(v) I accept the consequence I know is unattractive: a life in which a great deal of suffering is relieved, but which contains neither affection nor appreciation, would be a poor life however contented. That is a claim about where value lies, not about who counts morally.

My method is also Moorean: I test candidate goods by considering them in absolute isolation, asking whether I would judge the existence of a thing good if it existed entirely by itself with nothing else in the universe; and I fix the extension of "beautiful" as that of which the admiring contemplation is good in itself.

What would count against this? I want a map of the view's exposure, not a catalogue of objections I could absorb.

1. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and state for each what exactly it would have to establish — that the view is false, that it is indeterminate, that it is guidance-silent, or only that it is uncomfortable — together with the criteria you used to order them.

2. Conceptual exposure. Does (i) plus (iii) become partly true by stipulation, given how I fix "beautiful," and what is left at risk if it does? Does (ii) collapse into hedonism once "emotion appropriate to the object" is specified, or into a response-dependent preference theory once we ask what makes an object beautiful independently of anyone's response? Does allowing wholes to exceed the sum of their parts make every counter-case absorbable — and is that a genuine defect in a view about value, or an illegitimate demand imported from empirical science? Say which. Is the isolation method a real probe of intrinsic value, or does it measure only how badly we simulate a world containing one thing and nothing else?

3. Empirical exposure. Which findings would bear hardest and which could I digest unchanged: hedonic adaptation; evidence that cultivated taste is substantially a function of social conditioning and status signalling; whether appreciation is cumulative and stable across a lifetime or decays; whether the quality of one's friendships is determined more by temperament and circumstance than by anything one cultivates; what people actually judge when they review their lives near the end. For each, say what would have to be shown for it to matter, not merely that it is suggestive.

4. Three cases I cannot resolve, and for each say whether a committed defender must concede or can resist: a person whose appreciative engagement is intense and lifelong but directed at work connoisseurs judge inferior; a close friendship sustained by a partly idealized and inaccurate picture of the other, which fuller knowledge would erode, so that either it was worth less than it seemed or accuracy is not required; and whether (iii) commits me to weighting the preservation of beautiful uninhabited places, and how heavily against a modest amount of sentient misery.

5. State what this view predicts that hedonism and preference-satisfaction accounts of welfare do not, and identify the one place where the predictions genuinely diverge rather than merely sound different — in particular, what should happen if appreciation and pleasure are pulled apart.

6. Finally, separability: which single defeater, if it went through, would oblige me to give up (iv) while retaining (i) through (iii)? I want to know whether my axiology and my life plan can come apart, or whether the practical corollary is doing all the work and the value theory is only its decoration.

Assume I have no library access. I want the arguments and the reasoning, not references, attributions, or a reading list.
