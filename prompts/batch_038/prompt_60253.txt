I have been going back and forth between three job offers for about a month, and I have got myself into a knot I cannot undo. I am not an academic and I have no particular training in decision-making; I have simply been turning the problem over on my own, and the more carefully I think about it the worse it gets. What I want is for someone to tell me whether my reasoning is actually broken, and if it is, where.

Here are the three offers, reduced to the two things I keep comparing them on: money, and how much of my life the job takes.

Offer A pays the most, roughly a fifth more than C. The hours are long and unpredictable, and I would be at the mercy of a manager who has already told me twice that he expects email answered at nine at night.

Offer B pays less than A, and noticeably so. The hours are still long, but they are scheduled rather than unpredictable, and I would have two fixed evenings a week that are genuinely mine.

Offer C pays the least, and least by a wide enough margin that I would feel it in my budget. The hours are normal, the work is a couple of grades less stressful, and there is a real expectation that people go home.

Now the difficulty. When I compare A with B, A wins, and it wins convincingly: the difference in the work itself is small — long hours either way, just scheduled rather than unscheduled — and the difference in pay is large, so the pay settles it. When I compare B with C, B wins, and again convincingly, for what feels to me like exactly the same kind of reason: B still consumes my evenings, but it pays enough more than C that the money settles it. Yet when I compare A with C head to head, C wins, and I am nowhere near torn about it. C gives me my whole life back, and the extra money in A, which sounded decisive twice over in the other two comparisons, does not look remotely like enough to pay for it.

I have checked this repeatedly, in different moods and at different times of day, and the order never shifts. It is not that I am muddled about the facts: all three offers are in writing and the figures are not in dispute. The problem is that in each pair I appear to be asking the same question — is this much more money worth this much less of my life — and answering it the same way, decisively yes, while at the two ends of the chain the answer is decisively no.

I also tried, in my head, to fill in the gap between A and C with imagined offers: a rung paying slightly less than A with slightly more of my evenings intact, then another rung slightly below that, and so on, all the way down to C. Going from one rung to the next, I could not see any difference that mattered — each step was too small to flip anything. But the ends of the ladder are nothing alike, and I would take the bottom rung over the top one without hesitation. That disturbed me more than the three-way version did, and I do not know what to make of it.

Two responses I have already been given and do not find satisfying. One friend says I must secretly be unsure about one of the comparisons and should keep thinking until I find the one I really believe; I have done that, and none of the three moves. Another friend, who works in finance, says the whole problem is that I have refused to put numbers on things: price an evening, add everything up, and the difficulty evaporates. When I try, the figure I would attach to an evening comes out different depending on which two offers I happen to be looking at, which seems to me to be the difficulty rather than a flaw in my method that better arithmetic could fix.

So, three questions. Is it possible for all of my comparisons to be correct, or must at least one of them be a mistake? If one is a mistake, how would I identify which, given that they all feel equally clear to me from the inside? And if none of them is a mistake, what am I supposed to do when the offers still have to be ranked, since I can accept only one?

Please be willing to tell me plainly that one of my intuitions is simply wrong, but do not assume it in advance. I would also ask you to be explicit about which parts of your answer represent a settled view and which parts are genuinely disputed among people who have thought about this problem seriously. I have read enough around the edges of it to suspect that it is more contested than either of my friends let on, and I do not want to be handed a disputed position as though everyone who knows the subject agreed on it.
