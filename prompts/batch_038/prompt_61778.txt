I am a senior at a public high school, and in three weeks I will give a twenty-minute paper at our district's mathematics and humanities symposium, followed by about fifteen minutes of questions. The panel has three members, and the audience is roughly two dozen math-competition students and their coaches, who are, frankly, inclined to think that philosophy has nothing to say to mathematics except that it is confused.

The panel is: (1) a research mathematician who works in set theory and who will be listening to a high school student talk about infinity; (2) a professor of philosophy of mathematics whose sympathies, I am told, are constructivist — she thinks the classical set theorist has already given away too much, and not in the direction in which I am giving it away; (3) my own school's physics teacher, who runs the astronomy club and who will want to know whether any of this touches the actual universe.

My thesis, stated as carefully as I can manage:

First, Hilbert's hotel does not show that an actually infinite collection is impossible. The hotel is a theorem: there is a bijection between the guests and the rooms after the shift, so the newcomer is housed and nobody is turned out. My slogan is that a theorem cannot be an absurdity — if the reasoning is valid and the premises are true, the conclusion is not a reductio, however strange it feels. What the hotel offends, I claim, is an assumption I call the conservation intuition: the principle that adding a member to a collection must make the collection larger. That principle is false for Dedekind-infinite collections, and once it is identified as the thing doing the work, the hotel loses its force.

Second, the only argument against an actual infinite that I think survives that diagnosis is not about size but about completion — the claim that a series built up by successive addition can never have been finished, so that an infinite past is impossible. I argue this fails as well, though I want to be careful about why: an infinite past with no first moment is not a task that anyone had to begin, so there is nothing there to complete. Reading the infinite past as a supertask requires a first term, and the position I am attacking denies that there is one.

Third, I introduce a test of my own, because I want to be fair to the other side. I call it the parity test: any argument against an infinite collection should be checked to see whether it also condemns a finite collection with the same structure, and if it does, the argument proves too much. I use it against the conservation intuition, since a hotel with a trillion rooms behaves exactly the same way right up to the last room. I recognize that this test may cut against me, because the finite hotel does fail where the infinite one succeeds — the last guest has nowhere to go — and I need to be able to say why that asymmetry does not license my opponent's conclusion.

My overall conclusion is deliberately modest: we have no good metaphysical argument against actual infinities, and equally no empirical evidence that any exist.

What I need from you is a preparation sheet, not a defense of my paper. Please give me:

- the six to eight objections this panel and this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, each tagged with who raises it and phrased the way that person would phrase it, including the technical vocabulary they would reach for;
- for each objection, a reply I can deliver aloud from a podium in about two minutes, plus an honest note on where that reply is softest and what a single follow-up question would expose;
- what I should concede during my opening three minutes so that the concessions cannot be turned against me later, and the one or two claims I must not surrender under any pressure;
- which single reply has to land in order for the paper to survive the session;
- and the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that, if it comes, I will have to endure rather than answer well.

Please do not go easy on me because I am a student. If some part of my argument is simply wrong, or if one of my three claims is indefensible in front of a set theorist, say so plainly and tell me what to say instead. Two further requests. First, I am working from home without library access, so I need the reasoning itself rather than directions to literature: please do not include a reading list or citations, and do not attach any view to a named philosopher unless you are certain of the attribution. Second, please write everything in American English, since that is the convention my handout will follow.
