I'm trying to get my head around the whole "are virtual things real?" debate and every summary I read just restates the two camps at each other, so I figure a worked-out dialogue would actually teach me more. Write me one.

Two people, both sharp, and honestly both right about some things:

**A** thinks virtual objects are just... objects. A sword in a game is a real digital object: a data structure running on real hardware, with real properties (it's in this inventory, it does 40 damage, it's the specific one she spent six weeks grinding for). On this view "virtual" tells you what *kind* of thing something is, not whether it exists. So when somebody deletes your sword, you've genuinely lost something, and that's the end of the story.

**B** thinks that's a confusion wearing a lab coat. Fine, the data structure is real — but the sword isn't the data structure. The sword is a prop in a shared game of make-believe, and the properties anyone actually cares about (being sharp, being worth 500 gold, being the one your guild leader carried) aren't properties of any real object at all. Deleting it isn't destroying a thing, it's ending a pretense.

The case they're arguing over — please make them argue about *this* case and not a generic stand-in for it:

Mara spent roughly two years building, block by block, an exact replica of the house her mother grew up in, inside a persistent voxel game. Her mother died last spring. Mara never showed it to anybody; she'd just log in and walk through the rooms. Last week a griefer who'd gotten himself builder permissions flattened the whole thing in about ten minutes and posted the clip.

I want three movements:

1. The straight fight: was the house real, and was what the griefer did a destruction? Let each of them land actual hits. B should get A to admit that "it exists" isn't yet an argument that anything was *harmed*, and A should get B to admit that a purely make-believe reading has a hard time explaining why the griefer chose that build instead of a random one.

2. The twist: it turns out Mara's grief isn't mainly about the house. What she keeps saying she lost is "the place I could go to." B leans on this hard. A has to answer it without just retreating to "well, the meaning was real, which is what I meant all along" — because if that's the whole answer, B wins by default and I'll know you copped out.

3. The tiebreaker, which they both have to take a swing at: same replica, same two years of work, but in a single-player offline game, no audience, no clip, no griefer — the save file is shredded by a hardware fault. Is anything lost? Is anyone wronged? Does the distinction between A and B actually buy you different verdicts anywhere, or are they just two vocabularies for the same facts? I want them to try to construct a case where the views genuinely come apart, and I want to see whether they suceed.

Rules: nobody's a strawman, nobody gets talked into anything they shouldn't, and they respond to what the other one *actually said* rather than to the position they were arguing with before they walked in. Don't resolve it. But do have each of them concede one specific thing, and end with the disagreement located as precisely as they can manage — I want to be able to point at the last few lines and say "that's the real crux." Around 1000 to 1300 words.
