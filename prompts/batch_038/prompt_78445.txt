I was a solid-state physicist for thirty-eight years, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with much the same obstinacy I once brought to a recalcitrant crystal structure — mostly in the evenings, mostly without anyone to argue with. Two years ago my town appointed me to its Historic Preservation Commission, presumably because I am old, literate, and unembarrassed by public speaking. We administer a modest grant fund created by a state bond issue, governed by a local ordinance that permits awards for "structures of historic significance that are open to the public." A case comes before the seven of us on the fourteenth that has split the commission three ways, and I would like a document to circulate beforehand. Please write it as a political philosopher who has sat on funding and heritage bodies — not as a lawyer — and before you draft the memo itself, spend a short opening passage setting the alternatives side by side and testing whether any of them actually resolves the difficulty rather than merely relocating it. I reason better that way, and I suspect the commission would too.

The facts. A stone church built in the 1870s, the oldest surviving building in town, sits on our local register. It is known here for two things: its windows, made by a regional studio that no longer exists, and the fact that in the summer of 1963 its basement hosted a voter-registration drive whose organizers were arrested on the steps. The congregation has dwindled to something under forty members, most of them past seventy. The roof and the west wall need work, costed at roughly $380,000; the members have raised $60,000 and have asked us for $150,000. The building is used for worship on Sunday mornings, opens for tours on Saturdays, and hosts a weekly community meal and a food pantry run jointly by members and by neighbours who belong to no congregation. Three commissioners argue that we must refuse: the roof shelters an altar and a pulpit, and if we pay for it we are maintaining a place of prayer with tax money, which they take to be precisely what our tradition of separating the two forbids; they add that the meal and the pantry could be relocated to the public library's community room at a fraction of the cost, so the secular benefit is not tied to this building. Two argue that we must approve: the ordinance concerns buildings and not beliefs; declining because the building is religious punishes these people for being religious; and they note that we funded the restoration of an opera house and, before that, a former fraternal temple, without a moment's hesitation. The remaining two — quietly, myself among them — wonder about a third possibility: the town acquires the building and its lot for a nominal sum, undertakes the repair itself, keeps the congregation on a licence to worship there, and opens the nave as a heritage site on weekdays. The pastor is torn about this. Half her members would accept it gratefully; the other half would experience it as the town taking possession of their church in order to make it safe for tourists, and she has told me she cannot predict which half would be right.

The memo should be roughly 700 to 900 words, addressed to the commission, and it should end with a clear recommendation among the three courses, with conditions and a time frame if approval or acquisition is what you advise. State the principles you are relying on in plain language, defining any term of art you cannot avoid, because at least two of us are not philosophers and one is openly hostile to abstraction. Give the strongest case for the option you reject, in the voice its own advocates would use, and then explain honestly why it nonetheless loses — or, if it does not lose, what constraint it imposes on whatever you recommend. Say something explicit about whether the building's significance can be separated from its religious use, since the windows, the 1963 arrests, and the nave are what make it worth saving, and about what, if anything, we owe the congregation as distinct from the fabric. Close with two or three criteria a future commission could actually apply, because a synagogue and a disused chapel are both expected to come before us next year. Please do not give me case citations, statutory references, or a reading list; I want reasoning, not authority. If you think some legal rule bears on the answer, describe its rationale in general terms and flag that our town attorney must confirm the specifics before we vote.

One further thing, which may be only my inexperience talking. My brother has lived outside Heidelberg since 1979; a percentage is withheld from his pension each month and forwarded to the regional church, which he has never left, and he regards the arrangement as unremarkable and even fair, on the grounds that the church runs kindergartens and hospitals that the state would otherwise have to provide. When I mentioned this at our last meeting, a colleague concluded that American queasiness about public money and religion is a local eccentricity rather than a matter of principle, and that we should simply say yes to the roof. Please devote a paragraph to this: whether a state that collects on behalf of recognised religious bodies and a state that grants money to them case by case differ in principle or only in machinery, and whether the comparison ought to make us more or less comfortable writing the cheque. I should add that I have no vote to protect and no constituency to please; I would rather be shown that my own instinct is confused than handed something agreeable.
