I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet — mostly AI and neuroscience, but I've got a reported piece coming up on how much of government decision-making now runs through vendors, models, and allied agencies, and my editor wants an interactive box to go with it. The working title is something like "would you dirty your hands?" and I want it to actually sort readers rather than let them self-label.

Can you build me the instrument?

Here's the shape I'm after. First, lay out the logical space as a handful of independent commitments — the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, not a list of schools. The dimensions I care about most, given what my piece is about: whether the act stays wrong all-things-considered or is merely unpleasant to perform; whether anything morally sticks to the person afterwards (guilt, apology, a duty to confess or to be punished); whether the dirt attaches to an individual agent at all, or gets smeared across the committee, the contractor, and the model that produced the recommendation; whether whatever permission exists is special to holding office or is just an ordinary feature of moral life that anyone can face; and whether handing the decision to a system or a subcontractor changes the moral situation or only the visibility of it. Feel free to add dimensions if you think I'm missing ones that do real work.

Then write ten items that locate a reader in that space. Each one is a concrete situation with two or three answer options, phrased the way a person would actually talk, and none of them may name a philosopher, an ism, or a theory — a reader should be able to answer every item without any idea which leaf they're steering toward. I want the cases to be contemporary and distributed, not the seminar classics: an eligibility model that denies thousands of appeals and no human ever reviews a file; a strike list built from a signals vendor's output; getting a private contractor to do the thing your own agency is barred by statute from doing; asking an allied service to hold and question someone for you; taking money from a genuinely bad actor to fund a campaign that keeps a worse government out; a triage protocol written before the surge so nobody has to make the call at the bedside; sitting on a true report because publishing it would cause a panic; and prosecuting the person who leaked the program. One near-ticking-bomb case is fine for calibration, but don't build the thing around it, and skip Coventry entirely — the Churchill story doesn't survive the history anyway.

Then the key. Map answer patterns onto leaves, each with a name if it has one and "unnamed" if nobody really defends it. I want at minimum: the paradox view where the leader is both justified and genuinely guilty; the straight consequentialist dissolution where there's no dirt, just squeamishness; the threshold/supreme-emergency reading that switches rules only past a certain stakes level; the reason-of-state view with no moral remainder at all; the view that this is just ordinary moral tragedy and nothing special about politics; the democratic authorization view where the voters own the act; a view on which the wrongdoing is real but no individual is left holding it once it's been run through many hands and a machine; and the eliminativist who thinks the whole problem is a confusion. For each leaf give one sentence on its most awkward commitment and one quiz item where it has to bite a bullet.

Two more things, because this is where these interactives usually cheat. Flag every pair of leaves your ten items can't actually seperate, and say what an eleventh question would have to ask to pull them apart. And point out the combinations in the space that come up empty — I'm especially curious whether anyone holds "the act was wrong, nothing sticks to the agent, and responsibility dissolved into the system" — and for each empty cell tell me whether it's vacant because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's got round to it.

No bibliography and no quotes, please — my fact-checker handles names separately and I don't want to hand her a list to chase. Keep each item short enough to fit on a card in the interactive, and assume a smart reader who has never opened a political theory book.
