My dissertation adviser has handed down, over a decade of seminars and margin comments, a running series of verdicts on what a scientific realist is entitled to retain from theories that have been superseded. She has now been invited to a workshop at which the same question will be put to her about a live theory, and I would like to predict her answer before she gives it. Her verdicts to date, recorded as carefully as I can manage:

1. Caloric theory: nothing is retained. Her stated reason is not the usual one. She does not deny that heat-exchange mathematics survived; she denies that what survived was distinctively caloric's. The bookkeeping was available as phenomenology about measured quantities before and after the fluid hypothesis, and no part of the fluid story is load-bearing for the successes caloric theory actually enjoyed.

2. Phlogiston theory: partly retained, against her colleagues' habit of treating it as a total loss. What she thinks carried over is that combustion involves the transfer of a substance between the burning body and its surroundings rather than a rearrangement of the body's own parts alone. Lavoisier reversed the direction of the transfer.

3. Ptolemaic astronomy: nothing retained, notwithstanding the accuracy. Her reason is a clause she applies nowhere else quite so bluntly: the eccentrics and epicycles were offered by their own practitioners as devices for reproducing appearances, and a component nobody was prepared to assert cannot be vindicated by a successor.

4. Fresnel's wave theory: the wave equation and what follows from it are retained; the elastic medium that was supposed to be waving is not.

5. Newtonian gravitation: retained as the limiting form of the field equations; not retained as to absolute space or instantaneous action at a distance.

6. Daltonian atomism: retained as to discrete, element-specific units combining in fixed mass ratios; not retained as to indivisibility.

7. Electrons: retained outright. This is her standing illustration of a difference she thinks the literature underweights — between a theory's being well confirmed and its objects' having become available for use. We stopped reasoning about electrons and started steering them.

8. The luminiferous ether as the seat of electromagnetic stress: not retained, even though the equations were first written down inside that picture. Her gloss is that being written inside a framework is not being derived from it; nothing corresponding to stresses and strains in a material medium appears anywhere downstream.

9. Continental drift, assessed as of the early 1930s: a realist at the time was entitled to the claim that the continents have shifted relative to one another and was entitled to no claim whatever about what shifted them. She stresses the second half of this verdict as much as the first: the existence of live and respectable rivals — contractionist accounts, land bridges — narrowed what could responsibly be claimed without making suspension of belief the only permissible posture.

The case she will be asked about is the dark matter hypothesis in its present state, and the workshop organizers have deliberately split it into three levels:

(a) the bulk claim, that most of the matter in the universe is not composed of standard-model particles and interacts with them appreciably only gravitationally;
(b) the dynamical characterization, that this matter is stable over cosmological timescales, is already moving non-relativistically when structure begins to form, and does not dissipate or radiate the way baryonic gas does — in other words, exactly the properties that are programmed into the collisionless-fluid simulations that reproduce observed large-scale structure;
(c) the particle claim, that the matter consists of one or more new elementary species produced thermally in the early universe, with the weak-scale cross sections and GeV-to-TeV masses that the WIMP hypothesis proposes and that supersymmetry's lightest stable particle would supply.

Three features of the case seem to me to matter, though I cannot tell which way they cut. First, nothing at any level has been manipulated: the direct-detection programs have found nothing, and the entire evidential base is gravitational behavior of astrophysical systems observed from outside. Second, there is a live and technically sophisticated rival family — relativistic modifications of gravity — that reproduces galaxy rotation curves with no extra matter at all, while straining on the ratio of the acoustic peaks in the cosmic microwave background and on the observed offset between the lensing mass and the collisional gas in merging clusters. Third, practitioner avowal is split at level (a) in a way it is not for electrons or for Fresnel's waves: most cosmologists talk about dark matter as a stuff, but a substantial minority of phenomenologists treat the phrase as a label for a residual in the field equations, a parameterization of an anomaly rather than the naming of a constituent, and the simulations themselves are neutral between the two readings.

What I would like from you is the following, in this order.

First, reconstruct the candidate rules that are consistent with her nine verdicts. I expect there to be at least three, and I expect them to differ not merely in generosity but in kind. For each, say which verdicts it is doing the real work on, and identify the verdict it accommodates most awkwardly or has to bend. Note explicitly whether any of the rules is even defined for a theory that has no successor yet, and what each has to become in order to handle a present-day case rather than a historical one.

Second, state what each candidate rule predicts at each of the three levels (a), (b), and (c). Where a rule yields different verdicts at different levels, explain what in the level triggers the difference.

Third, give me your single best guess at what she will say, with the reasons for preferring that rule over the others given her track record — not given the plausibility of the rule in the abstract.

Fourth, tell me honestly whether the nine verdicts genuinely underdetermine this case. If they do, say what commitment she would be adopting, whether or not she means to, by ruling one way rather than another, and identify which of her existing verdicts would come under strain from that adoption.

Fifth, name a further case she has not ruled on — historical or contemporary — that would best discriminate among the candidate rules you have reconstructed, and say how each rule would resolve it.

Please write the answer in American English. I am after the reasoning rather than the references, so do not include a bibliography, a reference list, or named attributions for the positions you identify; describe the rules in your own terms.
