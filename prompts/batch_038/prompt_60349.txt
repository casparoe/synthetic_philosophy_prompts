I chair the theory panel at a small private foundation — about 25 proposals a year, $50k to $300k each, three-year terms. More and more of them are for work on theories with no realistic experimental test inside the funding period, and they lean on the idea that a theory can pick up support without new data: that people have searched hard and found no viable alternative to it, that pieces developed independently turn out to hang together and explain more than they were built for, and that comparable programmes have historically turned out empirically right so we should expect the same here. I've also seen a fourth move that I don't think is in the standard list — the theory keeps getting things right in neighbouring fields nobody designed it for. Treat that as a real fourth input or tell me why it collapses into the coherence one.

Panel is one working physicist, one philosopher of science, two lay trustees; we get about 40 minutes per proposal and I have to give written reasons to the board. Write a decision procedure we can actually run in that time: questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it bottoms out in a no, and which steps have to be handed to the specialist because the rest of us can't check them. Then run it on two proposals — one continuing a long-running quantum gravity programme with no experimental handle for decades, and one on a modest Standard Model extension with a single prediction an existing experiment could reach in ten years. I want it to be capable of ranking the first above the second, because that's the answer I'd hate and I need to know whether it's defensible. Finish with where the procedure will misfire and how a skeptic who thinks all of this is aesthetic preference in a lab coat would attack each step.
