I write a science-and-ideas column and I'm trying to pin down what I actually mean by "science" before I file, because I keep using the word as a compliment. My gut verdicts:

- Newtonian mechanics: science, even though we know it's wrong.
- Psychoanalysis, the Freudian kind: not science. Nothing ever counts against it.
- Astrology: not science, obviously.
- The dinosaur-killer asteroid hypothesis: science, even though nobody can rerun the experiment.
- Descriptive natural history — somebody spending ten years cataloging which beetles live on which ridge: definitely science, though I couldn't tell you what would falsify a beetle catalogue.
- Parapsychology: here's my problem. They pre-register, they run the controls, they publish there failures, they've been publicly humiliated for decades. On self-criticism they beat most of physics. But I want to say it isn't science.
- The black-box neural net that now beats the physics-based weather models, where nobody can say why it works: I want to call that science too — and I notice that sits badly with what I just said about parapsychology.

My own tentative rule, which I came up with after a long lunch with a physicist: a field is a science to the degree that there's some specific event in the world that would make its practitioners professionally embarrassed. Call it the flinch test.

Two things I want. First: does the flinch test actually deliver all seven verdicts? If not, tell me which of my verdicts has to go, or what clause you'd bolt on to save it — and what the clause costs, because I can feel a principle getting complicated enough to explain anything. Second: rule on a case I didn't list, which is interpretability research on large language models, where the subfield's entire argument right now is about whether its findings replicate at all.

Bottom line up front: give me the repaired principle, or your replacement, in two sentences before anything else, then work the cases. 450 words hard cap, and please don't pad it with a history of Popper.
