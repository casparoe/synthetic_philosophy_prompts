I teach a philosophy-of-history module, ~30 second-years, one 90-minute seminar. They've had exactly one lecture on path dependence, which means they've heard the phrase "history matters" and think they get it. I want an in-class game where they run into the thing themselves, and specifically I want it to pry apart two ideas that my lecture mushed together: that a small early event can swing the final outcome (contingency), and that once you're past some threshold you can't climb back out (lock-in). And the "would the same outcome have arrived anyway?" question shouldn't be something they argue about in the abstract — I want them to be able to answer it with data from the room.

Here's the device I've been chewing on, improve it if you can see something better: run five or six tables in paralell with identical rules but different randomised opening moves, then have the whole room compare tables at the end. So the seminar becomes a little set of possible histories and we can literally look at the spread. The thing I want them to leave seeing is that "which option wins" can be close to a coin flip while "some option wins and everyone's stuck with it" is close to guaranteed, and that those aren't in tension — they always think that's contradictory.

I also want a historian role at each table: one person doesn't play, just keeps a log, and at the end has to write two sentences explaining why their table landed where it did, in a confident causes-not-accidents voice. My bet is that nearly every historian will end up telling a story that makes a fluke sound inevitable, and I want the debrief to spring that on them by reading a few aloud next to the actual log. If you think that won't reliably happen, say so and tell me how to rig the role so it does.

What I need from you: full rules and a payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money (small free stakes are fine, chocolate is fine, I just don't want to be running an economy), a materials list I can prep in under an hour, a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 90 including the comparison phase, and debrief questions in the order you'd actually ask them. Nobody hears a piece of jargon until after the comparison.

Please don't reach for the war horses — no QWERTY, no VHS vs Betamax, no metric system, no Windows/Mac. Invent the thing they're locking into. It should have some texture, and the option that's clearly better in the later rounds should be visibly better to the players, who still can't get to it.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including the nasty case where several tables converge on the same option by luck and it looks like inevitability when it's really just a small sample. And be straight with me about which feature of path dependence the game can't reproduce: I already know the parallel-tables trick is a cheat, since real history doesn't get rerun and all my tables share one room, one facilitator and one rulebook — I want to know what else the cheat hides, and what I'd have to supply verbally to cover it.

Finally, how confident are you that this design actually delivers the two-part distinction rather than just a vaguer "yeah, early choices matter"? Point at the specific piece you're least sure about and say what you'd swap in if it flops on the day.
