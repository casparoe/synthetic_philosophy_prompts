**Right but Early? Malthus, the Verdict Problem, and the Accounting of Failed Long-Run Predictions**

**1.** Malthus's principle, stated at its most compressed, is that population, if unchecked, grows geometrically while the means of subsistence grow only arithmetically, so that subsistence constitutes a ceiling on population which is enforced either by the positive checks (mortality, famine, epidemic) or by the preventive checks (the postponement or avoidance of marriage and childbearing). The empirical record is awkward in both directions. For most of agrarian history, real incomes appear to have been pinned near subsistence, and English wages in the centuries before 1800 do not look obviously better than those of the Roman or medieval periods. Then the ceiling broke: two centuries of sustained growth in output per head accompanied, eventually, by falling fertility. On any straightforward reading the prediction failed. Yet it is still common to hear that Malthus was right but early, or right about the mechanism if not the schedule, or right for his own epoch. This paper asks a narrow question: what would have to be the case for any of those verdicts to be true rather than merely consolatory?

**2.** A failed prediction refutes a bundle, not a theory. The bundle contains the core mechanism plus auxiliary assumptions, and I want to divide the auxiliaries in two. *Boundary auxiliaries* are claims that the mechanism's enabling conditions obtain here — that the society in question is inside the mechanism's domain, that the relevant markets or institutions exist, that no countervailing force has been introduced. *Rate auxiliaries* are the claims that convert a mechanism into dated quantities — elasticities, speeds of adjustment, the magnitude of expected technical change. With that division in hand, a failed long-run prediction can be defective in one of four ways. It can suffer a *phase error*: the mechanism is intact but the timing is wrong. A *gain error*: the mechanism is directionally intact but its magnitude is negligible against other forces. A *domain error*: the mechanism holds, but only in a subregion of the territory originally claimed. And a *sign error*: the mechanism points the wrong way. My proposal is that the first three admit of a "right but early"-style defence and the fourth does not, because repairing a sign error requires substituting a different mechanism, at which point the continuity of the theory is a matter of courtesy rather than fact.

**3.** A further condition. The verdict "right but early" should be available only where the theorist could have specified in advance some observation that would distinguish *not yet* from *never*. Without that, every failed prediction is early until it is finally declared dead, and the phrase does no work. Call this the *pre-registered onset condition*. Malthus appears to fail it, since the principle is asserted as constant and universal rather than as operative up to a threshold or a date. But the test as stated may be far too strong. It would condemn most of pre-twentieth-century science, including celestial mechanics before perturbations were fully computed. So perhaps the test should be comparative rather than absolute: not whether an onset condition was registered, but whether the theorist's confidence in the schedule was warranted by the measuring apparatus actually available to him.

**4.** Where does Malthus in fact go wrong? There are two candidate answers and they belong to different boxes. The first is a rate-auxiliary failure: he underestimated the growth of yields, and more deeply he lacked any account of induced intensification of the kind Boserup later supplied, in which population pressure itself becomes the cause of agricultural innovation rather than its victim. That is a gain or rate error and it looks rescuable. The second is worse. The preventive check assumes that improving prospects lead people to marry earlier and have more children; the demographic transition shows improving prospects leading people to have fewer. If we describe the prudential calculus as the same mechanism with new parameters — children valued for quality rather than quantity, the opportunity cost of parental time rising with wages — then this too is only a gain error and Malthus survives. If we describe it as a reversal of the direction of response, it is a sign error and he does not. The whole question therefore turns on a criterion of mechanism identity that section 2 assumes and never supplies. This is the weakness I am least able to fix on my own.

**5.** I want to add an accounting vocabulary, with the caveat that it may be decorative. When a prediction fails and the community does not abandon the theory, the failure is placed in *escrow*: acknowledged, itemised, and unpaid. The theory's standing should then be marked down as a bond trades below par, and each subsequent rescue that is not accompanied by a novel successful prediction adds leverage. A theory is *bankrupt* when it can no longer fund new predictions out of its mechanism and can only fund them out of adjustment. On this accounting, Malthus's estate was largely inherited by the theories that superseded him: the pressure variable reappears in Boserup with its arrow reversed, and the prudential calculus reappears in the economics of the family with its parameters reset. That suggests a fifth verdict I had not planned for — *wrong but productive* — where the mechanism as stated is false but the variable it identified does genuine work in the successor theories.

**6.** The Simon–Ehrlich wager is instructive because it is the rare prediction whose window was fixed in advance: five metals, inflation-adjusted, prices in 1980 against prices in 1990, settled in Simon's favour. Commentators subsequently observed that over other windows Ehrlich would have won. That observation is not an objection to my point but its confirmation. A dated wager with a settlement rule eliminates the phase-error defence by construction, which is precisely why such wagers are epistemically valuable; it also over-penalises, because the loser may be right about the mechanism and unlucky about the interval.

**7.** Provisional conclusion. Malthus is *right but provincial* with respect to the pre-industrial era and *wrong but productive* with respect to the extrapolation from it. The residual worry is whether a domain restriction is ever anything other than a rescue wearing better clothes.
