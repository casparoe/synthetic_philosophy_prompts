ok so I just got out of a roughly three hour argument with my housemate (computational neuroscience PhD, third year, brilliant and insufferable) and I need a piece of writing to settle it, or at least to annoy him with. He thinks the free energy principle is the most important thing to happen to cognitive science since the computational theory of mind; I think its a tautology wearing a variational calculus costume. Where we do agree, weirdly, is that the discourse around it is genuinely funny — specifically the way it slides back and forth between "the brain literally implements variational Bayes and we can measure it" and "anything that has persisted long enough to be observed is, trivially, a free energy minimizer," which are doing very different jobs and never quite shake hands. He kept saying the second version is just the first one generalized, I kept saying generalizing until nothing could count against you isn't generalizing. Anyway. We agreed the argument was unwinnable and that the situation calls for satire, so that's what I want from you.

The piece: a parody agony-aunt column, "Dear Surprisal," running in the wellness supplement of a mid-market Sunday paper. Around 800 words, four or five reader letters with the columnist's replies, plus one small sidebar (a reader poll, a teaser for next week, whatever fits the genre). Invent the columnist — give her a name and a sign-off — and please don't name any real researchers or quote any actual paper; I want the target to be the style of discourse, not a person. The jokes have to come from getting the framework right rather than from making it sound vague: active inference, the way expected free energy splits into epistemic and pragmatic value, precision weighting and the advice to just "up the precision on your prediction errors," the Markov blanket and boundary disputes, hierarchical levels of inference, model complexity penalties, and above all the dark-room problem — the fact that a really good free energy minimizer ought to be sitting motionless in a windowless room, and the various elaborate ways people explain why we don't. The central engine should be the heads-I-win-tails-you-lose property: whatever the letter writer did, the columnist shows it was expected-free-energy minimization all along, whether that meant refusing to leave the house for eleven days (familiar policies, pragmatic value, low ambiguity) or impulsively flying to Lisbon with a stranger (epistemic foraging, resolving uncertainty about the self). At least one letter should ask, politely and in good faith, whether the theory is falsifiable and what single observation would count against it — and the reply should fail to answer that while being completely coherent in its own idiom, ideally by absorbing the question as further evidence.

One important constraint: don't let it be a pure dunk. Make one letter a real human problem — grief, say, or a parent whose adult kid has stopped calling — where the columnist's answer is technically flawless and totally cold, and where you can feel that the framework has something true to say and also cannot comfort anyone. Keep it deadpan throughout; the columnist should never wink, and a reader who's never heard of any of this should still find it funny as a piece of bad advice writing, while someone who has spent three years in that literature laughs at the specific mechanics. Then, after the column, a section titled Field notes, about 200-250 words: say which real feature of the literature each letter is exaggerating, and be honest about where the satire overstates the case — there is careful, testable work done under this banner, and I want the piece to be fair enough that my housemate forwards it to his lab group chat without being able to complain about it.
