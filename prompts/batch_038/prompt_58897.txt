I'm prepping a session for a bioethics seminar on the therapy/enhancement distinction and I want to build it around a real institution rather than the usual thought experiments, because the rules are on the books and someone has to apply them case by case.

The case is anti-doping. WADA's prohibited list works on a two-out-of-three test as I understand it: a substance or method gets banned if it enhances or could enhance performance, if it poses a health risk to the athlete, and if using it violates the spirit of sport — any two of those three is enough. Then separately there's the therapeutic use exemption system, which is where the philosophy gets weird. An athlete with, say, asthma or type 1 diabetes or ADHD can get permission to use a banned drug, but only if all four conditions are met: the drug is needed to treat a real medical condition such that withholding it would significantly impair their health; its use is highly unlikely to produce any performance gain beyond what you'd expect from returning the athlete to their normal state of health; there's no reasonable permitted alternative; and the need for it isn't itself the result of earlier doping.

So the exemption literally institutionalizes a line between restoring normal function and adding to it, and then asks a medical panel to certify which side of that line a given dose falls on.

What theory of health, or of normal function, does that presuppose? Is it a statistical-species-norm view where normal is just where the distribution sits, a proper-function view, a welfarist view, or something sports-specific that has no analogue in medicine? And what does the fourth condition — no gain beyond a return to normal — even mean as a measurable claim when the pharmacology is continuous and there's no clean pre-disease baseline for a lot of these athletes? Then look at the inconsistencies: simulating altitude in a hypoxic tent is permitted while EPO is banned, though both raise red-cell mass; beta-blockers are banned only in precision sports like archery and shooting, where steadying your hand is arguably the skill; caffeine is off the list entirely and only monitored; carbon-plated shoes are fine but the full-body polyurethane swimsuits got banned. Does the whole edifice presuppose one coherent account of what's wrong with enhancement, or several incompatible ones bolted together, and which parts are underdetermined enough that a utilitarian, a Kantian, and a virtue theorist could all sign off on the same rules for different reasons? Finally, tell me what would actually have to change in the exemption process if you dropped the return-to-normal baseline and replaced it with something like a pure harm-plus-fairness test — and whether the practice would survive that.
