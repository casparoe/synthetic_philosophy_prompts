I spent thirty-four years in experimental physics, the last twenty of them on a neutrino detector operated by a collaboration of some two hundred people drawn from nine institutes. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy rather seriously, and I have found that the questions I spent my career absorbing without noticing are ones philosophers have names for. I should like to put a case to you, and then an argument that grew out of it.

The case is this. In the collaboration's charter, the decision to announce a result was taken by the governing board — about forty people — under a rule I can only describe as premise-wise. A draft claiming evidence for an effect had to be supported by three separate findings, each put to the board as its own question: that the energy calibration was sound; that the model of the background was adequate; that the excess in a certain energy window survived the agreed statistical cuts. If all three carried, the charter treated the claim as following, and the paper went out over the names of every member who had not formally requested removal.

On the occasion in question all three findings carried comfortably. Yet when the question was put informally afterwards, a majority of the board said they did not accept the claim. Their reasoning was not inconsistent: each of them granted the calibration, the background and the significance at the stated level, and held that this still did not amount to evidence, because of how many energy windows the analysis had scanned before it found that one. They rejected the sufficiency rule, in other words, not the premises. The charter did not permit them to vote on the rule. The paper was submitted. The excess evaporated in later data, and a correction was issued years afterwards by a board of which perhaps a third had sat on the original one.

Two of my former colleagues — call them Edwin and Margaret — fell to arguing about this at an alumni lunch, and kept it up by electronic mail for some weeks. I was present for the first exchange and have the correspondence. Neither is a philosopher. I have set out their moves in order, and I should like you to go through them.

1. Edwin: The collaboration never believed anything. That sentence is shorthand for a complicated fact about individuals and a document. Documents do not believe; organisms do. So when the excess evaporated there was no group that turned out to be wrong — there were people who were wrong and a rule that was badly designed.

2. Margaret: Pressed consistently, that objection dissolves Edwin too. What believes is not Edwin but some pattern of activity distributed across regions of his brain, none of which separately believes the excess was significant. And his reduction cannot accommodate the datum: a majority of the board disbelieved the very claim that was published over their names. If a group's belief were merely a summary of its members' beliefs, that could not happen.

3. Edwin: It can happen, and there is nothing occult about it. The published claim is a deterministic function of the ballots and the charter. Feed in the votes and the rules and the sentence comes out. I looked up the aggregation result Margaret relies on, and it says only that you cannot simultaneously admit every possible pattern of votes, treat votes alike, aggregate each proposition independently of the others, and guarantee a consistent collective output. The charter sacrifices independence. Remove one condition and the paradox collapses into arithmetic. What is irreducible about arithmetic?

4. Margaret: What is irreducible is the direction of fit. Once the charter exists, the group can be committed to a conclusion most of its members reject, and those members can be required to act on it: to sign it, to run the supporting analysis, to answer for it at the press office. Edwin's account explains where the sentence came from, not why it now has authority over the people who produced it. And the group persisted. Half the original board has died or moved on; the correction was issued by the same collaboration, apologising for a claim most of those signing it had never accepted. On Edwin's account there is nobody left to apologise.

5. Edwin: There we see what the group agent really is — an instrument for relocating responsibility. If the collaboration is the believer, then I, who minuted my dissent and asked to have my name struck, am party to the error all the same. Margaret says I am bound because I joined under known rules; but then the obligation is mine, a promise I made to identifiable people, and the group has dropped out of the explanation. Every duty she can name is a person's duty, owed to persons.

6. Margaret: Precisely — and that is all a plural subject ever was. Not a super-person hovering above us, but each of us committed to the others to uphold whatever the procedure yields, including outcomes none of us would have chosen. Edwin cannot write that into a list of individual intentions, because each commitment is conditional on the rest: I intend to run the analysis as part of our intending to announce it. Take the others away and mine evaporates. The interlocking is a real structure, and it is not in his list.

7. Edwin: Then we agree about the furniture and quarrel over the label. If a plural subject is nothing over and above interlocking individual intentions plus a charter, the group agent does no explanatory work, and I will happily grant the façon de parler. But Margaret wants more than a manner of speaking: she wants the group's belief to trump mine. That has consequences for a real person who said no on the record. The question is not ontological; it is about who may be the subject of "was wrong", and the answer must be someone who could have done otherwise.

8. Margaret: He could have done otherwise. He could have resigned, refused the by-line, or gone public. He did none of it: he ran the final analysis and let his name stand. The dissent was expressive, not effective. And if Edwin believes the only alternatives are wholehearted endorsement or exit, that is his individualism talking. Being bound by a joint commitment one opposes is an entirely ordinary condition — of a marriage, a faculty, a collaboration of two hundred people.

I should declare my position in the old episode, though not in the argument about it: I was among those who voted against the claim, minuted my dissent, and allowed my name to stand on the paper. I mention this only so that you know where my account of the facts comes from. It does not, I hope, tell you which of them I ought to side with, and I have tried not to let it.

What I should like from you is an adjudication, move by move. For each of the eight, give a rating — clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, clearly right — with a sentence or two of justification, and say specifically which claims within the move you are rating up or down, since several contain more than one. Then tell me who is right about which of the two questions they keep sliding between: whether a collaboration can literally believe and intend something no member believes or intends, and whether a dissenting member is bound by a decision he opposed. Those may have different answers, and I suspect they have been conflating them. Finally, say what both of them are missing, and whether any part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal.

Two further requests. I am not after references or a reading list; I would rather you argued the matter out than pointed me at a shelf. But I do want to know where you are confident and where you are extrapolating, and I want you to flag any move whose verdict depends on a contested reading of a term — "irreducible", "bound", "the same collaboration" — rather than quietly adopting one reading and scoring accordingly.

And one last thing. I spent four decades being told when my analysis would not bear weight, and I found that far more useful than being humoured. So: if my report of this argument is itself confused — if the case as I have described it cannot carry the load either of them puts on it, if Margaret has garbled the aggregation result, or if the two are talking past one another in a way that makes move-by-move scoring the wrong instrument altogether — say so plainly and explain why, and tell me what the better question would have been. I would much rather have that than a tidy set of verdicts produced to oblige me.
