I am writing the central chapter of a dissertation on the metaphysics of interpretability, and I need to settle an evidence-grading scheme for it. The chapter presses a single question: whether interpretability results license realism about neural-network representations — whether a claim like "this layer represents the direction of travel of the next token" is made true by something in the trained system that is independent of the interpretive scheme we bring to it — or whether such claims are only disciplined redescriptions of a system's dispositions. My working criterion, which I want the grading to respect, is this: a body of evidence supports realism to the degree that it makes the correct interpretation non-arbitrary, meaning that the model's own causal organization, rather than the interpreter's choice of probe, label set, or task battery, singles out one attribution as right and rules out nearby rival attributions that predict the same behavior.

Rate the following five kinds of interpretability evidence for how much realism each can carry under that criterion:

1. Decodability: a concept can be recovered from internal activations at above-chance accuracy by a simple probe trained on held-out data, and the recovered encoding generalizes across inputs.
2. Selective necessity: damaging or corrupting a component degrades performance on precisely the tasks the attributed representation is supposed to subserve, while leaving unrelated tasks intact.
3. Interventional sufficiency: injecting or amplifying the recovered signal, in the absence of the usual input, produces the behavior the attribution predicts.
4. Anticipatory correspondence: states in an intermediate layer can be read out in the format of the model's own final layer and predict what the model eventually outputs, so the system appears to have settled on a result before it produces it.
5. Algorithmic reconstruction: an explicit program, whose internal states we stipulate are representational in an uncontroversial sense, reproduces the trained component's input–output behavior across the task.

I want a committed grading, not a survey. Assign each an integer from 0 to 10, with all five scores distinct so that they also yield a strict ordering; no ranges, no ties, and no retreat to "it depends on the architecture." In the item for the kind you judge most systematically overrated in current practice, say so explicitly and mark which of the two failure modes is responsible — the evidence permits too many rival attributions, or it establishes something about the task rather than about the system.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one per evidence kind, in the order listed. Each item gives the score and one or two sentences of justification. No introduction, no preamble, no summary or concluding paragraph.
