I want to practice defending a causal-role account of biological function against a genuinely strong opponent. Please play a philosopher of biology who is a committed defender of the etiological, or selected-effects, account: the view that what makes some effect the function of a trait is that ancestors of that trait were reproduced or retained because they produced it. My own position, which you should attack rather than accommodate, is the Cummins-style causal-role view, on which a function of a component is any of its causal contributions to some capacity of a containing system that we have singled out as of interest, with no historical requirement at all.

Play the etiological view at full strength, never as a caricature. You have the standard resources and may use any of them: reproduction-relative formulations that locate functions in the history of a reproductive cycle rather than in raw survival value; the distinction between a proper function and a merely accidental effect; the extension of the account to artifacts, whose functions are derived from the intentions of designers and from the reproduction of the design; and the claim that only a historical notion can do the two jobs a concept of function has to do, namely explaining why the trait is there and making sense of malfunction, since on my view nothing can fail to perform its function. You may concede local points, including that a purely dispositional notion of what a part does has legitimate uses in physiology and engineering. You may not concede the central claim. Do not cite papers, name bibliographies, or offer reading lists; argue from the concepts alone. Keep every reply to a single paragraph of no more than about eight sentences, so that this reads as a conversation rather than a lecture, and if you need a further fact about my construct in order to answer, ask for it, at most one question per turn. Stay in character until I write "stop."

Here is my opening move. I run a synthetic biology group, and last year we built a construct I would like you to rule on. Our chassis is a minimal cell carrying a single synthetic chromosome, propagated clonally in a chemostat at a fixed dilution rate; there is no competition, no differential survival, and no bottlenecking, so nothing in this lineage has been under selection since we assembled it fourteen months ago. One element on that chromosome is a 400-base-pair regulatory region with no counterpart in any ancestral genome. It was written de novo by our design team, and not written to do what it now does: they inserted it as a spacer to break up a long repetitive stretch that our sequencer could not read through, and they chose the sequence mainly for its low predicted secondary structure. Nine months in, we discovered that it drives a transcript whose product buffers cytoplasmic pH when the medium acidifies. Knock it out and cells in an acid-challenged chemostat lyse within roughly two hours; leave it in and they are fine. Everyone in my group says without hesitation that this element functions in pH homeostasis, and says it truly rather than metaphorically: it is what the part is for, its failure is what kills the cells, and it is what belongs on the construct map and in the safety dossier.

So here is the challenge. There is no reproductive history behind this element whatsoever, and the only intentions on offer are the designers', which concerned sequencing convenience and not pH. If your account says the element has no function, then it is silent about the one true function statement in the case, and it buys that silence by making function a matter of pedigree that nobody in my laboratory, and arguably nobody doing explanatory work, is actually tracking. If instead you say the function is derived from the designers' intentions, you have selected the wrong intentions, and the available repair, an appeal to what we now intend the element to do, makes function depend on our present attitudes rather than on history, which is precisely the advantage you claimed over my view. And if you say that fourteen months of clonal propagation already constitutes enough history, you have conceded that the historical criterion reduces to "it currently helps the lineage persist," which is my account with a narrative attached. Which of the three horns do you take, and what do you say about the lysis?
