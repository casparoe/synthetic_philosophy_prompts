I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student, and I've gotten roped into maintaining the case-card deck for week five of our department's intro seminar on meaning in life. The exercise works like this: each card is a hundred-word sketch of a life, the students vote on whether it was a meaningful one, and then I read out the convener's answer key. The convener is my supervisor — call her H — and she's been writing the key for eleven years, on three grades: yes, qualified yes, no. On some cards she adds one line giving her reason; on others she writes nothing at all. I've transcribed the current deck below more or less verbatim from her handwriting, so any errors are mine, she writes like a doctor.

1. **Rosalind.** Put on the cello at six by her father, world-class by twenty, never once wanted to play. No friendships to speak of, no partner, no hobbies, on tour eleven months a year until she dies at 61 in a hotel room. Her playing was, by any standard anyone has ever set, superb, and it changed how a lot of people hear the suites. Grade: qualified yes. Her line: "There was value in what she did; there wasn't value in it for her."
2. **Dov.** Fifty years in a shed classifying bottle caps by moulding defect, in meticulous ledgers, entirely self-taught, nobody asked him to. He'd have said he was doing something worthwhile, and he was happy in a way most people aren't. His sister bins the caps and pulps the ledgers. Grade: no. Her line: "He got exactly what he wanted and it amounted to nothing."
3. **Priya.** Thirty years organising a housing campaign on her own estate — meetings, legal letters, door-knocking, two decades of being shouted at. Deeply committed, deeply loved by the neighbours, genuinely good at it. The campaign loses outright: the estate comes down, the residents scatter, no plaque, and nothing she started survives her by five years. Grade: yes. Her line: "She was in it with other people and she never stopped."
4. **Aldo.** Spends his adult life building a small separatist militia: recruits, trains, funds it, gives bored young men a purpose and a brotherhood. Coherent, self-authored, difficult, partially successful. He's not a sadist and he believes every word of it. One of his people eventually shoots someone. Grade: no. No line, just the word, underlined twice.
5. **Margit.** Founds a hospice and works in it for thirty years; patients adore her; she is competent, warm, and completely clear-headed about death including her own. She is also sustained throughout by the conviction that her dead son sees and approves of what she does — a conviction she never tested, never could have tested, and systematically avoided evidence against. Grade: qualified yes. Her line: "The work was real. The comfort was a story she told herself."
6. **Ezra.** A drifter: two years as a genuinely fine boatbuilder; four years as a good father, then he leaves; a decade of small-time cons that hurt people without ruining them; six years of devoted hospice volunteering in a town where nobody knew his name; then nothing much. He dies in a bedsit. The good stretches were excellent while they lasted and none of them connect to one another. Grade: qualified yes. No line. (This one took her longest, apparently — the TA before me said she sat on it for a week.)

For what it's worth I've tried the obvious labels on her and none of them fit all six cleanly for me, which is embarassing given my dissertation is adjacent to all this. Wolf-style fittingness, Kauppinen-ish narrative unity, Metz's communion thing, Bradford's difficulty-plus-success, and some Nagel-flavoured two-register absurdism all seem to break somewhere in that list, and I can't tell whether the problem is her or my reading of them.

Next year I get to propose two new cards, and I want the first one to be a card where H and I actually disagree — a card we both grade "yes" is useless in the room, it just hands the students back their own intuition. Here's my draft:

**Tomasz.** Hired at 38 to catalogue the papers of a mid-century pharmaceutical chemist. Works alone for 22 years: no partner, no close friends, colleagues describe him as content but opaque. He is under no illusions whatever about the pointlessness of his own life — he tells people plainly that he is a man putting paper in order for nobody, and he's fine with it. What he doesn't know is that he reveres the chemist as a benefactor of humanity, and a large part of why he works at all is homage; had he known what the chemist actually did, he'd have burned the finding aid. He finishes it to an extraordinary standard, retires, and dies of a stroke at 63. Two years later that catalogue — its cross-references, its dating, its sheer completeness — becomes the evidentiary spine of the case against the chemist. Thousands of victims get redress. The finding aid is still in use today.

So, four things. First: what grade does she put on Tomasz, and what's the one-liner she'd write if she writes one? Second: lay out the candidate rules that are consistent with all six existing grades — including any I haven't listed — and say for each one what it predicts for Tomasz and why. Third: is there any single rule that fits all six, or is the key ad hoc somewhere, and if it's ad hoc, which card is where she has to fudge? Fourth: I owe her a second card too, so design the one further case that would actually settle which of the surviving rules she's running, and say what each rule would say about it.

Please be committed rather than even-handed. I don't want a survey with the choice left to me — tell me the grade you'd bet on, tell me which single rule you'd bet on, and tell me where you think you're most likely to be wrong about her. If two rules genuinely tie on all six cards and Tomasz doesn't separate them, say that flatly instead of averaging them into a mush. And tack on a short note at the end about how much your answer leans on treating her one-liners as evidence of her rule versus treating them as after-the-fact decoration, because that's the judgement call I can't make from inside the department.
