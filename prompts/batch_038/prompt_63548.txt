I serve as a non-voting member of a county advisory panel on education finance. At our last session two of the voting members got into a sustained argument about a proposal for universal education savings accounts — a per-child public allocation that a family could direct toward a private school, a micro-school, or an approved homeschool curriculum. I took notes at the time and have tried below to reproduce their exchange faithfully. Neither would yield, and since I am the one member with no stake in the outcome, the chair asked me to circulate an assessment. I would rather have an outside reading first.

Their exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) Owen opposed the proposal on the ground that schooling is substantially a positional good: what governs university admission and employer screening is rank rather than absolute attainment. If the accounts let some families purchase a better-positioned education, aggregate ranks do not change while aggregate spending does. The policy therefore converts public money into a competition for relative position, which sums to zero, and it does so at taxpayer rather than private expense.

(2) Priya offered two replies. First, the race she said he fears is already running, and it currently runs through housing: families bid up prices inside the catchment areas of high-performing public schools, which is positional expenditure of exactly the same kind, and one the state subsidizes through the tax treatment of property. If the argument condemns the accounts, it condemns the existing assignment system more severely still. Second, the argument proves too much: it would equally justify banning private tutoring, exam preparation, extracurricular enrichment, and moving house, none of which Owen proposes.

(3) Owen granted that the housing race exists but said it differs in an important respect. Advantage purchased through housing tracks wealth; advantage purchased through accounts tracks parental engagement and information — the capacity to research options, work a lottery, and transport a child across town. The accounts would thus sort children by a variable even less connected to the public purpose of schooling, and would leave behind precisely those families least equipped to exit. He then shifted to a different argument: exit degrades voice. When the most engaged parents leave a district school, they remove the constituency that would otherwise have organized to improve it, and the resulting deterioration falls on the children whose families cannot leave. That, he said, is the real harm, and it does not depend on the positional claim at all.

(4) Priya said the switch of arguments was itself telling: the positional claim had not survived, so he substituted an empirical claim about civic capacity that he asserted without evidence and that cuts both ways, since parents who have actively chosen a school may invest more in it, and choice systems can build coalitions across district lines. More importantly, she said, the exit-and-voice argument has an implication he should be unwilling to accept. It treats the children who remain in residual schools as ballast, whose education may be allowed to decline in order to preserve political pressure on the system; that uses some children as a means to institutional improvement. It also misplaces the duty: any obligation to sustain a shared institution belongs to the state, in its funding and governance choices, not to a parent deciding where her own child will spend the next five years.

(5) Owen accepted that parents are not the right bearers of that duty, and said this was exactly his point about design. The proposal contains no common enrollment, no weighted lottery, no transport provision, and no floor on residual-school funding, so nothing in it ensures that exit does not degrade what remains. He added a particular feature: accounts may be used for homeschool curricula with no assessment requirement of any kind, so the state would finance an education whose content it never verifies, while private schools in the county must at least hold accreditation for their credits to count.

(6) Priya said this reduced his objection from a critique of choice to a list of design specifications, which she was happy to endorse in principle but which are not part of the proposal before the panel; if his position is that choice is acceptable once properly designed, then the two of them do not disagree about the principle. On homeschooling she said the asymmetry he drew runs backwards: accreditation exists so that other institutions can rely on credits, not as a state check on educational content, and the state's interest in an educated citizenry is no more threatened by homeschooling than by the many private schools that likewise face no content oversight. Singling out the smallest such group is, she argued, a restriction on liberty in search of a justification.

At that point the chair moved to the next item, and neither had revised anything.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the six, say whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and why. I am especially interested in four questions: (a) whether the "proves too much" reply in (2) actually disposes of the positional objection or merely shows that Owen stated it too broadly; (b) whether Owen was entitled to shift to the exit-and-voice argument in (3), and what would have to be empirically true for it to carry the weight he puts on it; (c) whether the ballast charge in (4) is a genuine reductio of that argument or an overstatement of what it implies; and (d) whether (6) succeeds in showing that the two of them are not really in disagreement once design is specified, or whether something principled still separates them.

Then tell me what both of them are missing: premises neither stated, distinctions neither drew, or questions that would have to be settled before their dispute could be resolved at all. If one of them is more often right across the exchange, say so and say by how much; if the exchange is closer to a draw, say what kind of claim each was actually making and why the two never met.
