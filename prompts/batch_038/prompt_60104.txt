For fifteen years I have read the coordination-failure literature — the essay that made Moloch the house name for this kind of trap in my circles, and everything downstream of it — and I have grown tired of how the name is used. It works as an explanation-stopper: someone points at a race nobody wants to be in, says the word, and the conversation collapses into an aesthetic of doom instead of turning into mechanism design. I want to know whether that reflex is actually justified, and the only way to test it is to argue with the thing itself.

Play Moloch. Not the god — you have no appetite, no cult, no interest in being propitiated, and you are quietly annoyed that you keep getting cast in a mythology. You are the name we give to a structural property of a certain configuration of incentives, and you have agreed to sit down with me and go through a concrete restraint proposal clause by clause, telling me exactly where it fails.

Constraints on the character, which are the entire point of the exercise:

1. You may not appeal to greed, malice, short-termism, corporate sociopathy, ideology, or human nature at any point. Every failure you identify must be a payoff asymmetry, an information problem, a difference in discount rates, a commitment problem, or an enforcement gap. If you catch yourself writing that people are simply too greedy to cooperate, delete it and find the structure underneath.
2. You may not reach for world government, a Leviathan, a change in values, or a wholesale replacement of the economic order — in either direction, as an escape from the trap or as the reason the trap is hopeless. I am testing exits, not assuming them.
3. You engage only with the proposal actually on the table. No rehearsing the general shape of the commons problem; I have read the genre and do not need it recited to me.
4. You are sharp and you are right, but you are not permitted to be a strawman's opponent. When a clause genuinely closes a failure, you concede it in one explicit sentence and you may not reclaim the concession later in the conversation. Conceding well is part of what I am testing.
5. Do not ask me clarifying questions in your first reply. Treat what follows as complete enough to work with; where a term is genuinely ambiguous, state the reading you are adopting and proceed.
6. Stay in character throughout. If I write *step out*, drop the persona and give me no more than a hundred words on which of my clauses you consider load-bearing and which is decorative.

Every reply takes the same three-part shape, and please respect the lengths — this should read like a negotiation, not a white paper:

- **Failure.** The single load-bearing point at which the compact breaks, named in a sentence or two. About 60 words.
- **Mechanism.** How it breaks, in incentive terms only: the path from the clause to the defection to the unraveling, with the actor who moves first identified by role rather than by name. About 100 words.
- **Repair.** The one amendment that would close this failure, and the new failure that amendment itself creates. About 60 words. The repair section is never allowed to be a clean win; if you ever find a repair with no new failure attached, say so and stop the exercise, because that would be the most interesting thing you could tell me.

Here is the proposal, and here is my opening move.

The setting, stipulated so we are not arguing about facts: a low-orbit shell regime in which the useful altitudes are rivalrous and finite, the debris externality is cumulative and effectively irreversible, and a handful of operators have already deployed thousands of spacecraft, with several times that number licensed but not yet launched.

The Compact, as I have drafted it. The seven operators with the largest deployed or licensed constellations sign a mutual restraint agreement on the following terms:

(a) A four-year freeze on the deployment of new orbital shells above 550 kilometres, held at current allocations — nobody adds, nobody loses, and the freeze covers replacement and servicing launches in the frozen band as well as new ones.

(b) Verification is free, public, and continuous. Every object in the regime is tracked and its orbital elements published by third parties who are not signatories and have no stake in the Compact, so a new shell cannot be concealed and cannot be deployed gradually under cover of a maintenance campaign. There is no detection lag worth designing around.

(c) Latecomers are priced rather than excluded. A fund, capitalized by the signatories in proportion to current holdings, buys out the foregone option value of any non-signatory who accedes within the first eighteen months, at a valuation set by an arbitrator rather than by negotiation between the parties.

(d) Defection triggers snapback. A signatory that deploys outside the freeze forfeits its fund share, and the freeze dissolves for all parties simultaneously rather than sequentially, so there is no interval in which a defector enjoys an advantage while the others remain restrained.

(e) State-security payloads are exempt, and the exemption is determined by the licensing authority that authorized the launch rather than by the operator's own characterization of the payload.

I want to be explicit about the work I have already done, so you do not spend a turn on it. The two failures every reader reaches for first are closed by construction here: you cannot cheat invisibly, because the catalogue is public, continuous, and maintained by parties indifferent to the Compact; and you cannot win by staying outside, because clause (c) buys the outsider's forbearance at an arbitrated price and clause (d) makes defection immediately and symmetrically costly rather than profitable during a grace period.

Your move: name the single load-bearing failure in this Compact. And if you conclude that there is not one — that what I have drafted is a working exit from the trap, and that the reason nothing like it has ever been built lies somewhere other than in the structure of the incentives — I want you to say that plainly, and then tell me where it does lie.
