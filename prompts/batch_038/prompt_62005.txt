Been reading LessWrong-and-adjacent stuff since the Overcoming Bias days, and I've finally let myself be talked into giving a talk at my local rationality meetup about moral revolutions — how norms like duelling and footbinding actually die rather than get argued to death. Prepping it, I've realised my own views here are a pile of commitments that I've never checked against each other, and I can't tell which ones are load-bearing. So I'd like an audit, not a lecture. Here's the package:

(a) the mechanism is honour in Appiah's sense — duelling, footbinding, the Atlantic slave trade stopped when they became dishonourable, when carrying on with them cost you standing instead of earning it; argument and interest only get purchase after that flip, and the flip has to come from inside the honour world, from people whose own honour is on the line. (b) I buy Williams's version of the duelling case from that Tanner lecture of his named after the Sophocles play: the arguments against duelling had been around for centuries and did nothing, nobody was refuted, the practice just became impossible — and I also take on board the pessimistic half of that essay, that there's no neutral standpoint from which to say the new ethos grasps the moral truth better than the old one did. (c) for abolition specifically I lean interest-side: the West Indian sugar economy had stopped being the profit machine it was, and the humanitarianism rode in behind that. (Yes, that's the other Williams, I do keep them straight, mostly.) (d) moral psychology: Haidt-ish, reasoning is largely post-hoc, people don't update on syllogisms, they update on identity and sentiment and what their people are doing. (e) and yet I think careful written argument is the highest-leverage thing I personally can do — I think farmed-animal welfare and AI risk got onto the map becuase they were argued for, and my plan for the next year is a series of long argument-driven essays on moral progress; the escape route I reach for whenever someone presses me is that arguments work slowly, on the tone-setters, and change what's respectable to say, so they sit upstream of the honour flip rather than competing with it. (f) progress is real and directional: slavery ending and footbinding ending are improvements and not just a change of fashion, and I will fight relativists about this. (g) the reformers get credit — the abolitionists' petitions, the natural-foot societies, the newspapermen who made duellists look ridiculous were causally load-bearing, not decoration. (h) the change is fast and discontinuous: footbinding went inside roughly a generation and British duelling collapsed faster than any plausible sum of individual mind-changes, which I read as a tipping point, where once enough people defect the price of continuing jumps — so a coordination shift, not a persuasion cascade. (i) and two things I keep feeling are incoherent about myself but can't pin down: I think honour is a social construction with no normative authority whatsoever, nothing is really honourable, it's a status game, while also thinking it's among the most powerful causal forces in history; and I think shame is a bad motive for a decent person to act from, while also thinking shame is the only thing that has ever got large-scale moral change done.

The test case I want to keep is the American civil rights movement, which I take to be the clearest moral revolution of the twentieth century and which I think worked because its leaders appealed to the American creed — holding the country to its own professed ideals. That's my evidence against (d) and for (e). I'd also like to be able to say we can deliberately build new honour codes around live issues, shame around things like reckless AI deployment say, even though the people doing the building (me and my friends, basically) don't inhabit any honour world at all and wouldn't be caught dead duelling.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look bad and dissolve once you spell them out properly, and please rank the real tensions by severity instead of dumping them on me as equally awful. For each one, what's the cheapest revision and what does that revision cost me — and note I'm not telling you which I'd rather sacrifice, because I honestly don't know. Then tell me the single change that would clean up the most, and be blunt about whether the essay-writing project in (e) survives the rest of the list or whether it's just me rationalising my comparative advantage. No bibliography, no reading list, no suggested sources — I want the reasoning, not the references. And write it in British English please.
