I'm teaching a seventy-five-minute whiteboard session on Jeffrey conditionalization to first-year graduate students in computational neuroscience. All have done a statistics course and can apply Bayes' theorem mechanically, and all have encountered popular claims about the brain as a Bayesian inference machine, but none has seen subjective probability or coherence arguments. Plan the explanation rather than giving it to me. Tell me the order the pieces must come in and what each depends on: in particular, how much apparatus — a belief state as a whole probability function, ordinary conditioning as the special case in which some proposition becomes certain — has to be in place before the problem Jeffrey conditionalization addresses can even be stated. I was going to carry the whole thing on a colour-judgement case, a swatch seen under shifting light where the observer cannot settle on any proposition to become certain of; tell me whether that is the right carrier, what such an example must have and must lack, and what to substitute if it will not bear the weight. Then map the misconceptions. I expect "uncertain evidence just means noisy evidence, so put the noise in the likelihood and ordinary conditioning still does all the work," and "the new input probabilities are read straight off the experience," and probably "this contradicts Bayes." Say where in the sequence to head off each, and which will do the most damage if it survives to the end. Close with one question I can put to the room that would reveal whether they have grasped the rule as a constraint on the relation between two belief states rather than a recipe for producing the second, and with an explicit list of what I should leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving out.
