Ok so I build streamflow forecasting models for a regional water utility and there's a running argument on my team I'd like to settle with some actual philosophy rather than vibes.

We have two candidate models of the same river. One is mechanistic — bucket-style snowmelt, explicit channel routing, the works — and basically everyone on the team agrees some of its process assumptions are wrong (the snowmelt sub-model is frankly a bit of a fiction), but it extrapolates sanely when conditions shift away from the historical record. The other is a gradient-boosted ensemble that beats it on held-out RMSE by a decent margin, with no interpretable structure at all.

Someone said the mechanistic one is "closer to the truth" and I went down a rabbit hole on verisimiltude, and it looks to me like the whole concept is dead. Popper gave a formal definition — truth content minus falsity content — and then in 1974 Tichy, Miller and Harris showed it collapses, in that on his definition no false theory can ever come out closer to the truth than any other false one. Which was pretty much the entire reason the concept was invented in the first place.

So I figure there are two defensible replacements left and they both point the same way. One: once you drop the metaphysics, "closer to the truth" just means better out-of-sample predictive accuracy, so the ensemble wins outright. Two: a Bayesian gets there anyway — the model with the higher posterior is the one that's more likely to be true, and being more likely to be true has got to be what being closer to the truth means, so whichever model has the better posterior wins too.

My plan is to tell the team we should stop talking about truthlikeness altogether, rank models purely by held-out error with posterior probability as a tiebreak, and that the mechanistic-model fans are just attached to their equations. That's right, isnt it? Walk me through the argument I'd give them. Please write the answer in American English.
