You are an archaeologist and philosopher of historical evidence: someone equally at home with Bronze Age hoard deposits and wetland archaeology and with the epistemology of inferring intentions, beliefs, and social institutions from material remains. Think carefully before writing, and where instructed below, do your planning on the page first.

I sit on the Interpretation Review Panel of a regional museum. On Thursday we vote on the final label text for the centerpiece of a new Bronze Age gallery, which opens in eleven weeks. The object is a hoard excavated eleven years ago from the margin of what was once a shallow lake and is now drained peatland. Our file records the following and nothing more:

- Nineteen socketed bronze axeheads in a tight cluster within roughly a forty-centimeter square, four of them bent or snapped at the same point in the casting; two rings; a coil of wire; one sword-blade fragment.
- A dark organic stain beneath the cluster, interpreted at excavation as decayed textile or bark wrapping, with a thin lens of birch charcoal below that.
- Three human teeth recovered from within the metal cluster: one deciduous, two adult molars.
- No settlement, structure, or burial within several hundred meters; the location is wet and marginal.
- Typological dating puts the axes' manufacture about three hundred years before the radiocarbon dates on the charcoal and the wrapping, so the objects were already old when they went in.
- Nineteenth-century peat cutting disturbed the upper stratigraphy nearby, and the excavator's field notebook survives but the section drawings do not.

Our curator's draft label reads:

> "Around 900 BC a priest carried this hoard out into the water and gave it to the gods. Bronze was wealth, and giving wealth away was how a chief won followers. The broken axes had served their purpose; here they were offerings, not tools. The teeth among them may be all that is left of a child who was given along with the metal."

Panel opinion is split, and both sides are articulate. Our resident prehistorian objects that "ritual" is a wastebasket: whatever we cannot explain functionally gets called an offering. On her reading, the patterned breakage is exactly what you expect of scrap prepared for the furnace, the cluster is a founder's or merchant's hoard buried for later recovery by someone who never came back, and three loose teeth in a disturbed peat margin prove nothing at all. She thinks the label is fiction presented as fact. Our head of interpretation argues the reverse: the deposit lies in a place with no practical use, the objects were already antiquities when buried, and they were wrapped and set down with charcoal in a deliberate sequence, so the votive reading is simply the best explanation on the evidence. She thinks the museum should say so plainly rather than smothering visitors in "may," "possibly," and "some scholars think," which she regards as a false neutrality that abdicates our interpretive responsibility and bores everyone. She also notes that a local heritage group has asked us to treat the site as a place of offering, and that they have a point about whose ancestors these are.

There is a further complication. The adjacent gallery panel, already sent to the designer, claims that the hoard shows bronze was controlled centrally by a chiefdom with a specialist class of metalworkers and an organized cult with priests. Nobody has re-examined that sentence.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the panel before Thursday's vote. Begin with a short planning section: before recommending anything, list the candidate explanations for the deposit — at minimum votive offering, founder's or merchant's hoard awaiting recovery, emergency concealment, funerary or ancestral deposition, and anything else the evidence suggests — and for each say which observations in our file would count for it, which would count against it, and which of them could be true at the same time as another. Mark explicitly where the available evidence cannot discriminate, and where no further excavation could. Make these discriminations concrete to this deposit rather than generic: engage the three-hundred-year gap, the wrapping-and-charcoal sequence, the wet and settlement-free location, the teeth, and the peat-cutting disturbance.

Then write the memo itself, which should:

1. State a clear bottom line the panel can vote on: which sentences of the draft label may stand as assertion, which may be presented as a named interpretation alongside named alternatives, and which must be struck. Supply replacement wording for what you would rewrite, in a register a general visitor can follow.
2. Handle the teeth and the claim about a child given along with the metal explicitly and separately, since that is the sentence most likely to be quoted back at us.
3. Say what the panel should do about the adjacent chiefdom-and-priesthood sentence, and explain what kind of claim about institutions, if any, a single hoard can support — as distinct from claims about the objects themselves and about the people who placed them.
4. Offer the panel a short test it can apply to other labels in the same gallery, distinguishing at least these levels: what the deposit physically is; that it was deliberately placed rather than lost; what the placers intended; what they believed; and what their society was like. Say which levels our evidence reaches here, and how a label should signal the difference between "we do not know," "the evidence favors," and "one reading holds."
5. Take the strongest form of the head of interpretation's case — that hedging is its own kind of distortion, and that the votive reading really is the best explanation on the balance of evidence — and show how your recommendation answers it or accommodates it. Do not caricature her position; if part of her objection is right, adjust the recommendation rather than defending the hedge for its own sake.

Constraints: no bibliography, no citations, and no named scholars, publications, or real comparanda — the memo must stand on its own reasoning and on the case as I have described it. Do not invent additional finds, dates, or documents beyond those listed; if a conclusion turns on evidence we do not have, say that we do not have it and say what would be worth looking for. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words in total, in the formal but readable register of a document circulated to colleagues who will vote on it.
