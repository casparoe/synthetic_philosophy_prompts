I have no formal training in philosophy or in computer science. I have been blind since birth, I work as a copy-editor, and for several years I have been turning over a question I cannot get out of my head, mostly by reading what I can find and thinking it through on my own. I would like a serious answer that does not assume familiarity with the literature and does not consist mainly of directions to it.

The question concerns what is sometimes called the symbol grounding problem: the claim that a system which only ever manipulates symbols — text in, text out, with no eyes, no hands, no way of acting on anything — cannot attach genuine meaning to those symbols, because meaning supposedly requires some kind of contact between the symbol and the world. I want a number from you, not a survey.

The proposition I want your credence in is this: a system that has had no perceptual or motor contact with the world at any point in its existence, and whose only inputs and outputs are strings of text, can nevertheless genuinely understand at least some of the words it uses — where genuine understanding means that, if the matter were settled by facts about the system rather than by our willingness to treat it as an understanding agent, those facts would include its symbols being about things rather than only about other symbols. Behavior does not settle it. I grant that such a system could talk about the sea, about pain, and about the smell of rain in ways indistinguishable from a person who has known all three; the question is whether there is anything behind that performance. My own credence is about 0.7, and I want to know what it rests on.

One consideration I have not seen handled well should enter your decomposition as a term rather than as an aside. I have never seen a color. Everything I know about color I acquired from other people's descriptions, comparisons, and corrections, and I am confident I understand the word blue well enough to edit a paragraph containing it. If understanding requires perceptual contact, then either I do not understand that word, which I reject, or grounding can be inherited through description. But there is an asymmetry that bothers me: when a sighted person described blue to me, they were pointing at something they themselves stood in contact with. A training corpus contains no hand-offs of that kind. Nobody in it was addressing the system, and the chain it inherits may contain no link at which anything was ever handed to it. Does borrowed grounding survive that difference, or does it quietly depend on the borrowing being a genuine transaction?

Please answer in the following sections, in this order:

1. **My number** — about 100 words. State your credence in the proposition plainly, and say in one sentence what would have to be the case for it to be true.
2. **The decomposition** — about 500 words. Break the question into the sub-questions your credence actually depends on, with a probability and no more than three lines of justification for each. Please include at least: whether aboutness could consist purely in relations among symbols; whether perceptual or causal contact with the referent is necessary for it; whether grounding can be inherited by description, and how much weight my color case carries as evidence that it can; whether understanding is all-or-nothing or graded, and what happens to the proposition if it is graded; and whether the question has a determinate answer at all or is partly a verbal dispute.
3. **The combination** — about 150 words. Show how those numbers yield your headline figure, and identify which term that figure is most sensitive to: which one, shifted twenty points, would move the total furthest.
4. **The weakest link** — about 150 words. Which term you trust least, and the single piece of evidence, experiment, or thought experiment that would most change it.
5. **Where I am wrong** — about 150 words. If your number differs from my 0.7, name the one term you think I have mispriced and explain why.

I want a committed answer. Take a position, give your reasons for it, and do not retreat into the observation that both sides have something going for them. If you think the question is ill-posed, say so as your position, assign the credence that follows from that view, and defend it — but do not use ill-posedness as a way of declining to answer.
