I'm a doctoral student in an interdisciplinary archaeology-and-complexity program, and for the past year I've been building a dataset that assigns "collapse events" to a set of premodern polities. My operationalization is deliberately blunt. For each polity-region and each fifty-year window I score four proxies: total settled area across surveyed sites; the rate of dated monumental construction; the volume of standardized administrative and elite-marking material (sealings, inscriptions, coinage, palace storeroom ceramics); and osteological welfare markers, chiefly adult stature, enamel hypoplasia, and subadult mortality. A collapse is then a sustained and simultaneous deterioration across at least three of the four. My test cases are the southern Maya lowlands, the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Late Bronze Age, Rapa Nui, and the post-Roman West.

At a progress seminar last week a visiting historian waved all of this away with a single line: "civilizations don't collapse, they transform." He used it twice, for two different purposes, and I couldn't separate them at the time. First, against my framing: he said that calling the end of the Western Roman Empire a collapse rather than a transformation smuggles in the judgment that something was lost, when in fact Latin, Roman law, the episcopal hierarchy, and much of the rural settlement pattern all persisted, and the average person alive in 500 CE may well have been no worse off than their forebears two or three generations earlier. Second, and more damagingly, against the measurement itself: he said my index cannot in principle distinguish collapse from transformation, because everything I count — fewer inscriptions, smaller houses, less monumental building, thinner administrative archives — is equally consistent with a society shedding a superstructure it no longer needed, so that a decline in the volume of administrative artifacts is at least as good evidence for decentralization as for failure.

Could you lay out the distinct claims that one sentence might be making, as a menu I can bring to my committee rather than a single reading I must either accept or reject? For each, identify what kind of claim it is — stipulative or verbal, a claim about identity and persistence, a claim about welfare, a claim about the unit or scale of analysis, a methodological claim about what proxies like mine can and cannot measure, or a claim about periodization and teleology — and then give me a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and false, or unfalsifiable as stated. What I need most is the last step. Which of these readings, if any, actually defeats the index as I've described it, and which can I concede in a sentence and move on? Please also tell me whether the objection carries different force across my cases; my suspicion is that it does much more damage in the Roman case than in the Maya or Rapa Nui ones, and I would like either the reason for that or the reason it's wrong. I'm already fluent in the Diamond-versus-revisionist debate and in Tainter's diminishing-returns account of sociopolitical complexity, so don't spend space summarizing either — I want the disambiguation, not the literature review.
