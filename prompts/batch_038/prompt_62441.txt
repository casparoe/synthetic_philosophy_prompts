Two of the guests on a panel I helped organize last fall kept arguing in the hallway afterward, and because I was the one holding the door I ended up writing down most of the exchange. One is a bioarchaeologist who works on human skeletal series from early farming villages in Anatolia and the Levant; the other is a development economist who works on poverty traps and long-run growth. Neither would let it go, and I stayed out of it. Here is the argument as I reconstructed it.

1. Bioarchaeologist: The Neolithic transition made the people who lived through it worse off, and the claim is comparative, not romantic. Adults in well-documented foraging economies secure an adequate diet with roughly a couple of days' work a week; the villages that follow domestication show shorter adult stature, far more dental caries, more enamel defects, and more infection-related bone lesions. The same deterioration appears at every independent center of domestication, which is about as good a control on local history as this field ever gets. Farming bought a much larger population at a lower quality of life, and once the demographic ratchet closed there was no going back.

2. Economist: The comparison is rigged by the baseline. Your foragers come from ethnographies of groups pushed into marginal refugia — deserts, arctic coasts — or from post-contact populations already caught in trade networks and demographic collapse. Comparing Anatolian villagers to twentieth-century Kalahari foragers is not a comparison of subsistence modes; it is a comparison of centuries and continents. And "made worse off" presupposes that the option they declined was on the table.

3. Bioarchaeologist: Fair enough about the ethnography, but the osteology does not need it. Stature and dental pathology are measured on the actual people, in sequence, before and after domestication, in the Levant, Anatolia, the Yellow and Yangzi valleys, Mesoamerica, and the Andes. If the same markers move the same direction at independent origins, the baseline objection you just raised does not touch them.

4. Economist: Independent origins are not independent samples if they share a cause — the same handful of storable, high-carbohydrate crops, the same shift to permanent settlement, the same commensal animals. More importantly, the lesion evidence has an interpretation problem you cannot wave away. A skeletal sample is a sample of the dead, and the dead were not a random draw from the living. A population showing more healed lesions may be a population in which people survived their infections long enough for the bone to record them; and if frailty varies across the individuals in a cemetery, the same lesion frequency is compatible both with worse health and with better health plus longer lives. If you cannot separate those, lesions are not a welfare index at all.

5. Bioarchaeologist: That is a reason to be careful with lesion counts, not a reason to discard them — and stature and caries are not lesion counts. Stature is a cumulative record of insult across the whole growth period, and caries tracks chronic carbohydrate load directly; neither depends on who survived an infection long enough to scar. Besides, if survivorship ambiguity defeats lesion data as a class, it defeats every use of the same data, including the long-run improvement story you want to tell about the last two centuries.

6. Economist: Stature responds to disease load and maternal condition as well as to calories, so it measures developmental stress — and you have just quietly identified developmental stress with welfare. That is the real issue, and it is not empirical. Your metric is per capita: health, diet breadth, hours of work. Mine is aggregate: farming supported orders of magnitude more human lives than foraging ever could. If value sums across people, the transition may be the largest welfare-increasing event in the history of the species. No additional osteology settles that, because we disagree about how to add.

7. Bioarchaeologist: The aggregative switch answers a question nobody asked. The question was whether identifiable people were made worse off, not whether the species-level total rose. And there are non-aggregative goods the record does speak to. Storable surplus lets a third party lay claim to your harvest; crops that ripen together, stand visibly in the field, and can be assessed, harvested, and carried off are exactly the crops that get taxed. Granaries, fortifications, sharply differentiated burials, and administrative record-keeping show up in the same places and roughly the same order as those crops. The evidence on subordination is a good deal less indeterminate than the evidence on totals.

8. Economist: Then "mistake" is the wrong word, which has been my point all along. A mistake requires an agent who could have done otherwise and had some way of knowing better. Domestication was a feedback loop among climate, demography, and plant genetics that ran for millennia, with no decision point and no visible destination. What you have described is a trap: entered without error, and not escapable once entered. Accept "trap" and we agree on nearly everything except whether moral vocabulary applies.

9. Bioarchaeologist: I will take "trap" for the causation and refuse it for the verdict. "Trap" says how it happened; it says nothing about whether it was bad for the people inside it. And the no-choice premise is too strong even historically: there are sequences in which farming was taken up and then dropped, held at a low level for centuries without ever producing the administrative apparatus, or combined with seasonal foraging and herding. If refusal and reversal were available at the margins, the trap had doors.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than simply declaring a winner. For each of the nine moves, assign a rating on a five-point scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification — including, where relevant, cases in which a move is right but only against a weaker version of what it was answering, and cases in which a move is technically correct yet does no work against the position it was aimed at. Then, separately: identify the one or two points on which everything else turns; say whether what is left standing at move 9 is a factual disagreement, a disagreement about value, or a disagreement about words, and explain how you would tell which; give the strongest argument available to each side that neither of them actually made; say what both of them are missing, including whether there is some framing of the question on which they are not disagreeing at all; and finally say who came out ahead overall and whether that verdict would change if the metric dispute in move 6 were resolved in one direction rather than the other.
