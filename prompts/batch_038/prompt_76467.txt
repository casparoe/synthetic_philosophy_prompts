I did thirty-five years of geochemistry for a state water agency and retired last spring. Since then I've been reading philosophy properly for the first time, mostly stuff about probability and induction, and I've come to think that something that used to look like a purely technical nuisance in my old work is actually a philosophical problem. I'd like a second opinion on it, because it's live and people are waiting on an answer.

The situation: a town I still consult for is trying to work out how much lead people are getting from thier service lines. They have a map of the network and essentially no measurements. Everyone agrees they should sample "at random," but nobody can say what that means, and the four schemes on the table give averages that differ by a lot:

- pick a pipe segment, every segment equally likely
- pick a household, every household equally likely
- pick a point along the network, take whatever pipe it lands in
- pick a liter of water that actually gets drunk

Long pipes serve more households, the old neighborhoods have both longer pipes and worse metal, and each scheme weights all that differently. The engineer they hired has concluded that the whole exercise is therefore empty: nothing favors one scheme over another, so any probability you write down is invented, and the honest move is to refuse to produce a number. I don't buy it and I've been unable to say why, which worries me, because maybe he's right.

Then there's a second layer that's worse. Once they settle a scheme they still have to say what they assume about the lead level in pipes nobody has measured. Spread evenly between zero and some upper bound? Evenly in the logarithm, so a doubling counts the same at 1 as at 100? Evenly in the fraction of pipes that exceed the action level? Each is a defensible way of spelling out complete ignorance and each produces a different answer to the question the council actually asked, which is what the chance is that a given house is over the limit.

My own hunch, and I don't know whether it's worth anything, is that the two layers aren't the same problem even though everyone treats them as one. The first looks settled by the physical world: how you actually draw the sample fixes the weighting, and if what you care about is exposure you weight by water people drink, full stop. The second doesn't look settled by anything at all. But I could be fooling myself — maybe the mechanism talk is just a way of smuggling in a preference I happen to have.

So, two questions. Is the engineer right, and if not, what exactly is wrong with his argument? And practically, what should they do — pick one scheme, run all of them, or something else? Be as definite as you can, but tell me where you're leaning on a philosophical commitment that isn't forced on you, and tell me what would change your mind: which argument or which piece of evidence would move you over to his side. And point at the step in your own reasoning you think is most likely to fail.
