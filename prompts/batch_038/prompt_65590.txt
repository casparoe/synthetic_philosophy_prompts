I sit on the graduate committee of a mid-sized philosophy department, and this spring we were charged with reviewing our oral preliminary examination: two hours, three faculty members, closed door, no written component, essentially unchanged since the program was founded in the early 1960s. The founding faculty are gone, the archive contains minutes recording only that the requirement was adopted, and no rationale was ever written down anywhere. Two of my colleagues, Marcus and Ines, argued about it after our second meeting in a way I found myself unable to referee; the committee deferred the decision to the fall, so nothing turns on my own view, and I'd simply like a careful adjudication of their exchange, which I'll report as neutrally as I can manage.

(1) Marcus opened by saying the requirement should be abolished. The archive supplies no reason for it; the people who could have supplied one are dead or long retired; its outcomes correlate with nothing we can measure, since students who perform badly in it are no more likely to struggle later; it consumes some forty faculty hours per cycle; and it predictably drives away applicants from groups we are already failing to recruit. He said that Chesterton's fence, as it is usually wielded, tells you not to tear down a fence until you understand why it was put there, but that when the reason is unrecoverable the instruction simply never expires, which converts an epistemic caution into a permanent veto.

(2) Ines said he had misread the point. On her account Chesterton's claim is not that a discoverable rationale exists somewhere, but that the practice's having survived is itself the evidence: for sixty years the oral has been scrutinized by faculty who had every incentive to drop it had it been costly and pointless, and none of them did, and survival under that kind of continuous low-grade testing is information, just not the kind that shows up in a spreadsheet. She added that departments store their reasons in habits rather than in minutes, and that the exam may be doing something structurally invisible to student-level metrics — for instance, forcing the whole faculty into a room together several times a year to argue about what each other's students actually know, which is plausibly the real source of the cross-subfield cohesion the program is known for.

(3) Marcus said that was unfalsifiable as stated. If survival counts as evidence of function, and the failure of every metric we can construct is explained away by the function's being unmeasurable, then no practice could ever be shown to be useless — including the surviving requirement that dissertations be bound in buckram, which everyone in the room agrees is a dead letter. He asked Ines to say what would count against the exam's usefulness, on the grounds that if nothing would, her position is not a hypothesis but a standing veto dressed as one.

(4) Ines accepted the challenge and offered a distinction. Buckram binding, she said, has no candidate mechanism linking it to anything the department values, whereas the oral has at least two, each independently attested in other programs: faculty coordination across subfields, and early identification of students who cannot think under pressure, a capacity none of our instruments tests. She stressed that she was not claiming the exam is proven valuable; she was claiming that the reversibility of the two options is wildly asymmetric. Keeping it costs forty hours a year and some morale, and the decision can be revisited annually; abolishing it is a one-way door, because if the oral turns out to have been load-bearing we will not know for a decade, by which time the faculty who could have explained what it was doing will have retired, and reinstating it will not restore it, since whatever value it had depended on its being continuous. She tied this to Burke's image of a society as a partnership among the living, the dead, and those not yet born, and to his remark that the individual is foolish while the species is wise: institutional experiments return their results on a timescale longer than the career of the person who runs them, so the abolitionist is the one party who cannot possibly have run the control.

(5) Marcus said the argument collapsed under its own weight. If the relevant effects take decades to appear and the faculty who understand them retire in the meantime, then waiting produces no knowledge either, because the information decays on the same schedule whether or not anyone acts. And the irreversibility point, he said, condemns every reform rather than this particular one: any long-standing practice you abolish is irreversible in exactly the sense Ines needs, since the version you destroy has a history you cannot reconstruct. Weighted as heavily as she was weighting it, irreversibility entails that no fence ever comes down, which is status quo bias with a philosophical gloss. He added that the cost she called recoverable — students who leave the program — is borne by people who are not in the room and cannot be compensated afterward, so the asymmetry between the options is not merely epistemic but moral.

(6) Ines replied that she was not defending every fence and would happily abolish buckram binding tomorrow. Her criterion was that when a practice has a plausible mechanism connecting it to goods that resist measurement, and when its abolition is not reversible within the tenure of the people deciding, the default is to keep it while lowering the cost of keeping it: a forty-minute oral, say, or a failure that is not terminal. Marcus's final move was that this is a compromise wearing a principle's clothes, because "plausible mechanism" does no work once a mechanism can always be imagined for any surviving practice, and because shortening the oral destroys precisely the continuity that, on her own account, was where its value lived.

Three things I'd like from you. First, go through the six moves one at a time and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per rating; I want the exchange scored move by move, not merely a verdict on the whole. Second, tell me whether Marcus and Ines are disagreeing about the same thing at all, or whether part of the impasse is that they take Chesterton's fence to assert two different claims — one about recoverable reasons, one about survival under selection — and never notice the divergence; if so, say which reading the argument can actually bear and which one collapses under pressure. Third, say who is more right overall, and then say what both of them are missing — in particular, whether any principled test exists for separating the fences that deserve the benefit of the doubt from the buckram, given that each of them gestures at such a test and neither manages to state one that survives the other's objections.
