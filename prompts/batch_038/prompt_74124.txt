I write alternative-history novels, which means every book I've published is basically one long counterfactual, and I've started reading philosophy of language to find out whether I know what I'm doing. Before I go further I want the spec rather than the theories: what does an account of the truth of counterfactual conditionals have to deliver to count as adequate? Give me the desiderata — the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse — with the puzzle behind each one. The ones I care about: the Oswald pair, where you can cheerfully accept the indicative version and reject the subjunctive version about the same shooting; backtracking versus non-backtracking readings, and how anything is meant to decide which is in play; antecedents that aren't merely false but impossible; whether 'if X had happened, Y would have' and 'if X had happened, Y wouldn't have' have to exhaust the options; and the free will problem where the nearest world in which I choose differently apperently has to contain a law-breaking miracle. Mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are just the house style of one particular camp, and if some subset turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — I gather there's a result that kills equating the probability of a conditional with the corresponding conditional probability — say so, because that's the part I most want. Skip the scorecard on the existing views.

Add one more that I doubt is on the standard list. In fiction the similarity metric isn't set by the actual world, it's set by genre and by what I've primed the reader to treat as salient, so my 'if the Duke had survived' comes out true or false relative to a standard the text itself supplies. Is that a genuine extra requirement on a theory of counterfactuals, or a fact about pretence the theory can leave on one side — and what does either answer do to the rest of the list?

British English please, and don't hand me a settled view: tell me which requirement you'd ditch first if you were forced to, and what argument would change your mind about calling something non-negotiable.
