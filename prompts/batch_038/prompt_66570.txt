I lead the appraisal team in a national infrastructure ministry. Roughly ten times a quarter we assess projects whose consequences run fifty to five hundred years out — coastal flood defences, a geological repository for long-lived radioactive waste, large-scale peatland restoration — and our recommendation decides whether several hundred million in public money is committed. Our standing guidance is discounted net benefit, and it has two failures we can no longer defend. First, it treats a harm as cancelled when the people who bear it would not have existed at all without the project. Second, it takes any forecast of future wellbeing at face value, so a population that has adapted to a depleted environment scores as doing fine. A parliamentary committee has asked us to show that we are not treating future people as residual claimants, and the chief economist who signs off wants to see, step by step, where a judgement about value is being made in place of a calculation.

Write the procedure my analysts should run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, which thresholds are floors that cannot be traded away against aggregate benefit, how to treat a harm whose bearers owe their existence to the project, what to do when the relevant quantity is a probability nobody can estimate, and the exact points at which the procedure stops and refers the matter to ministers because it is a question of value rather than fact. Assume the analyst has one day, an economics degree, and no philosophy.

Then run it on three cases: the flood defence, whose construction emissions and deferred maintenance burden fall on unborn people; the waste repository, whose risk is small, late, and irreversible; and a cheap, high-return scheme whose only defect is a low-probability contamination of an aquifer that cannot be cleaned. One of these should come out against the project the ministry wants approved — say which, and say plainly what the procedure cannot decide at all.

Close by identifying where this design is most likely to be wrong: which single objection, if correct, would force us to rebuild it on different lines.
