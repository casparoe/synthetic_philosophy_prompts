so I've been in a reading group on infinite ethics — aggregating value over infinite populations / infinite time — for about six months and we have completely stalled. everyone's read the same things, everyone agrees on what the theorems say, and nobody has moved an inch. I want you to diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Not survey the options again, I've read the surveys.

the camps, as best I can state them:

1. the discounters. Koopmans-style impatience: you get a complete ordering, strong Pareto is respected, but you've weighted generations unequally and the whole answer rides on a parameter nobody in the room can defend except by appeal to how it makes the math work.
2. the overtaking / limit-of-means people (Ramsey–Weizsäcker–Gale line). anonymous, doesn't beg any questions against the far future, but it's only a partial order — some streams come out incomparable — and it can't be represented by a single real-valued function, so it doesn't give you a decision procedure.
3. the shift-invariant averaging / Banach-limit people. anonymous and translation-invariant, but they flatly violate strong Pareto (a world strictly better for generation 1 and identical thereafter can come out tied), and the object you need to build them isn't constructible without the axiom of choice.
4. the hyperreal / lexicographic people. they keep completeness by moving to a richer value structure, but then the ranking depends on which infinitesimal or ultrafilter you grabbed, so the arbitrariness has just moved house.

plus a fifth group who say none of it is live, becuase either the future is finite anyway or the genuinely infinite case is the cosmological one — Bostrom's canonically infinite worlds and infinitarian paralysis — which has a different structure and different problems, so the temporal-aggregation machinery is answering a question nobody has.

my hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than accept: the mathematics isn't in dispute at all. every impossibility result here is just a different axiom getting sacrificed, and the camps differ about which axiom is sacred, which is a normative commitment in mathematical drag. and underneath that there may be a verbal issue about what anonymity is supposed to capture — nobody in the group actually means invariance under arbitrary reshufflings of infinitely many generations when they say each generation counts equally, so the impossibility results might be demolishing a straw axiom that was doing no work in anyone's moral thinking.

is that right? or is there a real formal question buried in here that a new theorem could settle — say, whether completeness is something a moral theory is obliged to deliver? and is there a hidden empirical question (is the future actually infinite, is the world canonically infinite) that the formal debate is a proxy for, such that the philosophers and the economists are answering different questions under one heading?

tell me what would count as progress given whatever diagnosis you land on, including if the honest answer is nothing and here's why the thing is structurally stuck. please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, I can't check any of it and don't want it — just the argument.
