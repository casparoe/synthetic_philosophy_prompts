I cover a state legislature for a small nonprofit newsroom, and for two years Ive been tracking one member of our Substance Review Commission — call her H — because she's the swing vote on basically every scheduling recommendation. Her record so far:

- cannabis edibles: voted for a potency cap and child-proof packaging, against a ban, on the grounds that the problem was the product form rather than the plant.
- fentanyl analogs: voted for emergency up-scheduling aimed at suppliers, then in the same meeting voted to fund naloxone and test strips for users.
- kratom (plant leaf, mild opioid-ish effects, no approved medical use, scattered liver-injury reports, big organized user base that says it got them off painkillers): voted against scheduling it, for an age floor and mandatory contaminant testing.
- a lab-made stimulant with essentially no documented harm at the time, no medical use, sold online as "not for human consumption": voted to schedule it anyway, arguing you don't wait for the body count when the chemistry is new.
- psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression (decent trial data, no addiction signal, needs a sitter present): voted for a licensed supervised pathway, against any retail sale.
- alcohol: voted to recommend a bigger excise tax and against loosening sales hours, saying it does more total damage than half the stuff on our schedule.
- and twice she's voted to decriminalize simple possession across the board.

Here's what's coming to her next month and I want to call it before it happens. A synthetic sold online as a mood supplement — I'll just call it QV-40. It's chemically unrelated to anything we currently schedule, so the analog statute doesn't reach it and the commission would have to act on its own. Early data: low abuse potential, low acute toxicity, no approved medical use, no trials underway. The catch is that in a minority of users it seems to produce a lasting, maybe permanent, flattening of emotional response — the affected people describe it as the volume getting turned down and staying down. On top of that the product sold online varies something like twentyfold in potency, so nobody can dose it reliably; we've had a few dozen ER visits, no deaths. And the online community around it is loud and insists the flattening *is* the benefit: the people who want it to stay legal are largely the ones who used it to stop reliving something. Same mechanism, benefit for some, harm for others, and the harmed ones picked it themselves.
