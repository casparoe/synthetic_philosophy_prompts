I've read enough of the LessWrong-era material on Kelly betting and "geometric rationality" that I assumed I had this settled, and then a friend who has been deep in the non-ergodicity economics literature sent me a passage from his own write-up:

"Expected-value reasoning is broken because it averages over parallel universes you will never visit; you live one life in sequence, so the only quantity that matters is the time average. Kelly betting is the repair: it is the stake fraction that maximizes the long-run growth rate of your bankroll, and since growth rate is what compounds, Kelly is optimal for anyone who expects to face that kind of bet repeatedly — your utility function drops out entirely, because log utility simply falls out of the time average. This is also why Kelly cannot go bankrupt: staking any fraction at or below the Kelly number guarantees that you finish ahead of anyone who stakes differently. Full Kelly is therefore the rational default, and the half-Kelly that practitioners actually recommend is only a concession to people who find volatility unpleasant."

Much of this matches my intuitions, but "your utility function drops out" and "cannot go bankrupt" sit badly with me, and I don't trust my own instincts enough to say why. Could you audit the passage claim by claim, separating what a decision theorist or mathematical-finance person would endorse, what is a defensible simplification, and what is wrong or badly overstated? Three points I especially want resolved: (a) whether the growth-rate optimality theorem really licenses dismissing utility, or whether something has been conflated there; (b) what is and is not guaranteed about beating a different fixed fraction, and over what horizon; and (c) whether half-Kelly has a justification that has nothing to do with squeamishness. Finally, tell me which of his conclusions survive once the errors are corrected, and whether the corrected version still supports the strong claim he opens with about expected-value reasoning.
