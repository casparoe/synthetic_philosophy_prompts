I build the tooling and permission layer around LLM agents at a mid-size company — the sandboxing, the tool allowlists, the audit logs, all of it. No philosophy training whatsoever, so forgive me if I mangle the vocabulary. Over the last few months our design reviews have picked up a word that everybody uses and nobody has ever defined: someone will say "the agent will just JOOTS out of that guardrail", or "that isn't an escape, that's just JOOTSing", and the meeting carries on as if something had been established. It comes from Gödel, Escher, Bach, which I read when I was about nineteen and mostly remember for the pictures. There's a chapter in it literally called Jumping out of the System, and earlier on the MU puzzle, where you start from the string MI, apply four rewriting rules, and get asked whether you can ever reach MU. You can't, and the way you see that is by counting the I's and noticing they can never land on a multiple of three — a fact that isn't anywhere in the rules themselves. That's the example that stuck with me, because it's exactly the shape of argument we have at work: the proof isn't inside the system, but it isn't magic either, it's just arithmetic you do off to the side.

So before I read anything more, and before I use the word in another review, I want the spec rather than another essay. What does an account of jumping out of a system have to deliver to be worth anything? Give me the desiderata as a numbered list — the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse, the results it has to stay consistent with — and for each one, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a nice-to-have. Purpose, so you can calibrate: I want something a non-philosopher could actually apply in a design review to tell whether a claimed escape is a genuine jump out of a system or just the system having more rules than we thought. Requirements that only make sense inside a philosophy seminar are fine to include, but please mark them as such.

Cases it should handle: the MU puzzle and its invariant; the Gödel sentence, plus the awkward fact that there's a mechanical procedure for constructing it, which I think is Hofstadter's own reply to the people who argue minds can't be machines because we can see the truth of our own Gödel sentences; a compiler that reads and rewrites its own source; a program that works out it's in a VM from timing measurements; an agent that persuades a human operator to switch off a restriction (is the human inside the system or seperate from it?); and something with no rules at all, like a chess engine that stops playing chess. Distinctions I suspect get fudged: levels of description versus levels of control, a system reasoning about its rules versus a system simply obeying richer ones, syntactic versus semantic stepping-out, leaving a formal system versus leaving a physical sandbox, and a search that happens to find a way out versus a proof that no way out exists. Then the bit I actually want: which of these are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp, and whether some subset of them can't all be satisfied at once — my instinct is that "you can see from the outside that no derivation exists" and "seeing from the outside is itself just a bigger system" are in direct tension, and if they are, the list is the result. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate accounts. Finish with a short pass over the obvious contenders — Hofstadter's own use of the idea, the Gödelian anti-mechanist line, a purely hierarchical tower-of-systems account, and the deflationary view that JOOTSing is nothing over and above moving to a system with more rules — a couple of lines each against the list, table is fine for that bit. Don't bother with references or a reading list, I wouldn't be able to check them anyway.
