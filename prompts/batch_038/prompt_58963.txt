I got into a two-hour argument with my roommate last night (he's in nursing school, I'm not, which he kept reminding me of) and I want to know if I'm the crazy one. It started because he told me about a patient who died on a night shift where the nurse didn't respond to a pump alarm that was going off for like twenty minutes, and he said the nurse caused that death, plain and simple, and could lose her license over it. I said fine, I agree, but then I asked: was it also caused by the ten thousand other people on earth who could have been standing in that room and weren't? He said obviously not. I said then you can't really believe that not-doing-something causes stuff, something else is doing the work in your explanation, and he said I was being an annoying philosophy bro. Which, ok. But I've been chewing on it all morning and I can't get my own intuitions to line up either.

So here's what I actually think, going case by case, and you can see the mess. One: the on-duty lifeguard staring at his phone while a kid drowns ten feet from his chair — he caused it, full stop. Two: a hundred off-duty swimmers all watching the same kid from the sand, none of them going in — I do not want to say each of them caused his death, but I also think "the crowd let him drown" is true, so something caused it. Three: same beach, but only two people, neither on duty, neither moves — I'd say both caused it. So somewhere between two and a hundred my verdict flips and I have no idea where. Four: my neighbor promised to water my plants while I was away and didn't, and they died. I'd say he killed them. But if he'd never promised, and he'd walked past the same dying plants with the same key in his pocket and the same ability to help, I'd say the plants just died and he didn't cause anything. A promise switches causation on? Five: a stranger's kidney. A doctor who could donate one to save a stranger and doesn't — I refuse to say she caused the stranger's suffering, even though structurally it looks exactly like the lifeguard: she could easily have prevented a serious harm, she didn't, nobody was relying on her. Six: two of us in the kitchen both notice the burner is on under a towel and neither of us turns it off and the place burns down. Both caused it, and neither of us caused half of it. Which is weird because that's what I deny in the crowd case. Seven: the parent who genuinely forgets a sleeping kid in the back seat on a hot day. Caused it, definitely caused it, but I don't blame them one tenth as much as the lifeguard, so I'm clearly running cause and blame as two separate dials. Eight: a state that skips the levee upgrade and the town floods eighteen years later in a storm — I want to say they flooded it. And nine, the one I can't even decide on: my brother pulled the batteries out of the hallway smoke alarm because the chirping annoyed him, there was a wiring fire three weeks later, and I keep flip-flopping on whether he burned the house down or just failed to stop it burning down.

What I want is a test. Something I could apply to any not-doing — one rule, or a small number of clauses — that tells me when the absence is a cause and when it isn't, stated precisely enough that I could argue with it. Go clause by clause and tell me which of my nine cases each clause exists to handle, because I suspect at least two clauses are load-bearing in a way that's going to feel arbitrary. Then be straight with me about which verdicts it has to overrule: I'd honestly rather have a simple rule that tells me I'm wrong about the plants, or wrong about the crowd, than a five-part monster that saves everything, but say which one you're handing me and why. After that, run it on two cases I didn't list: somebody who unplugs the break-room router so the alarm's network notification never reaches the nurse's phone, and me skipping an election where my candidate would have appointed someone who would have pushed through the levee money. Last thing, and this is the fork I'm actually stuck on. My roommate's position is that I've framed it wrong, because there are infinitely many things the lifeguard didn't do — he didn't be a stronger swimmer, he didn't have a jet ski, he didn't teleport — so either every failure by every person is a cause of everything, or none of them are, and there's no principled place to stand in between. Is he right that it's all-or-nothing? Because if he is, I have to give up something big, and I want to know what.
