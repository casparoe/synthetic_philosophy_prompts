My sister runs a philosophy-of-science reading group and she lets me tag along even though I'm 16. Last week it was Friedman's essay on positive economics, the one where he says you can't judge a theory by how realistic its assumptions are, only by whether it predicts well. Two people in the group went at it for like an hour and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning.

Marcus opened with this: every model worth using has flatly false stuff baked in — frictionless planes, perfectly informed buyers, utility maximizers who don't actually exist. So if "is that assumption realistic?" counts as a fair criticism, no model ever survives it, and Friedman is right that prediction is the only test that isn't rigged.

Elena said that's exactly why prediction alone can't be the test. Suppose a minimum-wage model with a bogus story about labor demand happens to fit twenty years of data — it fits because of a coincidence of circumstances, and the first time you use it to set policy somewhere else it collapses. What licenses carrying a model over to a new situation is that its assumptions are roughly right about the mechanism actually generating the outcomes. Past predictive success doesn't validate itself.

Marcus said her standard would have condemned Newtonian mechanics, which everybody used for 200 years while knowing its assumptions were false.

Elena said physics isn't like that: a physicist can tell you which idealizations are negligible and why, and can put friction back in and check that the prediction moves toward the data. In economics you usually can't do that de-idealizing at all, and you have no way of knowing which false assumption is harmless and which one is holding the whole thing up. So the requirement isn't literal truth, it's being able to identify your idealizations as harmless.

Marcus said that's just Friedman with extra steps, and pointed to Friedman's own examples — the expert billiard player who sinks shots as if he could work out the differential equations, and the leaves arranging themselves as if they knew the botany. Those as-if assumptions are compact stand-ins for mechanisms nobody can state, so calling them unrealistic is a category mistake.

Elena said he's sliding between two different claims: (a) assumptions don't have to be literally true, and (b) whether they're true is irrelevant to whether predictions license intervention. She'll accept (a) flat out. Marcus said accepting (a) already loses the game, because whatever she needs for (b) has to be a truth claim about the assumptions, and she just said truth isn't required.

Near the end somebody said a later economist (Musgrave, I think?) argued the whole F-twist only works because Friedman runs together assumptions doing different jobs — a claim that some factor is negligible, a claim about where the model applies, and a claim about what actually causes the outcome. Neither of them picked that up.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each one, not just who wins overall? Also say if there's something both of them are just ignoring. And please mark clearly which of these points are settled among people who actually work on this stuff and which are still live disputes — don't hand me a contested position as if it were the consensus.
