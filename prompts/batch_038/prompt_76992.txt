My fourth novel is narrated in the third person through a behavioural geneticist who directs a long-running registry of adult twins reared apart, and in the course of eighteen months of reading for her I have found myself persuaded by something close to her position. I should like that position pressure-tested before the manuscript goes any further, because she states it publicly, in her own field, to people who would notice if it were wrong.

The position, as I now hold it and as she defends it in Part Two, runs as follows. In affluent populations the heritability of general cognitive ability in adulthood falls somewhere between 0.5 and 0.8; the estimate is low in early childhood and climbs steadily towards adulthood; therefore genetic variation accounts for most of the variation in adult cognitive outcomes, and environmental influences operate chiefly either as gross insults and deprivations whose removal matters or as differences that shift the whole distribution without altering anyone's rank in it. She draws the further, more controversial inference that a universal early-years intervention should be expected to raise the mean and barely dent the variance, and that this is a reason for pessimism about policy rather than about people.

I want to know what would count against this — not the general worry that hereditarianism is politically fraught, but the specific findings, arguments and internal inconsistencies that should move a well-informed specialist. Please address:

1. A ranking of defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, with a note on which of them a committed defender of her view would themselves concede, and which they would dismiss and on what grounds. I am particularly interested in concessions she could make without losing her thesis, since those make the best dialogue.
2. Whether the developmental rise in heritability genuinely evidences genetic amplification, or whether it dissolves once people are allowed to select, evoke and construct their own environments in proportion to their dispositions — and if the latter, what becomes of her inference in paragraph one.
3. Whether the persistent shortfall of DNA-based estimates relative to pedigree-based ones threatens the number she quotes, or only the interpretation she puts on it.
4. The horticultural objection she meets at a dinner party in Part Three — genetically variable seed sown in two different soils, so that within-soil heritability says nothing about between-soil differences. Does her answer survive, and what would a hostile reader say she has begged?
5. Whether the position has been insulated by qualification, notably any retreat to variance within a population under present conditions. If it has, I want to know whether that retreat is a legitimate clarification or a manoeuvre that forfeits the policy consequences she invokes it to support.

Three things are already committed to the manuscript and cannot easily be altered: the quoted figure and its range; the inference from within-population variance to the limits of universal intervention; and a plot turn in which one twin pair, matched for upbringing and schooling almost to the letter, diverges dramatically in midlife. Tell me which of these a specialist reader would catch, and which survive intact.

Finally, for every defeater you judge serious, tell me whether it can be staged as scene — a result arriving, a colleague at a conference, a pair in the registry that refuses to fit — or whether it can only be delivered as exposition, since anything of the latter sort I shall cut. Distinguish throughout between claims that are false and claims that are true but inert, doing no work in her argument.

I am not after a reading list or citations; I want the reasoning, and I should like it in British English.
