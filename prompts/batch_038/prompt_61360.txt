**Topic.** Whether the no-free-lunch (NFL) results for supervised learning — Wolpert's work on off-training-set error, and the companion Wolpert–Macready results for search and optimization — constitute a mathematical resolution of Hume's problem of induction.

**Participants.** Adaeze, an applied machine learning researcher who has read the formal proofs. Martin, a philosophy PhD student working on epistemic justification.

1. **Adaeze (opening).** Hume's problem is that no finite record of observed regularity can justify the inference that unobserved cases resemble observed ones, since any attempt to justify it either argues inductively, which is circular, or assumes the uniformity of nature, which is question-begging. NFL says the same thing, proved rather than intuited: averaged uniformly over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm has exactly the same expected off-training-set error, so no inductive procedure is preferable to any other from the armchair. The skeptic's conclusion is now a theorem.

2. **Martin.** It is not a theorem about us. Its premise is a uniform distribution over *all* functions from inputs to outputs — including uncomputable, maximally discontinuous, and systematically anti-inductive ones. Of course nothing generalizes under that prior; it is a limiting case of ordinary Bayesian machinery, where a flat prior over an enormous hypothesis space means data barely moves the posterior. What NFL actually establishes is that if you assume nothing about the world you can learn nothing about it — garbage in, garbage out, dressed in notation. Hume's problem concerns justification: whether our actually successful inductions can be shown to be non-accidentally reliable. A theorem quantified over priors nobody holds does not engage that question at all.

3. **Adaeze.** It engages it, because of what the result is *for*. The motivation is not the claim that the world is uniform-random; it is a counting claim: there are as many priors on which algorithm A outperforms algorithm B, appropriately weighted, as there are on which B outperforms A. So whenever someone asserts that gradient methods, or evolutionary search, or Bayesian model averaging carries a *general* warrant — that it is the right way to reason as such — NFL shows the warrant must come from outside the algorithm, from an assumption about which problems we face. That is exactly the Humean insight: the load is borne by an assumed fit between our methods and the world. It is also the assumption that the literature claiming universal superiority of one method class used to slide past.

4. **Martin.** Then it is a theorem about marketing, not about justification. And move 3 concedes my point: the assumption of fit is empirical and revisable. We find out that our problem classes are structured, and we adjust our priors accordingly. Nothing in that process is unjustifyable in principle. Hume's difficulty is that the uniformity principle cannot be justified *even in principle*; NFL only says your priors cannot be justified *from nothing*. Those are different theses, and nobody disputes the second.

5. **Adaeze.** Not so fast — the follow-up work sharpens rather than softens this. The equality of performance does not require uniformity over every conceivable function: there is a sharpened version on which, averaging uniformly over any finite set F of candidate objective functions, all algorithms perform equally if and only if F is closed under permutation. The problem classes we actually meet are not closed under permutation; they carry structure; so the equality fails and some methods genuinely dominate on them. But observe where that leaves you. The question *which F do we take the world to belong to* is prior to every methodological choice, and there is no algorithm for settling it. Choosing your F is the inductive leap. Dropping the flat prior does not make NFL go away; it relocates it to the one place a practitioner cannot escape.

6. **Martin.** That is a verbal victory. The claim that a formalism leaves the choice of its own assumptions open is true of every model of inference ever proposed, Bayesianism included, and of every methodological rule in science. If that is the argument, it is a point about frameworks generally and not a skeptical result, and calling it Humean inflates it. Moreover, you have now conceded what matters to me: the structured-F case shows that induction works because the world is structured and because we can *find out* that it is, empirically, using methods that keep succeeding. Whether or not that meets Hume's own impossible standard, it is the only kind of answer to skepticism that could conceivably matter.

7. **Adaeze.** Finding out empirically that the world is structured is itself an induction, so it cannot discharge a demand for justification without circularity — that is Hume's fork, and it is why I keep insisting NFL is his heir. Your structured world is also doing more work than you are admitting. The empirical claim underneath it is that real data is disproportionately low-complexity in the algorithmic sense — far simpler than a uniform draw over target functions would typically produce — and that our architectures share that bias toward simple solutions, which is why one model family transfers across apparently unrelated domains. Grant all of it. It is still a generalization from the problems we have happened to encounter. NFL does not refute it, but it does show the refutation cannot come from the mathematics of learning alone, so the low-complexity premise has to enter as a successful assumption. Successful assumptions are inductive assumptions.

8. **Martin.** Two replies. First, successful-but-undemonstrated is not the same as unjustified. Hume's fork demands a demonstration, and the fact that his standard cannot be met does not make the practice arbitrary — by that route you could equally prove arithmetic arbitrary, since its axioms are not non-circularly demonstrable either. Second, and more seriously: NFL's own setup presupposes what Hume denies. It assumes a fixed target function and independent, identically distributed sampling from it — that is, that nature is uniform across observations. A thoroughgoing Humean skeptic cannot so much as state the theorem. So NFL sits downstream of an assumption of uniformity; it is not a proof that uniformity is unjustified. If it bears on Hume at all, it bears on him in the opposite direction from the one you claim: it takes the disputed principle for granted.

9. **Adaeze (closing).** That cuts both ways, and I will take the half that helps me. If NFL presupposes a probability space over target functions, then it cannot be the general critique of induction, since the critique has to bite before such a space is granted. But your own move 8 then shows that you have no answer to the skeptic either — you have a description of a practice that works. What survives is this: NFL is the only result that makes precise the sense in which claims about a better algorithm are always relative to a distribution over problems, so there is no method-neutral notion of algorithmic quality. That moral is worth stating to engineers even if the Humean framing was too grand.

10. **Martin (closing).** I accept that moral entirely. But it is a claim about the semantics of performance statements — about what such claims are relative to — and not a claim about induction. Conflating the two is what generated this argument in the first place, and I would rather the seminar record separate them.
