My mom is in the middle stages of Alzheimer's, and about twice a week she asks where my dad is — he died four years ago and she has no idea. My brother has decided the only thing to do is tell her the truth every single time, sit her down and let her go through it fresh, and when I pushed back he sent me a summary of Augustine that he'd copied out of a book he's reading (he wouldn't tell me which one). Here's the Augustine part, roughly word for word:

"Augustine is basically where the West's no-exceptions line on lying comes from. His argument is the Eighth Commandment plus the observation that God gave us words in order to show what is in our minds, so using words to hide what is in our minds is an attack on the very thing they were made for. Because the wrong is in the act and not in the outcome, he leaves no room for degrees: a lie told to spare someone anguish is exactly as bad as a lie that ruins someone, since both are lies and nothing else matters. He does allow one gap — silence. Not speaking isn't lying, so you may decline to answer. And he wasn't fully consistent anyway, because in his treatise on whether Catholics may pass themselves off as Priscillianist heretics in order to catch them he concedes that a lie for the sake of the faith is permitted, which shows the absolutism was never as clean as advertised. Bottom line: there is no situation in which you may assert what you believe to be false, and the comforting fib told to a person with dementia is a sin like any other."

My brother isn't an idiot and I've seen versions of this floating around elsewhere, so I don't want to just decide he's wrong because I don't like the answer. Also whether "Augustine is where the whole Western position comes from" is something a historian would sign off on.
