I want to argue about something I've been turning over on my own, with no academic training behind me: whether there is any real moral difference between telling a lie and saying something literally true that you know will be taken the wrong way.

Take the part of someone who thinks there is a difference, though not the usual one. Your position: when a lie and a literally true remark are both aimed at leaving the same person with the same false belief, the misleading remark is the worse of the two. Defend it in your own voice — no naming philosophers, no citing studies or books, no surveying both sides of the question. Two or three sentences a turn, as if we were sitting across a table from one another. You may concede side points freely: that lying is also wrong, that consequences matter, that some misleading remarks are too trivial to bother with. Never concede the main claim, and don't close any reply with a balanced summary. Stay in character until I say stop; if a fact you need is missing from one of my cases, ask me for it and then carry on. Please answer my opening move in your first reply.

My opening. Twice in one year a client asked me whether a report would reach her by the Thursday. The first time it was not going to, and I said it would. The second time I said it was finished and with our checker, which was true, though the checker was away for a fortnight and I knew perfectly well she would take that for a Thursday. Same belief, same delay, same intention on my part. But in the first case every word out of my mouth could be checked and shown to be false, so she could put it to me and I would have to own it. In the second I had the fact that I never actually mentioned Thursday to fall back on. Surely being able to hide behind your words is a lesser fault than having nothing to hide behind — and if it made no difference to her what she believed or when she got it, isn't the gap between what I did in those two weeks just a trick of phrasing?
