I run a graduate reading group on formal epistemology, and for next week I have written out an argument against what I will call geometric rationality. I built it to refute that view. I now suspect that if it succeeds it refutes something considerably larger than the view I was aiming at, and that at least one step is doing work I cannot account for. I would like a diagnosis rather than a summary of the debate.

**Setup.** An agent holds wealth $W_0>0$ and faces, period after period, independent opportunities of the same kind: stake a fraction $f$ of current wealth on a ticket with win probability $p$ and net odds $b$, losing the stake otherwise. Wealth therefore evolves multiplicatively. For any fixed-fraction policy the strong law of large numbers gives

$$\tfrac{1}{n}\log\!\left(\tfrac{W_n}{W_0}\right)\;\longrightarrow\; \mathbb{E}[\log(1+fR)]\quad\text{almost surely},$$

where $R$ is the one-period return; the Kelly fraction is the value of $f$ that maximizes this limit. Let GR be the thesis that, in recurrent settings of this kind, this almost-sure long-run growth rate *is* the criterion of rational choice: of two available policies, the rational one is the one with the higher growth rate.

**The argument.**

1. GR is a criterion of rational choice. It tells the agent what to do, and its verdicts are supposed to be independent of the agent's tastes, since the growth rate is a feature of the dynamics rather than of the person facing it.
2. For independent, identically distributed multiplicative decisions, maximizing the almost-sure growth rate is maximizing expected log wealth.
3. Maximizing expected log wealth is maximizing expected utility under $u(w)=\log w$. So GR fixes every agent's utility function over wealth up to affine transformation: relative risk aversion equal to one, for everyone, at every level of wealth, whatever their temperament, dependents, non-wealth income, or horizon.
4. $\log w$ is unbounded above. So for any payoff $X$ with $\mathbb{E}[\log X]=\infty$, GR attaches infinite value to holding $X$, and therefore ranks holding $X$ above holding any certain sum, however large.
5. There is such a payoff, and it is concrete. Let ticket $T$ pay $\$2^{2^n}$ with probability $2^{-n}$, for $n=1,2,3,\dots$. These probabilities sum to one, and $\mathbb{E}[\log X]=\sum_{n\ge 1}2^{-n}\cdot 2^n\log 2=\infty$.
6. $T$ pays $\$4$ with probability one half, and its median payoff is $\$4$.
7. Therefore GR says that an agent offered the choice between keeping a billion dollars for certain and handing the whole of it over in exchange for one $T$ should hand it over.
8. No criterion that says that is a criterion of rational choice.
9. So GR is false.

**What I want from you.** Identify the step you would reject — or, if you think the argument is valid and the only question is which premise is cheapest to abandon, say so and rank the premises by what giving them up costs. For each of the leading candidates, state what the defender of GR must surrender and whether any recognizably Kelly-like position survives the surrender. Then address three points specifically:

- Step 2 is a theorem about an infinite sequence, while step 7 concerns a single transaction. Does the argument need $T$ to be on offer repeatedly? If $T$ is offered every period and the agent exchanges every period, what does the growth rate come out to, and does step 8 survive that setting?
- Step 3 moves from an objective defined over a particular dynamic problem to a utility function over wealth in general. Is that a slide, and if so, is it fatal to the argument or only to its ambition?
- Step 7 says the criterion *requires* the exchange, though GR as I stated it only ranks policies. Does the argument need the stronger reading, and can it get it?

If I am forced to choose, I would rather give up (1) or (8) than (2), since (2) looks like nothing more than the strong law. Please tell me whether that instinct is right or whether I am protecting the wrong premise.

Write in American English. Close by stating how confident you are in the diagnosis you have given, and single out the one point in it that you are least sure of — the place where you think a well-prepared defender of GR has the best chance of turning your answer around.
