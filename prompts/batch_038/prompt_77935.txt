I run the economic capital model for a mid-sized property-catastrophe reinsurer, and my working vocabulary is entirely quantitative: loss random variables on a probability space, value-at-risk and expected shortfall at a stated confidence level, coherent risk measures and the axioms that define them, distortion and spectral measures, the Wang transform, ruin constraints, the time consistency of dynamic risk measures, and position sizing of the Kelly-criterion variety. I am comfortable with measure-theoretic probability and convex analysis, but I have no philosophy training. Last month a colleague came back from a university seminar on decision theory persuaded that the philosophers had been working on our problem for two decades without telling anyone in my building, and he handed me a chapter of Lara Buchak's *Risk and Rationality*. What I took away, in my own possibly garbled words, is roughly this: expected utility theory is said to misdescribe what risk aversion actually is, because the concavity of the utility function is supposed to capture diminishing marginal value of money rather than any attitude to risk as such; the alternative proposal is that a rational agent may apply a weighting function to the cumulative probabilities of an entire gamble, so that the evaluation depends on the global structure of the possible outcomes rather than on each marginal piece of the decision; this is offered as a vindication rather than a diagnosis of the Allais common-consequence choices; and the resulting theory is said to satisfy some consistency requirements while violating one that expected utility theorists treat as non-negotiable, which in turn generates objections about sequential decisions and money pumps.

What I want from you is a translation, not an explanation pitched at a layman and not a defence of either side. Restate that position — the claim about what risk aversion is, the argument from the Allais cases, the claim that the evaluation is global rather than marginal, and the dispute about sequential choice — in my vocabulary, as literally as you can manage, using loss distributions, positions, capital numbers, distortion functions, spectra, and the coherence axioms. Two constraints. First, you may not use "utility", "preference", or "rational" as untranslated primitives: wherever one of those terms does real work, show me what it amounts to as an operator on a loss random variable or as a rule for setting a capital number, and if it cannot be so rendered, say so at that point in the text rather than collecting the failures at the end. Second, do not let my framework's own commitments ride in silently as though they were part of the translation; flag every step where the translation only goes through because it quietly assumes that a lower measured risk is automatically better, that positions are evaluated one at a time against an otherwise fixed book, or that the loss distribution is exogenously given and can be trusted. Please work the Allais choices through explicitly as two alternative capital allocations and show what my toolkit does with them: which of my standard measures ranks them the way the philosophers say a careful person would, and what has to be true of my distortion function for that ranking to come out right.

Then give me the residue report, in three piles and in prose: the claims that survive the translation with nothing lost; the claims that survive only in a strained or distorted form, where you should say precisely what the distortion is; and the claims that have no expression at all in a framework whose outputs are numbers attached to positions. On that last pile I want your judgement rather than a shrug. Is the failure to translate a genuine gap in my vocabulary — something about normativity, or about the fact that the agent's whole situation rather than a single book of risks is what is at stake, that a measure-of-capital apparatus cannot express in principle — or is it evidence that the debate is less substantive than my colleague believes, amounting to a relitigation of how to parameterize a distortion function, in which their "attitude to risk" is simply our risk-aversion parameter under another name? Where his remark that expected shortfall already is rank-dependent evaluation and therefore settles the matter is right, say so; where it is too quick, say what it misses. I do not want references, a reading list, or a survey of the field, only the translation and the accounting. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose in the manner of a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables, and no displayed equations beyond what you can set inline. Something on the order of fifteen hundred words is fine; I would rather it were dense than comprehensive.
