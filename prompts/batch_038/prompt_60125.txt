I would like a case analysis that runs in both directions: a real episode treated as a test case for a piece of metaontology, and the theory then judged by how well it survives contact with the case. The episode is the demotion of Pluto at the International Astronomical Union's 2006 General Assembly in Prague; the theoretical apparatus is the cluster of deflationary and anti-deflationary accounts of verbal disputes. No bibliography or citations, please — argue from the views themselves.

Facts I take to be settled, so that you need not reconstruct them and so that nothing turns on a detail you cannot check. Please do not spend space on the astronomy.

- Pluto had been counted among the planets since Clyde Tombaugh discovered it in 1930. By the early 2000s a rapidly growing catalogue of small icy bodies beyond Neptune — including Eris, comparable to Pluto in size and somewhat more massive — made the question of what counts as a planet acute, since on the old understanding the count threatened to keep growing indefinitely.
- A draft definition circulated a week before the vote would have enlarged the solar system to twelve planets, admitting Ceres and Pluto's own moon Charon along with Eris. The version actually adopted reversed course: a planet must orbit the Sun, be massive enough for its own gravity to pull it into a nearly round shape, and have cleared its orbital neighbourhood. Bodies meeting the first two conditions but not the third were placed in a new category, dwarf planets, and Pluto was named the prototype of a further class of trans-Neptunian objects. A rival label for that class was voted down by three ballots.
- More than 2,500 astronomers attended the assembly, but only a few hundred voted: the definition of planet itself passed by an uncounted large majority, while the resolution demoting Pluto carried 237 to 157 with 17 abstentions. Many planetary scientists, whose subject this arguably is, did not participate and have objected ever since. The definition also applies, by its own terms, only to bodies orbiting the Sun, which leaves the growing catalogue of exoplanets outside its scope.
- The IAU has no legal standing. Its authority over nomenclature is a matter of convention and of what downstream users — databases, textbooks, missions, curricula — find cheapest to adopt. In 2007 the New Mexico legislature passed a memorial declaring Pluto a planet as it passes overhead over that state. In 2017 a group of planetary scientists including Kirby Runyon, Philip Metzger and Alan Stern put forward a geophysical alternative on which any body round enough to have been shaped by its own gravity is a planet regardless of what else shares its orbit. The 2015 New Horizons flyby revived the argument in public.

Now the analysis. Work with the discipline of an actual mapping — who the disputants are, what sentence they contest, what would count as settling it — rather than applying labels.

(a) State the disagreement precisely. Two parties assent to and dissent from the same sentence, "Pluto is a planet." Say what each takes herself to be disagreeing about, and what she would cite as evidence. Then run Chalmers's method of elimination: taboo the word *planet* and see whether any substantive disagreement survives. Be explicit about what survives — my own guess is something about which properties deserve to organize the science, plus a boundary-drawing problem in a continuum of bodies, but test that guess rather than accepting it — and about what dissolves. Say whether the residue is a disagreement about the world or a disagreement about what to do with a word.

(b) Take each account in turn, saying what it predicts about this episode and whether the prediction holds: Hirsch-style quantifier variance and interpretive charity (can the two sides be read charitably as differing only in the convention they attach to a sortal term, and is there any shared fact they dispute?); Sider-style substantivity and joint-carving (does one candidate meaning — *round body* versus *dynamically dominant body* — carve the world better, and does substantivity settle the question or merely relocate it?); Carnap's internal/external distinction together with the idea of explication (is the IAU explicating, and if so does that show the question was never a first-order factual one?); and an easy-ontology or broadly deflationary account on which the answer follows trivially once the framework and the empirical facts are fixed. Where two accounts give the same verdict here, say so and say why that is uninformative.

(c) Work with a three-way distinction I do not think the standard vocabulary supplies. Call a dispute **merely verbal** when no substantive disagreement survives elimination of the term; **merely stipulative** when the disagreement could be settled only by a decision that is not answerable to how things are, so that an authority can close it without discovering anything; and **substantive-but-indeterminate** when the world underdetermines which candidate meaning the term has, so that a choice must be made on pragmatic grounds, yet the competing choices differ in what they get right about the world. Sort the Pluto dispute into one or more of these. Then say whether the frameworks in (b) can draw these lines at all — in particular, whether "stipulative" is a category they have room for — and if they cannot, say what would have to be added and at what cost.

(d) Address what I will call the direction-of-authority problem. If the assembly had voted the other way, would anything in the universe have been different? Distinguish carefully between two claims the episode invites us to conflate: that the IAU changed the meaning of a word, and that it changed the truth-value of a sentence that had been true for seventy-six years. Say what each theory in (b) is committed to here, and note that the participants did not present themselves as choosing a word, while the New Mexico memorial is in form a legislative override of an astronomical verdict. What does it take for a stipulation to bind a community, and does the fact that it can bind show that the dispute was never merely about language, or only that language can be settled by fiat?

(e) Say where the theories go silent or actively misdescribe the case. At minimum address: that one and the same sentence was contested by two different linguistic communities — professionals with a functional need and a public with seventy-six years of settled usage — and whether a dispute can be verbal for one and substantive for the other, so that the method of elimination requires an index to a community; that the dispute carried non-semantic stakes (mission design, funding categories, museum labels, school curricula) which none of these frameworks treat as relevant to whether a dispute is verbal; that an institutional referee with de facto authority exists here, whereas the paradigm cases in the literature are disputes nobody is competent to close; and that the continuum of body sizes makes a sorites-like worry live in a way it is not in the paradigm cases. Add any further mismatches you see, and say which of them are fatal to which account.

Deliverable: a structured analysis with a headed section for each of (a) through (e), followed by a brief assessment of which single account best handles the episode as a whole. Flag uncertainty about what a given view entails rather than pressing it into service. End with a one-sentence summary of your overall judgement, and then a final line in exactly this form, using one of the four labels from (c):

Verdict: <merely verbal | merely stipulative | substantive-but-indeterminate | substantive>
