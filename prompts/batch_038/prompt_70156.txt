Assume the role of a social epistemologist who has also spent several years building in-class simulations for large undergraduate sections, and think carefully before answering. Begin with a short section of explicit design reasoning — which structural feature of the phenomenon has to be implemented mechanically rather than explained, what the naive version of this game would get wrong, and what you propose to do instead — and only then give me the design proper. I want mechanics and trade-offs, not learning-outcome language.

The context. I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and next term I teach my own upper-level seminar on social epistemology: eighteen students, mostly philosophy and political science majors, one hundred minutes a week. Week five is on the structure of epistemic communities. The organizing distinction for that week is C. Thi Nguyen's between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers. As I will put it to them: a bubble is a structure of omission, in which members simply never receive the relevant outside testimony, and the remedy is exposure; a chamber is a structure of active discredit, in which members do receive that testimony but have been trained to treat its sources as captured, corrupt, or stupid, and the remedy is therefore not more evidence but the slow rebuilding of trust. Bubbles get popped; chambers have to be burst. My own gloss, which you may treat as available to the design, is that a bubble is a truncated source set while a chamber is a corrupted reliability function operating over a source set that is not truncated at all.

What I need from you is a single-session activity in which the students run into that distinction — and, more importantly, into the asymmetry of remedies — before anyone names it.

Hard constraints. The informational content must be invented and non-political; I do not want their prior partisan knowledge doing the work, and I do not want the room turning into a fight about an actual live controversy. No phones and no real money: whatever incentive structure you use must run on tokens, points, or graded participation credit I can award honestly. The diagnostic vocabulary — omission versus discredit, truncated versus corrupted, and the names of the two structures — is withheld until the debrief. Note that they already carry a folk concept of an echo chamber that lumps both structures together, so the activity has to break that concept rather than flatter it; treat this as a design problem, not an afterthought.

Two things must be built in. First, the chamber's discrediting rule has to be executed by the students themselves, under a scoring rule that rewards doing so, and not asserted by me from the front of the room — I want at least one participant who leaves the structure and tries to testify to the others, and I want the others' incentives to make dismissing that participant the profitable move. Second, the same evidential intervention — a flood of outside testimony — must be applied to both structures within the session, so that the differential response is visible to everyone rather than merely described.

I would also like my own heresy put in front of them. I suspect the binary is not exhaustive: a bubble that persists long enough can generate discrediting scripts endogenously, without anyone having been taught to distrust anything, simply because its members begin to notice and explain away the occasions on which outside testimony contradicts them. On that view chambers are partly a species of aged bubble, and the two remedies come apart less cleanly than the taxonomy implies. If you can construct a round in which that possibility is at least testable in play — students able to produce a structure that is neither and articulate why — do so. If you cannot do it honestly inside a hundred minutes, say so plainly rather than faking it.

Deliverables, in this order: the fictional content of the task and the mechanism that fixes the ground truth (including whatever answer key I need); the exact printable text of any role cards or handouts; the token or point scheme; a materials list that costs under fifteen dollars and can be assembled in an afternoon; a minute-by-minute timeline; the debrief, which should name the distinction only at the end and map particular moments of play onto particular components of it, saying which round shows omission, which shows discredit, and which shows exposure failing as a remedy; a frank account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the round that collapses, the student who refuses the role, the way the losing group reacts, the possibility that the differential never appears because one structure simply solves the puzzle early — with a fix for each; and finally your honest assessment of which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply by argument. My own guesses are the multi-year timescale, the fact that real chambers are embedded in communities one loves and cannot exit without losing one's social world, and the fact that the students know perfectly well they are in a game. Tell me whether you agree, and which omission you think is the most serious.
