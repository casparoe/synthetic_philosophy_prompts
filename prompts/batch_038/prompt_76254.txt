I spent thirty-seven years in trace analysis, mostly running inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry in a laboratory that measured metals in water, blood and soil, and I retired four years ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy more or less systematically, and more recently the argument, largely conducted outside philosophy departments, about how one is supposed to supervise artificial agents whose work one cannot check for oneself. I have drafted the short essay below. It is intended for readers who are technically literate but not specialists in either of the two fields it moves between. I should be grateful for your comments on it, of a specific sort, which I set out after the essay.

**Bias and variance in the supervision of artificial agents**

**1.** The discussion I have been following distinguishes process-based from outcome-based supervision: in the first case one evaluates the intermediate steps by which a result was reached, in the second one evaluates only the result. The comparison is nearly always conducted in terms of cost and information. Outcome checks are cheap — one judgement per episode — but sparse, which makes it hard to say which part of a long trajectory deserves credit for the ending; process checks are dense and informative but require someone, or something, to sit through the transcript, and they scale badly. On this picture the choice between the two is a matter of budget. I want to suggest that the budget framing hides the consideration that ought to decide the question, which is the error structure of the task and of the check itself.

**2.** An analytical laboratory teaches you, in the first week, that there are two quite different ways to be wrong. Random error is scatter about the true value; it is reduced by replication and averaging, and its size can be estimated from the spread of your own results. Systematic error — bias — is a displacement of the whole distribution; it is untouched by replication, since every replicate is displaced in the same direction and says nothing about the displacement. A method can be exquisitely precise and consistently wrong. Bias is not visible from inside the run. It is detected only by interrogating the method: reagent blanks, spiking and recovery, an independent determination by a different technique, and reference materials whose value was established elsewhere and by other means.

**3.** Related to this is a point about the scope of validation. A method validated for lead in drinking water does not thereby carry over to lead in whole blood; the matrix changes and with it the interference, the recovery and the detection limit. Validity is a property of a method over a specified range of conditions, never a property of the method as such. When the population of samples shifts outside that range, the validation lapses and must be performed again. I shall return to this.

**4.** Clinical research runs into the same difficulty under another name. Where the outcome one actually cares about is slow, rare or expensive to measure — survival over a decade, say — trials are run on surrogate endpoints: a biomarker, a tumour size, an electrocardiographic measure. The conditions under which a surrogate may be trusted are demanding, and roughly amount to this: the intervention must affect the surrogate, and the surrogate must capture the whole of the intervention's effect on the real endpoint, so that nothing is left over to act by another route. Those conditions are seldom met, and the failures are not exotic. The canonical example is a class of drugs that reliably suppressed the arrhythmia they were prescribed to suppress and nevertheless increased mortality; the surrogate improved, the patient did not.

**5.** Optimising an artificial agent against a proxy has, I think, exactly this structure, and for a reason that has less to do with reward functions than with paragraph 3. When you optimise hard against a surrogate you move the system out of the region in which the surrogate was ever shown to track the target. Goodhart's law is the folk formulation; the metrological formulation is that the matrix has changed and nobody has revalidated. Note that this is not the familiar complaint about a learner finding an implementation flaw in a reward function and exploiting it. It is a structural consequence of using a proxy at all: the better the optimisation succeeds, the further the operating point drifts from the validated region, and the drift is caused by success rather than by cleverness.

**6.** From these premises I want to draw a claim that I suspect is unpopular. Outcome-based supervision is a variance-control device. It asks whether the product lies within specification once the process has run. Replicating such checks improves the precision of your estimate of the system's performance; it cannot improve its accuracy, because the accuracy of the check is capped by the validity of the surrogate, and that validity is a property of the check rather than of the number of times you apply it. Process-based supervision, whatever else it is, is an interrogation of the method. It follows that outcome supervision is well suited to detecting an agent that is erratic and structurally blind to an agent that is consistently displaced — one whose every intermediate judgement is locally impeccable and whose results are systematically off in a direction the check itself shares. More checks of the same kind buy precision. Only a change of method buys accuracy.

**7.** The obvious objection is that process supervision is no less proxy-ridden than outcome supervision. A grader's judgement that step four looks sound is itself a surrogate for step four being sound, and a weaker one than an executable check. I grant the objection entirely. My claim is not that process supervision is more accurate; it is that the two styles are sensitive to different error structures and fail on different ones, and that choosing between them without first characterising the error structure of the task is the analogue of adopting an analytical method without validating it.

**8.** But then a harder difficulty, which I am not sure how to resolve. To check a method you need something whose value is known independently of the method — a reference material. In metrology the chain of such independences does not run for ever. It terminates in a stipulation: the kilogram is no longer a cylinder in a vault but a fixed numerical value of a fundamental constant, and the traceability of every balance on earth bottoms out in a definition. I take this to be the real content of the intuition that one cannot bootstrap one's way to an unbiased measurement. Applied to supervision: a calibrated judge requires ground truth established independently of judges, and there is no reason to expect the supply of such ground truth to scale with our demand for it. My impression — and here I should very much like to be corrected by someone closer to the work — is that nearly all the effort in this field is spent making the agent better and very little on establishing the accuracy of the instruments that grade it. If that impression is mistaken, the conclusion of the essay is mistaken with it.

**9.** So the question as usually posed — process or outcome? — looks to me malformed. The prior questions are: what is the error structure of the failure you are trying to prevent; is your outcome a measurement of the thing you care about or a surrogate for it; over what range was that surrogate validated; and what happens to that range once you optimise against it?

**What I should like from you**

1. Accuracy on the artificial-intelligence side. Is my gloss in paragraph 1 a fair description of how those two terms are actually used by people working on this, or have I quietly imported a laboratory meaning onto them? Where would a practitioner wince? Please tell me in your own words what the standard usage is and where I have bent it; I am not asking for sources and cannot chase them.

2. The central claim. Is the mapping in paragraph 6 — outcome supervision onto variance, process supervision onto bias — sound, or does it break somewhere I have not noticed? Identify the single weakest step in the argument and say plainly whether you regard it as fatal or repairable. If it is fatal, I would rather hear that than hear a polite summary.

3. Internal consistency. Read the essay against itself. Does any later concession, example or qualification quietly undo something asserted earlier with more confidence than it deserved? I am interested in whether the sections hold together, not in whether each is plausible in isolation.

4. Whether the apparatus is load-bearing. Would the argument be any weaker if the metrology were stripped out and the thesis stated in ordinary terms? That is: is the technical vocabulary doing genuine work, or is it decoration borrowed for authority? This is the criticism I most expect and least know how to answer.

5. Redundancy. Would a reader steeped in the supervision debate recognise my thesis as an existing argument wearing a laboratory coat? If so, state the existing argument in your own words and tell me what, if anything, my version adds. Please do not answer this one with titles, authors or a reading list; a description of the argument is what I need.

6. The neglectedness claim in paragraph 8. Is it defensible as stated, and if not, what would have to be true for it to become defensible or obviously false? I am aware that I have offered no evidence for it beyond an impression formed from reading at a distance, and I should like to know whether it is the kind of claim that can be assessed at all from where I sit.

7. Editorial judgement. What would you cut, what would you develop, and is the register right for an audience of philosophers and scientifically literate non-specialists? Tell me where the prose sounds like a bench scientist over-explaining his craft to people who do not need it explained.

A response of roughly the length of the essay, organised under these seven headings, would be ideal. Please do not be agreeable for its own sake; if you think the piece repackages a familiar point with an instrument on the cover, say so and say what the familiar point is.
