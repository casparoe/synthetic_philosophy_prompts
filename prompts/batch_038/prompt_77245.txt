Six weeks into a new job as a peer support specialist on a community mental health team, and a big chunk of my week is now sitting with people while they write psychiatric advance directives — the documents where you say, while you're well, what should happen if you get unwell and start refusing things. Half of it is logistics and phone numbers. The other half is one question I keep failing to answer well: when should the document say trust the future version of this person, and when should it say the future version of this person is precisely the one not to trust?

The reason this is urgent rather than academic is that the first directive I helped draft has gone sideways. It got used last month to override a woman who said, perfectly coherently, that it wasn't her anymore and she'd changed her mind, and my initials are on the assessment note. I still think we did the right thing. I can't articulate why, and I have to do this several times a month now.

I did a philosophy degree before I fell out of academia, and the thing rattling around in my head is van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, if you're confident your future self will believe something, you should believe it now too, because your future self has everything you have plus whatever you're about to learn. Which sounds like a clean argument for writing "defer to future me" into everything. But it obviously can't be, because half the time the entire point of these documents is that future me will be sleep deprived, manic, terrified, or surrounded by people telling her she's fine. I also vaguely remember the principle needing qualification — something about the difference between changing your mind because you learned something and changing it because something was done to you — and I can't reconstruct where that line goes or what it's doing there.

So: can you turn this into a procedure I can actually run in a room with someone, and that ideally they can run themselves with a piece of paper?

Constraints. Sessions are 45 minutes, sometimes less. People are often anxious and I'm often interrupted. Our team runs from nurses to ex-patients to a psychiatrist who signs off at the end and has zero patience for epistemology. Nothing can involve numbers or formulas. I want questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the point where it stops with "no, don't write that clause," and the point where it hands the decision to a human because it's a question of values rather than facts.

Then tell me which distinctions from the actual theory have to survive the dumbing-down and which can be dropped, correct me where I've mis-stated what Reflection says, and be straight about where the procedure will misfire even when followed exactly.

One thing I'll flag myself: most of what goes into these documents is about what someone wants done to them, not about what they believe is true, and Reflection is a principle about belief. Does that mismatch sink the whole idea, or is there a version that carries over?

Run it on three that are live for me:

1. M, bipolar I, stable right now on lithium. She wants a clause saying that if she's manic and refusing medication, the document wins and she gets treated. Fine. But she also told me, calmly, that she's come to think the diagnosis over-reads her life, that the medication flattens her, and that she expects to be convinced in a couple of years that she never needed it at all — and she wants that future conviction respected as well. She doesn't see the tension. My gut says the first prediction is distortion and the second is her thinking, and I dont have a way to show her that isn't just my preference.

2. S, 24, sharp and furious, in the middle of a legal case against her stepfather. She wants the directive to bar him from visiting, and to state explicitly that if she later changes her mind — she expects she will, once the case ends and everyone starts pressuring her — the bar stays. I want to give her this. But the change she's predicting isn't mania or drugs, it's her learning things about her family and about her own feelings, which looks exactly like the kind of change Reflection says to defer to. Which of us has it right?

3. T, 68, early memory problems, writing about refusing care at home. What he fears isn't distortion, it's that he won't remember making the document, or won't remember what the last crisis felt like. Does deference even make sense toward a future self who has strictly less information than I do? And does it change if the thing he's most afraid of is that future-T won't mind being neglected — that the change will be in how much things bother him rather than in what he believes?

Finally: tell me how confident you are in the steps you're giving me, and single out the one claim in your answer you'd be least willing to defend if someone pushed on it.
