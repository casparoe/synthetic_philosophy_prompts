I'm a second-year philosophy undergrad and something slightly unreal has happened that I need help with. I'm on a module about work and technology, and for the last six weeks we've been doing automation. Meanwhile the last manually-worked signal box on the branch line where my grandad has worked for 38 years is being closed at the end of the month — everything gets controlled from a centre two counties away. The four signallers have organised a small ceremony on the platform: last train through, one lever pulled one last time, then the box gets locked and everyone walks down to the village hall. They've asked me to say a few words, because I'm local and because I'm "the one who reads books", which is not the same as knowing what to say. About forty people will be there: the signallers and their wives and kids, some teenagers from the village, a reporter from the local paper, two enthusiasts from a heritage railway society, and the regional director of the train company, who is speaking straight after me.

Please write the speech for me. British English throughout, three to four minutes when read aloud at a normal pace, sentences I can actually say in one breath on a cold platform with a train idling behind me.

Here's the idea I want doing the work, because I don't want it to be a generic farewell. My seminar leader made a distinction in week four that I keep turning over: there's automation that saves you labour — the effort goes but you still decide — and automation that saves you judgement, where the deciding quietly goes too and nobody ever announces that part. Signalling turns out to be a really clean case, and I want the speech to make the case in plain words. A signaller never touches a train. The whole job is holding a future state of the line in your head, ten minutes ahead, and then using a frame of heavy levers to make that future physically unavoidable. And the frame argues back: the locking bed underneath it literally won't let you pull two levers that contradict each other, so if you've got it wrong your own hand is stopped. Grandad says you can feel the wire run all the way down to the points. The new centre is safer and I genuinely believe it is, and it takes away the effort — but nothing pushes back into anyone's palm any more. The refusal has moved into software that no one experiences. So what's leaving this box isn't the hard work, it's the argument with the machine, and nobody wrote that in the press release.

Difficulties you have to solve rather than dodge:

- It cannot be a protest or a lament. One of the four signallers is genuinely delighted to be getting rid of the night shifts, and he'll be standing at the front. The speech has to be able to hold that he's right and that something is still going.
- It has to say the true thing about the loss with the man who authorised the loss standing four feet away and speaking next, without making him a villain or a hero, and without sounding like I'm handing him his own speech back.
- Banned, please: "end of an era", "the human touch", "in these fast-changing times", "a sad day", and the words craft, heritage, alienation and progress. Also no railway jargon that a twelve-year-old wouldn't get — the interlocking point has to land with the kids and the reporter.
- There should be one line in there that only two people in the audience will properly clock, but it mustn't be a wink over everyone else's heads.
- Grandad is in the room and alive and would die of embarassment if I turned it into a tribute to him. So: nothing that reads like a eulogy, nothing about how proud I am, but I'd like one small concrete detail that only he'd recognise.

Just give me the speech, with a couple of bracketed stage directions where I should stop or wait for the noise. No plan, no list of tips beforehand, no notes at the end explaining your choices — if something needs explaining, put it in the speech itself.
