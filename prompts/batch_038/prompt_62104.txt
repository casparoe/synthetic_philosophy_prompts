For thirty-four years I maintained and repaired industrial equipment — pumps, compressors, the occasional production line — and since retiring I have taken to reading philosophy in translation, no doubt badly, but with real enjoyment. A few months ago I found a secondhand anthology of classical Indian thought at a charity shop, and one section described a school holding that knowledge can only ever arrive through a small number of distinct channels: something like direct perception, something like inferring an unobserved thing from an observed sign, something like coming to understand a thing by having been told what it resembles, and something like the word of a person who is trustworthy about the matter. I liked this immediately, because it matches how I have always thought about my own trade. But there is a pattern I noticed decades before reading that book, and I cannot work out whether the framework accommodates it or simply assumes it away.

Here is the pattern. Suppose someone asks me how I know that a certain compressor is about to fail. I say: the note coming off the housing has changed. Ask how I learned what the changed note means, and the honest answer is that an older fitter told me, years ago, and I had no independent way of checking him at the time. Ask why I took his word rather than assuming he was hearing things, and I say: because over the following months the machines he called sick actually went down, and I watched that happen. But what counted as my watching it? I relied on vibration readings, on oil analysis, on entries in a maintenance log. The instruments were calibrated by someone else, on a schedule, and I accept the calibration because a certificate says so; the log is kept by colleagues whose honesty I judge by, among other things, whether their written predictions match what I later hear and see. When I try to state where my knowledge came from, each channel I name turns out to be propped up by another channel, and if I follow the props far enough they come back around to the very thing they were propping. A second case, closer to home: my wife and I keep a blood-pressure cuff in the cupboard. I trust it because the numbers agree with how I feel. Yet how did I learn what a dangerous reading feels like? Partly from an occasion years ago when the number was high on a day I felt strange — so my feel for the sensation was itself partly taught by the machine, and now the machine is corroborated by a sensation the machine helped calibrate. And a third, trivial one: on a walk I trust the compass because it has always agreed with the landmarks; I picked out the landmarks from the map; I trust the map because I have checked it against the compass.

What strikes me is that no individual step in any of these looks objectionable. Each is, taken on its own, exactly the sort of step the framework I have been reading approves of — I perceived it, I inferred it from a sign, a reliable person told me. It is only the closed shape of the whole that seems strange, and the shape only becomes visible once you insist on naming a source for each link, which is precisely what the four-channel picture asks you to do. So I want to know whether this configuration has a name. I have some half-formed suspicions from reading around the edges of the subject: there is a charge, I think, that a source cannot be used to vouch for itself, discussed under an image involving one's own bootstraps; there is the older puzzle that you need a criterion before you can judge by any criterion; and there is a sceptical demand for a demonstration of the channels of knowledge themselves, which I vaguely recall being pressed by a Buddhist author against roughly the school I have been reading. Each of those seems to me to be aimed at something slightly different from what I have described, though I may well be wrong about that.

What I am after is the carving, not a label, and I should say plainly that a list of references would be useless to me: I have no library access and no patience for a reading list, so please work entirely from distinctions you can spell out. Specifically: what are the nearest established concepts; how does the pattern I have described differ from each of them, link by link; which of them fits best, if any; and does the four-channel picture have resources to answer this worry, or does it presuppose that the channels are severally trustworthy before any question of their warrant arises? If you think the configuration genuinely lacks an established term — a cycle of warrant running through several distinct sources, each link locally unimpeachable, rather than a single source certifying itself — then say so without hedging and coin one. Give it a definition sharp enough that I could apply it to a case I had not seen before, and give me two or three boundary cases: something that looks exactly like it but is not, something that does not look like it at all but is, and where you would draw the line between the two.
