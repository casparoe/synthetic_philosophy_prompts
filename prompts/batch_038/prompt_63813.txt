I'm a reporter — science and ideas, general-interest magazine, mostly biology-adjacent — and I've spent the last month inside a big research institute trying to write a piece about how they decide which animals count. What I've ended up with is a pile of rules that nobody there can explain to me in a way that hangs together, and before I write anything I want to know what the whole setup quietly assumes.

Pretend you're a philosopher of mind and ethics who has also actually sat on animal-use committees and knows how these regulations work day to day — not a textbook. Think carefully, and before you commit to a conclusion, brainstorm four or five candidate theories of moral status that could sit behind this stuff; then tell me which one the practice really presupposes.

Here's the practice, as best I've been able to reconstruct it:

- The research rules my institute lives under (EU ones) require ethics review, anaesthesia, housing standards and approved methods of killing for any vertebrate, plus cephalopods. The preamble of the directive justifies adding cephalopods specifically on the grounds of "scientific evidence of their ability to experience pain, suffering, distress and lasting harm." Octopus in. Squid in.
- The same rules cover mammalian foetuses only from roughly the last third of development onwards. Before that, no protocol, no review, nothing.
- Everything else gets zero. Flies, bees, worms, crabs, prawns, spiders: no anaesthesia, no record, no committee. An entomologist can freeze ten thousand fruit flies or pour them down the sink and file nothing.
- But the institute also has its own internal form — a welfare tier form — where a species is scored 1 to 5 for something like neuroanatomical complexity plus behavioural evidence of pain, and the score determines how strict the review is. Primates at the top, insects at the bottom, and bottom tier means "no protocol needed." The form says the tier assignments should be revisited as the science develops.
- Elsewhere in the same legal order: crabs and lobsters have been recognised as sentient for animal-welfare purposes in at least one country, and there's an ongoing fight about boiling them alive. Slaughter law requires stunning for mammals and birds, exempts ritual slaughter in some places, and reaches crustaceans and fish much more weakly, if at all.
- In the US, as I understand it, the main federal lab-animal law defines "animal" so as to exclude birds, rats and mice bred for research — i.e. the overwhelming majority of the animals in the building — and that exclusion was about money and lobbying, not about nociceptors.
- And pesticide regulation authorises the killing of staggering numbers of wild insects with no welfare consideration whatsoever.

So what I want from you:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory in plain language. Is this a threshold view (status is binary, we just can't reliably tell who's over the line, so we use taxa as a filter)? A scalar view with a bureaucratic cut-off? Taxonomy as a cheap proxy for evidence? A precautionary principle about doubt? Or something not about welfare at all — standing conferred by political salience and enforceability? Say which, and why.

2. Take the tier form apart. Does a 1–5 score commit anybody to the idea that moral status comes in degrees, or could the scale be entirely about expected harm per procedure times number of animals, i.e. about where review effort buys the most, with status still all-or-nothing? Note that a bee might have a very small amount of status but there are unimaginably many bees — does that aggregate point rescue the cliff at tier 1 or destroy it? I want to know whether one practice can coherently hold both a continuum and a hard ontological line, because that's the bit my editor will ask about.

3. The "revisit as the science develops" clause — what does that presuppose? That moral status is an empirical property a species can gain or lose as we learn more? What breaks if sentience turns out not to be a natural-kind property at all, but something closer to a matter of our attitudes and our interpretive practices? Same question for the last-third-of-development line: what kind of fact about the onset of standing does that assume there is?

4. Be strict about three different relations, and don't collapse them: what the practice *presupposes* (could not be justified at all without it), what it merely *fits well with*, and what merely *explains* it historically or politically. The rat-mouse-bird exclusion and the ritual-slaughter exemption seem to me to belong in the third bucket. Is it even coherent to say "these rules presuppose that rats have no moral status," or is that a category mistake about what a budget carve-out commits anyone to?

5. Tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — which rival theories would license exactly these same lines, so that I can't conclude in print that the lawmakers believe any one particular thing.

6. Finally: if we replaced the implicit view with a seriously scalar one — status proportional to something like evidence-weighted capacity for suffering, all the way down — or with a view on which only suffering intensity matters and species membership is irrelevant, what would concretely have to change? The tier form, the review triggers, the numbers of animals used, whether insect farming and pesticide policy become welfare questions at all. And where would the new view break administratively, as opposed to just being unpopular?

Aim for maybe 1,200–1,500 words, with headers, and write it for a smart non-specialist. Please dont give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I cant check any of them and I don't want names dropped as decoration. If a philosopher's name is genuinely load-bearing for a point, use it once and move on.
