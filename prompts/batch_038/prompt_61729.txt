So I'm three years into a novel and I've hit a problem that's more philosophy than craft, but it's currently wrecking chapter fourteen. My narrator spent twenty-odd years making markets in something thin and local — water rights in a drought valley, essentially who gets to pump what and at what price — and she has talked herself into a view she thinks is hard-edged and clear-eyed, and I've let her state it in her own words in a dozen places. The view goes roughly like this: if two people trade voluntarily, and each knows the other is sane and paying attention, and both have read the same public record, then at least one of them is making a mistake. A trade isn't a mutual gain, it's a bet with a loser in it, and the price is just a ledger of who's wrong. She treats this as a kind of moral realism about markets — no speculation without disagreement, no disagreement without somebody fooling themselves — and she also uses it to explain why she trusts no one and lives alone, which is doing alot of character work, so I can't quietly delete it and paper over the hole.

Two of my early readers are economists and both of them wrote "this isn't right" in the margin, then unhelpfully disagreed about why. One says the result she's groping toward only rules out speculative trade inside a very specific setup — shared prior beliefs, everyone knowing that everyone is rational, nobody trading to shift risk or raise cash — and that the moment you allow people to have different appetites for risk or different needs for money, willing trades between two people who both know exactly what they're doing aren't just possible, they're tedious and everywhere. The other says the setup is fine but that the knowledge condition never actually holds in a real valley with real gossip, so her whole edifice rests on an idealization that fails for reasons having nothing to do with anybody being stupid. I've heard both of those now; what I need is the whole map, and please be blunt rather than diplomatic, because the book is due in fourteen months and my agent has started using the phrase "forward momentum."

Take her position as I've stated it and tell me what would count against it. Which assumptions have to fail for her to be wrong, what would the world have to look like if each one did, and which of those failures are live possibilities a person could actually run into in a drought valley — I want scenes, not footnotes — versus logical hedges no character would ever notice. Sort them for me into the ones that would genuinely kill the view and the ones that would only make it look naive. Then tell me which of these she herself would concede as a real problem and which she'd wave off as beside the point, and what specifically she'd have to witness to change her mind without being able to explain it away: some trade where both sides plainly came out ahead, some case of two people who know each other to be rational still betting against each other with neither one self-deceived, some behavior of prices her story can't accommodate. And the thing I'm actually most afraid of — has she, have I, quietly padded the view with qualifications until it can't be wrong at all? If it's now unfalsifiable, say so. A character holding an unfalsifiable view is a different character from one holding a bold-but-refutable one, and im better off knowing which book I'm writing; honestly the unfalsifiable version might be the stronger one, if I can work out what she's really doing with the belief instead of holding it.
