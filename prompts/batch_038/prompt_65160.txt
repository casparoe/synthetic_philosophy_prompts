I'm slogging through Mill's System of Logic, Book III ch. XXI (on the evidence for the law of universal causation) and I'm stuck. My copy's a bad scan so let me paraphrase rather than quote: Mill says the causal principle isn't an instinct or an a priori intuition but an induction by simple enumeration — the particular inductions we already trust (this kind of sequence has always had a discoverable cause, etc.) are what license it, and he doesn't think that's circular because those particular inductions never needed the general law to go through. He then adds that simple enumeration is only admissible where the field of instances is very wide and no counter-instance has turned up.

Two things I can't get straight:

1. If the particular inductions were already good without the general principle, what does the principle actually add? And isn't the step from those enumerations have held so far to every event has a cause just the bootstrapping slide — using a source's own outputs to certify the source — that people object to in reliabilist accounts of basic knowledge? Or does Mill's extra condition (huge domain, zero counter-instances so far) genuinely block it, so that the conclusion is earned rather than free? I can't tell whether the condition is doing philosophical work or just restating that induction has worked.

2. Here's a thought of my own, tell me if it's confused: maybe Mill is safe because the causal principle isn't one track record but a summary of a great many separate track records, and easy knowledge worries only bite when a single source vouches for itself. Does that distinction survive scrutiny, or does it just relocate the problem to the aggregation step — and if so, is there a principled difference between Mill's case and a perceptual source confirming its own reliability from its own outputs?
