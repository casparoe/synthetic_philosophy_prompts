Three months ago I was appointed chair of my professional association's ethics and standards committee, and my first assignment has been to read eleven years of the committee's case determinations in order to draft a style guide for future panels. A pattern has emerged from that reading that I have been unable to name, and I should like to know whether it corresponds to any established concept or whether it needs one.

In determination after determination, the committee arrives at what is unmistakably a moral conclusion without ever straightforwardly asserting a moral claim. Two specimens, lightly disguised:

A finding against a researcher who had reviewed a competitor's funding proposal opens by stipulating, for the purposes of the report, that an arrangement is extractive where one party captures value created by another without reciprocal contribution. Six pages later the panel establishes that the review made use of material the applicant had disclosed in confidence, states that such use falls within the stipulated definition, and recommends a two-year suspension from review duties. The words wrong, unfair, and ought never occur outside a conditional clause, an attribution to the membership's standing policy, or the definition itself.

A conflict-of-interest opinion reads in relevant part: the committee does not regard it as within its remit to pronounce on the ethics of the arrangement described; it notes that a substantial number of members have expressed the view that arrangements of this kind are indefensible; and it observes that the arrangement before it resembles, in every particular the policy identifies as relevant, one the committee censured in an earlier matter. It then declines to renew the respondent's appointment.

I have read these closely enough to know the transmission works. I formed the beliefs the documents are arranged to produce before I noticed the mechanism, and so did every colleague with whom I have discussed them. Yet if you asked any of the authors to state a single moral claim they had asserted, they would honestly be unable to. What strikes me as distinctive is the systematicity: the avoidance of unembedded moral sentences is complete rather than occasional, and each individual device — the conditional, the stipulated definition, the attribution to the membership, the analogy to a censured case, the open question — is unobjectionable in isolation and reads as precision or institutional modesty rather than evasion. My own first draft of the style guide contained three instances of the pattern, which is what finally convinced me this is not a matter of individual evasiveness at all.

The neighboring concepts I have come up with all seem to fall short, and I would want any answer to engage them rather than pass them over:

The Frege–Geach problem, as I understand it, concerns how a view that ties the meaning of moral sentences to the expression of attitudes can account for their occurrence in valid arguments in which they are not asserted; correct me if I have mis-stated that. My problem looks like the mirror image: not that a theory struggles with embedded occurrences, but that a whole body of discourse is built out of them and still moves its readers. I want to know whether what I have described is a difficulty for expressivism, something it predicts, or something outside its scope altogether.

Plausible deniability, and innuendo in the sense familiar from defamation, both concern content conveyed without being stated, but both concern a speaker's strategic option to disavow; several of these authors, I am confident, have no intention to convey a moral claim, and the belief arrives in readers regardless.

Conversational implicature and insinuation also involve non-asserted content, but they hang off something that is asserted, and here there is nothing for them to hang off. Apophasis is the deliberate raising of a matter under the guise of passing it over, which is a rhetorical posture, whereas this pattern survives perfectly polite, technical prose with no irony in it.

Presupposition, and the fallacy of the complex question, are the closest logical analogues, but they locate the smuggled content in a single sentence that a hearer can challenge on the spot; what I have found is distributed across the architecture of an entire document, so there is no place to object.

So: does this have an established name, whether in the literature on assertion, in the expressivist debates about embedding, in legal drafting, or in rhetoric? If it does, give it to me and tell me precisely how it differs from each of the neighbors above. If it does not, coin one and define it rigorously enough that I could put the definition into the style guide — which means marking its boundaries: whether a judicial opinion that argues wholly by analogy to settled cases and never asserts a normative premise counts; whether a document that quotes a professional code verbatim and applies it to the facts counts; whether a purely descriptive audit report that is routinely used to justify sanctions counts. A bare label will not serve my purposes. What I need is the phenomenon carved, its edges drawn, and an account of what, if anything, is objectionable about it, including whether a committee operating under an express no-pronouncements remit can avoid it at all.
