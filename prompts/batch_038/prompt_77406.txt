I'm a staff engineer on a platform that builds ranked-voting and jury-style decision tooling for large online communities. I've been asked to write up the "theory of the product" for an internal design review — the part that says why a crowd verdict deserves trust at all. I have no formal background in philosophy or political theory; what follows is assembled from the Condorcet jury theorem, Arrow's impossibility theorem, and about ten years of staring at real voting data. Before this goes in front of the review, I'd like a consistency audit rather than encouragement.

Here is the package as I currently hold it.

1. Our justification is epistemic, not expressive. A vote is an instrument for identifying the correct answer to a question that has a correct answer, and we are entitled to say the majority is more likely to be right than a randomly chosen individual voter. This is the Condorcet jury theorem, and it is the main reason I think this product should exist.

2. Our system satisfies the independence condition the theorem requires, because ballots are cast privately, are committed cryptographically before any tally is revealed, and no voter can observe another voter's ballot at the moment of casting.

3. We also display the current aggregate standings in the voting UI — not ballots, just the running totals — because engagement falls off a cliff without it. I've satisfied myself that this doesn't touch (2), since the individual ballots stay private.

4. We weight each ballot by a reputation score computed from how often that user's past votes agreed with the eventual majority on comparable questions. On historical replay tests this measurably improves accuracy, and I've been arguing internally that it strengthens the (1) justification.

5. We elect by instant-runoff, not by looking for a pairwise Condorcet winner, because in our data Condorcet winners frequently fail to exist once there are more than four or five options. Cycles are common.

6. Arrow's theorem doesn't threaten us, because it concerns social welfare functions over unrestricted preference profiles and we only ever need a winner on the profiles that actually occur. Our users vote over concrete, comparable options, so I expect their preferences to be reasonably ordered rather than arbitrarily scrambled.

7. When a question is high-stakes and the crowd's answer looks plainly wrong, a small internal panel of domain experts can override the result. I have a nagging feeling this contradicts (1), but I've kept the feature and I want to be told whether the worry is real or whether I'm confusing myself.

8. More voters is better. I also know from our dashboards that measured accuracy dropped for two quarters following a large influx of users from a partner community, and I have been explaining that to myself as a sampling artifact rather than a counterexample.

What I want back:

- Identify the genuine tensions and rank them by severity, most damaging first. For each, name the specific pair of commitments that produce it, explain why the conflict is real rather than merely rhetorical, state which one would have to give, and state what we'd lose by giving it up.
- Explicitly separate the genuine tensions from the ones that only look like tensions, and say why the apparent ones dissolve. Several of my worries are probably wasted and I'd rather know which than have everything flagged at equal weight.
- Where a commitment is doing load-bearing work under a description I've gotten wrong, give me the accurate description — I'd like to know what (2) and (8) actually commit me to, as distinct from what I've been telling the review they commit me to.
- Tell me which single revision would clean up the largest number of problems, and what it costs.

Use whatever standard terminology applies to the assumptions I'm violating, but don't send me a reading list or references to chase; I need the reasoning, not the bibliography.
