For four years I have convened an interdisciplinary seminar on the free energy principle, whose members include a computational psychiatrist who works inside the Friston programme, an enactivist philosopher, and a systems neuroscientist who is a committed but unenthusiastic predictive processing moderate. Something has happened that I cannot yet name. They now agree on essentially all of the formalism — variational free energy as a tractable upper bound on surprise, action selection as the minimisation of expected free energy, the partition of a system into internal, blanket, and external states — and they agree on the empirical findings usually wheeled out in its support, including the attenuation of neural responses to predicted stimuli and the modelling of perceptual bistability and of dopaminergic signalling. They even agree on the standing objections: the dark room problem, and the charge that the framework can absorb any outcome after the fact. Yet on the question the seminar exists to answer — whether this apparatus tells us what a mind is — they have not moved an inch in four years, and I have come to suspect they are not so much disagreeing as occupying different questions that happen to share a vocabulary.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than to adopt, is that the divergence sits one level below where they actually argue: it concerns the status of the Markov blanket. For the psychiatrist the partition is a modelling device, and its fruitfulness is the whole of its warrant. For the enactivist the partition, once posited, quietly imports a boundary that the world is then supposed to supply, and everything downstream — representation, agency, teleology, the mark of the mental — inherits that smuggling. For the neuroscientist the partition is a genuine causal structure but a philosophically inert one, no more telling us about minds than a cell membrane tells us about cells. On my hypothesis each takes the others' substantive claims to be licensed or blocked by a view about partitions that they have never argued for and would disavow if it were stated to them plainly.

Please diagnose this impasse. First, reconstruct the three positions in terms their holders would recognise, and identify the single premise on which their substantive conclusions actually turn; tell me whether my blanket hypothesis is that premise or whether something else is load-bearing. Second, say what kind of disagreement this is: a verbal dispute over 'model', 'representation', and 'boundary'; a substantive one with hidden empirical content; or three distinct questions — design, mechanism, metaphysics — conducted under a single heading. Third, apply the diagnosis to one live case: when a defender says active inference derives action selection from expected free energy and a critic replies that this is reinforcement learning with an epistemic bonus under a new name, is there a theorem or an experiment that could decide the exchange, or does it merely replay the prior disagreement about what would count as deriving something? Finally, state what progress would consist in — a distinction, a formal result, a new paradigm, or a change of question — and give me an honest assessment of whether progress should be expected at all, or whether the impasse is evidence that the framing question is ill-posed. I do not want a survey of the framework, and I do not want it defended; I want its disagreement structure laid bare.
