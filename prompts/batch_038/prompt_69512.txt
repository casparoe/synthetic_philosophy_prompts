I am the curator of paintings at a regional museum, and next Thursday I give the opening remarks — six minutes, standing, in the gallery itself, no podium — for a new exhibition of Dutch seventeenth-century marine painting.

The circumstances are awkward. The centerpiece is a small seascape on panel, bought in the 1930s by a local shipping merchant and bequeathed to us by his widow. It has hung in this building, in this room, for eighty-odd years, and the family's bequest — the reason the museum has a Dutch collection at all — was made largely for the sake of that one picture. Two years ago, tree-ring dating of the panel and pigment analysis established that it cannot be what the old frame label says it is, and the file now attributes it, with some confidence, to a forger who was active in the 1920s. Our director and I have decided not to take it down. It stays where it has always hung, under the same lighting, with a new wall label saying what it is, how we know, and that it was bought in good faith. The family has not seen the wording of that label. Two grandchildren will be standing in the front row. A national newspaper has already run a piece headlined 'Museum's Pride Is a Fake,' and people who read it will be in the room with cameras, alongside the two scientists who did the analysis and a section of the public who have come because they like the picture and always have.

Please write the remarks. They need to do four things at once: welcome people to an exhibition; explain, to an audience most of whom have never considered the question, why a museum would keep a known forgery on the wall rather than quietly retire it to the stores; explain what the discovery does and does not cost us as a matter of looking; and say something to the family, who are learning the label's contents tonight, in front of everyone.

Two constraints on how it is done. First, no retreat into the consoling line that authenticity is a fetish and the only thing that matters is what happens between the picture and the eye. I do not believe that line and the speech should not pretend I do. I have looked at this panel for twenty years, and since the report came back I find it a mediocre picture: the assurance I used to admire now reads to me as strain, the weather in the sky is a formula repeated three times, and the signature is doing work the painting cannot do without it. I may of course be fooling myself — I can no longer see it with the old label in mind or without it — and the speech should carry that uncertainty honestly rather than resolving it in either direction. Second, the exhibition also includes, one bay further on, a marine by a pupil in the master's own workshop, painted in the right decade on the right wood and hanging under a label that says exactly that; nobody proposes calling it a fake, though it too was not made by the hand whose name once sold it. I would like the remarks to use that pairing, because I think it carries the real argument: the difference between the two objects is not quality and not age, but what each of them claims.

The result should sound like something a person says to a room, not an essay read aloud, and it should not lean on 'provenance,' 'aura,' or 'authenticity' as terms of art. It should end on the family, and end neither in apology nor in compliment. Above all, I would like the honesty about the painting's quality and the kindness to the grandchildren not to be two pieces of rhetoric competing for the same six minutes, but to turn out to require each other.
