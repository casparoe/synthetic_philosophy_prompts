I have been trying to reconstruct the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument in the Lucas–Penrose tradition as rigorously as I can, for a reading group, and the more carefully I state it, the more it looks like an argument against the possibility of sound reasoning as such rather than an argument against mechanism. I would like a diagnosis: which step to reject, why that step looked innocuous, and what each candidate rejection costs.

Stipulations. An arithmetical reasoner R is an effective procedure that takes the code of an arithmetical sentence as input and either halts — thereby asserting that sentence — or fails to halt. R is sound if every sentence R asserts is true. For any reasoner R that has an effective description available to me, the relation R halts on input x is arithmetically definable, so by a standard diagonal construction there is a sentence D(R) which says of itself that R does not assert it.

My argument:

1. If R is a sound reasoner, R does not assert D(R). For if R asserted D(R), then D(R) would be true, which is to say that R does not assert D(R).
2. I am a sound arithmetical reasoner.
3. For any reasoner R whose effective description is available to me and whose soundness I can recognize, I can recognize that D(R) is true.
4. So there are true arithmetical sentences no such R can assert that I can nevertheless see to be true; hence I am not such an R. This is the conclusion I wanted.
5. Now suppose, for reductio, that I am such an R — call it H. Let M be the machine that, on any input, runs H on that input and halts exactly when H halts. M is an effective procedure because H is one; M is sound because H is sound; and M's soundness is available to me for recognition, since M asserts precisely what I assert and, by (2), I am sound.
6. So by (3) I recognize D(M) to be true. Since M's assertions are mine, M asserts D(M).
7. But by (1), since M is sound, M does not assert D(M). Contradiction.
8. Therefore either the assumption in (5) fails, or one of (1)–(3) fails. On either reading the result is disturbing: the reasoning seems to show not that I am not a machine, but that no reasoner, human or machine, can be both sound and in a position to recognize its own soundness. And if that is what (1)–(3) really amount to, then the condition in (3) is never met for any reasoner built around me, which removes exactly the case the anti-mechanist needed in order to claim a power that machines lack.

Which step do you reject? If forced, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than anything else, since (1) looks like nothing but diagonal arithmetic and (2) looks like a modest claim about my own reliability. Beyond naming the faulty step, please address the following. Is the reasoning in (5)–(7) genuinely a reductio, or does it establish only a disjunction that the anti-mechanist can live with? Are the phrases is sound, can recognize the soundness of, and asserts doing uniform work across (1), (3), (5), and (6), or does the argument shift between a fact about a reasoner and a fact about my epistemic access to that reasoner? Is the composition in (5) legitimate at all when the component I supply has no effective description of its own, and does that matter differently depending on whether (4) is true or false? If your preferred exit leaves any residue of an anti-mechanist argument, state the weaker claim that survives it and say what that weaker claim can and cannot support. Finally, state the general lesson about diagonal arguments directed at a reasoner that is made to contain the agent running the diagonal.
