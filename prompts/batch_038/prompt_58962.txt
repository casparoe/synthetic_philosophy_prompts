So I run a little private sandbox server — modded, doesn't really matter which game — with about eleven friends on it, and last month somebody's account got hijacked and the person who got in burned down a town three of us had been building on and off for like two years. Not a tragedy in the scheme of things, obviously. But I was genuinely angry for a couple of days, and what's been bugging me since is that the anger felt exactly proportionate to something real, not to a picture of something. So I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about virtual stuff by just writing down my gut verdicts, and I want to know whether there's a principle underneath them or whether some of them have to go.

Here they are. The burned town: a real wrong done to real people, and my anger was appropriate rather than a confusion about what exists — but if someone had burned down my actual garden shed I'd have been angrier, so I'm clearly applying some discount and I can't say what sets its size. In-game gold on our server, which we trade for favors and which we've all agreed counts for something: taking it is theft, full stop, no scare quotes. Gold in a single-player game where nobody will ever find out: no wrong at all, and I don't even feel like anything was lost when that save file corrupts. My friend Ana died a couple years ago and her character is still standing in the town square; last year her husband put a chatbot on it trained off her old messages. Talking to it felt good and also not like a betrayal of anything. I'm confident it isn't Ana and that deleting it wouldn't be remotely like killing someone — and yet I'd want a reason before I deleted it, and I can't tell you what kind of reason. Then: a guy I know who spends every evening in a single-player game slowly killing NPCs that beg and cry, no audience, no multiplayer, not much else going on in his life — I think that's bad, in a way that isn't just bad taste. But a novelist friend of mine writes extremely detailed torture scenes into a private document she'll never show anyone, and I think she's doing nothing wrong whatsoever. Those two feel different to me and I honestly can't see why, beyond one of them involves pressing buttons. And an old friend I've only ever known inside these games, six years, never met: no discount at all there. Not "a virtual friendship," just friendship. Last one — getting attached to a companion character in a story game and feeling awful when I abandoned the playthrough: the attachment was real, the guilt was a mistake about the facts.

My own tentative principle is that the discount scales with two things: how many other minds are hooked up to the item, and how hard it is to replace. That seems to handle the town versus the shed, and it handles the two kinds of gold nicely. But it does nothing for the novelist and the NPC-torturer, since both score zero on the first factor and are infinitely replaceable on the second, and I'm obviously not neutral between them. And I'm not sure it says anything sensible about Ana. So: state the principle — or the small set of competing ones — as precisely as you can, clause by clause, and tell me which of my cases each clause is doing work for. Then run it on cases I haven't listed: cruelty toward NPCs that are procedurally generated to be maximally convincing but that everyone knows aren't sentient, versus cruelty toward a chatbot that might be; and a "server sunset," where the developer announces the world gets switched off in a year and everyone has known the date for months. And finally tell me straight which of my verdicts the best version has to overturn. If you think the novelist/torturer difference is real, give me the actual mechanism, not a vibe; if you don't, say which verdict I should drop and why the other one keeps pulling at me.

One more thing — I want to know how confident you are in whatever you land on. Point at the specific claim in your answer you're least sure about and say why. I'd much rather get a clear "this clause is load-bearing and shaky, here's what would break it" than an evenly confident essay all the way down.
