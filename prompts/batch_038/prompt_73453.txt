Here's the material I've gathered that I want the question to actually turn on (please build on these specifics, don't swap in other ones). The word got popular around 2000 through the atmospheric chemist Paul Crutzen, who pointed at the industrial revolution and the steam engine as roughly where it starts. A working group was set up in 2009 under the international body of stratigraphers whose whole job was to make the Anthropocene a formal epoch with a "golden spike" — one physical marker layer at one place that pins the boundary down. In 2019 they mostly settled on a mid-20th-century date, and in 2023 they proposed lake sediments at Crawford Lake in Ontario, boundary around 1950, marker being plutonium fallout from nuclear weapons testing. Then in 2024 the quaternary subcommission voted the whole proposal down by a wide margin — partly because the sediment record is so thin and the proposed start so recent — and the higher international bodies confirmed the rejection while saying the term would keep getting used informally. Meanwhile there's a competing "early Anthropocene" line going back to a palaeoclimatologist named Ruddiman, who argued the CO2 and methane curves started bending thousands of years ago with farming and rice paddies, and another camp pointing at a dip in atmospheric CO2 around 1610 that they connect to reforestation in the Americas after Indigenous populations collapsed following European contact.

So the question I want should not be answerable by a kid reciting "the Anthropocene means humans are wrecking the earth and it started in 1950" or by listing candidate dates in order. The 2024 rejection is the perfect hook for that: does a vote by a stratigraphy subcommission that it isn't an epoch settle anything about whether the Anthropocene is real?
