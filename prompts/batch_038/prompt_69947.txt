The episode I want to test it on is Ignaz Semmelweis and childbed fever in Vienna.

Here are the facts as I understand them, so you don't have to reconstruct them, and please correct me where I've got them wrong. In the late 1840s the Vienna General Hospital had two maternity clinics. The first, staffed by physicians and medical students who also performed autopsies, lost roughly ten percent of its mothers to puerperal fever; the second, staffed by midwives who did not attend autopsies, lost only a few percent. After a professor of forensic medicine died in 1847 of an infection contracted when a student's scalpel cut him during a postmortem, Semmelweis concluded that the professor's illness and childbed fever were the same thing, and he ordered chlorine-lime handwashing between the autopsy room and the delivery room. Mortality in the first clinic fell to somewhere near one or two percent. He had no mechanism to offer, only cadaverous particles carried on the hands; he was not kept on in Vienna, moved to Pest, published his main book in 1861, wrote increasingly hostile open letters accusing senior obstetricians of responsibility for the deaths, was committed to an asylum in 1865, and died there within weeks of a septic wound. Acceptance came afterward, once Pasteur's germ theory and Lister's antiseptic surgery supplied the missing mechanism.
