For five years I have sat on the machinery incident committee of a growers' cooperative in our county, and I would like help making sense of what we have actually been doing. We are seven members who meet in the back room of the co-op office on the first Tuesday of the month. I am the only one who is not a farmer; I run the hardware and feed store in town, which is presumably why I ended up keeping the ledger. Our job is narrow: when one of the co-op's autonomous machines — the sprayers, the harvesters, the automated gate systems, the irrigation controllers — damages a member's crop, livestock, or property, we decide who absorbs the loss. We have no written rules and no bylaws governing this. We have only our past decisions, which I have recorded case by case, and we treat them the way I understand a court treats its own precedents, though nobody in the room would put it that way.

Here are the six rulings that matter. First, an autonomous sprayer drifted onto a neighbor's certified-organic field and cost him his certification for three years; the drift happened because a wind shift outran the software's cutoff rule by about a second, and the route had been uploaded by the co-op's own agronomist. We made the co-op pay in full and cleared the agronomist, and my minute for that meeting reads that the wind was nobody's business. Second, an automated grain dryer's controller failed after a firmware update the manufacturer pushed against our maintenance schedule and scorched a member's entire barley batch; we billed the manufacturer under warranty and the member recovered in full, even though he had skipped eleven consecutive monthly inspections that our agreement required, and even though two of us argued on the night that his own neglect should have reduced what he got. Third, an autonomous tractor being supervised by a trainee who was looking at his phone rolled into a ditch and was written off; we held the trainee personally liable for a quarter of the loss and the co-op took the rest, and the reason we gave was that his hand had been on the stop button. Fourth, an autonomous harvester crossed a property line and cut a strip of the wrong member's crop, following a boundary that came from the shared GPS map this committee had approved years earlier; neither member had any input into the route, we split the loss between their two accounts, and the ledger says no fault found. Fifth, a member defeated the safety interlock on an automated cattle gate with a bolt so he could work faster, and the gate injured his hired hand; we held him fully liable and referred him to the board. Sixth, a contractor piled a tarp over a soil-moisture probe and left it there; the probe sent nonsense to an irrigation controller, which watered straight through a frost and cost a member part of an orchard. We held the contractor liable and cleared both the member and the co-op, on the ground that the machine had been in the state somebody else left it in.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the second and the third. A man who ignored a standing obligation to check on a machine recovers everything; a boy who ignored a standing instruction to watch a machine pays a quarter. Both were inattentive, both machines failed, and in both cases the co-op was the party that gained from running the equipment. My own working guess has been that we assign loss when a human hand can be pointed to and socialize it when none can — but that predicts the same outcome for those two, and we did not rule them the same way, so my guess is wrong or incomplete. A woman from the state extension office who attended one of our meetings used the phrase responsibility gap about cases like ours, and the phrase stuck with me, though I could not tell you what anyone who writes about such things would make of our ledger.

The case in front of us is this. Last autumn we installed an automated frost-fan controller that decides for itself when to start the orchard fans on cold nights, on the strength of its own temperature model. The manufacturer's manual says a person should walk the block each evening and check the controller's readings against a hand thermometer. Our practice, set by this committee three years ago and never revisited, is that nobody walks the block; we rely on the machine. In April the controller did not start the fans during a cold pocket, and a young member who had just put in forty acres of cider apples lost close to the whole planting. What makes it hard is that the member sited the temperature sensor himself, following the diagram in the manual, in a position that turns out to read about two degrees warm on still nights. He had no reason to doubt the diagram and neither did we. The controller did exactly what it was built to do with the number it was handed. Nobody tampered with anything, nobody was careless that night, the manufacturer's service record with us is clean, and yet if anyone had taken the evening walk that the co-op had told itself three years ago it would never take, there would be no loss to assign.

We meet next Tuesday. What I want is a prediction of how this committee will rule, and a recommendation of how it should rule if it wants to remain consistent with itself, arrived at rather than merely listed. Identify the candidate rules that fit all six of our past decisions — I expect there is more than one, and I expect at least two of them pull apart on the frost-fan case — and say for each what it delivers here, whether the loss falls on the member, on the co-op, on the manufacturer, or on everybody and therefore nobody. Then tell me which precedent is the closest analog to the new case and why, and if you think our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, say plainly which principle we would be adopting by ruling one way rather than the other, including whether either choice commits us to accepting that some losses simply have no responsible party at all. Repair or replace my tentative rule about pointing to a human hand, and state the revised version precisely, clause by clause, with the case from our ledger that motivates each clause. Also describe one further past case that, had we ruled on it, would have settled which rule we are running — the hypothetical decision that would have forced our hand. Finally, if you think one of our six verdicts was simply wrong and is distorting everything downstream, say which one and what we should have done instead. I would rather have a definite answer with reasons than a careful survey of possibilities; my brother-in-law, who is not on the committee, has already told me we should always bill the manufacturer because they are the ones with the lawyers, and I want to be able to explain to him, and to the other six, exactly why that is too easy.
