ok so quick context: I'm a junior in a philosophy of mind course this semester, and I also run the optional peer-led discussion section that goes with the lecture — about 16 students, 75 minutes, Thursday afternoons. We just finished the unity of consciousness / split-brain unit and honestly the section is stuck in a rut. Half of them read about the commissurotomy experiments and are like "obviously that's two subjects of experience in one skull, case closed," and the other half say "no, the patient reports being one person and acts like one person most of the time, so it's one," and neither side budges because nobody has actually felt the pull of the problem, they've just read about it. Sperry and Gazzaniga's stuff and that classic confabulation demo — the one where a patient is shown a chicken claw to one hemisphere and a snowy scene to the other, then picks a shovel and explains the shovel choice by reference to the chicken — gets quoted at each other constantly with nobody really getting why it's supposed to be evidence for anything.

Can you act as a philosophy-of-mind professor who has actually designed and taught these hands-on activities before, rather than somebody writing from an armchair? Think carefully about this. Before you commit, brainstorm two or three candidate activity designs out loud, tell me which one you'd pick and why, and then write that one up in full. Please write in American English, and don't give me a bibliography or a reading list at the end — I can't chase sources down and it's not what I need.

What I want is for the whole section to collectively *be* a single agent for about 40 minutes, with the class split into two teams playing the two hemispheres, plus somebody playing the body/output and somebody playing the experimenter who hands out tasks and collects the agent's verbal reports. The two hemisphere teams should share a "callosum" channel with a hard budget — a limited number of written notes per round, no talking across the table — and then at some point mid-activity that channel gets cut without warning, and they should not see it coming. I want real asymmetries after the cut, not just "now you can't talk": only one hemisphere gets to produce the public verbal report, the other one can only act through a designated hand-player, and the two hemispheres get shown different information about the task items so that each genuinely knows something the other doesn't. There should be a round where the speaking hemisphere has to explain, in front of the experimenter, why the hand just did what it did when it has no idea — and a round where the two hands end up working against each other on the same physical object. Also throw in some way to partially restore transfer at a cost, so the group has to decide whether to buy it.

For scoring, no real money — index cards, tokens, whatever — but make the payoffs do philosophical work: the agent should earn something for completing tasks and something separate for the accuracy of its self-reports, with a penalty when a report turns out to be confabulated, and near the end give the two hemispheres slightly divergent interests so the class has to argue about whether the agent has one set of interests or two. And here's the piece I care most about: at the very start, before anyone knows what's coming, have every student secretly write down how many subjects of experience they think the agent they're about to play has, plus one sentence of reasoning, and seal it. Re-poll at the end. The debrief should be built around comparing those two sets of answers.

The mechanism has to live in the rules and the incentives, not in a lecture I give afterwards.
