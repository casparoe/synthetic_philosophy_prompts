I'm auditing a grad social-choice seminar and we just did the paradox of not voting, and I left more confused than when I walked in. Everyone in the room reached for one of the usual fixes — tack on a D-term for civic duty, say people vote expressively rather than instrumentally, or insist the 1/N pivotality number is too pessimistic — and our TA basically said all three are patches on a model thats asking the wrong question, then never said what the right question is. A friend of mine has an idea she calls, half joking, the "closeness implies correlation" principle, and I think that's what he was circling. The thought goes: you can't assign yourself a probability of being pivotal without also having some account of *why* the race is tight, and every realistic account — a late shock, a get-out-the-vote push, a big chunk of the electorate genuinely on the fence — describes a bunch of people whose reasoning is hooked up to yours through the same news cycle, the same feeds, the same friends dragging them to the polls. So conditioning on "this one is inside a few hundred votes" doesn't just raise the direct chance your ballot flips it, it raises the chance that your decision and theirs move together. If that's right, the voter isn't placing a one-person bet against a fixed state of the world at all, and the paradox of voting isn't a paradox about costs and benefits but about how you're supposed to reason when your evidence about a situation partly comes from people who are in that same situation.

Can you argue that position for me, as a thesis I could actually hand in rather than a survey of who said what? I want the positive case built up carefully and explicitly — I don't care whether you dress it in decision-theoretic notation or plain English, use whichever is clearer — and then the two or three objections that would kill it if they landed, with your best answers. The obivous ones are the "that's just magical thinking, my ballot can't causally move anyone else's" objection, and the reflexivity worry: if this reasoning makes voting rational for every member of the correlated bloc, they all vote, the race stops being close, and the premise that got the argument going is gone — so is the view self-undermining, or does it survive that? And tell me what's left of the standard paradox once you grant all this: does it dissolve completely, or does something of it survive in a nastier form?
