Not a lawyer or a historian, just someone who cant stop noticing a particular argument shape.

Here's the thing I keep running into. Somebody with authority wants to punish conduct that no writen rule covered at the time it happened. And instead of owning that — instead of saying "yes, we're laying down this rule now, and yes, we're reaching back with it" — they insist that some rule already on the books really did cover it all along, it just hadn't been spelled out or applied that way before. The newness gets denied rather than defended.

Three places I've hit it:

Reading up on the big war-crimes trials after WWII. The defence says starting an aggressive war wasn't a crime you could be charged with when the defendants did it. And the answer isn't "true, but this is so grave we'll make an exception." The answer is "actually it was — everyone had signed treaties renouncing war, so the prohibition existed, it just had never been enforced as a hanging offence."

A friend got booted from a forum over a post that was written up under a clause the mods added to the rules months after he posted it. When he pointed out the timing they said the new clause was just codifying what the rules had always meant.

Workplace one: a colleague got put on a performance plan for behaviour that only entered the employee handbook this year, and HR's line was that the older "unprofessional conduct" clause obviously covered it, the new wording just made it explicit.

All three use the identical move: the rule gets stretched backwards, and the stretch is described as a discovery rather than a change.

So — is there an established concept for this? And I mean the carving, not the label, a name by itself won't help me. I already know there's a general principle against punishing people under laws made after the fact, and I also know judges stretch and reinterpret rules all the time and nobody treats that as a scandal. So where exactly is the line? At what point does "the old rule always covered this" stop being ordinary interpretation and turn into making new law while insisting you aren't? Is there a genuine difference between the two, or is it the same move dressed up differently depending on who's doing it and how bad the conduct was? And does the war-trials case differ in kind from the forum and the handbook, or just in stakes?
