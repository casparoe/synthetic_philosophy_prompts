ok so — six weeks from now I'm giving a 25-minute talk plus Q&A at a type design research day, and I mostly want to rehearse the hostile part, not the slides. The room will be about thirty people: mostly working type designers who came up in the Dutch school, so they think in masters, interpolation, and the discipline of a designspace, and they have strong feelings about what counts as a real argument; plus four or five people from a visual word recognition lab who are pretty committed to the idea that letter identification is graded similarity to stored feature-based prototypes and who will smell a category error from the back row. Nobody there has read the old gridfont / letter-spirit work except one guy who will bring it up twice. My thesis is that there's no such thing as the 'a': letter categories have no essence that survives being pulled out of a particular family, and what we call a stable letter is a stylistic invariant that's only definable relative to a designspace. I'm basically claiming the FARG people had the hierarchy backwards — they treat the letter as the fixed thing and style as what varies inside it, and I want to say style is the continuous quantity and letterness is a threshold that gets imposed on top of it, locally, by whatever you've just been looking at.

The evidence I'll show is a study I ran last spring, and this is the part I need pressure-tested. I took the double-story 'a' from three faces — a humanist text face, a geometric, a slab — and built a 2-D interpolation space between them, then sampled 90 glyphs on a grid across it. Forty participants rated each glyph on two separate scales, presented in counterbalanced blocks: "is this still an a?" and "does this belong to the same family as the one you saw a moment ago?". I expected the two curves to track. They didn't. What I got is what I've started calling the shoulder collapse: the letterness ratings sit at ceiling until roughly 70% of the way along a path and then fall off a cliff, while the family-matching ratings degrade smoothly from the very first step and are near chance long before letterness has moved at all. So there's a wide region where a glyph is still unquestionably an 'a' but is definitely nobody's cousin, and the two functions cross in a way that looks to me like they're measuring different kinds of thing. I also have a control that I think is my best card: when the same glyphs are shown in shuffled order instead of as a sequence, the letterness cliff shifts by about 12% of the path length, which I take to show the threshold is being set by context rather than by the shape sitting on the screen.
