Our sentencing guidelines run on something like a table of exchange rates. A guideline for a given category of assault states a starting point in weeks of custody with a range either side of it; treats a suspended sentence, an unpaid-work requirement of between forty and three hundred hours, and a fine as positions on a single scale of severity; converts days spent under a curfew condition of bail into days of custody at a fixed ratio and deducts them from the term eventually imposed; and sets fines not as fixed sums but as a fraction of the offender's relevant weekly income, on the express ground that what must be equalised between a wealthy and an impecunious offender is the burden rather than the payment.

What theory of punishment does this machinery presuppose? Does it require that penalties be commensurable quantities of one underlying bad, measured in experienced hardship, or can a retributivist view that needs only to rank offences by seriousness and penalties by severity — matching the two orderings without ever fixing a rate of exchange — sustain the practice exactly as it stands? Say which rival accounts the machinery rules out and which it merely fits comfortably. Then address the discrepancy I cannot resolve: if the currency of punishment is burden, why is a custodial term never adjusted for the far heavier weight that the identical sentence bears on an offender with dependent children, a serious illness or an unusually fragile disposition? Does a system willing to recalibrate the fine but not the prison commit itself to a conception of penal currency that it nowhere avows? Finally, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely convenient for sentencers to assume, and tell me what would have to change in the guidelines, and in the reasons given for departing from them, if the presupposed view were replaced by one on which punishment is essentially the communication of censure rather than the imposition of a measurable quantity of hardship.
