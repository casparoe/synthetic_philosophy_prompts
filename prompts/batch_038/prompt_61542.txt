Two conversations from the past month have left me with the same puzzle, and I have run out of places to look it up. In the first, a colleague of mine — eight years of medical training at punishing hours, a mortgage that will bind him until he is sixty-five, two relocations for his partner's career — treated the suggestion that he give away a tenth of his income to highly effective charities as an obviously excessive demand. Pressed, he granted that the money would do more good elsewhere and granted that the lifetime cost of the mortgage dwarfs the donations, and still maintained that the two cases differ in kind. In the second, a friend who spent two years caring for her mother through a terminal illness, and who describes those years as the hardest and most worthwhile of her life, considers it a reductio of utilitarianism that it might ask her not to have children so that she could donate what raising them would cost. I work in public health policy and have been auditing a graduate seminar in normative ethics for a year, mostly to sharpen my thinking about cost-effectiveness, and I have ended up keeping a private list of these exchanges; the entry they all share is that the resistance does not seem to track the size of the sacrifice at all.

The second feature on the list is the one I find harder to state. What appears to trigger the objection is not magnitude but the absence of a completion point. Demands with a terminus are accepted however steep — a degree, a loan, a sick parent, a house, a project one can finish — while demands that run on indefinitely, with no state of affairs in which one has finally done enough, are refused however modest. My working hypothesis is that people are not estimating how much morality asks of them but checking whether the asking fits inside a socially recognized script and whether it ever ends. I know this is complicated by parenting, which has no finish line and no obvious point of sufficiency and which nonetheless never attracts the objection, so the second feature cannot be the whole story; whatever the right account is, it has to explain why that case comes out differently.

So: does this diagnosis have a name? I am not asking about the demandingness objection itself, which I know of, nor about Williams's complaint that impartial moral theories undermine the agent's integrity, nor about Wolf's moral saint, nor about proposals to reserve a protected sphere for the agent's own projects — all of those concern whether the demands are genuine or corrupting, whereas what interests me is a claim about the pattern of what people actually object to. Is there an established concept for the thesis that the objection tracks script-fitting and terminability rather than cost? If nothing in the literature captures it, say so plainly and propose a term with a definition tight enough that I could tell whether a new case falls under it, and tell me which of the neighbors it is most likely to be confused with and how to keep them apart. I also want to know which way the diagnosis cuts. Is it meant to explain the objection away, by showing that the intuitions behind it respond to a factor with no normative weight — and if there is a general name for that style of move in ethics, does invoking it here beg any question against the objector? — or is it meant to reveal what the objection was really about all along, in which case the objectors have been reporting their own psychology inaccurately? Finally, where are the edges: does it extend to demands that are unscripted but clearly bounded, or scripted but open-ended, and does it travel outside consequentialism to, say, a Kantian duty of self-improvement or to the demands of tax compliance? A single label would not help me much; what I need is the carving.
