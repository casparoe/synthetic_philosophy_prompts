So, quick background: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, and the one thing in this area I've actually read is that Moloch essay a friend sent me years ago — the one where nobody wants the bad outcome but everyone's individually sensible moves pile up into it anyway. Lately I've been in a running arguement with two coworkers about whether that kind of trap is always just a prisoner's dilemma wearing a costume. My position is no: I think there's a version where the trap is powered by people trying to be *responsible* instead of people being selfish, and where the escape route is blocked by something other than mutual distrust. I want to see if that can actually be built rigorously rather than asserted. Act like a philosopher who does formal work on collective action problems but who has also spent real time around how governance bodies like sports leagues or standards committees actually operate, and think carefully before writing — I'd much rather have a case that genuinely works than a fast one.

Here's the build. Construct a thought experiment set in an invented-but-realistic professional sports league, where a race to the bottom happens under these constraints:

- The sport, the rule, and the loophole can all be made up — they just need to be real-shaped, the kind of unintended strategy that's technically legal and wins games while making the product worse. Scenario itself maxes out at two paragraphs.
- No villains, no cheating, no lying, no rule-breaking, no stupidity. Every owner and exec is competent and sincere, and each one's reasoning has to be good enough that I read it and think "yeah, I'd probably do the same."
- Full information: everyone knows everyone else's incentives and reasoning, and there's a public, correct, unanimous diagnosis among the owners that the loophole is wrecking the league. So the trap cannot be a misunderstanding or a signaling failure.
- The engine has to be each actor's belief that they're the most responsible party available and that sitting it out just hands the field to people who'd do it worse.
- It can't collapse into a prisoner's dilemma or a tragedy of the commons. If you can write it as a 2x2 where defecting strictly dominates, you've missed the spec. (If it helps, the adoption phase can be more bandwagon-shaped than PD-shaped — I care much more about the phase after everyone can already see the trap.)
- The damage has to come back around onto the people making the decisions, not mainly onto fans or bystanders, otherwise it's just a pollution problem with extra steps.
- Include a ratchet: once everybody has adapted, getting out has to be individually irrational *even with* unanimous agreement and perfect information. Sunk roster and front-office investment plus a supermajority rule-change process is one way, but pick whatever's cleanest.
- No perfect predictors, no oracles, no sci-fi, no AI.

What the case is for, so you know what counts as cheating: I want to demonstrate to my coworkers that "rational actors + full information + good faith" doesn't automatically buy you a prisoner's dilemma. A scenario that just stipulates the bad ending ("the owners are shortsighted") instead of generating it out of the actors' own reasoning doesn't count.
