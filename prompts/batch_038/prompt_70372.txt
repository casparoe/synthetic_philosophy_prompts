I am revising a paper in political epistemology whose central device is a model of iterative deliberative convergence: a population of agents who, over rounds, render their beliefs and degrees of belief mutually coherent, and who then treat as justified those principles that survive that process. Two referees raised computational objections that my draft has no resources to answer.

The first notes that consistency-checking a set of propositional beliefs is NP-complete, and that the problem for graded belief is no better: if an assessment assigns probability one to each member of a set of formulas, then coherence of the assessment — the existence of a probability measure agreeing with it — entails that the set is satisfiable, so coherence-checking is at least as hard as satisfiability itself. The second notes that my convergence step is an equilibrium-selection problem, and that computing a Nash equilibrium even of a two-player game is PPAD-complete. Their joint conclusion is that the model does not merely idealize but requires the impossible, and that a requirement no agent can meet cannot guide action or confer justification.

I have five candidate repairs: (1) keep the exact norms and reinterpret them as regulative limit ideals, the way a frictionless plane functions in mechanics; (2) replace them with explicitly resource-bounded norms — bounded coherence, approximate equilibrium, satisficing; (3) keep the exact norms but relocate the computation, so that the requirement binds institutions, tools, and divisions of cognitive labor rather than individual heads; (4) argue from typical-case easiness, that the instances real agents face are small or structured enough that the worst-case results simply do not apply; (5) deny that complexity results bear on normative theory at all.

Before I commit to one, I want the specification rather than the choice: the desiderata that any complexity-sensitive account of idealization in normative theory must satisfy to count as adequate, where adequate means good enough to tell me, for a given norm in a given model, whether a hardness result defeats it, weakens it, or leaves it untouched — without waiting on open problems.

At minimum the list must cover: worst-case versus average-case versus typical difficulty and which of these a normative theory may lean on; the gap between asymptotic statements and the finite, small instances a human actually carries, including how a resource budget is supposed to be specified at all; the case where a problem is in P yet yields no usable algorithm because the constants are unknown and the existence proof is non-constructive, and its mirror image where an NP-hard problem is solved daily on industrial instances; outcome norms versus process norms, since it matters whether the agent must perform the computation or merely have the resulting property; whose computation counts — the agent's, the theorist's, an auditor's, a machine's; whether the idealization is doing predictive, evaluative, or justificatory work, since the objection bites differently in each; whether substituting approximation guarantees actually lowers the complexity or merely relocates the hardness; and robustness to the unresolved status of P versus NP and of one-way functions. Add whatever else you think is load-bearing and that I have missed.

I suspect two pairs are in tension — robustness to open problems against determinacy of verdict, and preserving a norm's critical bite against making it executable — but I may be wrong about which pair is real.
