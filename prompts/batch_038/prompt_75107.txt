I am leading a session on diachronic coherence in a graduate reading group, and there is a conceptual knot I have not been able to untie. What I want is an explanation of the knot itself, not a map of the surrounding debate.

The knot arises from two claims that are almost always advanced together. The first is the Reflection principle: if you are certain that at some later time your credence in a proposition will be x, your present credence in that proposition should already be x. The second is the diachronic Dutch book standardly offered in its support: an agent who now accepts a wager on the proposition and who, foreseeably, will later accept a wager against it can be led into a pair of bets whose payoffs jointly guarantee her a loss.

My difficulty is that the two appear to come apart, and I cannot say where.

(a) Identify the premise in the Dutch book argument that does the actual work — the one that licenses the step from "my future self will accept that bet" to "I should refuse it now." My suspicion is that it is not a premise about credences at all, but about the future self's utility function, or about what her evidential situation will be, and that it carries considerably more weight than is usually conceded. If that suspicion is correct, state the assumption explicitly and explain why conditionalization can be secured by a weaker form of it while Reflection apparently cannot. Relatedly: when the stock counterexamples are raised — the agent who knows she will be intoxicated tomorrow, the one who knows she is about to be shown evidence she can predict will mislead her — the customary reply restricts the principle to agents who retain self-trust. Given your account of the premise, is that restriction principled, or does it merely restate the problem?

(b) Here is what generated the question. Reflection is nearly always illustrated with cases in which the future credence is produced by evidence the present self lacks: tomorrow I will have read the paper, run the assay, seen the photograph. But suppose my future credence in a mathematical conjecture is produced instead by nothing but thinking about it harder — no new data, simply a further year's work on the same problem. I am confident that in a year I will assign it 0.95; today I assign it 0.5. Applied literally, Reflection tells me to move to 0.95 now, which makes the year's work superfluous and seems to imply that a rational agent can never expect her own reasoning to improve her opinions. Yet the two cases look structurally alike: in each, the future self is responding rationally to something the present self does not have. Explain what, if anything, separates them. Is there a principled distinction between anticipated evidential change and anticipated cognitive change, or is the second case incoherent in some subtler way — for instance, because one cannot genuinely be certain in advance of a credence that will issue from reasoning one has not yet performed?

(c) There is a familiar accuracy-based route to conditionalization, on which any non-conditionalizing rule for revising credences is dominated, at every possible world, by some alternative rule available to the agent. I do not know what the corresponding argument for Reflection would look like, since my future credences have no greater claim to approximate the truth than my present ones. Set out what such an argument would need to establish and say why it is unlikely to succeed; or, if you think nothing is lost here, explain why not, conditional on the Dutch book route in (a) being repairable.

Please work at the level of the structure of these arguments. I am not looking for attributions, bibliographies, or a list of who defends which position.
