I'm a staff engineer at a mid-size B2B software company. Somewhere in the last two years I quietly became the person who settles design arguments that two engineers can't resolve between themselves — roughly twice a month, usually a disagreement between me and one colleague of comparable seniority, occasionally between two other people with me as tiebreaker. I have an engineering degree and no philosophy training, so I'd like you to build the procedure out of the epistemology of disagreement rather than assume I already know it. My understanding of that debate is thin: I know there's a long-running argument between people who think that when someone you regard as equally informed and equally careful reaches the opposite conclusion you should move most of the way toward their view, and people who think you're entitled to stay put if you've re-checked your reasoning and found no flaw in it. I don't know which side is right and I don't want an essay adjudicating it. I want you to identify which parts of that dispute are load-bearing for an actual decision procedure, explain those parts in plain language, and then give me the procedure.

**The setting.** Stakes range from trivial to a decision that will cost us two quarters of rework if it's wrong. Anything above roughly one engineer-month of downstream rework needs my engineering manager's sign-off, so the procedure has to emit a written rationale that a busy non-expert can follow and that the colleague who lost can read without feeling steamrolled — they have to be able to audit the reasoning and see that the same rule would have gone against me in other circumstances. Small cases need to be resolvable in under an hour, because the argument is blocking work; the large ones can take a week. The person running it, and the person on the other side of it, are both engineers who will not read anything with technical philosophy vocabulary in it.

**Design requirements.** (i) It must terminate in an action, not a confidence level: we ship or we don't, we split the service or we don't, and "split the difference between our estimates" is usually not a buildable option. I need the procedure to say how something credence-shaped gets converted into a binary choice when the choice itself can't be fractional. (ii) It must be genuinely symmetric — it cannot be a rule that in practice only ever tells me to cave, and it cannot be one that only ever tells me to hold my ground. Where asymmetry legitimately enters, if it does, say so explicitly and say why it isn't special pleading. (iii) It must not let "we should just test it" become a way of dodging disagreements that are really about which costs and which risks we're willing to accept, where no experiment will settle anything. (iv) It must flag every step that is actually my judgment wearing a procedural costume.

**The thing I most want audited.** For about eighteen months I've kept a log of resolved disputes: what the question was, what each person said, and what turned out. It says I've been vindicated more often than not, and I've started using it to discount a colleague's dissent on calls similar to ones I've won before. From where I sit this is the most objective data in the room. I suspect you're going to tell me it's inadmissible, and I suspect the reason has something to do with the log not being independent of me, but I can't articulate how, because the entries look like plain facts. Two wrinkles I should mention up front: a lot of disputes never get a recorded outcome at all, and the ones that do were mostly resolved by tests or postmortems whose success criteria I had a hand in choosing. Tell me exactly what, if anything, the log can be used for.

**Run the procedure on these three.**

*A.* A colleague is confident that moving our nightly batch job from one worker to twenty parallel workers will exhaust the database connection pool and cause timeouts at peak; I think the pooling layer absorbs it comfortably. A load test in staging would settle it in half a day. My instinct is to stop treating this as a disagreement and go run the test — except that we also disagree about the test: they want it against production's current connection settings, I want it against the settings we'll have after next quarter's infrastructure migration.

*B.* Whether to split the billing domain out of our main service now or keep one service with a shared schema. My colleague has been through three migrations like this at previous employers and is certain that deferring the split costs an order of magnitude more later; I think splitting now buys coordination overhead we don't need and that they're generalizing from a different company's architecture. Nothing here can be tested inside our time horizon, we have to pick one and live with it for years, and this is exactly the kind of call where I've been reaching for the log.

*C.* We're scheduled to delete a deprecated sync path. The colleague who maintained it for four years says removing it will break something in our European customer deployments, but cannot say what: only that the code has surprised them repeatedly and they don't trust our dependency map. I've read the same code and see no such dependency. On every other question we are evenly matched; on this one they have four years of accumulated familiarity that cannot be handed to me as a document. I find it very hard to defer to a claim with no stated mechanism, and my manager will ask why we spent three weeks on a migration nobody can justify.

**What I want back.** The procedure as an ordered list of questions with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the explicit rule for converting the resulting state into an action under a hard deadline; the verdict on the log; the three cases worked through it, with at least one of them ending in an answer I won't like — don't soften that one; the steps where judgment is doing the work behind a procedural label; and finally a short, honest statement of what this procedure cannot settle at all, so I know when to stop running it and just make a call. No reading list, no citations, and no advice of the "engage your stakeholders" or "it depends on your context" variety unless you also give me the rule for the dependence.
