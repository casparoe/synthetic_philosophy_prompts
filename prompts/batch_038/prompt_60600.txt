I am a high school senior working on an independent study for my school's philosophy seminar, and my project concerns how social conventions arise and what, if anything, binds us to them. My reading has centered on David Lewis's analysis of convention, together with the more recent program that tries to explain the same phenomena by means of evolutionary game theory: populations of simple agents who imitate whatever appears to be working, without representing one another's beliefs at all. I have written down the position I intend to defend at my oral examination, and I would like it tested before I commit to it in public. I am not looking for a general survey of the literature, and I do not need the background explained to me. What I want is an audit of my own commitments.

Here is the position, as precisely as I can state it.

1. A convention is a regularity in the behavior of members of a population in some recurrent situation, such that each of them prefers to conform on condition that the others conform, and each expects the others to conform. The reason to conform is generated by that expectation; it is not a standing requirement that survives the collapse of the expectation.

2. Common knowledge is essential, and I mean this strictly. It is not enough that everyone in fact conforms and that everyone believes everyone conforms; it must be common knowledge that they do, since the expectation that makes conformity rational has to be mutually available at every level. A population in which the relevant beliefs are widely held but fall short of common knowledge does not, on my view, have a convention.

3. Conventions are arbitrary in the sense that matters: some alternative regularity would have served the population just as well, and there is no fact about the intrinsic merits of driving on the right rather than on the left. Nothing in my account requires that anyone chose the convention, agreed to it, or negotiated it, and I do not regard the absence of any such decision as a gap to be filled.

4. The question of which of the equally eligible candidates actually gets established is, I think, an evolutionary one. In a large population, agents copy whoever appears to be doing well, and a regularity that gains an early foothold spreads by differential imitation. The agents in this story need not reason about one another's beliefs, and need not have beliefs about beliefs. What explains the outcome is the dynamics of the population, not anyone's inference, and I want the same sort of explanation to cover both the emergence of a regularity and its persistence.

5. I am willing to say that nonhuman animals have conventions in this same sense: stable greeting displays, spacing patterns, and the like, which keep fights from starting. That the animals plainly cannot represent what others represent about them does not strike me as a reason to withhold the word.

6. In addition to the expectation-dependent reason in (1), there is a moral reason to conform to an established convention: one who deviates enjoys the benefits of a shared practice while declining to bear one's share of the cost of maintaining it, and that is free-riding. I take this moral reason to apply to conventions as such, not only to those backed by law or by explicit agreement.

7. Promising is a further instance of the same thing. To promise is to invoke a practice, and the reason to keep the promise is the reason to conform to the practice; I do not think any non-conventional obligation stands behind fidelity.

I suspect that some of these sit together more comfortably than they appear to, and that others are simply incompatible. So I would like the following. First, identify which pairs of commitments are genuinely in conflict and which only look that way, and explain what makes the difference; please do not treat every strain in the list as real, and please do not manufacture one where there is none. Second, rank the genuine conflicts by severity, from the one that would take down the position as a whole to the ones I could afford to live with. Third, for each genuine conflict, say what the least costly revision would be, what I would lose by making it, and whether the revision propagates, that is, whether abandoning one commitment forces adjustments elsewhere in the list. Fourth, tell me whether a single revision would repair most of the damage at once. I have no particular attachment to any of the seven items and I am not asking you to preserve them all, so please do not assure me that they hang together merely because each is individually respectable.
