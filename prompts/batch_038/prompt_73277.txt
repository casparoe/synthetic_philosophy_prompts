I'm arguing with a colleague about Hobsbawm and I want to steal the best version of a position I think is right but havent actually argued: that "invented tradition" isn't a real category.

Here's the rough shape of what I want you to defend, then the obstacles you have to clear.

The claim: the distinction between invented tradition and genuine tradition does no work, because Hobsbawm's own criterion — a practice that is recent but presents itself as immemorial — is satisfied by basically every tradition there is, and where it isn't satisfied the case isn't interesting. So the concept can't debunk anything. It looks critical and is actually vacuous.

To make that go through I think you need my typology (or a better one, but if you keep it, defend it):
(i) ex nihilo — a brand new practice plus a new claim of ancient pedigree;
(ii) re-dating — an old practice that gets a new claimed pedigree or a new meaning bolted onto it;
(iii) re-assembly — a new practice built out of genuinely old parts.
Then the thesis proper: every famous case in the Hobsbawm and Ranger volume is (ii) or (iii). The Highland kilt business, the British monarchy's ceremonial being gussied up over the late nineteenth and early twentieth century, the Third Republic picking 14 July, the public school rituals, the colonial administrations in Africa codifying "customary" law. And (ii) and (iii) are just what tradition is — there's no such thing as a tradition that wasn't re-dated or re-assembled at some point, because transmission across centuries *is* re-dating and re-assembling. Which means the contrast class Hobsbawm needs, traditions simply passed down unchanged that make no claim to antiquity, is empty.

Now the obstacles, and this is most of what I want:

1. The best objection is that the invention isn't in the practice but in the representation. Nobody seriously believes the kilt is ancient; the ideological work is done by the claim of continuity, which is a political act, and that's the thing worth debunking. Answer it. I suspect the answer has to be that a community's claim of continuity about itself isn't a factual assertion with a truth value in the right way — it's closer to a vow, or to adopting a rule, or to promising — but you have to make that stick without sliding into "well, it's all just symbolic, whatever," which would concede the whole point.

2. The periodization objection: Hobsbawm's real claim is a historical one about a particular window, that there was a sudden burst of ritual production in the late nineteenth century because the older social cement was dissolving, and I'm reading it as a conceptual claim it never made. Say whether that rescues the concept or just relocates it, and which version is more interesting to be wrong about.

3. Anderson already made the analogous move for the word "imagined": he says the question isn't whether communities are false or genuine, since anything larger than a face-to-face village is imagined, but the manner in which the imagining is done. Does that rescue Hobsbawm by showing him the fix, or convict him of not making the move he needed? Take a side.

4. If I'm right, is anything left? Seperate out what survives: give me the narrowed concept and say what would have to be true of a case for it to be a genuine invention, i.e. a member of a non-empty contrast class, and tell me whether any real candidate meets it. The Sobieski Stuart brothers' codification of clan tartans? Ossian? Some piece of colonial customary law written down for the first time by a district officer with no prior practice behind it? And if the honest answer is "a handful, and they're all textual forgeries rather than practices," say that flatly — I think that's the most damaging result for the concept and I want it stated up front rather than buried in a concluding paragraph.

Argue it. Don't survey the literature, no bibliography and no reading list, and don't land on "the truth is somewhere in the middle." I want a case that would make a Hobsbawm defender sweat a little.
