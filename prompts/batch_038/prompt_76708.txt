I've been doing data cleanup and report-writing for about six months now for a city program that's basically a small-scale version of universal basic income, and the longer I sit in the meetings the more something bugs me that I can't quite name. The setup: a mid-size California city — think Stockton, which is the one everyone copies — pulled 125 people at random out of neighborhoods at or below the city median income and gave them $500 a month for 24 months, launched in 2019, no work requirement, no reporting, no conditions of any kind. There are dozens of these now, plus a mayors' coalition pushing for a federal version. Everyone involved is extremely emphatic that the cash is unconditional; that's supposed to be the whole moral point, the thing that separates it from welfare as we know it, the thing that gives recipients dignity and agency rather than making them prove they deserve help.

But then look at how the results get talked about. In basically every press release, panel, and slide deck I've helped with, the first number anybody quotes is the employment one — did people stop working? — and close behind it is how little of the money went to alcohol and tobacco. A program designed to be unconditional ends up justified to the public almost entirely by showing that the recipients kept being productive and didn't blow it on temptation goods. That's the thing I can't square. If the money is supposed to be thier as a matter of right, why does its legitimacy hang on evidence that they still show up for work? It feels like the practice is smuggling in the assumption that a person is only entitled to an income they earn or are willing to earn — precisely the assumption unconditionality was supposed to reject. And there's the classic worry about the healthy 25-year-old who lives off the grant and surfs all day: does the design rule that person out, or does it just not care about him, and which answer is the honest one?

So what I want is an account of the theory of entitlement — or desert, or the work/income relationship, whatever the right label is — that this practice actually presupposes. Reconstruct it as carefully as you can, and then separate what it strictly needs from what it merely happens to sit comfortably with, because I can already hear someone say the labor-supply evidence isn't a moral premise at all, it's just politics, or just an empirical reply to a free-rider objection. Tell me which rival theories the practice rules out without arguing against them, and where it's underdetermined — i.e. where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same $500 debit card. Then help me with a contrast I keep coming back to: Alaska has paid every resident an annual dividend out of oil royalties since the early 80s, and as far as I can tell nobody defends that one by pointing out that Alaskans still go to work — it's framed as your share of something commonly owned, not as help for the needy. What does that difference in framing imply my city's program is committed to, and what would have to change — how people are recruited, how long it runs, who gets picked, what gets measured — if we rebuilt it on the ownership model instead? Not looking for a defense or an attack of UBI, just the philosophy the existing practice rests on and whether its parts rest on consistent philosophies.
