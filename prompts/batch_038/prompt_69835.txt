I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of machine learning, and I should like you to write an essay on a controversy that is, in my experience, resolved in either direction within the first paragraph and then never revisited.

The controversy concerns the standing of representational talk in interpretability research. When such research reports that a trained network represents something — that a direction in activation space corresponds to a particular natural language, that a component performs a comparison, that a sparse unit picks out a concept — is this claim literally true of the system's internal organization? Or is it an instrumentalist idiom borrowed from psychology, which we should be prepared to paraphrase away entirely in terms of predictive and interventional success?

I do not want the essay to be settled by appeal to how researchers speak. What I want pressed is the prior question: what would have to be the case for a physical state inside a network to have the content *P* at all? The most developed philosophical answers disagree, and — this is the point — they disagree in ways that interpretability methods can, in principle, adjudicate. Consider three. On an indicator or covariation account, content is fixed by what a state reliably tracks across some specified range of conditions. On a teleosemantic account, content is fixed by the function a state was selected to perform, which requires saying whether training on a corpus under a loss function constitutes a selecting environment in the relevant sense, and whether the function so fixed is determinate enough to yield a content rather than a gerrymandered disjunction. On a deflationary or interpretationist account, content is ascribed to a system as a whole for predictive purposes, and it is a category mistake to expect any individual internal state to have content except derivatively.

The essay should turn on the following two findings, which I have stylized. Assume both concern the same property *P* in the same network.

1. A linear probe recovers a direction *d* for *P* with high accuracy. *d* is stable across seeds, model scales, and independent retrainings. Yet causal interventions on *d* alter behavior only within a narrow band of inputs, and the model's competence on *P* can be fully restored by fine-tuning that routes around *d* altogether. So far as one can tell, *d* is a decodable but largely redundant correlate.

2. A low-dimensional subspace *S* resists decoding: no simple readout yields anything interpretable from it. But interventions on *S* produce systematic, targeted changes in *P*-related behavior, and these changes generalize compositionally to circumstances absent from the training distribution, in a manner that interventions on equally predictive but causally inert directions do not.

Say, for each of the three accounts, whether 1 and 2 are representations of *P*. Then confront the possibility that the resulting classification runs backwards relative to current practice — that the field canonizes 1 as a representation and is largely mute about 2, while the best available theory says that 2 is the representation and 1 is at most a shadow of it, or that neither qualifies. A further question the essay must face: if the realist and the instrumentalist agree on every probe accuracy, every patching result, and every steering outcome, is anything substantive left in dispute, or has the controversy collapsed into a choice of vocabulary? If you think something is left, identify what it is and what would settle it.

Finally, reach and defend a verdict on the original question: is representational talk about neural networks literally true, true only of a restricted class of findings that you should characterize, or a systematic fiction with predictive value? A conditional verdict is acceptable provided the conditions are stated precisely and you say which of them current methods actually meet. State the strongest objection to your verdict and answer it rather than noting it.

Two housekeeping points. I am not asking for a bibliography, and you may name the standard theories without citation — the argument must stand on its own reasoning rather than on authorities. Aim for roughly two to three thousand words, written in a register suitable for a philosophy seminar rather than a survey article.
