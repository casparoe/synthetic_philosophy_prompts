I'm assembling materials for an undergraduate reading group on coordination failure. We've spent two sessions on the material in Scott Alexander's essay Meditations on Moloch — the litany of prisoner's dilemmas, races to the bottom, and multipolar traps: arms races, the tragedy of the commons, the way a labor market can push wages down even though every employer individually deplores low wages, and the general puzzle of how outcomes nobody wants can be produced by agents each acting sensibly. The difficulty is that my students have started treating Moloch as a single diagnosis that explains everything, and I want a piece of writing that shows the category splitting into genuinely distinct problems with genuinely distinct treatments.

So please write a dialogue of roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words between two advisers who have been asked to brief a fictional body — call it the Coordination Directorate — that has the mandate and the budget to intervene in one multipolar trap, but must first decide how to characterize the trap it is looking at. The two advisers disagree about the diagnosis, and the disagreement is not merely verbal, though part of the dialogue's job is to establish how much of it is substantive.

The first adviser is a Hobbesian. On this view the trouble is mutual fear: each agent's best response depends on what it believes the others will do, those beliefs are mutually reinforcing and privately held, and so the group settles into a bad equilibrium that no member endorses on reflection. The remedy is accordingly a change in what each party can see and can count on — binding commitments, verification, third-party enforcement, a sovereign that makes defection expensive, or any other device that reshapes the payoff structure until cooperation is dominant rather than merely desirable. The second adviser thinks this mischaracterizes the trap. On their view the problem is not fear of others but the fact that each party's operative ends have been specified in a form hostile to its own principals' reflective values: the labs, hospitals, or firms are optimizing market share, throughput, or cost per unit, quantities their own backers would disown if asked. Adding transparency and enforcement to such a race does not dissolve it; it makes the race more efficient and more legible, and may even accelerate it, since each party can now see precisely how far ahead of the others it has to run. Their remedy is renegotiation of the ends rather than assurance about the means. Give each of them something real to say about why the other's instrument is not merely insufficient but potentially counterproductive.

Two constraints matter to me above the others. First, neither speaker may be a strawman: both should be competent, well-read, and willing to concede what is actually conceded. Second, the exchange needs real back-and-forth — each speaker should take up the specific argument just made rather than a generalized version of it, and at least twice a speaker should be pushed into modifying a position instead of restating it. It is entirely fine, and I think realistic, if they end by agreeing that they were partly talking past each other; it is not fine if they end by trading slogans. Please anchor the discussion in a single invented case that both speakers keep returning to — perhaps two or three rival AI labs choosing how fast to train their frontier systems, where each lab's own researchers privately prefer a slower schedule, or a set of city hospitals competing for the same pool of donor funding — and let one speaker identify a place in that case where the other's remedy would make things worse, and let that objection land. I would rather the dialogue stay inside the invented scenario and the conceptual vocabulary sketched above than wander into historical examples or the surrounding literature; my students will read it cold and I want it to stand entirely on its own.
