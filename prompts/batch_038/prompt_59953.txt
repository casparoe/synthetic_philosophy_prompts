I need help reconstructing someone else's position, and I would rather have it done carefully than quickly. Last night I argued for four hours with a friend — I will call her H — about how many streams of consciousness a single human body can contain, and we reached no resolution, chiefly because I could not pin down the rule she is actually applying. She works on the unity of consciousness and has a long set of settled verdicts, which I wrote down as faithfully as I could while we were still talking. What follows is that record, together with two glosses she offered on her own view and one case that arose only at the very end, which she declined to rule on before going to bed. I want your reconstruction of her rule and your prediction about that case.

Her verdicts, in roughly the order she gave them. A normal adult: one stream — though she is emphatic that this is not because the corpus callosum is intact, which she dismisses as a means and not a criterion. A complete commissurotomy patient under lateralized testing, so that the right hemisphere points confidently at an object the left hemisphere sincerely denies having seen: two streams, and she is willing to say two subjects, while objecting strenuously to "two people." That same patient at dinner, with ordinary bilateral input and fluent unforced conversation: one stream. She is untroubled that the number changes with the testing conditions and says a stream count was never meant to be a permanent property of a body; when I pressed her on the obvious objection — that two halves receiving duplicate input are still two fields, however similar — she said I was confusing similarity with sharing, and that what makes two is the halves being out of step. Callosal agenesis, where no corpus callosum ever developed and transfer is carried by the anterior commissure and subcortical routes with unremarkable cognition: one stream, because the function is there and the anatomy is not the point. A partial callosotomy sparing the splenium — a case she described from the literature and then sharpened for argument, so that what one hemisphere sees is available to the other but what one hand feels is not: one stream, "frayed at the edges"; she will not say two, will not say one and a half either, but does allow that unity can come apart by modality. Hemispherectomy in infancy, with the remaining hemisphere reorganized around the work of both: one stream, and quantity of tissue is irrelevant to her. A single brain, commissures intact, presenting with a dissociative identity pattern — distinct self-reports, distinct autobiographies, mutual amnesia: one stream, pathologically gated but numerically single, and she is irritated by the suggestion that reports of separate selves should count for anything. Finally, a case the two of us invented: a subject whose commissure is silenced by an implant for twenty minutes and then restored, with continuous memory and continuous behavior across the interval. She says one stream throughout, and her explanation was that the connection is "still wired for it" — she appears to think unity can be a standing property of a system rather than something that has to be occurring.

Her own gloss, repeated twice: what matters is whether there is a single point of view from which the whole is given. She also insists that she is counting streams and not persons, and that the count owes nothing to anyone's intuitions about responsibility or desert.

The case she would not answer. Suppose the implant is not a switch but a translator. The commissure is severed and replaced by a device that several times a second passes to each hemisphere a compressed summary of the other's current perceptual and cognitive state — accurate, and fully usable by the receiving hemisphere in reasoning and in speech, so that the left hemisphere can report without guessing that there is a red square in the right half of the world, and can plan around it. But no sensory character crosses. The receiving hemisphere acquires something closer to a belief about the other's experience than a share in it, and there is never a moment at which the fine-grained qualitative content of both sides is present to one subject. The halves are never out of step, in her sense, because the summaries keep them in step; and yet nothing it is like to see the square is ever given from a single point of view. One stream, or two?

Here is what I want. State the rule that would generate every verdict in her record, and then state the rival rules that fit that record about as well — my suspicion is that at least a rule about occurrent sharing of content, a rule about a single phenomenal field, a rule about standing dispositions to share, a rule about divergence between the halves, and some rule about the organism as a whole are all live candidates, and that they cannot all be right. Say which of them survive contact with the whole list rather than only with the convenient parts of it. Then give the prediction each surviving rule makes about the translator case, and your single best guess at her answer, with a rough degree of confidence in that guess and the reason you prefer it to its rivals. Tell me which recorded verdict is doing the most constraining work and which is nearly uninformative, so that I know where to press her next. If her record is not in fact consistent, say so plainly and say which verdict has to be surrendered to save the rest. Finally, describe one further case — something I could put to her tomorrow morning — that would discriminate between the surviving rules, and say what each rule predicts about it.

I am not asking for names, references, or a survey of what anyone has published on this; I have no way of checking any of that, and I care only about the architecture of her view. Please write your answer as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis and no tables — the way one writes a considered letter, with the argument carried by the sentences themselves rather than by the layout.
