I run a four-person democratic innovation team inside a regional government. Councils, combined authorities and the occasional health board ask us to design citizens' panels, and so far everything we've built has been advisory: a randomly selected group hears evidence, deliberates, publishes recommendations, and the elected body then decides whether to notice them. In the last six months three clients have asked for something different — panels that actually decide. Before we commit to designing anything I want a procedure we can run on each request, because at the moment we improvise and I'm fairly sure we're being inconsistent about it.

The thing I want the procedure to get its teeth into is why we're using a lottery at all, and it's the reason I'm asking you rather than googling. We've started using some house shorthand, "mirror or ladder". If the reason is the mirror — the panel is meant to be a scaled-down copy of the place, so its verdict carries the weight of "this is what people like you would conclude if they were properly informed" — then everything rests on the seated group actually resembling the population, and our recruitment numbers are a disaster for that. We write to or knock on roughly 4,000 randomly picked addresses and end up with 40 to 60 people who say yes, so a 1–2 per cent response rate. Quotas on age, sex, ethnicity, tenure and rough geography patch up the variables we can see, and we pay people, but nobody has a fix for the invisible ones: interest in politics, confidence about speaking in a room, having a free Saturday, not caring what the neighbours think. If the reason is the ladder — the point being that nobody had to win a contest to get in there, so the ambitious, the well-connected and the comfortably-off lose the head start an election hands them — then who turns up matters far less and the self-selection problem mostly dissolves. Two reasons, two different designs, two different legitimacy stories, and I dont think we've ever been clear which one we were selling. Worth saying that the Athenians may have had both at once: people put themselves forward into the pool that was then allotted, terms were a year, you couldn't hold the same office twice, there was a check before you took it up and an account rendered afterwards, and the posts that needed real skill — the military ones — were elected rather than allotted. So possibly the honest position is that sortition is never doing just one thing, and the procedure should force us to say which one is load-bearing in this case.

Some constraints: nobody on the team is a statistician; we typically have about six weeks between the request and the committee paper; the director who signs off is not a democratic theorist and will read one page, not twelve. No bibliography or citations, please — I want something I can hand a colleague on a Tuesday morning.

Please structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** (about 80 words) — the one or two things you'd tell me to stop doing straight away.
2. **Mirror or ladder, made operational** (about 200 words) — a test we can run in ten minutes at the start of a request to establish which value the client is actually invoking, including the tells, i.e. what clients say when they mean one and not the other, and what to do when they say both or refuse to say.
3. **The procedure** (the bulk, roughly 900–1,200 words) — the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it hands the decision back to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. Build in at least one question designed to catch a sponsor who wants a panel in order to avoid being blamed for the outcome rather than to reach one. That's my real worry about case (c) below and it's the hardest thing to put to a client politely, so tell me how you'd phrase it.
4. **Where it will misfire** (about 250 words) — which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume, and what the procedure does when the honest answer is "we don't know".
5. **Three cases** (about 250 words each) — run the procedure on:
   (a) a metropolitan borough wanting a 50-resident panel with binding power over how £2.4m of capital money is split between some thirty local projects, because their vote-based version keeps getting captured by whichever group can turn out the most people on the night;
   (b) a combined authority wanting to fill its scrutiny chair — the role that runs the committees asking awkward questions of the executive — with a randomly selected resident on a one-year rotating term instead of a councillor;
   (c) a district council deadlocked 24–24 for two years on whether to allocate a site for 300 homes, whose leader's office proposes putting it to a citizens' panel with both sides agreeing in advance to implement whatever it decides. The leader chairs the board of our largest funder. Say the no plainly if that's where it lands, and don't soften it into a maybe.
   For each, say which way it comes out and what would have to change to change the answer.
6. **What this procedure can't do** (about 150 words).
