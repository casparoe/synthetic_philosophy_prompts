I am in the second term of an upper-level course on determinism, chance, and prediction, and this week we are reading the chapter on chance in Poincaré's *Science and Method* (the English translation of *Science et Méthode*). My seminar paper is on whether his treatment of unpredictable phenomena commits him to anything more than an epistemic account of probability, and I am having trouble getting the argument straight. I cannot reproduce the translation verbatim here, so what follows is my own close rendering of the core of the passage, in the order in which he gives it. Please work with this rendering as it stands.

1. If we knew the laws of nature exactly, and knew the state of the universe at the initial instant exactly, we could predict the state of the universe at any later instant exactly.
2. Even if the natural laws no longer had any secret for us, we could know the initial state only approximately. That would suffice, and we should say the phenomenon has been predicted and is governed by laws, provided that approximate knowledge of the initial state lets us predict the later state with the same degree of approximation.
3. But this is not always so. It may happen that small differences in the initial conditions produce very great differences in the final phenomena: a small error in the former yields an enormous error in the latter. Prediction then becomes impossible, and we have the fortuitous phenomenon.
4. His illustration is meteorological. A cyclone seems to spring up by chance, yet had we been able to observe the atmosphere closely enough a day earlier we would have seen it forming. What makes us call it fortuitous is that some slight disturbance elsewhere, recorded by no one, settled the question of why it arose here rather than there.
5. Later in the chapter he presses a further difficulty: if chance merely names our ignorance, it is hard to see how a calculus built upon it could deliver results that experience confirms — mortality tables, the distribution of molecular velocities, the behaviour of games of chance. He responds by separating two sources of stability: cases where the number of causes is very great and each is individually negligible, and cases where the distribution of outcomes settles into a fixed form whatever the initial distribution, so long as that initial distribution is a continuous function.

With that in front of you, four things I would like help with.

(a) In items 2 and 3, is the failure of prediction a failure of knowledge or a failure of the world? Does this chapter commit him to objective chance, or only to unpredictability by creatures like us under strictly deterministic laws? What in the rendering above, if anything, decides the question?

(b) My tutor suggested that the second source of stability in item 5 is an early version of what later writers call the method of arbitrary functions: repeated application of a mixing or many-to-one transformation drives any continuous initial density toward a fixed output distribution, so that ignorance of the initial density becomes harmless. I am not sure the label fits, and I am less sure how it relates to the first source, which looks more like a law-of-large-numbers move. Are these rival answers to one question, or two distinct arguments doing two distinct pieces of work?

(c) It is a cliché in this literature to say that he anticipated chaos theory, and I want neither to repeat the cliché nor simply deny it. Modern treatments insist that where individual trajectories are unpredictable the statistical behaviour need not be: invariant measures exist, Lyapunov exponents price the loss of information at a fixed rate, and shadowing results guarantee that a computed trajectory stays near some true one. Does that vindicate item 3 or quietly revise it — is his claim about the impossibility of prediction a claim about single trajectories only, and does the statistical rescue leave any of his argument standing?

(d) Please be explicit about where the line falls between what is settled among people who work on this material and what remains genuinely contested. My impression is that the epistemic-versus-objective reading of him on chance is disputed rather than resolved, and I do not want my paper to assume one side of a live disagreement. If it is live, say what the disagreement turns on and lay out the main options, rather than presenting a preferred reading as the consensus.

One last request: since the passage above is my paraphrase rather than his prose, please flag any place where my wording seems to have flattened a distinction the argument needs — for instance, if I have collapsed two different senses of the fortuitous into one. Do this whether or not you feel confident about the exact original phrasing, and say plainly when you are reasoning from my rendering alone.
