Write me a short story, maybe 1800–2500 words, that actually dramatizes the idea that a self can be spread across other people — the Hofstadter-ish thought that when someone dies, chunks of their "I" go on running inside the heads of the people who knew them, imperfectly and out of sync with each other. Don't name-drop him or any book or essay, I just want the idea doing real structural work rather than being a theme you announce.

Here's the setup I want you to build on. Flesh it out however you like, but keep these features: in some near-future jurisdiction there's a civil instrument called a residuary apportionment. When a person dies leaving certain things unsettled, the people who register as her residuaries each take a weighted share of her continuing agency for a set period — call it a year. During that year, the unresolved decisions (the house, the unpublished manuscript, the dog, whether the organ donation registry entry gets honored late) go to a vote among the residuaries, and the majority outcome is entered in law not as the survivors' decision but as an act of the deceased herself. The registry office has a stock formulation for this, something to the effect that the tally doesn't estimate what she would have decided — it *is* her deciding, and saying otherwise would make the whole instrument fraudulent. Have one character who believes that formulation completely and sincerely, and don't let the story sneer at her.

Your protagonist is the estranged younger brother, who holds the single largest share because his is the only claim nobody contested. A proposal comes up that three of the five residuaries support and that he's certain she would have hated — and I want the story to sit in the genuinely hard question of whether he can be *right* about that, or whether there's any fact left to be right or wrong about once she's gone and the shares are distributed. Does his larger share mean more of her, or just more of him?

A few constraints. I want at least one scene that's purely procedural — the forms, the quorum rules, the counting out loud, the person who brought the wrong pen — because the banality is where the dread should be. Second, no twist where it turns out she left a letter, or a recording, or a neural backup, or anything that settles it; the whole point is that there is no such thing and everyone has to keep going anyway. Third, keep the ending small and unresolved, and don't resolve it in the last line with some neat piece of symbolism. Fourth, no epigraphs and no quoted philosophers anywhere in the piece.

Oh, and one more: I'd like the apportionment to have some dumb little administrative detail that ends up mattering alot more than anyone expected — a rounding rule, a quorum threshold, a clause about what happens if a residuary dies mid-term. Let that detail, not a speech, carry the last third.
