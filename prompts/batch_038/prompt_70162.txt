Below are my verdicts on a series of cases about what a choice reveals about utility. I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. Taking $20 today over $30 in a month, and then paying, a month later, to have my earlier self locked into the $30: the distal, calm choice expresses my real preference; the impulsive one does not.
2. Being auto-enrolled in a retirement plan and staying in it for twenty years: this expresses no preference at all, not even a faint one.
3. Preferring A to B from a two-item menu, and then preferring B to A once a third, plainly inferior option has been added: neither choice reports anything.
4. Paying six dollars more for the branded drug than for the chemically identical generic, because I believe the branded one works better: a genuine preference, and satisfying it leaves me better off even though the belief behind it is false.
5. Working eighty hours a week at a job I tell everyone I hate and insist I chose freely: the choice does not show that I prefer it.
6. A woman raised where there were no schools who no longer wants one and reports herself content: her choices are not evidence about what is good for her, though I do not think that licenses me to say what would be.
7. Paying $5 to avoid a fifty-fifty bet of losing $6, while calling it a genuine preference to pay $5,000 to avoid a fifty-fifty bet of losing $6,000: the first is a bias, the second is not.
8. Being led around a cycle — A over B, B over C, C over A — paying a premium at each step, until I am back where I began with less money: each choice was free and each revealed a preference, and I have still been made worse off by all of them together.

My working rule is that a choice reveals a preference only if I would endorse it on reflection, with the relevant facts vivid before me. That rule handles 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6; it wrecks 4, since on reflection I would not endorse the drug purchase; and in 7 it condemns both bets alike, because reflection has nothing different to say about the scaled-up version.

So: state the principle, or the two or three candidate principles, that fits this pattern best — precisely enough that I can see which case motivates each of its clauses. Then give its verdicts on cases I have not listed: an advance directive refusing treatment, signed while competent, that a later self with dementia begs to have disregarded; a choice between two options I have been told are identical and cannot tell apart; a rat pressing a lever. Finally, say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot preserve and which single one I should give up, and whether what survives is anything a welfare economist could measure.
