i'm the only analyst at a small family foundation — about $25m a year, and our whole portfolio is "emerging risks," which is a polite way of saying we fund stuff where the data doesnt exist yet: novel pathogens in specific regions, water security, governance of new tech, that kind of thing. Four times a year i put about ten proposals in front of a board of four people, none of whom have any stats background. One of them is an ex-trader who is convinced everything can be written as a distribution if you just try hard enough, and another is a retired pediatrician who is convinced the opposite. Right now i run everything through a scoring rubric that makes me type a single probability and a single impact number into a spreadsheet and multiplies them, and i've become pretty sure that's fake precision wearing a lab coat — the number looks like knowledge but its actually just me picking something out of the air and rounding it. Last quarter i killed a proposal basically because "there's no evidence base," and i've been chewing on it ever since, because i think i might have just done the urn thing. You know the setup: 90 balls, 30 red, and 60 that are either black or yellow in some proportion nobody will tell you, and everyone bets on red over black and then on black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow, which is not allowed if you're being a good Bayesian. That's roughly my entire job, except the urns are grant applications.

What i need is a decision procedure i can actually run on a proposal in about 45 minutes, maybe an hour on a big one, and that survives being handed to a board member who has never heard the word "ambiguity" used as a term of art. So: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and how i'm supposed to sort a proposal into "we have frequencies for this," "we don't have frequencies but we could bound things," and "we have no idea and no amount of thinking this afternoon will change that." I want it to be explicit about what to do when i genuinely can't produce a number — sets of priors, worst-case reasoning, whatever the right move actually is — rather than letting me smuggle a single point estimate back in through the side door. And it needs a step where i check whether my reluctance about the unknown-urn option is real caution or just the fact that i spent six years working in the field the well-evidenced option lives in, since i know ambiguity aversion mostly evaporates when people feel like the expert in the domain. The mirror-image failure needs handling too: the proposals that are gambles on the unknown urn dressed up as bold vision, where the ambiguity is doing all the work of hiding a bad bet. Mark clearly where the procedure stops and hands the thing to the board because what's left is a values question and not a facts question.

For the deliverable, assume the output is a one-page brief per proposal: a recommendation, the assumptions that carry it, and a line saying what evidence would change the answer. Then run it on three cases for me. One: a program with fourteen randomized trials behind it, modest but real effect, boring, scalable, and it costs almost exactly what our average grant costs. Two: a genuinely novel intervention with a plausible mechanism, one small pilot, no comparison group, and an upside that is maybe ten times anything else in the pile. Three: a proposal where the ambiguity is entirely self-inflicted — the applicant could have generated the missing evidence cheaply and chose not to — and i want to know whether that changes how the procedure treats it. At least one of those three should come out with an answer i personally will not like, and you should say which one and why. Finish with a short section on what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire, and be honest about which of the steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. I don't need references or a reading list, i'm not going to chase any down — i need the procedure itself.
