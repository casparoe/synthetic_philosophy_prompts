Our department's speaker series has booked a philosopher who defends Benatar's asymmetry while disowning its usual gloss. On her account, the presence of pain counts against a state of affairs whether or not anyone is there to bear it, but the absence of pleasure counts for nothing unless some actual subject is thereby deprived — and she insists that never existing cannot be better for the person who never exists, since there is no such person for whom it could be better. She describes the asymmetry as being about what is eligible to enter deliberation at all, not about what is good or bad for whom.

Write six interview questions that make this position do work rather than advertise itself. At least one question must target each of the following: (a) what, on her view, the slogan that it is better never to have been can even mean once the person-affecting reading is disowned; (b) whether she has actually answered the charge that the asymmetry covertly shifts between an impersonal and a person-affecting standpoint, or has only relocated the shift inside her notion of eligibility; (c) whether the view yields a prohibition on all procreation or only on procreation above some expected-harm threshold, and what determines that threshold if there is one; and (d) a case built to break the view — for instance, whether it is wrong for the last generation to conceive one more contented child who will leave no descendants. For each question, supply the answer she is most likely to give and a single follow-up that refuses that answer rather than restating it. Exclude anything about her biography, her influences, or what first drew her to the topic.

Keep the entire response under 600 words, and close with one sentence naming which of the six questions is most likely to extract a concession from her.
