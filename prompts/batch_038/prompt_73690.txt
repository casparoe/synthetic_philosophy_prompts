I've been asked to give the opening address at my city's annual new-citizen welcome ceremony next month, in the council chamber. I write firmware for a living and have had no philosophy training, so I'd like to state the constraint I care about as precisely as I can and leave the rest to you.

The room will hold about three hundred people: the new citizens and their families, who between them cover most of the world's religions and a good many of none; the mayor and two council members; and the local secularists' association, which filed a formal complaint last year after the previous speaker led what was unmistakably a Christian prayer. My mother, who is devout and who was sworn in in that same room twenty years ago, will be sitting in the second row.

The constraint is this: every reason I offer for the claims I make should be one a person could accept without first having to accept anything about God, or scripture, or the ultimate point of a human life. Call it a no-undisclosed-dependencies rule. But I have two further requirements that pull against it, and I need the speech to satisfy all three at once. First, the organizers have cleared me to include one passage drawn explicitly from a religious tradition, provided I don't leave it standing on its own authority: the room must be given, in the same breath, a reason to accept the point that doesn't depend on the text's being sacred. Second, and this is the one I most want handled well, the address must not make my mother feel that her convictions are the sort of thing one strips out before speaking to one's neighbors. If the burden of putting things in neutral terms falls only on the religious people in the room and never on anyone else, then the rule I gave you two sentences ago isn't actually neutral, and I don't want to be enforcing it by stealth.

Please write the address itself: spoken language, first person, deliverable by an ordinary person without sounding like a seminar. I don't want a talk about these constraints; I want a speech that happens to satisfy them. Give it to me as exactly five numbered passages, of roughly equal length, that read in sequence as one continuous address, with nothing from you before or after them.
