For about fifteen years I have been a regular reader of rationalist blogs and their comment sections, and for the last several I have helped run a small monthly meetup where we work through a decision- or game-theory puzzle together. Last month I put the following game on the board, and two members — call them Owen, an economics doctoral student, and Nadia, who is self-taught but reads voraciously — ended up in a sustained argument that I did not feel equipped to referee. I took notes at the time and have tried to render both sides fairly; I am genuinely unsure whether either of them won.

The game. There are six decision points and a pot that starts at $2. At each decision point the player whose turn it is may Take or Pass. Taking ends the game immediately: the taker receives three-quarters of the current pot and the other player receives one-quarter. Passing multiplies the pot by two and a half and hands the turn to the other player. If the player at the sixth decision point passes, the game ends and the pot is divided evenly. So taking at the first node nets the first mover $1.50, whereas if both players pass all the way through, each walks away with roughly $98 — and the sixth player, by taking there instead, could have about $146. You may find the exact numbers worth working through; my impression is that the payoff gaps at the late nodes are proportionally much thinner than the ones at the start, and I do not think that is incidental to the dispute.

The exchange, as best I reconstructed it:

1. Owen: Backward induction settles this. The sixth player takes, since three-quarters of the pot beats half of it; therefore the fifth player takes, since three-quarters of her pot beats the quarter she would get by handing the turn over; and so on back to the first node, where the first mover takes and collects $1.50. That is the unique subgame-perfect outcome. The well-known laboratory finding that people pass early and almost never reach the end is not evidence against the analysis. It is evidence that undergraduates playing for pocket change are not playing the game you wrote on the board — they have smuggled in reputations, repetition, or a hunch about the experimenter.

2. Nadia: The argument is self-undermining. Its verdict at the first node is purchased entirely with claims about what happens at the fifth and sixth, but anyone who is actually standing at the fifth node is there only because the first four players passed — which flatly contradicts the common-knowledge-of-rationality premise the argument used to predict that they would take. So the argument cannot prescribe conduct at the fifth node, and with no determinate answer there, nothing propagates back to node one. The conclusion rests on evaluating a counterfactual whose antecedent is inconsistent with the premises invoked to evaluate it.

3. Owen: Two replies. First, subgame perfection is not an epistemic notion. It is a statement about strategy profiles: at every node, on the path and off it, no player can gain by deviating unilaterally. Nothing in that definition refers to what anyone believes or has learned, so an objection built from beliefs and evidence simply does not engage it. Second, your principle proves far too much. If "the situation you find yourself in falsifies the assumption, therefore the assumption yields no guidance" were accepted generally, we could never use any solution concept to assess play that departs from equilibrium — which is to say we would have to give up game theory as a critical tool altogether.

4. Nadia: On the first reply, you are defending a different claim than the one you opened with. Subgame perfection may well be belief-free, but "a rational player takes at node one" is not a claim about strategy profiles; it is a prescription addressed to an agent, and an agent can only act on forecasts. A Bayesian player at node five must condition on the observed history, and that history is powerful evidence that her opponent is not whatever the argument assumed. On the second reply, your reductio fails because it does not generalize. Where a player has a dominant strategy, evidence about her opponent cannot be relevant to her own choice, so no undermining arises. The centipede is precisely the case where every node's best reply depends on a forecast of later nodes. That asymmetry is the whole point, not an oversight.

5. Owen: Then notice what you have done. Types, priors, posteriors — that is a game of incomplete information. The game I described has complete information, and in that game your reasoning has no purchase. Posit a positive probability that your opponent is a "pass until node k" type and you have not refuted backward induction; you have quietly rewritten the problem, and of course a different problem gets a different answer. Moreover, the type-based story unravels on its own terms. Any genuinely rational type takes at the sixth node no matter how the play got there, and once the probability of that is high enough, the earlier passes stop paying for themselves.

6. Nadia: I concede the game changes and deny that this is a retreat, because the idealization I dropped is exactly the one that made the original counterfactuals unintelligible. A theory whose central predictions can only be evaluated at histories its own premises rule out is not buying rigor with that idealization; it is buying vacuity. And on the unraveling: the reputational logic does not need to sustain passing all the way to the end to defeat you. It only needs to show that taking at node one is not uniquely rational. On any modest prior over types, passing at node one has the higher expected value, and your prescription is gone. Finally, note that your own diagnosis of the laboratory data — that subjects imagine repetition and reputation — is itself a type-based explanation. You cannot deploy it against the experimenters and refuse to deploy it inside the theory.

7. Owen: That last sentence concedes the argument to me. If the correct description of the data is that subjects were playing something other than the game on the page, then backward induction was never overturned; it was never applied to those subjects, which is all I claimed in move one. As for incoherence, there is nothing incoherent in reasoning inside a counterfactual you regard as impossible — we do it constantly. At node six, Take beats Pass no matter how you arrived, because it is a one-person decision with a strictly better option. That is all the induction's final step requires, and every earlier step has the same shape. And your evidence does not run one way: if she is the sort who passed to node five, she may equally be the sort who takes at six. You have not shown the inference favors you rather than merely existing.

8. Nadia: "Evidence exists and cuts both ways" is a concession that the answer at each node is an empirical, prior-sensitive matter rather than a theorem, which is the entirety of my case. And it exposes the ambiguity that generated this paradox in the first place. "Rational players take at node one" oscillates between a claim about what an agent ought to do and a claim about what agents satisfying a definition will do. The first is normative and prior-dependent; the second is a tautology about the definition. You have been trading on the second to establish the first.

Here is what I would like from you. Adjudicate this exchange move by move rather than in aggregate: rate each of the eight moves on a five-point scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating in a sentence or two. Where a move contains a claim that is simply false about the game as I described it — a miscalculation, or a misstatement of what subgame perfection requires or does not require — say so explicitly and show the correction. Then tell me which specific contested propositions each of them is actually right about, since I suspect the scoreboard is mixed rather than belonging to either side; identify any point at which they are talking past one another rather than disagreeing; and say what neither of them raised that a third participant should have. Finally, tell me whether the verdict would change if the same game were played for serious money by two people who each knew the other's track record at this game — and if so, which of the eight moves would change status as a result.
