I work nights in a data center and there's not much to do between alerts, so I've been chewing on the same problem for about a year now. It started with an argument with a guy I work with, Dale. He said that if the future is genuinely endless — conscious life keeps going forever, no heat-death cutoff, no last person — then nothing anybody does now actually matters, because whatever finite amount of good or harm you cause gets swallowed by an infinite total either way. I told him that was obviously wrong and then couldn't say why, which annoyed me. I've never taken a philosophy class, so I've been working it out on the backs of shift logs, mostly by making up worlds and checking my gut.

Here's the setup I use. Chop time into moments. At each moment, add up how good things are going for everyone who exists then — happiness minus suffering, roughly — and call that the moment's number. A world is then just an endless list of numbers. My verdicts on pairs of worlds:

1. One happy person forever vs. two equally happy people forever: the two-person world is better. Easy one.
2. World B is exactly like world A except that for the first million years it also contains a trillion ecstatic people, then it settles back into being identical to A forever. B is better. Not a tie. The million years counts for something even though both worlds have endless totals.
3. A million years of bliss followed by endless torment, vs. the exact same world except the torment starts one day later. The second is better. Only a tiny bit, but better. I refuse to say they're equally bad just because both contain infinite suffering.
4. World C: one happy person on every odd moment, nobody on every even moment. World D: the reverse. These tie for me, neither is better. But notice C's running total is ahead of D's at every odd moment and never behind at any moment — so "whoever is ahead on the running total wins" can't be the rule, or C would beat D.
5. World E: three happy people for three moments, then nobody for three moments, repeating forever. World F: one happy person at every moment, forever. E is never behind F on the running total — it surges ahead, F reels it in, but F never actually passes — and E has more good in it spread over any long stretch. I think E beats F. Which feels inconsistent with 4, and I can't see why.
6. World G: a thousand happy people exist at every moment forever, but they're always different people — a fresh thousand each moment, each one getting a single good moment. World H: the same thousand people, happy at every moment forever, so each of them has an endless good life. The moment numbers are identical in both worlds. H is better. I feel this one really strongly and I have no idea how to fit it into the framework above, since by moment totals they're a dead tie.

My tentative rule, which I've been calling the scoreboard rule: line two worlds up moment by moment, keep a running total of the difference, and see where that total is heading as time goes on. If it heads toward a definite amount, up or down, that tells you which world is better and by how much. If it just bounces around without settling, fall back to comparing the long-run averages — total good divided by number of moments, as the stretch gets longer and longer. If even that doesn't settle, they aren't comparable and neither is better. That seems to get 1 through 5, althought I'm fuzzy on whether "heading toward a definite amount" is allowed to mean "keeps growing forever" (which 1 and 2 kind of need). And I'm pretty sure it flat-out fails on 6.

So: is there a principle that gets all six, or five of the six, or that tells me which one I should give up? I want it stated precisely enough that I could hand it to a stranger along with two new worlds and get the same answer back — not a vibe. If one of my verdicts has to go, tell me which and argue for it rather than just announcing it; 4 and 6 are the ones I'd fight hardest for. Then predict my verdict on a case I havent listed: a world that matches the steady one-person-forever world on average over any long stretch, but in stretches it runs ahead by more and more — first by a moment's worth, then ten, then a hundred — and each time it eventually gives all of it back. Better, worse, or not comparable? And does your principle ever produce a cycle, where A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A? Last thing, and this might be the part that sinks everything: does the answer change depending on how finely I slice time? If I count in centuries instead of moments, some of these worlds stop looking like the same worlds. If the verdict flips when I re-slice one and the same world, is that fatal or just bookkeeping?
