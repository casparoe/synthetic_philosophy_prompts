I teach a 75-minute session in an upper-division interdisciplinary course on the measurement of welfare: 24 students, about half philosophy majors and half public-policy majors, seated as six teams of four at movable tables, one whiteboard, no laptops, and no teaching assistant. This week's topic is a family of views I do not want named until the debrief. As I hold them in my head, the view asserts four things at once. First, harms differ in kind and not only in degree, so that above some threshold of intensity no quantity of sub-threshold harm, however enormous in number or duration, can be worse than even a small quantity of super-threshold harm. Second, below that threshold ordinary aggregation applies, so large numbers of mild harms can and must be traded off against one another. Third, suffering and happiness are not marks on a single scale, so a unit of pain cannot be offset against a unit of pleasure at some fixed exchange rate; what morality is fundamentally about is the relief of the bad, not the maximization of the net. Fourth, as a consequence: no number of people with mildly painful hangnails, however vast, adds up to something worse than one person tortured for two years — and a world containing no sentient beings at all is not worse than, and may be better than, a world full of comfortable, mildly contented lives, provided neither world contains severe suffering. A colleague in health economics insists this cannot be taught in 75 minutes without degenerating into a graduate seminar on cardinal utility; I would like to prove otherwise, though I have learned from last year's attempt. I ran it then as a described case plus open discussion, and within ten minutes it had collapsed into the two or three confident aggregators in the room steamrolling everyone else while the students who actually share the lexical intuition could not articulate what they were objecting to. So this year the structure of the exercise has to do the work: the students should walk into the contradiction themselves, on the record, before anyone says the word "lexical."

What I want is a game, not a demonstration. The teams should have to make choices that get written down and later come back to bite them. My rough sense of the machinery — improve on it freely — is that each team receives a fixed allocation budget and a set of harm-aversion programs varying along intensity, duration, and number of people affected, and must commit to an allocation; that they then answer a secret escalating trade-off ladder (would you accept N people each with a day of mild itch to spare one person two years of the worst harm on the scale, for N equal to the number of people in this room, in this city, in every generation of human history, among all mammals alive today, and so on) and mark the first N at which they refuse; that they separately walk a chain of adjacent comparisons down the intensity-and-duration spectrum, each step individually easy to make, so that the endpoint contradicts the starting point; that they must state, as a number on an anchored 1-to-100 intensity scale with fixed clinical anchors such as a bad toothache, a kidney stone, or unmedicated childbirth, exactly where the threshold sits and then explain why the line falls there rather than one point lower; and that the entire ladder is then repeated verbatim on the pleasure side, with ecstatic joy in place of agony and faint amusement in place of an itch, to see whether they trade off freely where the sign is positive and refuse where it is negative. If at all possible I also want a betting round with some small non-monetary stake — tokens convertible into a minor course perk — arranged so that a team holding the view can be walked through a sequence of individually rational swaps that leaves it strictly worse off by its own lights, because I want them to feel the cost of the position in the hand rather than in the argument.

Please write this up so I can run it cold: the materials list, the exact wording of every item on both ladders and of the anchor descriptions, the scoring and token scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes including how to collect switching points from 24 students quickly with nothing but slips of paper and a whiteboard, and the debrief as a sequence of questions that maps specific moments in the game onto each of the four commitments above, revealing the name of the view only at the end. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students treating the numbers as notional and answering strategically, teams reading the intensity anchors differently so the data are incomparable, a room full of aggregators producing no cycle to exhibit, the pleasure ladder turning into jokes — and how to design or facilitate around each. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply verbally or simply concede. I already have one worry and I want it assessed rather than reassured: if teams are scoring anything at all, and if I hand out a token that buys a single kind of reward, then the medium of the game has already granted the one common currency the view denies, and the exercise may quietly argue against its own subject. Tell me whether that is right, whether it can be designed around, and what other feature of the view — I suspect something about what it is actually like to undergo the top of the scale — no classroom simulation can deliver. No reading list, no citations, and no names of positions or philosophers anywhere except in the final minutes of the debrief.
