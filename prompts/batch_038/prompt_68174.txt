I'm in a philosophy of technology seminar this term and I also help book our department's tiny podcast, so I got handed next week's guest. He's a philosopher of technology who defends a definately strong reading of Winner's old question about whether artifacts have politics — his own version he calls the sedimentation thesis. Roughly: political arrangements get baked into the material design of things, and once they're baked in they go on doing political work on their own, without needing anybody's intentions, anybody's beliefs, or the social system that produced them to stick around. His signature move is the low-clearance overpasses on the Long Island parkways — he'll say the exclusion is still being carried out by the concrete today even if you could show nobody ever intended it, because the politics is in the artifact rather than in the story we tell about the artifact. He's also into the maintenance angle: the politics survives because keeping a thing standing is always cheaper than re-deciding it.

He's charming and slippery and I know exactly how the hour goes if I let him — he restates the thesis in new words instead of answering. So write me eight questions, each with a one-line follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to actually give.

Four of them need to hit specific pressure points:

- the difference between an artifact that requires a given political arrangement and one that's merely compatible with it, and which of the two his thesis actually needs — if everything lands in the compatible column, in what sense is the politics in the thing?
- the archival work that picked the parkway story apart (Joerges and others went and looked at the records); I want to know whether his thesis loses anything if the historical facts go against it, and if not, why that isn't a problem
- a demand that he name an artifact that would come out politically inert on his account, and what happens to the word politics if nothing does
- a live policy fight — transit, grid interconnection, housing retrofits, whatever he picks — where his view recommends something the deflationary position doesn't, the one that says politics lives in social relations and artifacts are just where it shows up

Also mark which of my questions he'll try to dodge by re-describing the thesis, and end with a single question he can't dodge that way. No filler about what got him interested in technology, please, and nothing that requires him to have read my seminar syllabus.
