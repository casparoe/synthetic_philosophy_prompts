I'm in my third year of a political theory PhD and last week our department co-hosted a two-day workshop on multi-level governance with some people from a law faculty — EU folks mostly, plus one guy who does American state legislatures. I only sat in on the first afternoon because I had teaching, and then ended up next to two of the speakers at the workshop dinner, where they got into an argument that went on far longer than the panel had. I'm writing it up from notes on my phone, so some of it is reconstructed, but the moves were roughly these. Ana was arguing that corporate tax base rules should be harmonised at EU level, since tax competition is a textbook race to the bottom. Jonas said that's just an article of faith, and offered Delaware as a counterexample: the internal market for corporate charters is a race, states compete by making corporate law friendlier to management, Delaware wins, collects a huge chunk of its general-fund revenue from franchise taxes (he said something like a billion and change a year, for a state of under a million people), and corporate law is arguably *better* for it. So competition between jurisdictions isn't automatically destructive — sometimes it produces the best rules anyone could have designed. Ana said the analogy fails because Delaware sits inside a federal system with a supreme court and an interstate commerce clause that can knock out the worst externalising moves, whereas the EU has no comparable coercive backstop: any tax harmonisation measure needs unanimity, so the member state that benefits most from the externality holds a veto over fixing it. Delaware is competition under a sovereign; the EU is competition with no sovereign, which is a different animal. Jonas then said fine, but that just relocates the question, because on his view the real principle is subsidiarity — the presumption runs toward the lowest competent level, and the burden sits on whoever wants to move authority upward to show there are cross-border spillovers the smaller unit can't internalise. Under that principle, Delaware-style charter competition is legitimate unless you can show the harm falls on people who have no say, and in the EU case, unlike the climate case, you often can't. Ana said his principle is doing no work at all. Take it seriously and run it upward: carbon emissions are a spillover no sub-planetary unit can internalise, so the same reasoning that says corporate tax base belongs in Brussels says climate belongs in a world government. Jonas said he's happy to bite that bullet, more or less, and that the principle then just becomes "match the scale of governance to the scale of the problem, all the way up", which he called a deflationary but perfectly good account of federalism. She said that's not federalism any more, because the whole content of federalism was supposed to be that there are *levels* with their own standing, not just whatever functional unit happens to fit each problem. And problems don't come pre-sliced into neat scales: a river basin crosses three cantons and two member states, migration policy is simultaneously border, labour-market and foreign policy, so the matching rule can't actually pick out a level of government without a prior decision about which effects count as the relevant ones and whose interests get aggregated. At which point, she said, you aren't applying subsidiarity, you're using it to decorate a decision that was already made politically about who the units are and who counts inside them — and that question is prior to the principle, not answered by it. Jonas's last move was that she'd conceded his point: if the principle needs a prior theory of unit-individuation, then it isn't empty, it's just incomplete, and every allocation theory including hers has to start from some given set of units, so she owes exactly the same account she's demanding from him. She said there's an asymmetry, because she never claimed to derive the units from the principle, and he did. Most of the table went over to her side by the end, and I couldn't tell whether that's because she actually refuted him or just because she was more confident and he was visibly tired.
