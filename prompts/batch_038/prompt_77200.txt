I'm running a social theory reading group for first-years — a mix of historians, two economists who are pretty sure none of this is science, and one guy who turned up because he'd heard of Marx. Everybody's read the preface where the base/superstructure image comes from, and half of them have already decided what they think about it. I want to open the second session with a self-scoring quiz: "which version of historical materialism do you actually hold?" My guess is a fair number of them will find out it isn't the one they'd have claimed last week.

Ten questions. Every one a concrete case, historical or present-day, three answer options each. The bit I care about most: no question may ask about a label, and none of them should be answerable by someone who's only memorised the vocabulary. The words "base" and "superstructure" can appear in the answer options at most twice in the whole quiz, and never as the entire content of an option. People should get sorted by what they say about enclosures or about which machines get adopted, not by which -ism they like the sound of.

Cases I'd like you to use or improve on: a parliamentary act enclosing common land, with wage labour spreading a generation later; a factory owner choosing between a machine that replaces workers and one that just speeds the existing workers up; a data-protection rule that the biggest platforms lobbied for and then did better under than their smaller rivals; a legal regime where the key productive resource only exists at all because the law defines it (spectrum licences, carbon permits, that sort of thing); a religious or moral doctrine that seems to encourage a particular kind of economic conduct; and at least one item that turns on prediction, i.e. whether the theory lets you say in advance which legal change is coming rather than explaining it afterwards. Also include one where two societies with basically the same productive forces hold on to different property systems for a couple of centuries — I want to see what people do with that one.

Then the scoring key. Map answer *patterns* onto positions, not single answers: the hard version where the forces of production do the driving, the version where class struggle is the motor and technique gets chosen, the functional version where legal and political forms persist because they stabilise the economy, the relative-autonomy version, the deflationary version on which the base/superstructure line isn't marking anything real, plus any leaf you think belongs there that I've left out. Name them the way people in the field actually name them. No bibliography and no reading list, though — no book titles or page numbers, I can't check them and neither can they. Just the names of the positions.

Three more things:

- Handle the messy patterns. Somebody who's a determinist about law and an autonomist about religion needs a diagnosis of their own, and somebody who picks the middle "they interact" option every single time should be flagged as dodging rather than scored as a moderate.
- Tell me which pairs of positions my quiz probably can't seperate, and what question I'd have to add to pull them apart.
- After the key, list the combinations of answers that are logically available but nobody defends, and say in a line each why not — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And one sentence per leaf on the commitment that position finds most embarassing.

It has to be self-administerable in about twenty minutes, so keep the cases tight enough to read at a table.
