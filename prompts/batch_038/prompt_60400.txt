I'm a postdoc drafting a short paper for a workshop on multi-agent AI safety; the room will be roughly half formal game theorists and half people from the alignment community who know the informal literature. Below is the core argument in condensed form — what will become sections 1 through 4 of the paper. I want your comments on the argument, not on the writing.

**The thesis: commitment races select for agents that cannot reflect their way out of a commitment.**

1. Call it a commitment race when several agents each stand to gain by binding themselves to a policy in advance, but the gain from binding falls off as others bind first — once the terms of the eventual interaction are fixed by everyone else's commitments, your own commitment has nothing left to offer. So there is standing pressure to commit early, on whatever information you happen to have when the window is open.

2. Committing early means committing before you know. That is not an accident of the setup but the source of the value: a commitment that waits on the evidence which would make it advisable conveys no information and buys nothing. Credibility requires that the commitment be non-conditional on exactly the facts you would otherwise want to condition on.

3. It follows that the race selects against reflective endorsement. The agent that does best is one whose commitment-forming process is unresponsive to the considerations that, once known, would tell against the commitment. An agent that forms commitments only after weighing whether they are right will arrive late and take the residual. What wins is a psychology that commits fast and holds — dispositions anchored in something other than case-by-case judgment: temperament, standing rules adopted once, identification with a role, loyalty.

4. This is not merely a trade of speed for accuracy; it relocates the seat of rationality. On the standard picture the rational assessment attaches to acts, and dispositions are instruments at best — Kavka's toxin puzzle is usually read as showing you cannot rationally intend what you know you will not do. A commitment race inverts the picture: the payoff attaches to the disposition, and the acts are its downstream output. If the disposition is what earns, the operative question is not whether it is rational to act thus but whether it is rational to be the kind of agent that cannot help acting thus — and the answer looks like yes, because the cannot-help-it is precisely what makes the signal credible.

5. The upshot I want to defend: commitment races are a mechanism by which agents are selected for holding positions they cannot articulate and could not defend on request. That is a bad result for anyone hoping that powerful agents will be tractable because they are reasonable rather than because they are stubborn. It also implies that deliberately building agents which reflect before committing is a handicap in any competitive environment, which is an uncomfortable thing to say to people building such agents now.

**Objection I expect and my reply.** This proves too much. Commitment devices are ubiquitous and are normally praised as instruments of rational agency: constitutional rules, vows, Ulysses lashed to the mast, reputation. My reply is that those are adopted with full information about one's own future temptations, whereas the race forces you to adopt the device before you know what it will bind you to. I am not confident the reply holds, because in a race you usually do know the shape of the future interaction — what you lack is the timing.

**What I want from you:**

- Whether 2 to 3 is too quick. I suspect I slide between not-conditioning-on-evidence and not-reflecting-on-values, and that an agent could be perfectly reflective about ends while being evidentially committed early. If so, does anything of the thesis survive the repair, or does 5 collapse with it?
- Whether I am using the toxin case correctly in 4, or exploiting an ambiguity between intending and being disposed to intend.
- Where 5 overclaims relative to what 1 through 4 actually establish, and what the weakest version of 5 is that is still worth a workshop slot.
- Whether the objection I flag is the strongest one available. I would rather be given a better objection than be reassured about this one — in particular I want to know whether a game theorist would say that what I am describing is just signaling with commitment costs, already handled, and that my talk of dispositions adds nothing.
- Whether the argument can survive being told it needs a model. Say what such a model would have to contain for 3 to come out as a theorem rather than a story, and whether you think it would.

Please argue with the essay rather than sending me a bibliography: I am not looking for references, attributions, or a reading list, and I would rather you flag a step as unsupported than guess at who has defended something like it.
