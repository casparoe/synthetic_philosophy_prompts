I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy — my degree is in computer science and I have never taken an epistemology course — so please write for a reader who is exact about systems and inexact about your vocabulary. What I want is a classification instrument, not an essay.

Here is the situation that made me look into this. I own a fraud-screening service. For each transaction it emits a calibrated probability that the transaction is fraudulent, and downstream automation acts on that number. The cost structure is asymmetric, and we set the action threshold in the usual way, as the cost of a false block divided by the sum of the costs of a false block and a false approval.

On our high-value flow, a legitimate transaction that we block costs us about twelve dollars in fees, support time, and goodwill, and a fraudulent transaction that we approve costs us about four thousand. The threshold therefore sits near 0.003: we block on what looks like extremely weak evidence. On a low-value flow we shipped last quarter — small in-app purchases — a wrongful block aborts a purchase and generates friction and support work costing us about thirty cents, while a wrongful approval costs us about five cents. The threshold there sits near 0.86. The model, its features, and its calibration are identical on both flows; only the cost numbers differ.

Both deployment documents use the word "knows." Each says that the system knows a transaction is fraudulent when the calibrated probability exceeds the configured threshold for that flow and the feature set passes a coverage check, and each tells the human analysts who work the review queue that anything the system "knows" requires no independent re-verification. So the same model, facing the same transaction with the same evidence and the same calibrated probability of, say, 0.02, is described in one document as knowing that the transaction is fraudulent and in the other as not knowing it — and the analysts' own obligations shift accordingly, since on the high-value flow they are permitted to skip re-verification at 0.02 and on the low-value flow they are not.

My engineering instinct is that this is sloppy vocabulary: there is a probability, there is a cost function, and "knows" is a placeholder for "above the action threshold." But a colleague who reads philosophy tells me that whether practical stakes can bear on whether someone knows is a live dispute with several incompatible positions, and that the positions are not what I would have guessed. I would like to find out what I am actually committed to.

Please write a decision tree that takes a reader from "I accept the description of these two deployments" to a position on whether practical stakes bear on knowledge. Requirements:

1. Size and shape. Seven to nine leaves. Each node is a single question. Every node must be answerable by someone in my position: pose it as a concrete case, as a prediction about what an ordinary speaker would say, or as a yes/no question about my two deployments. No node may require me to choose between technical terms I have not defined for myself.

2. Tags. Tag every node with which kind of question it is — a question about what the word "knows" means or how its truth conditions get fixed; a question about whether knowledge itself, with the meaning of the word held fixed, is sensitive to practical stakes; or a question about norms of action and assertion that leaves knowledge untouched. I want to see at a glance whether the tree is mixing semantic questions with substantive ones.

3. Whose stakes. One node must ask whose practical situation is the relevant one. I can identify at least three candidates in my own system: the system itself, which has no interests and no goals of its own; the analyst who relies on the verdict; and the party who pays when the verdict is wrong. Make the candidates explicit, say which leaves each answer leads to, and tell me whether the candidate I find most natural — whoever bears the cost of the error — corresponds to a position people hold or to an empty cell. Also tell me whether any position can apply at all to a knower with no practical interests whatsoever, since that is what my model is.

4. Direction of effect. As my colleague summarized the standard examples, higher stakes demand more evidence before someone knows. My system runs the other way: the flow where a mistake is most expensive in absolute terms is the flow on which we act, and hence on which we say the system knows, on the weakest evidence, because the act we are licensing is itself cheap to get wrong. Include a node that asks whether the stakes parameter enters through the cost of the error one would make by acting on the belief or through the cost of the error one would make by failing to act on it, and make sure the tree does not quietly assume the standard direction. A further distinction I want represented somewhere: whether what varies between my two flows is the magnitude of the stakes or only the structure of the cost ratio. If some position predicts my inversion and some cannot accommodate it, that should drive the sorting rather than appear in a footnote.

5. Leaves. For each leaf give three things: its defining claim in one sentence, stated so it could not be confused with the defining claim of any other leaf; what it says about my two documents — specifically whether, on that view, the high-value document's claim that the system knows at 0.02 is true, false, or not in the business of being true or false; and the single most awkward commitment the position takes on. Label the leaves by their claims, not by names. If you are confident that a standard term of art or a particular thinker corresponds to a leaf you may add that in parentheses, but do not guess at attributions, and include no bibliography, no reference list, and no suggestions for further reading anywhere in your answer.

6. Empty cells. Identify at least three combinations of answers that your nodes permit but that no position occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it looks to you merely unexplored. Include among the candidates you consider the combination on which practical stakes bear not on a threshold applied to fixed evidence but on which propositions count as the system's evidence in the first place; if you think that cell is actually occupied, say so and say by what, rather than forcing it onto the empty list.

7. Indistinguishability. Flag every pair of leaves that no question in your tree actually separates — where both positions would answer every node the same way. For each flagged pair, say what further evidence would separate them, in a form I could act on: an additional case to put to ordinary speakers, a divergence in predicted verdicts on some new scenario, or a difference in what the position owes me about my system.

8. Where I land. Close with a short section that runs my practice as described above through the tree, states which leaf it arrives at, and gives the path. Then state which leaf I would arrive at if I made one specific change: deleting the word "knows" from both documents and speaking only of calibrated probabilities and cost functions. Say plainly whether that change is a philosophical concession, a clarification, or nothing at all.

Keep the result to something I could hand a colleague at a design review — the tree, the leaf descriptions, the empty cells, the flagged pairs, and the closing section. No preamble on the history of the debate, and no definitions of terms I did not ask about.
