The question was:

> The problem of logical omniscience. State precisely what the problem is: identify the conditions in the standard possible-worlds semantics for belief (or knowledge) that generate it, and explain why those conditions are thought to be objectionable rather than merely idealizing. Then describe two proposed remedies, and assess whether either can be adopted without giving up whatever it was that made the possible-worlds framework attractive in the first place.

Here is the student's answer in full:

> Possible-worlds semantics represents an agent's doxastic state by a set W of worlds, those the agent takes to be live candidates for the actual world: the agent believes φ just in case φ is true at every world in W. Two conditions of this representation generate the problem. If φ is valid, then φ is true at every world and so at every world in W; hence the agent believes every validity, and the corresponding proof rule is necessitation, from ⊢ φ infer ⊢ Bφ. And if the agent believes φ and believes φ → ψ, then ψ is true at every world in W, so the agent believes ψ; the corresponding axiom is K, the distribution of belief over implication. Since a normal modal logic is exactly one with both, doxastic logic on the standard semantics is normal, and Hintikka's logic of knowledge, which adds KK to T, is omniscient in this sense.
>
> KK is in fact the main culprit. It says that whatever the agent believes, the agent believes that she believes it; iterated, it represents an agent who believes p as believing an endless hierarchy of higher-order beliefs, and it is this hierarchy, not K, that makes demands no finite reasoner could meet. Dropping KK while retaining T — moving from S4 down to the weaker logic T — therefore removes the worst of the problem without abandoning possible worlds at all, which is why the objection, though genuine, is routinely overstated in the AI literature.
>
> Two remedies that go further are worth comparing. Awareness logic, following Fagin and Halpern and presented systematically in *Reasoning About Knowledge*, adds an awareness operator and defines explicit belief as implicit belief conjoined with awareness of the formula in question. Because awareness is a resource-bounded, quasi-syntactic matter, the logic of explicit belief is non-normal: no closure principles hold for it at all, and an agent can fail to believe a validity simply by never having entertained the vocabulary in which it is expressed. This is elegant, but it relocates the difficulty rather than answering it, since the awareness function is itself defined over the same possible-worlds structures and inherits their closure properties. The second remedy is impossible-worlds semantics, associated with Rantala and with Levesque's distinction between implicit and explicit belief, which widens the space of worlds so that logical truths fail at some of them; a belief state is then a set of worlds closed under nothing in particular. Stalnaker's contextualism belongs to the same family: what a belief ascription says depends on the contextually determined partition of the space of worlds, and a partition can be drawn finely enough to separate contents that are necessarily equivalent. In both cases the cost is that content becomes hyperintensional, so the account no longer explains why believing φ and believing an obvious consequence of φ usually go together.
>
> One further point is often pressed. For propositional epistemic logic, validity is decidable, so unlike the first-order case the semantics describes a state an agent could in principle occupy, and the difficulty becomes acute only when the language is expressive enough for undecidability. Decidability of the logic is of course not tractability of the reasoning it attributes to the agent, but the idealization involved is benign in the way that frictionless planes are: no observation we could make of a finite reasoner would distinguish closure under known implication from closure under a very large finite fragment of it.
