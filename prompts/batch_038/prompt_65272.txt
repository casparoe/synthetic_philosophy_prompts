I'm in the second term of a seminar on distributive justice, and for our closing study session I want to hand out a diagnostic that sorts each of us by what we actually believe about the Wilt Chamberlain example, rather than by which label we're willing to claim in front of the group. Could you write it as a decision tree?

The starting commitment I want every participant to grant is minimal and shared: that Nozick's story is possible. Begin from a distribution D1 that everyone, including you, judges just; a contract under which Chamberlain receives twenty-five cents of the price of each ticket; one million fans who pay it; and a D2 in which he holds $250,000 more than he did and everyone else twenty-five cents less. Nobody is allowed to escape by disputing that setup. The tree's job is to locate where we part company afterwards, and each branch node should be phrased as a question a participant can answer without knowing which leaf the answer leads to — no node may ask anyone whether they are a libertarian, an egalitarian, or a Rawlsian.

I'd like four modified versions of the case to supply the concrete tests at the branch nodes:

(a) Teammate gifts. There is no surcharge at the gate; the same $250,000 reaches Chamberlain because his teammates each voluntarily hand him a slice of their own salaries. Does it matter to the justice of D2 whether the transfer arrived as a purchase or as a gift?

(b) Scarcity rent. Nobody gives Chamberlain anything directly. The extra money exists because the arena has a fixed number of good seats and the club charges what demand will bear, paying him a share of the take. Is a holding that arose from the interaction of a scarce resource with aggregate demand, rather than from anyone's deliberate giving, still one he is entitled to keep?

(c) Bystanders. The fans' purchases are exactly as in the original, but Chamberlain uses the proceeds to buy the only apartments within walking distance of the arena, and people who never attended a game end up worse off in D2 than in D1. Does a chain of transfers among consenting parties retain its presumption in its favor when third parties lose?

(d) Maintenance. The state never touches an individual transaction, but the gate takes the surcharge off the top and remits it, so the pattern is restored continuously; alternatively, the pattern is restored only across lifetimes, through annual levies and transfers. Is a patterned principle upset by liberty only if the pattern must hold at every instant?

Beyond these, please let the tree turn on whatever further splits you think are load-bearing — whether a principle's shape over time matters at all or only its profile at a moment; whether individuals own their talents in a sense that grounds a claim to whatever those talents fetch; whether principles of the form "to each according to X" get a claim on the whole social product or only on what institutions allocate after the rules of property are fixed; whether a principle with a threshold or floor still counts as patterned in the sense the argument attacks; whether the example is doing conceptual work about the grammar of distributive principles or moral work about what people may do with what is theirs.

As leaves, I need at least: a Nozickian unpatterned historical view; a Rawlsian view resting on the basic structure; a luck-egalitarian or resource-based view that is sensitive to ambition but not to endowment; a Cohen-style egalitarian who rejects self-ownership altogether; a left-libertarian or Georgist who keeps full self-ownership but treats the windfall as a rent owed to everyone; a sufficientarian or prioritarian for whom the surcharge is unobjectionable unless it pushes someone below a floor; a conventionalist who denies that there is any pre-institutional baseline of holdings against which taxation could count as taking; a rectification-first view on which the actual D1 is already unjust, so the Chamberlain step never gets off the ground; and a rule-consequentialist about property. Add any others the logical space calls for, and identify combinations the tree permits but nobody occupies, with a line each on whether the vacancy is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Cap it at seven questions along any path and fourteen leaves. Include an answer key mapping response strings onto leaves, and — this part matters as much as the tree — flag every pair of leaves your instrument cannot genuinely separate, along with one tiebreaker case that might.

Finally, please mark for each branch and each leaf whether the disagreement it encodes is something specialists regard as reasonably settled, something genuinely contested, or a question of how best to read Nozick's own argument. I especially want the handout to avoid presenting either of two claims as consensus: that the example refutes the difference principle outright, and that Rawlsians have a clean reply that dissolves it. Where you think one side is clearly winning, say so, but present it as your assessment rather than as the state of the field.

Write in American English. I don't want a reading list or citations; put no words in quotation marks as if quoting anyone, and describe each position's reasoning in your own prose, using the standard labels only at the leaves.
