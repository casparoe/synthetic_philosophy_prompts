So I've got no philosophy training at all, this is entirely me reading on my own and half-listening to podcasts while doing dishes, but two things crashed into each other in my head a couple weeks ago and I can't shake it, so I figured I'd just ask someone who actually knows this stuff.

Thread one: I picked up a beat-up anthology at a library book sale and read Charlotte Perkins Gilman's "The Yellow Wallpaper." You probably know it — the young mother whose physician husband decides she has some nervous condition after the baby and rents a big old house for the summer, sticks her in the barred upstairs nursery, forbids her to work or write, so she keeps a secret journal; with nothing to look at but the hideous yellow paper on the walls she starts to make out a subpattern, then a woman stooping and creeping behind it, then lots of women, and by the end she's locked the door and stripped the paper off and is creeping round the room herself, telling her husband she's finally gotten out in spite of him. He faints, she keeps circling over his body. Thread two: I'd been listening to something about the science of consciousness and ran into Dennett's heterophenomenology, as best I understood it from a summary — the idea that you take a person's reports about their inner life as texts, as data, and you build up the world the way the subject believes it to be, sort of like reconstructing the world of a novel, while officially staying neutral about whether any of the stuff in there really exists. Which makes the woman behind the wallpaper feel like a perfect resident of one of those worlds: an object the narrator takes to be there that we readers are pretty sure isn't.

My hunch is that the story is either a really nice showcase for that method or a genuinely damaging case against it, and I can't tell which. Who is doing heterophenomenology inside the story — is it me, the reader, treating her journal as a text? And isn't the husband the exact opposite, since he doesn't treat her reports as data at all, he treats them as symptoms of the thing they're describing? Does the story expose a limit, though: a subject who doesn't have the vocabulary for what's happening to her, so what she "reports" is partly borrowed scripts (nerves, self-control, the wallpaper as the culprit) rather than her experience? And where the method says stay neutral, the story seems to scream that neutrality is not available — for us, and for her, it matters enormously whether that creeping woman is real. Also, does the ending break the method's whole setup, since by then she isn't reporting anything, she's just creeping?

Please tell me where you think I'm right, and where I'm forcing the story into a picture it doesn't fit or bending Dennett into something he'd flatly deny — I don't want to reduce the story to an illustration or caricature the method. And be straight with me about what's settled versus what's a live fight among people who work on this: I get the impression a lot of philosophers think heterophenomenology is eliminativism with a nicer hat on and Dennett and his defenders think that's a misreading, so don't hand me either side as if it were the consensus. End with a sentence or two on what the story brings to that argument that arguments alone wouldn't. Keep the whole thing under 700 words — no reading list or bibliography please, I just want the reading.
