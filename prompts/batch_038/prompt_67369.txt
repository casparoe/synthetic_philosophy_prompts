I spent thirty-four years in an invertebrate physiology lab, mostly on the neuromuscular side of cephalopod arms, and only since retiring have I been reading philosophy of mind with any seriousness. What pulled me in was Dennett's way of sorting minds into grades — Darwinian creatures, whose endowments are fixed by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures, who can have their behavior reshaped by the consequences of what they happen to do; Popperian creatures, who can, in his telling, let their hypotheses die in their stead by running them offline against an internal model of the world before acting; and Gregorian creatures, whose thinking is amplified by externally generated tools that accumulate across generations. It is a beguiling ladder, and my reading group treats it as though it were a set of natural kinds. I am not so sure, and I have noticed that whenever I try to say precisely what would have to be true for the ladder to be more than an appealing story, I come up with a different complaint each time and no way of weighing them against one another.

So before I judge any particular version of this project, I want the specification. Write out the desiderata that any account of grades or levels of mindedness — Dennett's or a rival's — would have to satisfy in order to count as a genuine theory rather than a taxonomy of convenience. For each item, give me the requirement, the case or consideration that motivates it, and a tag marking it as non-negotiable, negotiable, or merely the parochial preference of one camp. Among the things I would expect you to address, though you should feel free to add to or prune this list: whether each rung is supposed to be identified by a distinct selection mechanism, and if so, what is doing the selecting and what is being selected, since that question has an obvious answer at the Darwinian rung and a much shakier one higher up; whether the rungs must be cumulative, so that every Gregorian creature is also Popperian, Skinnerian, and Darwinian, and what follows if they need not be; how the account is to handle mosaic and dissociable distribution, given that a common octopus is a superb problem-solver through its arms and a poor candidate for anything resembling offline rehearsal, while certain corvids cache, plan, and appear to reason about competitors, and given the jumping spiders whose elaborate visually guided predatory routines seem to require internal modeling in animals with no cortex at all; whether the grades are claims about the design of a lineage, about the history of an individual, or about both, and what to say about cases where they come apart, as with an animal raised in impoverished conditions or a person whose scaffolding is stripped away by illness; whether the categories earn their keep by predicting and explaining experimental findings or only relabel them after the fact, and whether circularity between the behavioral criteria for a grade and the grade invoked to explain the behavior can be avoided; what the account owes to continuity with evolutionary biology, and whether the demand that it avoid any hint of a ranked scala naturae is a real constraint or a piece of contemporary taste; and how it is to treat machines that learn by reinforcement and systems trained on the accumulated external products of human cognition, which seem to land in several rungs at once.

Then tell me where the list bites. Which single requirement eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive accounts? And is there a subset of the desiderata that cannot be satisfied jointly — my own suspicion is that mechanism-exclusivity, cumulative embedding, and unique assignment of real organisms to rungs are in tension, and I would like to know whether that is right or whether I have simply mis-stated one of them — because if so, the impossibility is the result I most want in hand. Finish with no more than two or three sentences each on how three rival ways of dividing minds (for instance, a purely gradient reinforcement-learning picture, a predictive-processing picture on which offline modeling is just higher-order model selection, and Dennett's own intentional-stance framing) fare against the list; I am not asking for a full scorecard and I do not want a bibliography, since I have no way of chasing one down and would rather have your reasoning than your references. Please write in American English. Lead with your bottom line — the single sentence stating which requirement does the most work and whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable — and only then give the list and the reasoning, with no preamble, no throat-clearing, and no restatement of my question.
