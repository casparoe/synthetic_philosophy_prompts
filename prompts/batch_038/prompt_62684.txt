I am revising a thesis chapter on probabilistic reasoning about mathematical claims after my supervisor rejected its framing assumption. The assumption is that logical omniscience — credence one in every theorem of arithmetic, zero in every contradiction — is the correct regulative ideal, so that a bounded agent's uncertainty about a conjecture is merely a failure to approximate that ideal. Her objection is not the familiar one about finitude: she holds that a norm which no computable process can satisfy even in the limit cannot be doing any work in evaluating actual mathematical reasoning. Please give me your credence that the assumption is nonetheless correct, and decompose it — roughly, the probability that an ideal unattainable in principle can still be normatively binding on agents like us; the probability that the diagonal constructions driving the impossibility results (sentences that assert something about the probability assigned to themselves) reveal a conceptual confusion rather than a merely computational barrier; and the probability that the rival norms proposed for bounded reasoners, such as non-exploitability by efficient traders or treating mathematical uncertainty as a distinct epistemic kind, genuinely answer the same question rather than substituting a different one. Give a figure for each term, show how they combine into your number, state which term the number is most sensitive to, and say what consideration would move you most.
