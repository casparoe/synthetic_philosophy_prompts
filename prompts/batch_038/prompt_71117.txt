I run a Tuesday night philosophy meetup out of our local library — about a dozen of us, nobody's got a degree, I taught myself basically all of it from library books and podcasts. At the end of each term we do a silly-serious thing where I set an essay question, we all write answers at home, and then we mark each other's. Trouble is I'm running out of questions that are actually hard. People have gotten good at looking the topic up and handing back a wikipedia-shaped answer, so I want one that can't be done that way.

This term we spent six weeks on minds and machines and it went off in a specific direction I want to turn into the question. Stuff we kept circling:

- One of our members works in a datacenter. He pointed out that the exact same program can be running on two racks of machines at once, or twice on one machine, and from the outside there's nothing at all that tells them apart. So if a mind is a program — most of us agreed something like that has to be right — are two identical copies of a mind one person or two? He reckoned one, because it's the same pattern. Most of the group reckoned two, because you can delete them separately. Nobody could say what the disagreement actually hinged on.
- Then someone said: suppose ten thousand copies are running in perfect lockstep, same inputs, same everything, and at the end you delete 9,999 of them. Mass murder, or just tidying up? And if your answer is different for ten thousand copies than for one copy run twice, what exactly changed?
- Then the thing that broke my brain, from one of the quieter members: if a mind is just a structure, the structure is there whether anyone runs it or not, the same way the rules of chess are there whether anyone plays. So the "pattern" of a suffering mind is already satisfied somewhere by... weather? plumbing? dust in a room? Either everything is in agony all the time, or the pattern on it's own can't be what matters and it has to be about something actually running. But she couldn't say what "actually running" adds, since the running copy has no extra ingredient in it — it's the same pattern plus a clock.

That last one is the crux I want the question built around. I want the student to have to commit to a principle about what it takes for there to be a subject there at all, and then eat the cost of it, including the dust-and-weather problem and the lockstep duplicates. A good answer should show that "one copy is one person, ten thousand copies are ten thousand people" and "it's always just the one person no matter how many times you run it" both collapse, and then say where the least bad place to land is and what you have to give up to get there.

So: write me the question itself, self-contained enough that someone who wasn't at the meetup could do it (restate whatever cases they need inside the question — don't assume they know what we talked about). Pitch it at a smart beginner with no philosophy beyond our six weeks, answerable in about 50 minutes, worth 25 marks. Then a model answer of roughly 700 words — and please no book or philosopher names in it, my students can't look anything up and I want the answer to stand on its own. Then a marking scheme splitting the 25 marks across five or six things a top answer actually has to do, and for each one tell me what the common failure looks like: the move that sounds right but isnt, like announcing you're a functionalist, or saying "it depends whether they're conscious" without saying what settles that, or splitting the difference down the middle and calling it a conclusion.

Last thing — a couple of sentences on what separates a solid B answer from an A answer here. That's the bit our peer marking always gets wrong; everybody marks generously for length.
