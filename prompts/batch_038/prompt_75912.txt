I'm a fourth-year doctoral student in a history of science program, and this spring I'm convening the second-year methods seminar for a cohort that includes two early modernists, a medievalist, someone working on postwar development aid, and a student from the school of education writing on the professionalization of teaching. Week four is devoted to Butterfield's short 1931 book on the whig interpretation of history, and I want to open the session with a poem printed on the handout. It will also appear on the inside cover of our department's graduate conference program, so it has to work for a reader who has never opened the book and may not be a historian at all. I'd like a pantoum of five to seven quatrains — nothing longer, since it has to share a page with the week's schedule.

The form should carry the argument rather than decorate it, so please observe the mechanics strictly: the second and fourth lines of each quatrain recur, word for word, as the first and third lines of the next, and the final quatrain takes up the opening quatrain's first line as its own second line and the opening quatrain's third line as its own fourth, so that the poem ends on the line with which it began. The repeated lines must be identical — no rewording, no adjusted punctuation, no softened verb. That is the whole point. What I want a reader to experience is that the sentence never changes; only the position we assign it does, so that a line first offered as a remark about some eighteenth-century actor returns two stanzas later as a premise about us, and the transformation is entirely the historian's doing rather than the past's. Alternating rhymes are welcome if they arrive without strain, but do not trade verbatim recurrence for a rhyme. And the closing return should feel like a trap shutting on the narrator rather than a resolution earned: a narrative that arrives back at its own first line has demonstrated nothing, only disclosed that its ending was assumed at the outset.

On content, I would rather the poem avoid our seminar vocabulary altogether — no teleology, no presentism, no anachronism, nothing that lets the noun progress do the work, and please do not name Butterfield anywhere in the text. Work instead with the charges as they can be rendered concretely: that the past is consulted chiefly for what it says about the here and now; that a sequence of events which happens to have led to us is mistaken for a chain of causes, so that the actual causes stop being investigated; that the narrative is built by abridgement, retaining only what looks consequential from where we stand, which leaves the dead-end hypothesis, the uncatalogued bundle of depositions, and the losing side surviving merely as foils credited with contributing nothing to the making of the present; that the historian feels loose threads hanging until he can pronounce which party was in the right; and that the actors come out sounding far more like us than they were. Keep whiggishness a way of seeing rather than a faction — I do not want the poem turning into something about Burke and Fox, or about 1688 — though you may let it register how the habit migrated into other fields, including the mid-century confidence that societies travel a single track toward a destination already known. Two further demands, and they are the ones I care about most. Grant, in at least one quatrain, that every narrative must select and shorten, and that this is a real embarrassment for a critique of selection: the poem should not pretend to stand outside its own complaint. And let the reflexive turn arrive late — that the accusation has itself become a universal slur, and that narrating our discipline's escape from the whigs as a maturation is precisely the move under criticism, so that the loop closing the form closes on the poet too. Dry, wry, no uplift, no sermon; if a line wants to be consoling, cut it.
