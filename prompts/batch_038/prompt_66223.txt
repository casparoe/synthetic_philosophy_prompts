I spent 34 years doing physical chemistry in a government lab, mostly surface science, and I've only recently gotten into philosophy now that I'm retired. Last week I sat in on a grad reading group at the university about minds and machines (they let me come, I bring the donuts). One of the students kept saying "do what I mean, not what I say," and he used it to argue that writing down a goal or a utility function for an AI is a dead end, that the only sensible route is to train a system to aim at human approval instead. When I pointed out that in my lab a protocol followed by its "meaning" rather than its wording would have cost somebody their funding and possibly their fingers, he said that was exactly my problem.

Its clear to me the slogan is doing several different jobs at once and I can't pull them apart on my own. Can you lay out the separate claims "do what I mean, not what I say" could be making in this argument — running from the ones any engineer would grant straight through to the ones that are genuinely contentious or maybe just false? For each of them, I'd like to know what it implies about whether approval-seeking and goal-seeking are really two different kinds of system or two vocabularies for the same machinery. And then tell me which reading, if any, actually carries him from the slogan to "so forget goals, train on approval."

One more thing, and forgive me if this is a scientist's tic. I keep thinking of it as a calibration problem. My whole career I measured proxies for the quantities I actually cared about, and the worry was never whether the proxy was wrong on average, it was whether its error blows up precisely in the regime you push the instrument into. Does that intuition line up with one of your readings, or am I smuggling something in that isn't there?
