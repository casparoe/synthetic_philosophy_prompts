I cover psychology and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and over roughly fifteen years of interviews I have accumulated the same transcript shape again and again. I want to know whether the pattern has a name in the philosophical literature on meaning in life, because I have been offered several names and none of them seems to pick out the thing I keep seeing.

The pattern runs as follows. Someone finishes a long, difficult project — a book that took eight years, a company sold, a building designed, a prize awarded. During the years of work they describe their life as meaningful in terms they find easy to articulate. In the weeks after completion there is a flatness, occasionally close to bereavement, which they generally prefer not to discuss on the record. Within months they have taken up a new undertaking of comparable scale and the sense of meaning returns to its earlier level. None of that is surprising, and I am not asking about it.

What interests me is the double description they maintain throughout. Ask what the project was for and the answer is about the finished thing: the book that now exists, the company that runs without them, the building people walk into. Ask what they would tell a younger sibling and the advice is entirely about the shape of the striving — choose something that will resist finishing, keep a version of yourself in reserve, do not marry the outcome. The value is said to live in the achievement; the advice points at the activity. And the discrepancy is not treated by anyone, least of all by my subject, as a discovery. Point it out directly and you get a brief flash of irritation, as though a rule of the interview had been broken, after which the achievement-account resumes, unmodified, in the next sentence. The fiction looks collaborative rather than private: my impression is that most of the people I interview know privately that the trophy did not deliver, say publicly that it did, and expect the same courtesy from one another.

I have been told this is hedonic adaptation, that it is the paradox of hedonism, that Schopenhauer has something about a pendulum, and that Camus handles it with a man and a rock. Each of those seems to me to be about a different piece of what I am describing, and none of them touches the part where everyone agrees to keep saying the wrong thing.

Please answer in the following sections, in this order:

- **Bottom line** (about 80 words): whether any established concept covers the whole pattern, and if not, your name for it in a single sentence.
- **Nearest concepts** (about 250 words): the three or four candidates closest to it, from the philosophy of meaning in life and adjacent territory, with a sentence or two on what each actually claims.
- **Where the fit fails** (about 250 words): for each candidate, what the pattern contains that the concept does not — in particular the collaborative element, the split between the two descriptions, and the irritation when the split is surfaced.
- **A working definition** (about 200 words): your term stated so that a reader could apply it to a case they had never seen before, including the conditions under which it does not apply.
- **Boundary cases** (about 200 words): whether it extends to undertakings that cannot be finished, such as raising children; to projects abandoned rather than completed; to windfalls received rather than earned, such as inheritance or sudden fame; and to communities where the finished product is never emphasized in the first place.
- **How I would check it** (about 120 words): what would count as evidence that this is a real phenomenon and not simply a journalist's habit of hearing the same story twice.
