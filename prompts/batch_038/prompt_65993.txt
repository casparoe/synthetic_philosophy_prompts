I am a teaching assistant on a third-year undergraduate option course in the history of political economy, "Empire, Trade, and the Wealth of Nations," with 26 students. The final assessment is a 90-minute unseen examination in which students answer two questions from a list of six; no texts or notes are permitted in the room, though each question reproduces short passages for the candidates to work from. Scripts are anonymised, double-marked, and then moderated at a board, and the department's scale is 0–100, with 70 and above a first, 60–69 an upper second, 50–59 a lower second, 40–49 a third, and below 40 a fail. I have been given one script to mark and need a defensible assessment of it.

The question, as set, was this:

**Question 4.** The examination paper supplies the following passages from *The Wealth of Nations*:

- Book IV, ch. ii: "The general industry of the society never can exceed what the capital of the society can employ," and "No regulation of commerce can increase the quantity of industry in any society beyond what its capital can maintain. It can only divert a part of it into a direction into which it might not otherwise have gone…"
- Book IV, ch. ii: "every wholesale merchant naturally prefers the home trade to the foreign trade of consumption, and the foreign trade of consumption to the carrying trade."
- Book IV, ch. ii: "defence is of much more importance than opulence"; "The defence of Great Britain, for example, depends very much upon the number of its sailors and shipping"; and, of the Navigation Acts, "the act of navigation is, perhaps, the wisest of all the commercial regulations of England."
- Book IV, ch. vii: "Some moderate and gradual relaxation of the laws which give to Great Britain the exclusive trade to the colonies, till it is rendered in a great measure free, seems to be the only expedient which can, in all future times, deliver her from this danger."

The paper also reminds candidates that in Book IV, ch. vii Smith argues that Great Britain gains nothing from the dominion she assumes over her colonies, and that he nevertheless sketches a scheme for admitting the colonies to representation in the Parliament of Great Britain in proportion to their contribution to the revenue of the empire.

*Assess whether Smith's treatment of the colonial system is coherent. Your answer should explain what he takes the exclusive colonial trade to do to British capital, why he treats the Navigation Acts differently, and what work the scheme for a legislative union is doing in his argument.*

Here is the script, reproduced in full (approximately 850 words, written under examination conditions):

> Smith's position on empire looks inconsistent only if one assumes that his free trade is a principle admitting no exceptions. It is not. Once one sees that his objection to the colonial system is an objection to a particular mechanism by which regulation diverts capital, the Navigation Acts, the American monopoly, and the scheme for a legislative union fall into place — though, as I shall argue, the third of these sits badly with the first two.
>
> Smith begins from a proposition about aggregates: "the general industry of the society never can exceed what the capital of the society can employ," and no regulation can increase that industry but only "divert a part of it into a direction into which it might not otherwise have gone." The question for policy is therefore never whether a regulation promotes industry in the abstract, but whether the direction it imposes maintains more productive labour than the direction capital would have chosen of its own accord. Since a merchant's capital returns sooner in the home trade than in the foreign trade of consumption, and sooner in the foreign trade of consumption than in the carrying trade, a system which pushes capital toward the most distant employments maintains less labour per unit of stock over any given period.
>
> The American system did precisely this. The monopoly of the colony trade reserved an immense and rapidly growing market to British merchants, and British capital followed the profits on offer away from the agriculture and manufactures of the home market. Smith's claim is not merely distributive, though he makes that too: the decisive harm is that the ordinary rate of profit rose, which discouraged the improvement of land and made British manufactures dearer in foreign markets. The colonies, meanwhile, contributed nothing to the general defence or to the support of civil government; they required a large peace establishment and repeatedly drew Britain into expensive wars. Dominion, on Smith's reckoning, was expense and nothing else.
>
> The Navigation Acts are treated differently, and Smith says why: "defence is of much more importance than opulence," and "the defence of Great Britain … depends very much upon the number of its sailors and shipping." The Acts purchased sailors and ships at a known and accepted cost in opulence; the colonial monopoly purchased nothing comparable. But this is where the system shows its softness. If the colonial regulations and the Navigation Acts are instances of the same kind of thing — a restriction of trade evaluated against the national interest — then Smith's hostility to empire is not principled but conditional upon Britain's naval position. Had French sea power remained what it was in 1660, the reasoning that commends the Acts would equally commend the American monopoly, since the colonial trade sustained the shipping and the naval stores on which the fleet depended. Smith's free trade is accordingly a rule of thumb, and his case against empire a calculation about the present balance of power rather than a claim in political economy.
>
> The union scheme confirms this reading. Confronted with colonial resistance to taxation, Smith proposes that the colonies be admitted to representation at Westminster in proportion to their population, on the ground that government must ultimately rest on the consent of those it governs. This is a practical constitutional settlement, addressed to a Parliament he supposed capable of adopting it, and its purpose is conciliation: to hold the colonies inside the empire by giving them a share in its government. It is hard to reconcile with the argument from capital. A union would not restore the direction of British stock; it would merely extend the fiscal reach of a legislature already committed, on Smith's own diagnosis, to imperial grandeur. Where Smith does offer a remedy in his own terms, it is the "moderate and gradual relaxation" of the exclusive trade; yet elsewhere he urges that Britain should acknowledge the independence of the colonies at once and be done with the expense. Both cannot be the policy that follows from the analysis.
>
> The Indian material exhibits the same pattern. The abuses of the East India Company are, for Smith, the abuses of a monopoly: open the trade and the oppression ends. His genuine insight — that a company of merchants cannot govern, because the interest of the merchant and the interest of the sovereign point in opposite directions — is stated but never integrated, since integrating it would require a theory of institutions that his capital-and-industry framework cannot supply.
>
> In sum: Smith's argument against empire is powerful but prudential; his defence exception dissolves the principle beneath it; and his constructive proposals conflict with one another. *The Wealth of Nations* offers an excellent diagnosis and an unstable prescription.
