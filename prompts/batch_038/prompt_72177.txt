So my cousin Dana and I have been having the same argument on and off since Thanksgiving 2019, and at this point we keep a shared doc where we paste things at each other like it's a debate club. Her grandfather left her family a working farm — real acreage, equipment, the whole thing — and she thinks any estate or inheritance tax is straightforward confiscation of something people already earned twice over. I think untaxed dynastic wealth is feudalism with better PR. The problem is that neither of us can actually say where the line goes, because every time I try to state my view I end up either condemning things I do every day or letting through exactly the thing I'm objecting to. What I'd like is for you to look at my actual gut verdicts and tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here they are, roughly in the order we argued about them. Reading my daughter books every night and dragging her to museums on Sundays: completely fine, and I'd defend it against anyone, even though I know perfectly well it's making her more verbally fluent than the kid two doors down. Paying $4,000 for test prep: not fine. Paying for private K–12: not fine. Buying a house in a district with better public schools when the same house costs $250k more elsewhere: not fine in principle — but I would never support making it illegal, and I'd probably do it myself, which I admit is a problem. Passing on a paid-off $400k house to your only kid: fine, I think. Passing on enough in trust that your kid never has to take a job he doesn't want: not fine. Passing on a family farm or a family hardware store where the kid has been working since she was twelve: I keep saying fine because it's Dana, and I don't trust my own answer here. Stipending your kid so they can take the unpaid internship at the statehouse or the museum while other interns can't afford to: not fine. Paying off your adult kid's student loans: this is the one I flip-flop on constantly, because it looks identical to paying for college in the first place, which most of my friends and I do without blinking. And a $25k down-payment gift at 28: fine? but also I can't explain why it's fine and the trust isn't, except that the trust is bigger, which feels like a non-answer.

My own tentative principle, such as it is, is something like: what's objectionable isn't making your kid better off, it's buying them a place in the queue — converting money into a positional slot they couldn't otherwise earn, so that fair equality of opportunity is a fiction by the time anyone starts applying. But you can see it doesn't hold, because bedtime stories are positional too (there are only so many spots at the good school, and fluency wins them), and the farm is literally a slot — a job and an asset nobody else can have. So maybe the real variable is something else entirely: consumption vs. capital, gifts while you're alive vs. transfers after you're dead, whether the kid did anything to earn it, or just the dead-hand worry about people controlling property from the grave. I want you to test those and tell me which one, or which combination, actually delivers my verdicts — state each candidate precisely, clause by clause, and say which of my cases each clause is there to handle. Then run it on cases I haven't listed (inter vivos gifts timed to dodge the tax, a kid who inherits modestly from two different relatives, forgiving a loan to your kid's startup but not your nephew's, a scholarship funded by naming a building after your father) and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I have a feeling it's one of the private-school ones, and I'd rather find out from you than from Dana. One last thing: don't send me off to the literature or a reading list, I want your own reconstruction — and I specifically want to know whether the principle you land on cares about taxing the estate as a whole versus taxing what each individual recipient receives, since that seems like it might split the four-kid dynasty from the one-kid one.
