I run the technical review process at a mid-sized engineering org. I have no philosophy background, but I've ended up writing the rules by which small panels of reviewers approve or reject proposals, and my gut verdicts on a handful of real cases don't look like they come from a single rule. I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to — stated precisely enough that I could put it in the process doc and apply it without knowing anything about the stakes of the particular proposal.

The cases, and what I decided at the time:

1. An RFC is approved only if all three hold: the design is sound, it fits this quarter's budget, and our team will own the maintenance. Three reviewers. Ana: sound yes, budget no, maintenance yes — so reject. Bo: yes, yes, no — reject. Cy: yes, yes, yes — approve. Taken criterion by criterion, each of the three has majority support, so the RFC passes; taken as a whole, a majority rejects it. My call: approve. Nobody on the panel actually thought the design was bad.

2. A release is blocked if any of these holds: an open P1 bug, an untested rollback path, missing on-call sign-off. Dana cites only the bug, Eli cites only the rollback, Fay cites nothing. Criterion by criterion, no blocker has majority support, so it should ship; two of the three say block. My call: block. If it goes wrong, I want to be able to point at the two people who called it.

3. The same board, using the same written criteria, approves "add a caching layer" in March and approves "remove the caching layer" in September, with three of five members unchanged. I see no problem here: new data arrived, and a board is not a person.

4. A leveling panel decides a promotion on technical depth and scope, but one of the three members holds that mentorship is also part of the bar, which the other two deny is a criterion at all. We skipped the criteria and voted on the overall outcome. I still don't know whether that was right or a cop-out.

5. Last quarter, badly handled: three reviewers score an incident's severity from 1 to 5, our doc says block if the mean is at least 3, and the scores come back 5, 2, 2 — so the mean blocks it even though only one reviewer thinks it should.

What I'm after: the best candidate principle or principles behind 1–4, and what each one costs me; which pair of verdicts is hardest to reconcile and which single verdict I should give up to repair it; whether 1 and 3 are genuinely in conflict or only look that way; and what your principle says about case 5. Rank the problems by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and don't assume I'm attached to any of these verdicts — I mostly want to know which ones are unsustainable.
