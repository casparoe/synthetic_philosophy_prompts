I'm putting together the capstone session of my seminar on Du Bois's aesthetics, and I want the central exercise right before I hand it out, since it's doing basically all the work that week. The exercise is: given his track record of actual judgments about particular works, what does he say about a work that didn't exist in his lifetime?

Here's the record I've compiled — paraphrased, not quoted, so nobody has to go hunting:

- The big pageant he staged, sweeping through Black history from ancient Africa forward in tableaux, he held up as the model of what the art ought to be doing.
- McKay's Harlem bestseller about working-class nightlife, which white reviewers loved for its rawness — he went after it publicly and hard, even though a lot of the people arguing with him granted the prose real power.
- A teenager's short mythic poem about rivers, published in the magazine he edited — he backed it, and it isn't agitational in any obvious sense.
- The gifted young poet who said he wanted to be judged as a poet, not as a Negro poet: Du Bois treated that wish as the problem rather than the aspiration.
- The criteria he laid out for a Black theater: plays about Black life, written by Black people, staged for Black audiences, sited in Black neighborhoods.
- His own novel from the same year as the McKay fight — a political romance with a princess, a conspiratorial plot, and a near-utopian ending, nothing realistic about it. He stood by it as serious work.
- The essay collection from a decade earlier that mixes polemic on lynching with lyric and satirical fiction in one volume.

The new case is hypothetical, which I want, because then the students argue about the principle instead of about the book. A first novel by a Black woman in her twenties: formally dazzling, unsentimental, set in the neighborhood she grew up in. Violence, addiction, sex, no redemptive frame, and no institutional villain — the harm is distributed among the characters themselves. Bought and marketed by a white-owned house that sells it to white readers as raw and authentic. The author refuses the spokesperson role, says shes writing only what she knows. No political argument anywhere in it. But it's wildly popular with the Black teenagers in the very neighborhood it depicts, who trade lines from it back and forth, and one city councilmember cited it in a debate about policing.

Which way does he go: endorse, reject, or endorse on conditions? I want the candidate rules that fit those seven verdicts about equally well, what each one delivers on the new case, and your best guess with reasons. Then name the further case — one more hypothetical work, a couple of sentences is plenty — that would cleanly seperate two of those rules. And if you think the record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, say so flat out and tell me what he'd be committing himself to on each side. My own hunch, which I want tested, is that one of the candidate rules isn't about content at all but about who controls production and who's in the room, and thats doing more work in the list than I've been crediting it with.

Last thing: if any item I've listed sits oddly with the rest, flag it. I'd much rather hear it from you than from a grad student in week nine.
