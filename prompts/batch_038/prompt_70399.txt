I am co-organizing a workshop that puts comparative cognition and the science of artificial agents in the same room, and the move people invariably make is to reach for a stage-based taxonomy of minds: creatures whose repertoire is fixed by natural selection, creatures that can additionally acquire behavior by trial and error, creatures that can try out options internally before committing to them, and creatures that draw on tools and knowledge accumulated outside their own skulls. I want to stop handing out that ladder by default and instead write down what would have to be true of any such ladder for it to earn its keep.

So: write the desiderata — the specification an adequate stage-based taxonomy of cognitive kinds must satisfy. Fix the purpose as follows, since adequacy depends on it. The taxonomy is meant to do work, not to edify: it must license predictions about the behavioral flexibility of a system the classifier has never observed, and it must be applicable to engineered systems as readily as to animals.

My own suspicion, which I want you to engage with directly — either build it into the list or explain why it is mistaken: the ladder is being asked to do two jobs at once. One job is a claim about the mechanisms that install and revise a system's dispositions; the other is a claim about the format and the provenance of the information those dispositions carry. Those two come apart. A system can be saturated with the externalized products of a culture without being able to test its own hypotheses internally, and the bottom rung looks either trivially satisfied by everything, if selection is read liberally, or empty, if read strictly. Worse, the classification seems to shift with the surroundings rather than tracking the system: strip away the scaffolding and the creature appears to drop a step. I do not yet know whether these are three problems or one.

What I want back:

1. The numbered desiderata, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. Include at least one that concerns engineered systems specifically, and at least one about what the taxonomy must not do — some distinction it must preserve or some collapse it must avoid.
2. For each, mark whether it is non-negotiable or is an artifact of the evolution-first, animal-centered setting in which such taxonomies were invented. Say which of the latter should be dropped for my purposes.
3. The part I most want: identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which member has to be given up and what the loss costs. If you think the list is jointly satisfiable, argue that rather than assuming it.
4. Which single desideratum does the most to exclude candidate taxonomies.
5. A closing note of no more than 120 words on how a four-stage ladder of the kind described above fares against the list, compared with the most obvious repair — a two-dimensional scheme that separates mechanism from format rather than ordering them. Keep the weight on items 1 through 4; the scoring is secondary.

Paraphrase positions and arguments rather than quoting them, and give me no bibliography; I will attach the references myself. Hard limit: 850 words for the entire response, and no single desideratum, statement plus motivating case, may exceed 70 words.
