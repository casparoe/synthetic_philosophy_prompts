I am preparing a short statement of my own position on discounting the future for a workshop at which the stated views will be audited by the other participants, and I would like a candid check on whether the package holds together before I circulate it. Here is what I hold.

(1) Pure time preference is ethically indefensible. That a good accrues later is not in itself a reason to count it for less. A positive social discount rate can be justified only by the risk that the good will fail to materialize, by the opportunity cost of committing capital now, and by the expectation that future people will be better off, so that the marginal utility of the good will be lower.

(2) For applied appraisal I nonetheless use a descriptive rate in the 5–7 percent range, benchmarked to observed returns on capital. I take this to be a matter of democratic humility: it is not my place to substitute my own philosophical view of the right rate for the one revealed in the behaviour of the people whose money is being spent.

(3) I accept the reductionist account of personal identity. Identity is not what matters; what matters are psychological continuity and connectedness, and these admit of degrees. I also accept the further consequence, that the strength of an agent's reason to care about a later stage tracks the degree of connectedness she has with that stage rather than the bare fact of identity.

(4) Over long horizons I endorse a declining rate schedule. When the correct constant rate is itself uncertain — uncertain as to how impatient people will prove to be as well as to how fast the economy will grow — taking the expectation of discounted values is equivalent to discounting at a rate that falls as the horizon lengthens. I regard this as settling the long-run question.

(5) At the level of the individual, I think exponential discounting is a defect rather than a preference to be respected. Once temptation, uncertainty, and the agent's anticipated changes of taste are removed, there is nothing to be said for a smaller sooner benefit over a much larger later one.

Colleagues have pressed (1) against (2), and I am not persuaded they have found anything real there; but I have a residual unease about the set as a whole that I cannot locate. Tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and which only appear to, and for each genuine conflict state the cheapest revision and what it would cost me. Rank the conflicts by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and identify the single revision that would repair the most. I want a definite verdict with reasons, not a survey of the options: commit to a view about where this set fails.
