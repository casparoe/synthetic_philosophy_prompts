ok so context: I'm a junior and I somehow ended up chairing this new panel at my school that rules on complaints about student government election campaigning. It got made after last years election was a disaster. The panel is me, a sophomore, our SG advisor, and one senior, so basically nobody on it has taken philosophy. We get like 10-15 complaints a year, we have 40 minutes per meeting to decide them, and then we have to write a paragraph to the accused person explaining the ruling, which they can appeal to the whole student senate.

Our only rule right now is something I made up on the spot: if what you said is true, it isn't manipulation. I'm pretty sure that's broken but I can't articulate why.

Can you write us an actual decision procedure we can run on a complaint? Like the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where the procedure should just stop and say "no violation, moving on," adn where it has to hand the decision off to a human because it's a values call and not a fact call. Please use the real ideas about manipulation vs legitimate persuasion — bypassing someone's reasoning instead of engaging it, whether an emotional appeal is tracking reasons that are actually relevant, whether the person would endorse the influence if they noticed it happening, whatever else there is — but no reading list or citations, we are not going to read it. Just explain the concepts enough that a sophomore can apply them to a flyer. Keep the procedure itself to about a page.

Then run it on three things that actually happened here:

1. A candidate built a bunch of private instagram close-friends lists and sent different stuff to different groups — told the band kids he'd protect the band budget, told the athletes he'd protect sports funding, and never said anything in public that flat-out contradicted either one. Nothing from the private lists ever came out during the campaign.

2. A flyer that said "my opponent missed 60% of student council meetings." True. She was an ex officio member who wasn't required to attend, and she had mono for six weeks.

3. A club put its petition clipboard on the same lunch table as the free pizza and got something like 300 signatures. People signed without reading it. When we asked a bunch of them later, most said they'd still sign it if they actually read it.

Two more asks. Give us at least one case where the procedure spits out an answer I'm personally going to hate — my own favorite campaign tactic should probably get flagged by it, and I want to see that happen on the page. And end with the kinds of cases the procedure genuinely can't settle, so we know when to punt to the senate instead of pretending we figured it out.
