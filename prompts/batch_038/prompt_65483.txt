The view I want stress-tested is a conciliatory one about peer disagreement, and I have tried to state it in its strong form rather than in a version that is easy to defend. On this view, when I discover that someone I take to be my epistemic peer on a question — equally informed, equally capable, equally conscientious — has arrived at a contrary conclusion, that fact is itself evidence bearing on the question, and the rational response is to lower my confidence substantially, often to something close to suspension. The disagreement, not my first-order evidence, does the work. My private sense that I have simply seen the matter clearly is precisely what is under suspicion, so it cannot be used to clear itself; a view on which I may retain most of my confidence provided I find no independent flaw in my reasoning gives away the point, since the absence of a detectable flaw is exactly what I should expect if I am wrong.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters worked out rather than listed, and I want them ranked from fatal, through seriously damaging, to awkward-but-absorbable. Specifically:

1. Self-application. My view directs me to lose confidence in itself whenever my epistemological peers reject it, and the field is visibly divided. Is that fatal — can anyone who knows the state of the debate rationally hold the view — or does it survive? Say what has to be true for it to survive, and assess the reply that the requirement to suspend comes from the view itself and so remains binding.

2. Peerhood. Is my notion of an epistemic peer doing covert work? If peerhood means "as likely as I am to be right about this question," it looks assessable only by the reasoning whose reliability is in issue. Consider whether any non-question-begging account is available, and if none is, whether that renders the view unfalsifiable, trivial, or neither.

3. Formal results. State in a sentence or two what Aumann-style agreement theorems actually require, how much support they lend conciliationism, and what happens when their assumptions fail. Then consider the Bayesian point that learning another agent's credence is not a self-contained input to conditionalization absent a model of their evidence and reliability, and whether that defeats the idea that the disagreement alone can do the work. Do results about pooling and about the order-dependence of updating on evidence and on opinions bear on whether any single conciliatory rule is even well defined?

4. Findings from outside epistemology. Group polarization, information cascades, the comparative accuracy of independent aggregation versus open deliberation, and forecasting studies: do any of these count against my view, either by suggesting conciliatory updating degrades collective accuracy or by suggesting that its antecedent rarely obtains in practice?

5. Cases. Which concrete cases — real or constructed — would be most damaging: the many-disagreeers case, the peer I can see is wrong, the threat to knowledge on any contested matter, moral and religious disagreement, disagreement about the epistemology of disagreement itself? For each, say whether a conciliator could absorb it by qualification and what that qualification costs.

6. Differential risk. What does my view predict or entail that a total-evidence or steadfast rival does not? If nothing, say so, and address whether the absence of differential predictions is a defect for a normative thesis.

What would move me most is a consequence the view is committed to that I cannot accept, rather than a general charge of circularity — but include the circularity charge too, and tell me where it bites hardest. Throughout, distinguish what a committed conciliator would themselves concede as counting against the view from what they would dismiss out of hand. Conclude with a verdict on whether the position, as stated, is in fact well insulated from refutation, and whether insulation of that kind is a vice in a view of this sort or an unremarkable feature of normative epistemology. Please do not soften the position to make it easier to defend, and do not refer me to the literature; I want the arguments themselves.
