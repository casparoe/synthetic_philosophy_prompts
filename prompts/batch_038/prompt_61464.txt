I'm a third-year PhD student in behavioral econ and I'm supposed to be drafting my quals answer on informational cascades, but I've gotten way more interested in a messier question that the models don't touch: when is it actually OK to go along with what everyone else is doing? The standard story — after the first couple of people, everyone ignores their own private signal and the whole thing locks in and can be dead wrong — makes copying look like a failure mode, but that can't be the whole picture, because half the time I copy people I think I've done nothing wrong. So I started writing down my own verdicts on concrete cases and they will not line up. Here they are, roughly in the order I wrote them.

Standing in a neighborhood I don't know on a Friday night, I eat at the place with the line out front and skip the empty one across the street — fine, obviously. A restaurant pays an agency to have twenty people mill around its door every night for a month, and a tourist joins that line — I want to say the tourist did nothing wrong, but I also want to say something went badly wrong *for him*, and I can't hold both at once. Fire alarm goes off in a building I've never been in: I follow the crowd down the back stairwell even though the exit I came through is closer and I see no smoke myself — fine, maybe required (and note that here the crowd *is* each person reacting to the person ahead of them, which is exactly the dependent chain I want to condemn in the planted-line case, so I'm clearly not being consistent). In an ER, a first-year resident goes along with the attending's diagnosis against her own read of the labs — fine. That same resident goes along with a bare majority of her co-residents against her own read — not fine. I bought the sunscreen with 40,000 five-star reviews over the one dermatologist writeup I'd actually read, and later found out most reviewers had been sent free samples — my choice was fine at the time, but now I feel dumb rather than vindicated, which is odd. I checked three news sites, all three reported the same city council vote the same way, and I counted that as triple confirmation — not fine, and I should have known better even though I didn't in the moment. Every lab in my subfield uses the same preprocessing pipeline; when I finally traced the citations they mostly cite each other in a ring — adopting it was fine for me three years ago, keeping it now that I know would not be. And a hiring committee: I'm the only member who actually interviewed the candidate, I think she's excellent, the other five who just read the file say no, and I cave — I flip-flop on this one constantly and cant say why.

My tentative principle, which my advisor thinks is too cute: copying is rational only when the crowd's behavior is what it would have been had you not shown up — each member reacting to something of thier own rather than to the others or to me. Call it the separate-sources rule; N people in line ought to mean roughly N independent bits rather than one bit viewed N times. It handles the citation ring and the planted line and the three news sites (one wire story, counted thrice). But it gives me no grip at all on attending-versus-co-residents, since in both the crowd moved independently of her; it seems to make the fire-alarm case irrational, which is crazy; and it lets the planted-line tourist off completely, so my feeling that he was somehow wronged goes unaccounted for. So: is there a principle — one, or maybe two or three clauses that actually fit together — that gets all or nearly all of these? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, tell me what it delivers on the two I keep flip-flopping over (planted line, hiring committee), and give me its verdict on two cases I haven't thought of at all. Then tell me straight which of my verdicts it forces me to give up — don't rescue everything with epicycles, and if the honest answer is that a simpler principle plus one dropped verdict beats my messier set, say that instead. Finally, say what would change your mind: which clause you'd bet against, what kind of case would show the principle is wrong rather than merely incomplete, and how much of your confidence comes from thinking my verdicts are just confused to begin with.

Format: do the reasoning in prose however you like, but end with a JSON object using exactly these fields — `principles`: array of objects with `clause_id`, `text`, `motivating_case`; `cases`: array of objects, one per case I listed, with `case`, `my_verdict`, `principle_verdict`, `saved` (true/false), `clause_used`; `forced_to_drop`: array of strings, one per verdict the principle makes me abandon, each with a one-sentence reason; `new_cases`: array of objects with `case`, `predicted_verdict`, `why`; `most_fragile_clause`: string, saying which clause you'd least want to defend and what would break it.
