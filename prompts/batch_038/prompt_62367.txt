I'm a junior, and this past August our county mosquito abatement district announced that it would begin truck-mounted spraying of a broad-spectrum insecticide in three neighborhoods after two locally acquired cases of West Nile virus were confirmed. My household is genuinely divided about it, the public comment hearing went about as well as you'd expect, and I ended up spending the fall reading whatever I could find about whether the insects being killed matter morally. I'm also one of the captains of my school's debate team, and for our first meeting of the year I want to run a demonstration round that shows the new members something our format usually hides: that the interesting disagreements are rarely between two conclusions, and that two people can end up on the same side while believing they have nothing in common. So I'm asking you to write me a dialogue, roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, set in the parking lot of the community center after the hearing has been adjourned, between two people who have both stayed to the end.

The first character — call her A, an invertebrate-welfare advocate who has been doing this for years and is not a crank — holds that even a modest credence in insect sentience, multiplied by the sheer number of individuals a single spraying season kills, produces an expected harm that outweighs almost any human benefit short of a genuine epidemic, and that the usual reply (we simply don't know whether they feel anything) is a reason to act rather than a reason to stand down, since the uncertainty runs in only one direction and the downside is astronomically large. The second character — call him B, a retired county extension agent who is neither a speciesist nor a defender of the district — does not dispute A's arithmetic step by step; he disputes that the arithmetic is the right way to assign moral status at all. On his view, we owe strong but bounded protections to beings whose capacities the evidence actually supports, and a small probability smeared across trillions of individuals is not the kind of consideration that can be permitted to swamp a specific, documented human harm; he thinks A's method, taken seriously, would license equally confident conclusions about organisms nobody wants to defend, which for him is a sign that the method and not the conclusion is what needs examining. He also thinks the district's program is bad policy, because truck spraying has not been shown to reduce human cases more than larviciding and source reduction would, and because it kills the pollinators and the bats and swallows that everyone already agrees are sentient. Give both of them real force: neither should be a strawman, neither should get the last word by default, and every move one makes should be a response to something the other actually said rather than to a position the other doesn't hold.

The structural turn I want — and this is the part I'd like you to treat as the point of the piece rather than as a tidy ending — is that about two-thirds of the way through, they notice that they both want the spraying cancelled and that their reasons are not merely different but mutually hostile: A suspects B of reaching for an ecological argument he doesn't need in order to avoid ever saying whether a mosquito larva counts for anything, and B suspects A of being pleased to have an ally whose reasoning he considers close to monstrous. From there they should argue about what an agreement like that is worth — whether it is a genuine convergence or a truce that will collapse the first time a case arrives where their reasons point in opposite directions, such as a real epidemic in which spraying demonstrably works. Please keep the dialogue free of citations, footnotes, and name-dropping of academic literature; I want the characters arguing from reasons, not from authorities, and I want a smart sixteen-year-old in the audience to follow every step.

After the dialogue, please add a separate paragraph — not a summary of what you wrote — telling me where you think the exchange is weakest and where your own judgment actually sits. Specifically: which of the two positions you find yourself leaning toward by the end and why; what argument, distinction, or piece of evidence would push you to the other one, or would make you think the disagreement is not really substantive at all; and whether you suspect you have handed either character a move that a serious defender of that view would refuse to own. If you think the convergence I've asked you to stage is a cop-out, or that it makes one side look better than an honest treatment would, say so plainly and say what you'd do instead.
