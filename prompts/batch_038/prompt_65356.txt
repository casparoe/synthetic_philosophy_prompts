I've talked myself into defending something I'm not sure I fully believe yet, and I want you to make the strongest honest case for it — then tell me where it cracks.

The position: Harsanyi's aggregation theorem (the 1955 one) has to be read as either trivial or question-begging, and there is no third option.

Setup I have in mind. Take the standard version: individuals have vNM preferences over lotteries with utilities u_i, the social preference over lotteries also satisfies the vNM axioms, and Pareto indifference holds — if everybody is indifferent between two lotteries, so is society. Out comes a list of weights λ_i such that social utility is the weighted sum Σ λ_i u_i, with λ_i positive for anyone who isn't Pareto-irrelevant. Fine, agreed, that's the theorem.

But now: each u_i is only pinned down up to a positive affine transformation, which is a fact about the math, not an interpretive choice. So suppose I double person 1's utility and add 7 to it. The social ranking is completely unchanged so long as I halve λ_1. Which means the weights aren't fixed by anything inside the theorem — they're fixed by the normalization I picked for each person's scale, i.e. by where I put each individual's zero point and unit. And if you hand me n free positive rescalings, I can land on any positive weight vector I want, including equal weights, and including weights under which one person barely counts at all.

So the dilemma. Horn one: if the theorem is just what follows from those axioms plus the affine structure, then it's a representation theorem about a vector space — it says the social ranking is a linear functional over profiles of individual utilities. There is no ethics in it, and selling it as a derivation of utilitarianism is a category mistake, because utilitarianism is a thesis about which scales to use, not about whether a weighted sum exists. Horn two: if the ethics is meant to come in somewhere, it comes in through the fixing of the scales — the impartial observer's identification of extended alternatives with the individuals' own prospects, plus the equal-chance assumption that generates the 1/n lottery. But fixing scales across persons just is interpersonal cardinal comparability. So on horn two the theorem assumes the thing it was advertised as deriving from innocuous premises.

The part I actually want argued: that defenders slide between the horns rather than picking one, and that the slide isn't a cheap gotcha but reflects a genuine ambiguity in what the λ_i are supposed to be. When you press them on where the equal weights come from they retreat to horn one (the weights are simply read off the social preference, they say, there's nothing to justify). When you press them on why anyone should care about a bare representation result they jump to horn two (but the veil tells you which comparison is the correct one, they say). Show that those two roles — weight as something read off a given social preference, and weight as something expressing an interpersonal comparison — make incompatible demands on the utility scales, so you can't occupy both at once.

No references or bibliography please, I've got all that; I want the argument itself. And be straight with me about the downside: say which horn a good defender would escape from and how, name the single most likely point of failure in my reasoning (I suspect it has to do with whether the rescaling freedom is really as unrestriced as I'm claiming), and say what argument or consideration would change your mind about the conclusion rather than just about how I've phrased it.
