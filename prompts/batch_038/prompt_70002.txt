I read a chapter in a general-audience book on the world's moral traditions that took up the relational, ubuntu-flavoured account of personhood, and it summarized the view in a single paragraph that I've been turning over ever since. It runs roughly like this:

'Ubuntu is usually rendered as the claim that a person is a person through other persons. What this means is that personhood is not a biological fact but a social achievement: you are not born a person, you become one by taking up your place in the community and discharging your obligations to it. It follows that personhood comes in degrees and can be forfeited. Someone who has cut themselves off from communal life, or who persistently refuses its obligations, is not a person in the full sense and does not enjoy the same moral protection as the rest of us. The individual as such has no independent standing: where an individual's interests conflict with the good of the community, the community's good prevails, which is why this way of thinking is sometimes said to be incompatible with a liberal morality of rights.'

Two things snagged for me, and I don't have the background to resolve them. The first is that the philosophers most often credited with developing this tradition also seem to be the ones arguing hardest that it can ground human rights and constrain the state — which the paragraph makes almost unintelligible. The second is that if personhood really is something earned through participation, the view looks to me like it has a serious problem about infants, hermits, and people with profound cognitive disabilities; yet the careful defenders I've encountered seem entirely unbothered by that charge, as though the paragraph had built something they wouldn't recognize as theirs. So I can't tell whether I've read a fair summary of a demanding position, or a fair summary of one contested strand dressed up as the whole tradition, or something quietly unfair throughout.
