I write literary fiction, and last spring a quarterly returned one of my stories with a brief covering note: the submission had been put through an automated classifier, the classifier had judged it more likely than not to have been composed by a language model, and the magazine's screening policy was applied uniformly and was not subject to appeal. My reply — that the story was mine, that the notebooks are still on a shelf in my study, that I could send photographs of the drafts — drew a courteous response explaining that the magazine does not adjudicate such claims and makes no accusation against anyone. What has kept me turning the episode over for months, as material rather than as grievance, is a detail about the story itself. The flagged piece is the one in which I had deliberately written flat: a bereaved man's long passage built out of four or five recycled sentence patterns, with the punctuation almost suspended, which I wanted to be tedious in exactly the way grief is tedious. Out of curiosity I fed the story to a free version of the same kind of tool in sections, and the suspicion tracked the austerity almost perfectly — the flat pages scored as machine-made, the two or three baroque descriptive paragraphs in the same story scored as comfortably human. It is as though the flatness of the prose, which was the whole artistic point, became the evidence of my nonexistence.

Around the same time I had been reading about the nineteenth-century connoisseurs who settled disputed attributions by looking at earlobes, fingernails, and the hems of drapery, on the reasoning that a copyist's attention never reaches that far and that these marginal features betray a hand its owner cannot control. I could not shake the sense that the classifier and the connoisseur are doing the same sort of work, and that the magazine is treating that work as a verdict rather than as a guess about a hidden cause from its residue. I am aware that there is a philosophy-of-science vocabulary for reasoning from traces of this kind, and that the detective story is supposedly built upon it, but I do not have the terms, and I would rather not be handed a label without the analysis that makes it mean something. I should add that I am currently drafting a novel whose engine is a disputed manuscript, which is perhaps why this has become a private laboratory for me instead of a merely annoying afternoon.

So I would like you to reconstruct what the magazine's policy presupposes, and to be as exact as you can about the difference between what it strictly requires and what merely sits well with it. First: what sort of inference is the policy licensing, from what to what, and what would have to be true of prose — and of the relation between prose and the person who produced it — for that inference to work even in principle? It seems to assume that a writer's hand leaves a stable signature in features the writer does not choose, that this signature is a property of the individual rather than of the genre or the period or the sentence-level task, and that a pattern in commas can settle a question about a causal history; say which of these are separate commitments and which collapse into one another. Second: which rival ways of thinking about evidence does the policy quietly rule out? I have in mind, for instance, a view on which the only sane question is how probable machine authorship is given how much machine-made text is now arriving in the slush pile at all, and a view on which the author's own report of how the story was made is simply the best evidence available about how it was made, since she was there. Which of these is the policy committed against, and which does it merely fail to consider? Third: is the policy internally coherent? The same magazine accepts my word without question that the story is unpublished and unwritten for hire, insists that the flag imputes nothing to me personally, describes the tool as a neutral screen, and yet treats the output as final in a way that no amount of testimony from the person with the most direct access to the facts can unsettle. Tell me which of those can survive together and which cannot, and whether the practice has in effect promoted a best-available explanation into something treated as beyond defeat. Fourth: the policy divides the world into texts with a human origin and texts without one, but my own working life is full of cases that straddle it — a story dictated into speech-to-text with a grammar autocompleter whispering suggestions, a paragraph drafted by a model and then rebuilt word by word until nothing of the draft remains, a translation touched up by software. What does the policy presuppose about whether there is a fact of the matter in such cases, and is that presupposition defensible or merely convenient? Finally, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several quite different theories of evidence would license exactly the same policy — and what would actually have to change, in the magazine's procedures or in its picture of what an author is, if it adopted a view on which a text can be partly machine-made with no bright line anywhere in it. I am far less interested in whether the classifier was right about my story, which it was not, than in what has to be true for an institution devoted to language to think that the question could be settled that way at all.
