Our reading group just finished Acemoglu and Robinson, and the friend who organized it — call him R — has been running the argument hard in the group chat ever since, mostly by way of North's old metaphor that institutions are the "rules of the game." The way R deploys it is doing a lot of work. Geography is just the board the game happens to be played on; culture is the local style of play, which adapts to the rules rather than the reverse; but the rules are what actually determine payoffs, and — this is the punchline he keeps returning to — the rules are the one part of the setup somebody could sit down and rewrite on purpose. So if a country stays poor, that's a political failure with a political fix, not a mystery about latitude or values, and anyone who says "you can't just transplant institutions" is treating the rulebook like the weather. He also leans on the book's opening move, the two Nogales seperated by a fence, and calls it a natural experiment: same board, same players' ancestry, same climate, different rulebook, different outcome, done.

My instinct is that the metaphor is a motte-and-bailey machine, and I want to check that instinct before I say so out loud. The motte is trivially true — of course the rules matter, they're the rules! — and the bailey is that the rules are an exogenous lever you can pull. Several things feel off to me but I can't articulate them cleanly. In an actual game, who writes the rules? Someone standing outside the game, who isn't playing for stakes. Who enforces them? A referee who is also outside the play, and whose authority nobody in the game has to consent to. Can the players rewrite the rules mid-match to their own advantage? No — that's not playing, that's ending the game. But in a society the players write the rules, enforce them, and are the ones who benefit from them being what they are, which makes the rules look less like a lever and more like an equilibrium, which is precisely what R needs them not to be. Also: does casting geography as "the board" and culture as "style of play" already decide the debate before any evidence arrives, by assigning the three candidates their roles inside one metaphor? And the rulebook of chess is complete, public, and written down, whereas most of what institutionalists mean by institutions is informal norms nobody authored and nobody can point at — so I suspect the clarity of the source is being quietly imported into the target.

Can you audit this properly? I don't want citations or a reading list — just the reasoning, and flag anywhere you think I'm strawmanning North, since I gather he cared a lot about informal constraints and might have been more careful than R is. Structure it like this:

- **Bottom line** — 3–5 sentences: does the rules-of-the-game framing actually support institutionalism as a causal claim and a program, or is it just good vocabulary?
- **The mapping** — a table. Source element on the left (rulebook, the designer who wrote it, the referee, the board and terrain, the players, the moves, the score, changing the rules between seasons, house rules vs. official rules), what it's supposed to correspond to in the middle, and on the right whether the correspondence is tight, loose, or empty.
- **What it earns** — ~250 words on the legitimate work the metaphor does, including why it was an upgrade on the vocabularies it replaced.
- **Where it breaks** — ~500 words, and please rank them: which disanalogies are fatal to the use R makes of it versus which are just the tolerable imprecision of any metaphor.
- **What it smuggles in** — ~250 words on features of games the target lacks that are doing covert work.
- **The petri dish** — ~200 words auditing the second metaphor: in the twin-Nogales comparison, what exactly is the treatment, what's the control, and what plays the role of randomization. Say whether "nothing does" sinks the inference or just discounts it.
- **A better analogy** — ~250 words: if you were making the same argument without games, what comparison would you reach for, and what does your replacement make harder to say?
- **Verdict on the conclusion** — ~200 words: does "fix the rules and the country gets rich" survive, even in some weakened or conditional form?
