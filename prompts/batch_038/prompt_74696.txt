So here's the situation, kind of personal, and I'm not writing a paper — I just need to think this through and my own brain keeps going in circles.

I volunteer as the archivist for a small county historical society. Four of us, we digitize stuff and answer emails from people doing genealogy. Two months ago we found out that one of our most-quoted oral history sources — a woman who gave us maybe fourteen hours of tape about the 1911 mill fire and the company town that grew up around the mill — was six years old and living two states away when it happened. A lot of what she told us was invention, or stuff she'd soaked up from her mother and then repeated for sixty years as first-hand memory. Half our local history page has to come down.

Now the board wants an actual written intake rule: when does a claim get into the archive as attested, and when does it sit in a holding folder until somebody finds independent support? I've been reading around this and I think the two camps are the reductionists (Hume-ish: you need positive reasons, grounded in something other than testimony, before you're entitled to believe a witness) and the anti-reductionists (Reid-ish: testimony is a basic source, you're entitled to accept unless you have a reason not to). Flag it if I've got either label wrong.

What I want from you: you're an expert on the epistemology of testimony, think like a working academic philosopher in that subfield who has actually sat on committees. Before you draft anything, do a short brainstorming section — what's the strongest case each side can make, and what's the one problem each side genuinely can't wiggle out of? Also restate my intake-rule problem in one sentence so I know you've got the target. Then write the dialogue.

The two speakers are my two fellow board members:

- Ruth, retired teacher, the anti-reductionist. Let her use whatever resources her tradition actually has, including the ones I don't know the names of.
- Malik, ex-audit guy who now does data quality work, the reductionist.

Set it as the two of them alone in the archive after hours, tape boxes on the table between them, trying to settle the wording before Thursday's meeting.

Rules for how it should go:

- Neither one is dumb and neither is a strawman. Smartest version of each view, and both land real hits.
- Genuine back-and-forth: each responds to what the other just said rather than delivering alternating lectures. I want to see them revise mid-conversation.
- Structure: each of them proposes a one-sentence candidate rule for the guidelines, the other breaks it with a specific case, they try again. Two full rounds at least.

Work in our hard case, which is the whole reason I'm bothering. The county courthouse records burned in 1948, so for the mill fire there is no independent support available and there never will be. Everything anyone will ever know about it came in through somebody's mouth, and much of it came in through chains — our tape woman heard things from her mother, who heard them from neighbors who were there. So there's two seperate problems. When Malik demands positive reasons that aren't themselves testimony, I don't see how he can be answered for that event. When Ruth says accept-unless-defeated, I don't see how her rule could ever have caught our forger in advance, because at the time there was nothing to catch. I want them to fight about exactly that, not around it.

Also push into whether the number of people saying something counts for anything when they all trace back to one mouth, and whether a rule for an institution can legitimately differ from a rule for an individual believer — that last one is where I'm actually stuck, because I'm not trying to fix my own beliefs, I'm writing policy for a collection other people will trust.

No bibliography, no citations, no reading list at the end. Philosopher names can come up naturally in their mouths, but I can't chase down sources and this isn't for a class. Roughly 1200–1500 words of dialogue. Don't have either of them convert. End with what they actually agreed to put in the guidelines, what they had to bracket, and a couple sentences each on what evidence would change their mind.

Think carefully about the mill-fire case before you start writing, and if one side gets to wave it away with a technicality, I've learned nothing from this.
