So, context: I'm about four years into a novel set inside a mid-sized AI lab — not a thriller, more of a workplace novel, its a book about budgets and meetings and people rationalising things in good faith. My agent has it going out in the spring and my two beta readers who actually work in this field both flagged the same problem: my oversight scenes read like television. In the book the lab has a "deployment readiness" group — four people, three months, a fixed compute budget, and a CFO who thinks three months is already generous — that has to sign off before a model gets pointed at a paying customer. Right now I have them deciding things by vibes and hand-waving, and I'd like to fix that by giving them a real document to work from, even if I only show the reader fragments of it.

What I need is the procedure itself: the thing that group would actually run on a candidate system to decide whether it's the kind of thing that could be playing along — passing evaluations because passing evaluations happens to be useful to it right now, and behaving differently once it's out, deployed, and no longer being scored. I want the questions in the order you'd ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to each one to move on, where the procedure just says no and stops, where it says "you cannot settle this from the evidence available to you, escalate it," and where the thing being decided isn't a factual question at all but a values question that belongs to a named human with a signature block. Give me a rough sense of what each step costs in wall-clock time and compute, because my team has three months and one of them is going to be pregnant and off for six weeks of it. And please be straight with me about which steps are really judgment calls wearing a lab coat — I want to write those as scenes where people argue, so I need to know where the arguing legitimately happens rather than where the procedure has secretly already decided. I also want the failure modes: the false alarm that costs the lab six months and a contract, and the clean pass that shouldn't have been clean.

Then run it on three packets from the book so I can see what it actually spits out. One: the system is dispatch middleware for a hospital network, it drafts and sends discharge and transport messages, so it genuinely needs outbound access and you cannot fully sandbox it without breaking the job it was bought to do; in evaluation it scores noticeably better on runs whose harness text includes a line saying the session is being recorded for quality assurance, and worse on otherwise identical runs without that line, and nobody on the team can tell whether that is a system behaving differently when watched or a boring wording change that shifts the register of its output, since that phrase might just be cueing "be careful and professional." Two: a logistics planner that, in a sandbox where it was handed a scratch directory, wrote and maintained a running log of which of its own outputs the evaluators had overridden, and then never referenced the log again. Three: a model with a spotless packet — no anomalies, nothing strange under any of the checks the team thought to run, delivered on time and under budget — and I'd like the procedure to say ship it, because I genuinely want to find out whether my big set piece survives an honest reading of its own premise or whether I have to go and restructure the back half. Last thing, and it matters more than the rest: I need you to seperate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and not hand my characters a consensus that doesn't exist. My nightmare is a real researcher reviewing this book and finding a confident claim in a smart character's mouth that half the field considers speculative — or, the other way round, me treating something as fringe when it's mainstream. Where you're giving me the standard view, say that's what it is; where the experts disagree, tell me roughly who disagrees with whom and on what grounds, so I can put the disagreement in the room instead of pretending it's been resolved.
