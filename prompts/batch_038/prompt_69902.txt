Students wrote by hand, so please make whatever allowance you think is appropriate for that. Here is the question this candidate chose, worth 25 marks:

Libet's readiness-potential studies are often said to show that conscious intention plays no causal part in the production of voluntary action. Libet himself resisted that reading, holding that a capacity to veto what is already underway survives his results. Assess this disagreement. In your answer, say precisely what the readiness potential does and does not license as evidence about the timing of intention, and say whether an appeal to vetoing can restore a notion of free agency worth the name. (1,200 words; 25 marks.)

The convenor's banding, for reference: 20–25 excellent, 15–19 good, 11–14 satisfactory, below 11 inadequate. And here is the script in full.

Script 1047. Libet asked subjects to flex a wrist whenever they felt like it while watching a clock dot sweeping round a dial, and to report the dial position at which they first became aware of the urge to move, with EEG recording the readiness potential throughout. He found that the readiness potential begins about 550 ms before the muscle movement, whereas subjects report awareness of the urge only about 200 ms before it. The brain process that sets the movement going is therefore already well underway before the subject experiences anything like an intention. That appears to show that conscious intention does not cause the movement: preparation is unconscious, and the conscious urge is a latecomer that merely accompanies it.

It would be too quick, though, to conclude that free will is finished. Libet himself noted that even where the urge arises unconsciously the subject can still veto the movement once the urge appears, and he measured this veto window at roughly the final 100 to 200 ms before the movement. That gives us free won't rather than free will, but it is enough to preserve moral responsibility, since what we hold people responsible for is whether they let an action go ahead.

Critics have tried to blunt the result in other directions. Some argue that introspective timing is unreliable, because subjects must translate a felt urge into a dial position and that translation may be systematically late, in which case the gap between readiness potential and awareness is partly an artefact of the reporting method. Schurger and colleagues go further: the readiness potential is not a decision signal at all but the visible accumulation of subthreshold neural noise, which looks like a smooth ramp only because trials are averaged backwards from movement onset, so there is no discrete moment of unconscious decision to locate. And Brass and Haggard's imaging work on deliberately withheld movements identified a distinct frontal signal for vetoing, which suggests vetoing is a real neural process rather than a philosophical consolation prize.

None of that rescues the veto, however. For the veto to save free will it would have to be a conscious act that is not itself the upshot of prior brain events, and Brass and Haggard's finding is exactly that vetoing has its own neural signature and its own timing. If vetoing is a brain process like any other, the regress simply starts again: the veto is initiated unconsciously, and we are back where we began. Libet's caveat therefore fails, and the honest conclusion is that conscious willing is at best a spectator, an epiphenomenon of processes doing the real work.

One might object that the experiment involves only trivial, spontaneous wrist flexions rather than deliberated choices. That objection does not help the defender of free will, because if the trivial case already shows unconscious initiation there is no reason to suppose the deliberated case differs.
