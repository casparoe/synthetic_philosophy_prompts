I am three hundred pages into a third novel and have run into a structural problem that my workshop has been trying to solve for me with a piece of philosophy, which I would like audited by someone who knows the philosophy properly. The book concerns a deceased seismologist — call her the subject — whose life is reconstructed by three narrators who never agree: an estranged son, a former doctoral student, and the trustee administering her estate through a decade of litigation. She never speaks in her own voice, and there is one act at the center of the book, her destruction of forty years of field notebooks, which all three describe and none of them can motivate. In my working notes, and currently in the manuscript's epigraph, I have been describing her as a center of narrative gravity, following the analogy Dennett develops in his essay The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity: just as a physicist posits a center of gravity for a body — not one of the body's material parts, an idealization, a fiction belonging to the theorist rather than an item in the world's inventory, but one that earns its keep by letting you predict how the body will behave — so, I have been saying, a reader posits a person as the point around which narrated events organize.

At a residency in the spring a fellow novelist seized on this epigraph and drew two conclusions for me. First, that I owe my readers no interior consistency at all: since a character is nothing over and above a posit of that kind, a reviewer who calls the subject inconsistent or unmotivated is making a category mistake, exactly like someone who complains that a center of gravity is not made of atoms. Second — this one I have been resisting — that I am therefore free to leave the notebook-burning permanently unexplained, and that the three narrators' incompatible portraits are all equally warranted, since one and the same posit can be reached from quite different bodies of evidence. My editor likes the epigraph and wants to keep it; I would like to know whether it can carry the weight these two conclusions hang on it, and I would rather hear that it cannot from you now than from a reviewer in print.

Please work as an analytic philosopher of mind who reads fiction seriously and who has thought about how craft talk borrows from philosophy of science: begin by working out, privately and at length, the full correspondence and every point at which it fails, and if part of what is doing the work here is a misreading of Dennett rather than a weakness in the analogy itself, treat that as one of the failures. Then give me your findings and nothing else — exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction, no summary, and no closing remarks. The slots, in order: (1) the correspondences that actually bear weight, naming what plays the mass distribution, what plays the equations of motion, and what plays the idealizing step; (2) the disanalogies that matter for my fellow's two conclusions; (3) the disanalogies that are genuine but do no damage to those conclusions; (4) what the physics picture quietly imports into fiction that fiction lacks, including anything about determinacy, uniqueness, and whether the posit can be refined against an underlying distribution the way a center of gravity can; (5) whether each conclusion survives, whether the epigraph survives with them, and if the analogy has to go, what I should replace it with.
