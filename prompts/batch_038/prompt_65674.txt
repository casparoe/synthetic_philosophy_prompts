You are a philosopher of game theory with serious expertise in the logic of cooperation between computational agents: fixed points and self-reference, the limits of bounded reasoning, and what happens to classical solution concepts when players choose programs rather than actions. Think carefully and do the analysis before you draft anything.

**Situation.** I produce a small but technically serious podcast. Next week's guest is a researcher who defends a particular cluster of claims about program equilibrium — the framework in which each player in a game submits a program, each program is handed the other players' source code as input and returns an action, and a profile counts as an equilibrium when no player can do better by unilaterally swapping in a different program. The guest's preferred mechanism is *simulation*: your program runs the opponent's program on some input and copies whatever it returns. Treat the guest as an unnamed archetype of this position; do not attribute any of the following to a real, identifiable person.

Here are the six commitments the guest has stated publicly, in their own framing. Take them as given:

1. **Equality-testing is brittle.** Older constructions cooperate only when the opponent's code matches a blessed exemplar, so trivial reimplementation breaks them. The guest's programs cooperate on the basis of what the opponent's code *does*: implementations that behave alike are treated alike.
2. **Proof-searching is impractical.** Programs that cooperate only upon finding a proof that the opponent cooperates do achieve mutual cooperation in the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, but only by assuming unbounded logical resources. Simulation is the workable substitute.
3. **Simulation is unexploitable.** A simulation-based cooperator never cooperates against an opponent that would defect against it. Therefore making your own code legible carries no strategic downside.
4. **Transparency scales.** The same construction extends past two players and to games with private information, in which agents conditionally disclose information to one another.
5. **Markets select for legibility.** Opaque agents get shut out of valuable deals, so there is competitive pressure to publish source code, ideally carrying the signature of an auditing program that has verified the published code is the code that will actually run.
6. **It really is an equilibrium.** Immunity to unilateral program swaps is the correct analogue of Nash. No new solution concept is required, and folk-theorem-style results carry over.

**Task.** First, in a short preamble of about 150 words: rank the six commitments by how much pressure each can absorb before it gives way; identify the two pairs that are in direct tension with one another; and flag anything above that I have mis-stated or that cannot hold as written. Then draft nine to eleven interview questions, ordered from the most accessible to the hardest, each formatted as:

- the question itself, no more than three sentences and readable aloud on air;
- a bracketed line naming which commitment it targets and what a good answer would have to establish;
- the reply the guest is most likely to give, in one or two sentences;
- a follow-up that is usable *only* if that reply is given, and that closes off the escape route it opens.

At least one question must address each of the following. (a) The regress problem: what exactly is fed to the simulated opponent as *its* input — your own source code, which risks mutual recursion, or a stand-in, which gives away the point that an opponent's behaviour may depend on which input it is looking at — and whether "behaves alike, therefore treated alike" survives that choice. (b) Mismatched computational budgets: both programs simulate each other and each defaults to defection if the simulation fails to return in time, so a fast agent paired with a sincere but slow cooperative agent lands in mutual defection that is invisible from the inside; who can exploit that, and what the default should be. (c) Whether unexploitability is worth the name, given opponents that simply punish cooperators (so the best a transparent agent gets against a bully is the mutual defection it would have got anyway) and given an opponent that receives your code and therefore knows precisely when it is being simulated. (d) The move to three or more players: who simulates whom, in what order, on what inputs, and whether the agents need access to a shared source of randomness merely to settle on the same equilibrium. (e) The gap between published and executed code, and what becomes of the solution concept if there is a small probability of a decoy — including who audits the auditor. (f) Whether "program equilibrium" earns its name: does best-response reasoning still apply when a unilateral change alters not only your action but what others can compute about you.

The final question must be one the guest cannot discharge by restating the framework. Make it about what observable behaviour would distinguish program-equilibrium cooperation from ordinary repeated-game reciprocity, from reputation effects, or from mere prediction of the other's action, and then state in two sentences what a satisfying answer contains and what the evasive version sounds like.

**Constraints.** No question may ask the guest to cite, name, or recommend a paper, book, or researcher; the guest is on a microphone with no whiteboard and no search access, so everything must be answerable by reasoning from commitments 1–6 plus the scenarios you supply. No career, motivation, or "how did you get into this" questions. No question that could be answered adequately by repeating one of the six commitments back to me. Total length: 1,200 to 1,600 words.
