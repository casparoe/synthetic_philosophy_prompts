I am a high school junior, and for an independent study I have been teaching myself to draw letters: brush pens first, then a clumsy attempt at a small typeface of my own. The more I do it, the less I understand a question I had assumed was trivial — what makes every "a" an "a"? I have read a little about the Letter Spirit project, the attempt to get a computer to draw new letters in the spirit of an existing typeface, and I found it less useful than my own sketchbook, so I would rather work from my verdicts on particular cases. They are as follows.

1. A single-story "a" (a closed bowl with a stem, as in Futura or in most handwriting) and a double-story "a" (as in Times) are unquestionably the same letter; the difference is style, not identity.
2. Take a double-story "a," cut the bowl open, and remove the little ear — the stencil treatment. Still an "a," instantly.
3. Take the same "a," keep every stroke, but let the upper arc curl around until it joins the bowl, so the whole form becomes a closed ring on a stem. Now I want to say it is an "o" with a decoration and not an "a" at all. Cases 2 and 3 are what trouble me: the version that loses material survives, and the version that keeps it does not.
4. A mirror-reversed "a" has exactly the same strokes. I still see an "a," but a defective one, of the kind a young child produces. It is an "a" and it is also wrong.
5. A blackletter "a" that I cannot read at all, though a reader trained in that script can. I want to say it is still an "a," yet then being an "a" is not a fact about the shape and me alone; it seems to involve other readers.
6. A Greek alpha resembles my handwritten single-story "a" and is nevertheless not the letter "a," and "@" descends from "a" without being one. So the same shape can be a different letter depending on which alphabet recruits it.

My tentative principle was that a shape is an "a" just in case it has a bowl attached to a stem on its right, closed or open. But that admits case 3 and excludes plenty of stencil and script forms I would call "a"s without hesitation.

Is there a single principle, or a small set of them each tied to the case that motivates it, that reproduces all six verdicts? Please state it precisely, say which clause does the work in each case, and tell me what it predicts for two cases I have not settled: a display face in which the "a" and the "e" are deliberately made nearly identical as a design joke, and a word read quickly in which a smudge could be either an "a" or an "o" and the neighboring letters seem to decide. Then say which of my verdicts your principle must overturn, if any, and — this matters most to me — what would change your own mind: which argument or consideration would push you toward a different account, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
