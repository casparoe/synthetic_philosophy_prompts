I'm writing a novel whose narrator is a European museum registrar, and to make her convincing I've had to work out what I actually believe about giving things back — turns out I don't have one view, I have about six that don't fit together. Here they are, roughly in the order they hit me:

1. The pieces British forces took out of the Benin palace in 1897: back, no strings, no conditions attached. If my registrar wants a caveat here I don't want to write her.
2. The Parthenon sculptures: back to Athens — but notice I reach for a different reason there, that the building is still standing and the carvings belong to it. The "they complete something" line is doing work I'm not sure it can do anywhere else.
3. A plaque bought in good faith off a dealer in the 1930s by a small museum, no violence anywhere near that particular transaction: still goes back. This one surprizes me.
4. Something a chief genuinely gifted a missionary, documented, no coercion I can find: stays. Though I keep worrying that "no coercion in a colony" its a fantasy, which would swallow 4 into 1.
5. Human remains and funerary objects: back, and not into a museum at all — burial, or the family.
6. A piece that survived only because the holding museum conserved it, while the temple it came from was wrecked in a later war: stays. I know this fights with 3.
7. Where the claiming government would lock it in a presidential palace and bar scholars: return anyway. But in one draft my registrar offers a "long-term loan" instead and I can't tell if she's being sensible or being a paternalist, and I want the principle to settle that rather than my gut.
8. An object carved specifically for the export market in the eighteenth century and sold willingly: stays.

Is there a principle under all this, or a small set of them? State it clause by clause and tell me which of my cases forces each clause, then say plainly which verdicts have to be sacrificed to keep the rest — I'd rather lose 6 or 8 than lose 1. And whether "long-term loan" can ever be a principled answer or is always a polite dodge.

Then run it on three I haven't decided. An object looted before Europeans got involved, by a neighbouring kingdom rather than a colonial power — does the principle still fire? A private collector in Benin City who buys a looted piece at a London auction to bring it home but won't hand it to the state or to the palace? And a piece whose meaning has changed after a century and a half of being looked at as art in the West — does that history generate any claim of its own, or is it just an excuse my registrar would like to believe?
