Slightly unusual request. I teach philosophy of social science and next month I'm giving a 40 minute talk at a history department's methods seminar — as in, their actual methods seminar, which is basically the quants. The room will be economic historians who live in regressions on parish registers, tax rolls and probate inventories; two historical sociologists who think in comparative case designs and Mill's methods; one digital humanist who text-mines big corpora of trial records; and a couple of early modernists who are friendly to me personally but will defend their sources to the death. I'm the only philosopher there, and somebody in the department has already billed me as doing "philosophy of science," so half the room is braced for a lecture about laws and explanation.

The talk defends microhistory — the history-from-below tradition that takes one village, one trial, one miller — against the charge that it's dressed-up anecdote. I don't want the standard defense. My thesis is roughly these four claims:

(1) Shrinking the scale isn't taking a small sample, it's swapping instruments. Aggregation destroys certain kinds of information as a matter of structure and not of effort, so the close-up case is a measurement device for connections and mechanisms that are literally not in the macro data: who lent the miller his books, how a village relayed an idea, what a household actually did with a doctrine it half understood.

(2) So the general claim it licenses isn't frequentive. It's modal and structural: this arrangement was possible, here is the mechanism that produced it, and — the part that will annoy them — your macro categories ("peasant religion," "the village community," "the people," "class") don't carve the joints. Frequencies have to come from somewhere else, and I say that out loud rather than pretend.

(3) The anomaly is selected on purpose. I choose the case because it doesn't fit the categories, and the mismatch is evidence about the categories rather than noise about the case. That's the part I know is exposed.

(4) The archive is mediated — inquisitors' leading questions, a notary's Latin, a defendant talking for his life — and I argue the mediation is not a contaminant to be discounted away but the object under study: the transcript records a negotiation, and reading it for what the filter did is itself a generalizable result.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the eight or so objections this room will actually raise — not the ones I'd get from other philosophers, and not the ones I'd get from literary people — ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it. Phrase each one the way that audience phrases things, in first person and in their vocabulary (n, selection, identification, controls, external validity, ecological inference, coding, hindsight, cherry-picking), so I can hear the question before it's asked. For each: a reply I could genuinely say out loud in about two minutes, and then a blunt note on where the reply is soft — the sentence after which a good quant gets to say "so you're conceding that...". Then tell me: what I should concede in the first sixty seconds to take the wind out of the room; which single reply has to land or the talk fails; the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss; and the question I'm praying nobody asks, along with the honest answer to it.

Two constraints. Assume nobody in the room has read the case studies I'll be using, so an objection that only works if they know that literature doesnt count. And no bibliography, no reading lists, no titles — I know the literature, spend the words on the arguments instead. Don't be gentle with me; these people won't be.
