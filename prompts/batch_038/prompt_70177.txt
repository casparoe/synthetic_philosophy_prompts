I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and over the past several years I have found myself reporting on the same structural problem without having a name for it. I would like to know whether one exists, and if not, what the thing should be called.

The pattern is this. A technical term leaves the discipline that produced it and travels outward by a chain of small, individually defensible analogies. Each new user adopts the word because it fits their case roughly the way it fit the case they borrowed it from, and each extension is reasonable when judged only against its immediate predecessor. But the chain has no shared core: the usage at the far end has nothing in common with the usage at the near end except a route of pairwise resemblances running through the middle. Two people can then use the same word in the same sentence, cite the same founding literature, and be asserting incompatible things.

Two cases I have covered. First, resilience, which begins in materials work as the capacity to absorb energy and return to an original shape; becomes in ecology the capacity of a system to absorb disturbance and retain its function and organization rather than return to a prior state — already close to the opposite of the first, since one is about coming back and the other about persisting precisely by not coming back; becomes social-ecological resilience, then community resilience in disaster preparedness, then resilience as a property of individuals in psychology, and finally a word applied to supply chains, power grids, and, in a municipal arts funding document I read last year, to the morale of a theatre company. Second, trauma, which moves from physical injury, to an overwhelming psychological event, to cumulative childhood adversity tallied on a checklist, to harm carried across generations within a population, to the effects of a news cycle or a contentious school board meeting. I want to stress that I am not alleging fraud at any step: the intermediate usages are real, productive, and used by serious people.

What turns this into a problem rather than ordinary semantic change is that institutions are keyed to the word. A single funding program, a single index, a single statutory definition, or a single dataset running across thirty years treats every link in the chain as one quantity. So a comparison of resilience in 1995 with resilience in 2024 may be a comparison of incompatible properties under one label, and no one can say in which year the change occurred, because it never occurred all at once.

A biologist I interviewed said the structure reminds him of what is supposed to happen in a ring species: populations arrayed around a barrier, each able to interbreed with the next, while the two ends of the ring cannot interbreed at all — non-transitivity of sameness-of-kind with no boundary you can point to. The standard textbook case is a salamander whose populations ring California's Central Valley. I have also been told by other biologists that genuine cases are rarer and messier than textbooks imply, so I do not know how far the analogy travels; but the shape is what I keep meeting in concepts rather than in organisms.

My editor's response is that this is simply family resemblance, that all concepts work this way, and that I am demanding a precision ordinary language never had. That may be right, but I want it argued rather than announced, and in particular I want to know whether the family-resemblance picture actually licenses a serial chain of this kind or whether it presupposes something denser — cross-links binding distant usages to one another, not merely a route through adjacent ones.

So, specifically: is there an established term, in philosophy of language, linguistics, science and technology studies, or the methodology of the social sciences, for drift by adjacency under a single load-bearing label? Where the nearest candidates fall short, name them and say exactly how — I can think of the general idea of a concept widening over time, and of the error of assuming one label picks out one thing, but neither seems to capture the stepwise, non-transitive structure or its consequences for measurement. If nothing fits, coin a term, define it, and mark its edges: what would count as a benign extension rather than this; whether the phenomenon has a determinate starting point in the chain or none at all; and whether it can arise inside a single discipline or only at disciplinary boundaries. Finally, tell me whether there is a diagnostic a reporter could actually apply — something checkable in a document, an interview, or a time series — that separates a healthy family-resemblance concept from a chain of this kind. A label by itself will not let me write the piece.
