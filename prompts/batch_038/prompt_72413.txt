Next month I present a constitutional design proposal to a state revision commission, and I want to be ready for the question period rather than surprised by it. The proposal keeps the citizen initiative but adds a double majority: passage requires both a statewide popular majority and a majority of votes in a majority of legislative districts, on the model of Switzerland and Australia. The novel part is what happens to a measure that wins the popular vote but fails the district majority — it is not defeated; instead it triggers a randomly selected citizens' jury, which hears expert testimony and must within a year either enact a modified version of the measure or let it lapse. My argument is that the second majority buys acceptance from geographically concentrated losers, and the jury converts a defeat into a deliberative second chance rather than a grievance.

The commission is four election lawyers, two quantitative political scientists who work on redistricting and voter behavior, two county party chairs, and a retired state supreme court justice; none are political theorists, and several have spent their careers either in ballot-access litigation or on the assumption that a majority is a majority.

Give me the eight or so objections this particular group will raise — not the textbook critique of direct democracy — ordered by the damage each would do to the proposal, each phrased the way its most likely raiser would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is thinnest. Then tell me what I should concede in the opening minutes so it cannot be turned against me later, which single reply has to land for the design to survive, and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
