A captured soldier in an international armed conflict who wore a distinctive sign, carried his arms openly, and observed the rules on targeting cannot be tried for the killings themselves: he receives prisoner-of-war status and immunity for lawful acts of war. The same body of law makes aggressive war a crime, but confines that crime to people positioned to direct a state's political or military action, so that a conscript cannot commit it no matter how many enemy soldiers he kills. The same law denies that conscript a superior-orders defense when the order before him was manifestly unlawful, treating him as an agent who must judge the act he has been told to perform. And it withholds combatant status entirely from a fighter who joins for pay and belongs to no state's cause.

What picture of agency, permission, and justification does this architecture presuppose? I want to know whether the immunity commits the law to the view that soldiers on both sides of a war have an equal right to kill, or whether it is equally consistent with the view that only those fighting a just cause are permitted to kill and that the immunity is a pragmatic concession that asserts nothing about morality; whether the deeper presupposition is instead something about the state as the collective agent whose deeds are not the individual soldier's own; and whether that last reading can be squared with the refusal of a superior-orders defense, which seems to hand moral judgment back to the individual after all. Please separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits well with, mark the points at which rival theories would license it equally, and say what would have to change in the law if we replaced the presupposed view with its revisionist rival — including anything that would thereby become unadministrable, and what the treatment of mercenaries indicates about which replacement is actually on offer. Close by stating how confident you are in your main claims and singling out the one point you are least sure of.
