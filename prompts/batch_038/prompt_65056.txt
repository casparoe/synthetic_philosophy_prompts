Construct a matched pair of cases to be used in a seminar on the ethics of deception, designed to test the standard asymmetry between lying and merely misleading — the thesis that, other things equal, lying is worse than inducing a false belief by way of something one believes to be true. The usual support for the asymmetry is that a hearer invited to draw an implicature is on notice and can protect herself, whereas a hearer addressed with an assertion is entitled to rely on it.

In Case L the speaker plainly lies. In Case M the speaker says nothing she believes to be false and deceives wholly by conversational implicature. Design requirements:

1. Case M's speaker must come out more blameworthy than Case L's, and the gap may not come from the consequences (the hearer's resulting belief, decision, and loss are to be matched), from a difference in the speakers' motives or stakes, from any additional falsehood uttered in Case M, or from Case L's speaker having a special excuse.
2. Both cases must be realistic and institutional — a hiring process, a warranty claim, an audit, a rental application — with no emergencies, threats, or matters of life and death.
3. Each case is one paragraph of at most 120 words, and the utterance at issue should be quotable in a sentence.

Then demonstrate that the design works. State what each of three accounts of lying says about Case M — one on which lying requires an intention to deceive, one on which lying requires asserting what one believes to be false, and one on which lying requires putting forward a claim one does not believe is warranted — arranging the case so that these accounts do not all deliver the same verdict, and saying for each whether Case M is a lie, a merely misleading utterance, or something the account cannot classify. Identify the single structural feature of Case M that generates the extra blame, explain why the same feature is unavailable to the liar in Case L, and show that it defeats the on-notice rationale rather than sidestepping it. Close with the strongest reply a defender of the asymmetry could make without simply accepting your verdict, and say what remains of the case once the feature doing the work is removed.

The reversal has to be generated by the structure of the cases and the accounts applied to them. A pair in which the reader simply agrees that Case M's speaker is a worse person, for reasons independent of how she communicates, fails the brief.
