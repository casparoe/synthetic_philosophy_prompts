I co-teach a graduate seminar in medieval Jewish philosophy with a colleague from Jewish studies, and three years running the session on Maimonides' treatment of the sacrificial cult has ended in exactly the same place: nobody has moved, and both sides claim the same texts. The materials are familiar to everyone in the room. In the third part of the Guide, in the long chapter devoted to the reasons for the commandments, Maimonides argues that the sacrificial system is best explained historically: the Israelites had been formed in Egypt and in a world in which worship simply meant slaughter and offering, and asking them to serve God by inward devotion alone would have been as impossible as asking a pious person today to accept that prayer be abolished and replaced by silent contemplation; the Torah therefore kept the form and redirected it, confining sacrifice to the Temple and the priesthood while making prayer obligatory on everyone, everywhere. Then there is the Mishneh Torah, which devotes an entire book to the sacrificial order and another to the Temple's structure and vessels down to their measurements, and which states in the Laws of Kings that the messianic king will rebuild the sanctuary and that in his days the observance of all the statutes will return to its previous state, with sacrifices offered again and the sabbatical and jubilee years kept as the Torah describes them.

Two camps form, and they never converge. One holds that the Guide gives the real view and the code reflects a professional obligation: sacrifice has no enduring religious value, the messianic clause reports what the halakhic tradition requires rather than what Maimonides thinks God ultimately wants, and the whole apparatus is best read as a meticulous archive of a defunct institution, preserved out of fidelity to a legal system that cannot abrogate itself. The other replies that this misreads what a "reason for the commandment" is: the Guide's account explains how the law came to have this shape at this moment, not what it is worth, and an etiological explanation is perfectly compatible with holding that offering retains intrinsic value as worship — which is why the same author could codify the details with total seriousness and predict their restoration without embarrassment. Hovering over both is Nahmanides' much older objection, which cuts differently: on his view the account fails on its own terms, because sacrifice long predates the idolatry it is supposed to cure (Noah leaving the ark, Abel, Balaam's seven altars), and because slaughtering precisely the species the idolaters revered would entrench their belief in those species' power rather than dismantle it — the disease would be worsened, not treated. My own hunch, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that this is not one disagreement but two tangled together: an exegetical question about what Maimonides meant, and a philosophical question about whether the genetic explanation of a practice can ever bear on its normative standing, with the second quietly determining how each party reads the first.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on who is right. Classify the disagreement as precisely as you can: is it substantive, or largely verbal over the phrase "reason for the commandment"; are the two camps answering different questions under one heading — one asking what Maimonides believed about the value of sacrifice, the other asking what he was licensed to say inside a legal code; is there a hidden textual or historical question (about genre, about audience, about the status of the messianic passage) doing the real work; or is the bedrock a pair of intuitions about esoteric writing that neither side can argue for and that no amount of exegesis could dislodge? Support the diagnosis by pointing to what each side actually says and which textual moves each treats as decisive, and tell me whether the Guide-versus-code tension is the same kind of dispute as Nahmanides' objection or a categorially different one that the seminar has been conflating with it. Finally, say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a piece of internal evidence, a reframing of the question, an acknowledgment that the question is ill-posed — or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and explain why. If you think the analogy with obsolete-but-still-valid statutes in legal theory genuinely illuminates the case rather than distracting from it, say so and press it; if not, say that instead. Please write your answer in American English.
