I retired from a national lab four years ago after a career in condensed matter theory, which in practice meant thirty years of using functional analysis without ever once thinking about its foundations. Lately I've been in a reading group that's slowly working through philosophy of mathematics, and last week we hit set theory and the axiom of choice, and it turned into two genuinely heated hours, which suprised me — I'd assumed working mathematicians regarded choice as settled business. Apparently not.

So here's what I'd like you to write: a dialogue, roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words, between two mathematicians who disagree about choice and who are both completely sharp. No strawmen, please, I've read enough of those. One is a set theorist of a fairly platonist temperament who thinks AC is simply true and that the hand-wringing about it is a hangover from the early twentieth century. The other works in a setting where choice is restricted or fails — a constructive analyst, a topos theorist, someone who wants to do ordinary mathematics in ZF plus dependent choice, your pick — and thinks the first one is being complacent about a principle whose consequences are genuinely bad. I want them to actually engage rather than trade prepared speeches: the skeptic brings up the Banach-Tarski business and the broader worry that choice lets you prove things about objects nobody could ever point at, and the defender has a real answer, not just a shrug about non-measurable sets. Get into the weeds on which weaker principles suffice for which parts of ordinary mathematics, what specifically breaks if you go down to ZF or ZF plus dependent choice, why one formulation of choice feels self-evident while a logically equivalent one feels absurd (that old joke about the axiom of choice being obviously true and the well-ordering principle obviously false came up in our group and nobody could say what it was getting at), and whether the argument is ultimately about truth, about mathematical practice, or about what counts as a legitimate object in the first place.

I don't want a bibliography or a list of further reading at the end — I'll chase those down at the library myself — and I don't want either character to win outright. What I do want is to come away able to say precisely where the two of them part company. My hunch, which may be naive, is that after an hour they'd discover they agree on far more than they think, or that they're using the word true differently without noticing; if that's right, let it surface in the dialogue rather than being announced. Give them names and a setting if you like — an office, the walk to the coffee machine, whatever — and a little personality, but keep the mathematics serious throughout.
