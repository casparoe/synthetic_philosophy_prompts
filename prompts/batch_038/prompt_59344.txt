I run the Learning Review function at a ground-handling company based at a mid-sized European airport: roughly 900 staff across ramp, pushback, loading, marshalling and de-icing. My panel of four (me, a line supervisor, the union safety representative, and a rotating agent from the crew involved) reviews two to four events a week: a jet bridge scoring a fuselage, a tug making contact with a nose gear, a belt loader into a winglet, a pushback begun before the door was confirmed. We have 45 minutes per event, of which perhaps ten can go to the written statements, and none of us has any training in philosophy; the accountable manager signs our findings, and by written policy we are non-punitive, so we can recommend changes to procedure, staffing, training or equipment but cannot recommend discipline against anyone. What we do have, uniquely, is the statement each involved person files in their own words on the day, in a free-text field on our form. Three years of those statements have convinced me that this field holds the real information and that we have no disciplined way of reading it.

I drafted a four-label scheme that the panel now uses informally: CONSTRAINT, for "there was no feasible alternative given the situation"; JUDGMENT, for "there were feasible alternatives and I chose one"; KNOWLEDGE, for "I did not know then what I know now"; and ROUTINE, for "this is how the task was already being done here." The trouble is a recurring fifth kind of statement that fits none of them, and it is the kind that most reliably precedes a repeat event. It looks like this: the writer concedes that an alternative existed and was physically available to them, but denies that it was theirs to take. "I could have called a stop, but I'm not the one who calls stops." "It wasn't my aircraft." "Nineteen years on this stand and it has always been done this way." "I'm a loader, I don't second-guess the marshaller." These are not claims about what was possible; they are claims about who the writer is and about where the authority in the rules lives. I came up through de-icing myself, twenty-two winters on the ramp, and I have written sentences like those. Since then I have been reading Sartre on bad faith, on facticity and transcendence, and on the attitude that treats values as though they were inscribed in things rather than projected by anyone. It gives me the vocabulary for what I am seeing and no instrument whatsoever.

So: write the instrument. I want a decision procedure the panel can run on a single written statement in a few minutes, with the questions asked in order and a stated standard for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, plus explicit stopping rules and explicit hand-offs, where the procedure stops sorting and passes the question to a named human because it is not a question of fact. Ground it in Sartre's distinction between facticity and transcendence and his account of bad faith, but make it usable by four people who will never read him, and tell me which of his distinctions must survive the translation and which can be dropped without changing what the tool does. Then confront the objection I cannot get past: Sartre holds that freedom is absolute though situated, and that no constraint ever removes the choice, only the field in which it is made. If that is right, a procedure that ranks statements by how constrained the writer was is not Sartrean at all, and neither is one that lets a panel decide that someone was in bad faith. Say plainly whether a usable procedure can be built inside the theory or whether what you are handing me is a different instrument wearing Sartre's vocabulary, and if it is the latter, name what was traded away and why the trade is still worth making. Also tell me where it will misfire: statements from people writing in their second language, statements written within the hour with a supervisor standing behind the writer, statements from someone who has so thoroughly absorbed the rulebook that the diagnosis and the description come out identical. And note the reflexive risk, which I suspect is real: a panel that runs a bad-faith test on people who do not know it is being run is treating them as objects to be decoded, which may be the same move the test exists to detect.

Run the procedure on three statements, and be prepared for one of them to produce a finding I will not like. Statement 1, from a loader with two years' service after a belt-loader struck a winglet: "The stand was full and the marshaller had already given the forward signal, so there was nothing to do but drive in. If I had stopped we would have had the aircraft sitting on the taxiway and we would have lost the slot." Statement 2, from a pushback driver with nineteen years' service, the most careful reporter on the site, who files longer statements than anyone and has twice called a stop on his own initiative, after a tug contacted a nose gear: "I checked the door indication as the procedure requires. I follow the procedure exactly and I have never been found short on it. When the procedure is followed, the outcome belongs to the procedure." I should tell you that our own form asks the writer "which procedure step were you performing at the time?", so he may be answering my question faithfully, and any finding against him is partly a finding against my form. Statement 3, from a de-icing team leader after a release signed at the edge of the holdover time: "I knew the holdover time had probably expired and I said so out loud. The captain said we would be de-icing again anyway, so I signed. I could have refused, and I want it on the record that I could have refused and did not. I am not asking anyone to pretend otherwise."

Format. Give the procedure as prose with numbered steps first. Then a table of the three cases with exactly these columns: `statement_id`; `first_step_resolved_at` (the step number and its question); `classification` (one of your labels); `distinction_doing_the_work` (the Sartrean distinction, in one clause, plus what is lost by stating it that plainly); `finding_the_panel_may_write` (one or two sentences of language safe to put in front of the accountable manager under a non-punitive policy); `finding_the_panel_may_not_draw` (what the panel is entitled to refuse to conclude even if tempted); `residual_judgment` (what is left over and which of the four panel roles owns it). Then a single valid JSON object I can hand to the team that maintains the reporting system, with two top-level keys: `labels`, an array of objects each having `code` (an uppercase string usable as a dropdown value), `name`, `one_line_definition`, `applies_when`, `does_not_apply_when`, and `panel_entitled_to_conclude`; and `escalations`, an array of objects each having `trigger` and `hands_to_role`. No bibliography and no reading list, please. If some claim you make about Sartre's framework is contested by people who work on it, flag the contest in a single line rather than litigating it, and mark in the table any step whose philosophical warrant is that contested.
