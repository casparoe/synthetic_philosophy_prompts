I am a graduate student in a formal epistemology and decision theory seminar, and at the end of term each of us presents the paper we have been working on. I get twenty minutes to present and fifteen for discussion, so I need to know in advance which handful of questions will land. My paper defends a view I will state as precisely as I can, because I want the objections aimed at that statement rather than at a slogan.

The setting is a single agent who faces the same multiplicative gamble over and over: each round she may commit any fraction f of her current wealth, a fair coin is tossed, and her wealth is then multiplied by (1 + f) if it lands heads and by (1 − 0.6f) if it lands tails. Committing everything maximizes the expected value of her wealth at every round, yet it drives her fortune toward zero over the long run, whereas committing about a third does far better by any ordinary measure of how a fortune develops. My paper argues three things. First, the correct criterion for such an agent is the long-run growth rate of her wealth, not the expected value of anything. Second, this criterion is not a covert restatement of expected-utility maximization under a logarithmic utility function: it is derived from the dynamics of the agent's own wealth sequence and requires no assumption whatever about her preferences over one-shot prospects. Third, it follows that strategies which are almost surely ruinous are inadmissible for any agent whatever her utility function, so concavity of utility is not doing the work the St. Petersburg literature assigns to it.

The audience will be my professor, who works on Dutch-book arguments and takes Savage seriously; three other PhD students in formal epistemology; and a visitor from the finance department who works on portfolio choice. None of them has encountered the growth-rate literature I am drawing on, and at least two hold that the St. Petersburg problem was settled long ago by concave utility. They are entirely comfortable with the mathematics, but they will be hostile to the claim that preferences are irrelevant to a normative criterion.

Please prepare me for the question period with exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no opening remarks, no closing summary. Order them by how much damage they would do to my argument, and mark which of the five is the one I should most be hoping nobody raises. Each item should carry three labelled parts, each a sentence or two: the objection, phrased the way that particular member of this audience would actually phrase it and attributed to them; the reply I should give, including what I ought to concede immediately; and the softest point in that reply, meaning the place a follow-up question would hurt most. I would rather have five objections that target specific moves in the argument than a general survey of the complaint that any criterion must be preference-based.
