I work on trust and safety at a mid-size social platform — nine people, mostly ML, one policy lead — and I own the pipeline that flags hate speech. I did an ML master's and picked up my ethics from blog posts and conference hallway conversations, so I don't have the vocabulary for what my team is actually arguing about, but I can tell the argument isn't engineering. We're drafting the design doc for the next version of the classifier and we're deadlocked on a question that sounds philosophical to me. Rather than a summary of positions I'd have to translate back into my own terms, I'd like you to dramatize the disagreement as a dialogue, because I learn better from watching two competent people spar than from a list of bullet points.

Here is the setup, stated as precisely as I can manage. Camp A holds that the derogatory content of a slur is part of what the word literally means — something like a presupposition or a conventional implicature attached to the lexical item itself, which is why the sting seems to survive negation, quotation, conditionals, and even sentences whose speaker plainly repudiates the term. On this view a slur is defective in the way a claim with a failed presupposition is defective: it misfires rather than merely offending, and there is a fact of the matter about what it encodes. Camp B holds that nothing derogatory is encoded in meaning at all — a slur is simply a taboo word, and the offensiveness is a property of the act of uttering it in a context where the prohibition is live. On this view the slur and its neutral counterpart for the same group are synonymous in every truth-relevant respect, and what looks like content traveling through negation and quotation is really the taboo attaching to the bare mention of the word-form.

Our queue keeps producing cases the two camps score differently, and we have to ship a policy on at least one of them next quarter. The cases, with the slur itself replaced by "the term" throughout:

1. A screenshot, reposted out of context by an account that has no relationship to the original conversation, of a private group chat in which members of the targeted group use the term about one another affectionately and ironically.
2. A user writing in apparent good faith that the term is not really a slur and should not be banned, and using the term repeatedly in the course of making that argument.
3. An automated transcript, generated by our own speech-to-text service from an archival public broadcast of the 1930s, in which the original speaker uses the term; the transcript is surfaced by a history-education account with no commentary attached.
4. A counterfactual condemnation: someone writes that if anyone ever called their brother the term, they would respond violently — using it precisely in order to condemn its use.
5. A case with no human speaker at all: our translation layer renders a benign foreign-language post into English and, through a mapping error in the lexicon, emits the term where the source had a neutral descriptor. Nobody chose the word, nobody intended anything by it, and the recipient does not experience it as an act addressed to them by anyone.

Write a dialogue between two colleagues on my team, one from each camp, sitting down with this queue in front of them. Let them be roughly the same age and equally smart, with the same access to the same five cases, so that neither wins on insider knowledge; give them names and a bit of texture — a whiteboard, a cold coffee, a standup in twenty minutes — but keep the texture thin enough that the argument carries the piece. Neither may be a strawman. I want real back-and-forth: each should answer the other's actual move, concede where conceding is correct, and press where the other has genuinely overreached. Specifically, the Camp A character has to handle cases 1 and 4 without bolting on ad hoc machinery, and explain what the difference is supposed to be between a slur's being false-presupposing and its merely being offensive; the Camp B character has to explain why the term retains its force under negation and quotation and reported speech, and why locating everything in the act of use does not make case 5 — where there is no act and no speaker — either obviously harmless or mysteriously still bad. I also want them to confront the question of whether their disagreement is substantive or partly terminological, since Camp B can always redescribe Camp A's "encoded content" as a rule of use wearing a semantic costume, and I suspect that move is either devastating or empty and I cannot tell which.

Push them toward an actual decision on case 2, because that is the one we ship on, and have them be explicit about which of their principles the decision rests on. End not with a fake reconciliation but with whatever convergence or clarified stalemate they reach, and have each state what would change their mind — evidence from the cases, from ordinary speakers' intuitions about paraphrase, from how the words behave grammatically, from anything else that is not just authority. Please don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or named philosophers to look up; I have no access to a library and I want arguments I could check myself by thinking about the five cases. Two thousand words or so is fine, and please keep the placeholder convention — write "the term" rather than any real slur anywhere in the dialogue.
