I am a second-year graduate student in a formal-philosophy program, and my sequential decision theory seminar this term is organized around an adversarial-collaboration exercise: pairs of students who hold opposing views have to produce a single document that both would be willing to sign, rather than the usual duelling position papers. I drew the absent-minded driver, and my partner and I have now spent eight weeks talking past each other, so before we attempt another draft I would like to see what the finished document ought to look like. To keep things self-contained, here is the version our seminar uses. A driver must pass two successive highway exits that are indistinguishable to him, and he knows in advance that he will not be able to tell them apart when he reaches one. Leaving the road at the first exit scores zero for him, leaving at the second scores four, and driving past both scores one; his only decision, at each exit, is whether to leave or to continue. Because the exits look alike, the planning problem and the action problem come apart: the ex ante best strategy is a randomized one, in which he continues at each exit with probability two-thirds — worth four-thirds — yet once he is actually at an exit, a straightforward expected-value calculation conditional on being there and on having adopted that plan assigns probability two-fifths to its being the second exit and recommends leaving, which is worth more than the plan he chose at home was.

The two camps in our seminar are these. The first is planning-first and precommitment-inclined, the sort of view that treats this case as a paradigm for updateless reasoning: the driver at the exit is not facing a fresh decision problem but a node inside a problem whose description was fixed at home; the value of the two-thirds plan can only be realized if the agent at the exit executes it; and reasoning that says "recompute now, and leave" is self-defeating, since if everyone reasoned that way the driver would get one rather than four-thirds. On this view the general lesson is that policy evaluation must sometimes be done from a standpoint before the agent acquires self-locating information, and the driver case is evidence for it. The second camp is action-time and Bayesian: there is no revision of decision theory here, only an imperfect-information problem with centered credences, and the right answer is whatever maximizes expected value given everything the driver now knows, including his own strategy. On this view the apparent paradox is a bookkeeping error produced by mixing two decision problems and by treating an intention to randomize as though it were a genuine implementing device; a theory that tells an agent to act against his own current best calculation is not decision theory but a theory of obedience to an earlier self; and the case is an artifact of memory loss that licenses no general lesson, since the moment the exits are distinguishable the disagreement vanishes.

Please draft the joint statement these two could sign. I want a numbered section of agreed claims first, covering what is not in dispute about the setup and the arithmetic, about the formal facts each side accepts, and about what each view recommends in the case and in the obvious variants; then a table of the residual disagreements, each row stating the disagreement twice, once in wording the planning-first side would sign and once in wording the action-time side would sign, with no slanted paraphrase of either. After that, identify the cruxes, evaluating at least these four candidates and adding any I have missed: whether the planner at home and the driver at the exit face one decision problem or two; whether it is legitimate for the driver to feed his own adopted plan into his credences at the exit, and what should happen if deliberation crowds out prediction; whether handing him an irreversible randomizing device before departure, so that the two-thirds strategy is implemented rather than merely intended, changes the recommendation, and which side that change favors; and whether the case generalizes to problems involving self-locating belief with no memory loss at all. For each crux, give the argument, thought experiment, or formal result that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, so that the pair can be held to it later. Where you judge part of the dispute to be verbal — my own suspicion is that "what the driver should do" is doing double duty between "which plan should he adopt" and "what should he do at the exit given the plan he adopted" — say which part and explain why it is only apparent. Finish with a short list of claims that, if established, both sides would agree settles the matter, or an explicit statement that no such claim exists and why not. The deliverable has to read as one document with two signatures on it, not as alternating sympathetic restatements of each position.
