I finished law school in May and am sitting for the bar this summer, which means I have spent the last six weeks inside the character-and-fitness process, and it has left me with a question that my jurisprudence seminar — we read Hart and Devlin on legal moralism — gave me no vocabulary for asking. The application, in my state as I understand it in most, requires me to disclose a misdemeanor from when I was nineteen, every academic discipline action in my life, any unpaid debt or bankruptcy, any occasion on which I sought evaluation or treatment for a psychological condition, my organizational memberships, and effectively my entire online conduct; it requires signed releases authorizing the board to pull records; and it treats a failure to disclose as an independent wrong, one that I am told is usually pursued more aggressively than the underlying fact concealed. Once admitted, the same regulator polices sitting lawyers under a conduct rule whose official comment expressly repudiates the traditional test of crimes involving "moral turpitude" and states that a lawyer "should be professionally answerable only for offenses that indicate lack of those characteristics relevant to law practice." Yet the provisions that carry most of the disciplinary weight are a flat ban on dishonest conduct and a ban on conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice, neither of which is defined by reference to any client or court having actually been harmed. Three features struck me as philosophically load-bearing rather than incidental. First, sanctions appear to be calibrated to publicity: the same off-duty behavior draws a private reprimand if nobody heard about it and a suspension if it reached the local paper. Second, the board's published statement of purpose is consumer protection, which sounds like a harm-based rationale, while its behavior looks honor-based. Third, the standards come apart across the two roles — an applicant can be delayed or denied over a defaulted student loan, conduct that would never be charged as misconduct against a twenty-year member.

What theory of the purpose of occupational licensing does this practice presuppose? The candidates I can see are these: a Devlinian one, on which the bar is a society in miniature, constituted by a shared morality such that the regulator's enforcement of that morality is self-preservation rather than moralism, with the disintegration worry transposed from a nation to a profession; a Hartian one, on which the regulator may police only conduct that harms or risks harm to identifiable clients, courts, or the machinery of law, so that everything else is the enforcement of conventional respectability under a professional label; a purely epistemic one that is neither, on which character disclosure is a predictive instrument whose value lies in the signal it extracts, so that concealment is graver not because lying is intrinsically worse but because it destroys the signal; a role-morality or fiduciary account, on which lawyers occupy a position of asymmetric trust and the profession's demonstrated willingness to exclude is part of what makes that trust rational in the first place; and something about the state's delegation of a monopoly, on which the licensing body is custodian of a public franchise whose legitimacy depends on the general standing of its members rather than on their conduct in particular cases. Which of these does the practice strictly require, as against merely fitting comfortably? Does it presuppose that the bar has a shared morality of the kind Devlin's argument needs — and if so, what could support that, given that the membership is drawn from the whole population and disagrees internally about most contested moral questions? Which rival views does it quietly rule out: a purely act-based account of professional wrong, or the view that professional discipline is just the criminal law applied a second time? Where are the parts in tension with one another — do the consumer-protection purpose and the repudiation of moral turpitude sit consistently with publicity-sensitive sanctioning and with the admission/discipline asymmetry, or does one of those features betray a different presupposition than the others? And where is the practice simply underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly what the board does? Finally, if my state adopted a strict Hart-style constraint — exclusion and discipline only on demonstrated harm, or on a demonstrated and articulable risk of harm to clients or to the administration of justice — what specifically would have to change: which questionnaire items, the treatment of nondisclosure, the publicity gradient in sanctions, the relationship between a criminal conviction and a professional consequence? I suspect the screening rationale survives that constraint intact while the honor-based rationale does not, and I want to know whether that suspicion is right or whether it flatters the screening account.

Please be explicit throughout about the difference between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it is merely consistent with, and about where you are reasoning from general expectations about how bar authorities behave rather than from anything I have described here. I would also like you to say where your reconstruction is most likely to fail: which of the candidate theories you have dismissed too quickly, what argument or what evidence about actual disciplinary decision-making would move you to a different verdict — in particular, what would persuade you either that the practice is coherent under a single theory after all, or that it presupposes something other than the view you settle on. Please write in American English.
