I am in the first year of a doctorate in the history of science, and the convenor of my department's research seminar has a stock interjection. Whenever a discussion begins to presuppose a tidy break between pre-modern natural knowledge and its modern successor, he says, flatly, that there was no Scientific Revolution, and the topic moves on. Last week I used the seminar to float the proposal that structures my first chapter — that the printing of Copernicus's De revolutionibus orbium coelestium in 1543 is a defensible point at which to date a genuine discontinuity in European natural philosophy — and the reply was exactly that sentence, delivered not as an objection to my evidence but as though evidence were beside the point.

He is a careful historian, so I do not think he was merely being rhetorical; I assume the sentence carries a real thesis. What I cannot tell is which one. What I want from you is a disambiguation, not a defence of either side.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct and precise claims that this slogan can be used to assert. I expect them to differ along several axes at once, though no doubt there are axes I have not thought of: whether the target of the denial is the phrase or the phenomenon; whether what is denied is determinate boundaries, or a shared kind across whatever falls inside those boundaries, or only that the amount of change rose to the level implied by the word revolution; whether the claim is a continuity thesis about scholastic natural philosophy, a thesis about when anything answerable to the word science first existed, a thesis about the later coinage of the label and the ideological work it was made to do, or a general anti-realism about historical periods as such; and whether it is put forward as a claim about the past or as a claim about how the past ought to be written up.

For each reading in turn, state the thesis explicitly enough that one could see what would confirm or refute it; say whether it is trivially true, substantively true, false, or a live dispute among historians; and say whether it would in fact license dismissing my proposal about 1543, since I suspect that most of the readings would not. Finally, tell me which reading a working historian most plausibly has in mind when the sentence functions conversationally as a stopper rather than as an argument, and flag any reading on which the sentence is both true and fully compatible with everything I wanted to claim — I should like to know whether he and I disagreed at all.
