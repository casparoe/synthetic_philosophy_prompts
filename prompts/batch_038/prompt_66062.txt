I teach a unit on Turing's 1950 paper and I'd rather test his learning-machine stuff against a real episode than another thought experiment. Take the AlphaGo match against Lee Sedol in March 2016 -- move 37 in game two, the fifth-line stone the commentators first read as a blunder, which AlphaGo's own policy net reckoned a human pro would play maybe once in ten thousand tries -- and then AlphaGo Zero a year later: no human games at all, self-play from random, 100-0 against the version that beat Lee.

Set that against what Turing actually proposes: build a child program, then educate it -- a teacher, rewards and punishments, some curriculm, plus the expectation that a learner will start doing things its teacher didn't anticipate, which is how he handles the machines-take-him-by-surprise objection.

So: how much of that apparatus is genuinely present in either system? Who's the teacher, what does duty as reward and punishment, is there anything like a curriculum, and does the learner have anything like the child's exposure to a shared world? Is move 37 the surprise Turing predicted, or a different thing, given it was instantly legible to the experts as brilliant rather than as departure-into-error? And does Zero needing no teacher confirm the picture or quietly replace it -- what's lost when the environment is a closed rule set with one number at the end instead of a teacher with judgement? Point out where the analogy goes silent rather than stretching it.
