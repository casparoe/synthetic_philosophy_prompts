Two months ago I was appointed to the clinical prioritisation panel of a regional health authority, a role I accepted partly on the assumption that a background in normative theory would finally be of some use. Our live case is this. A fixed annual budget can fund either a trauma and road-safety programme that our analysts project will prevent roughly forty premature deaths a year across the region, or a chronic pain service that will deliver substantial and durable relief to about thirty thousand people a year who currently live with moderate-to-severe pain. The two are mutually exclusive. The panel's written methodology is QALY-based and therefore settles the matter arithmetically in favour of the pain service; three members refuse to vote that way, and their refusal is principled rather than sentimental. The most articulate of them, a colleague I shall call N., holds something close to a contractualist line: moral principles must be justifiable to each person separately, and since no one in the second group would bear anything remotely comparable to what the forty would bear, the claims cannot be pooled. When I pressed him, he accepted that this commits him to saving one life rather than relieving a billion people of a year of migraine, and he said he was content with that commitment.

The dispute has ceased to be academic for me. My dissent has been minuted, and I have been told, in effect, that I am willing to let people suffer for the sake of a principle. I should like to know where I actually stand.

Please give me your credence in the following proposition, stated as sharply as I can manage it.

P: In any choice of this shape — preventing a serious and irreversible harm, paradigmatically death, to a small number of people, versus preventing a markedly less serious but genuine harm to a much larger number — the first option is morally required, however large the second number; and the reason is not a threshold or a difference in kind between the two harms, but that claims belonging to different persons may not be summed.

I want a number, and I want the derivation exposed. Decompose P into the sub-claims you take it to rest on, and give each a probability with a sentence of justification. At minimum, treat these: whether the individualist restriction on moral reasoning is itself true; whether the intuitions that support it track something normative or are in part an artifact of our inability to represent very large numbers, which is an empirical question about scope insensitivity and I want it handled as one; whether a threshold or incommensurability diagnosis is better placed than an outright ban on aggregation; whether P survives the gradient cases in which the harm to the many is quite bad indeed — chronic, disabling, spanning decades — yet still short of death; and whether there is a determinate fact of the matter here at all, rather than a choice between internally coherent frameworks. Add whatever further terms you think belong, including one for the intermediate rule I have come to favour, tie-break aggregation, on which numbers never increase the strength of a complaint but may settle a genuine tie between complaints of equal strength — so that one headache still loses to one death, while a million headaches defeat one headache. Then show me how your terms combine into your number for P, identify the single term to which that number is most sensitive, and say roughly how far it would move if that term shifted.

My own credence in P is about 0.25; N.'s is about 0.9. Tell me which term you think carries most of that gap, and whether your own number places you nearer to me or to him. Finally, say what argument or evidence would move your number most, and note anything about the case as I have described it — the fixed budget, the projections, the institutional setting — that you think bears on the truth of P rather than merely on what our panel ought to do. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, and I do not want citations or suggestions for further reading; I want your number and the reasoning that produces it.
