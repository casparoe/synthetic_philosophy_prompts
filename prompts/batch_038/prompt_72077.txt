I sit as one of five jurors on a small private foundation's biennial fiction prize — fifty thousand dollars, awarded to a book published in the previous two years, decided by a jury of two novelists, a poet, a critic, and a bookseller. The foundation's bylaws leave the standard almost entirely to us, except for one clause, added by the donor's widow, permitting the jury to weigh "the work's capacity to reach readers beyond the literary world" without saying what capacity or reach means. This cycle the shortlist has narrowed to two books that could hardly be less alike. One is a six-hundred-page novel, largely in the second person, about a Victorian hydrographer charting a river that keeps altering its course; it carries a marginal commentary in a smaller hand throughout, and its central section is a forty-page register of the drowned, each name annotated with one trivial fact about the dead person's life. The other is a hundred-page book, in short declarative sentences, about a municipal gardener who goes on tending the rose beds of a closed school after his wife dies, and the neighbour's child who brings him tea. Nobody on the jury disputes that both are serious. The dispute is about which of them, if either, does the thing the prize exists to reward.

At our last meeting the critic argued that only the novella transmits anything, and she argued it explicitly in Tolstoy's terms: art is the infection of one person by a feeling another has experienced, and a work whose effect depends on the reader's prior formation is not transmitting feeling but performing for initiates — a pastime of a class, which is precisely what he thought of Shakespeare and of late Beethoven, and precisely what he was willing to say about his own celebrated novels. She added that the bylaws clause, whatever else it means, points the same way. The poet answered that every transmission runs through an acquired code: roses mean what they mean in that novella only because readers have absorbed a whole tradition about gardens and grief, and the hydrographer's register of the drowned works on the very feelings Tolstoy said art ought to work on — pity for people whom official history never recorded — so on the content half of his criterion the difficult book may be the more moral of the two. The critic replied that there is a real difference between a code anyone picks up by living and one picked up only by reading a great deal, and that she is happy to concede Tolstoy's list of approved works is a mess while insisting the criterion survives its author's applications. The poet said that then the criterion is hers and not his, since she is keeping the accessibility half and discarding the religious-perception half, and a jury that discards half a theory has no business invoking it. She said the content half is unusable by five laypeople pretending to know the religious perception of the age, and that she would rather apply a crude test honestly than a sublime one selectively. There is also an evidentiary wrinkle none of us has resolved. I watched my own sister — who reads perhaps two books a year — finish the novella in a night and cry at it; and one of the novelists on the jury reports that her sixteen-year-old daughter, who reads nothing assigned and less unassigned, was undone by the register of the drowned. If that is true it damages the critic's distinction at its foundation, since it is an untrained reader being infected by the trained book; but the daughter will not be produced, will not describe the experience, and the report arrives second-hand from a juror with a strong preference.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should end in a definite recommendation — award to one of the two, or state plainly why the jury should be advised to let the prize go unawarded this cycle and what that would commit us to saying publicly — and it should give the reasoning that gets us there, not a survey of the reasoning available. It must set out a decision rule the jury could actually apply to next year's shortlist for distinguishing a feeling a work transmits to a reader who has merely lived from a feeling it transmits only to a reader who has already read, and it must say what the bylaws clause should be taken to mean once that distinction is in hand, including whether it can bear the weight the critic puts on it. It must give the strongest honest version of the losing side — including the poet's objection that the critic is running a truncated theory under a famous name — and say why it nevertheless loses. It should address the unverified report about the sixteen-year-old directly, as a question of what a jury may treat as evidence about a book's reach. And it should be candid about whether Tolstoy's criterion can be operationalised by five non-specialists at all, or whether importing it into a prize deliberation is chiefly a way of dignifying a preference we already have. I do not want a reading list, references, or any quotation from the treatise; I read it years ago and remember being annoyed by it, which is a separate matter from the question in front of us. What I need is the argument, in about twelve hundred words, in a register the foundation's director could read without embarrassment.
