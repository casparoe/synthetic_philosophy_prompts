At work I maintain a risk-scoring service that has moved from single-number probabilities to sets of them, so that a failure mode we know essentially nothing about is not forced into a fake-precise 0.03. A colleague has produced the following argument that the move commits us to stop gathering evidence altogether, and I cannot locate the error.

(1) Uncertainty about a hypothesis H is represented by a set of probability functions, and the interval for H is the range of values those functions assign.
(2) Learning E updates the set by conditioning every member on E; the posterior interval is the range of the resulting values.
(3) Dilation occurs: for some prior sets and some partitions, whichever cell of the partition you learn, the posterior interval for H is strictly wider than the prior interval. The concrete case, which I have checked in code: two tosses of a coin whose bias and whose toss-to-toss dependence are both unknown, so the set contains every distribution with P(heads on toss 1) = P(heads on toss 2) = 1/2 and correlation between the tosses anywhere from -1 to 1. The prior interval for heads on toss 2 is the single value 1/2; after observing toss 1 it becomes the full range from 0 to 1, whichever way toss 1 lands.
(4) A strictly wider interval means strictly less information about H.
(5) So in any case like (3), every experiment we might run leaves us strictly less informed about H, whatever the outcome.
(6) It is irrational to pay a cost to become strictly less informed.
(7) Therefore we should run no experiment bearing on any H whose prior set admits dilation; and since dilation appears to be the norm once sets are permitted at all, we should collect no evidence.

Of these I most want to keep (3), the only part I have verified numerically myself, and I am reluctant to give up sets entirely, since point estimates are what produced the overconfidence we are trying to fix. Tell me which step you reject, why it looked harmless when I first read it, and what the alternatives cost: specifically, what a single-prior Bayesian says about the coin case and what that response gives up. If your diagnosis lands on (4), I would also like the criterion that replaces interval width, since "information" is carrying all the weight in (6) and I do not know how to measure it when a state of opinion is a set. One further wrinkle: my attempted fix was to widen the prior interval for H as well, so that nothing can widen on learning, but then the prior asserts nothing and I seem to have bought immunity to dilation by throwing away content. Does that fix work, or does it merely relocate the problem?
