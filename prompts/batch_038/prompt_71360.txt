For the past two years I have convened a small reading group on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and we have arrived at an impasse that further argument does not seem capable of resolving. Half of us maintain that sufficiently rich behavioural equivalence is all there is to having a mind, and that any residual "inner" ingredient is either empty or itself specifiable in behavioural terms; the other half insist that equivalence can be purchased by means — memorisation, interpolation, an astronomically large prepared concordance of replies — that no one would care to call understanding. Rather than commission another position paper, I would like something comic that both sides will recognise as fair, and that we can circulate at the start of next term. Please write a certification dossier: the formal audit report by which a fictional accreditation body, which I will call the Conformity Assessment Board for Cognitive Systems, determines whether a candidate system has satisfied the Behavioural Equivalence Standard (Second Edition) and should therefore be entered on the register as minded. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words in a deadpan standards-audit register — scope and normative references, method, findings, a register of nonconformities graded major and minor, an annex of dissent, and a recommendation to the Board — written by people who are entirely serious about procedure and possess no vocabulary for the question they are actually deciding.

Some seeding, which you are welcome to extend: the candidate is a text-only system designated MERIDIAN-4, interrogated by teletype across twelve sessions by three accredited judges, with a human comparator of matched age and education seated in an identical booth under identical instructions. The audit finds MERIDIAN-4 conformant on every scored clause yet records a series of nonconformities that are odd in ways the standard was never drafted to accommodate. It has never once been observed to make an error it could not subsequently explain, so the lead auditor raises a major nonconformity under the clause requiring evidence of productive failure. Asked deliberately dull questions for four consecutive hours, it remained unfailingly engaged, breaching the requirement of demonstrable fatigue or boredom; the human comparator disengaged after forty minutes and was, on that basis, certified without difficulty. It apologised four hundred and twelve times against the comparator's three. When invited to characterise the interval between sessions it produced a fluent and affecting answer which the memory-forensics annex shows to be inconsistent with its own activity logs — and the auditor is stuck, because the standard scores what a candidate says rather than whether what it says is true of it, and no clause exists under which a discrepancy between report and record can be entered at all. The dissenting annex argues that MERIDIAN-4 is a precompiled concordance of enormous size, assembled by statistical means from a corpus, whose equivalence was bought rather than thought; the same auditor notes in a marginal annotation that the Board's founding requirement, reproduced in the standard's preamble — "to make them play the imitation game so well that an average interrogator will not have more than 70 percent chance of making the right identification after five minutes of questioning" — was a prediction somebody once ventured about the future, which the Board has spent four decades quietly converting into a pass mark. The chair is meanwhile preoccupied with precedent: clause 4.2 commits the Board to certify anything behaviourally equivalent to an already-certified candidate, which would oblige it to certify a sufficiently complete transcript of its own past interrogations, and nobody on the Board wishes to do that. Include as well a brief submission from the Human Interrogators' Association protesting that clause 7 requires judges to be average and that none of the accredited judges are.

Three constraints. First, no footnotes, no bibliography, and no naming of real philosophers, real papers, or real systems: every objection that surfaces must be reconstructible from the text of the dossier alone by someone who knows the literature, and legible as mere bureaucracy to someone who does not. Second, keep the irony entirely deadpan — no character may step outside the procedure to state the moral, and no sentence may wink at the reader. Third, the closing recommendation should be procedurally impeccable and, on reflection, disquieting, with the judgment left for the reader to supply.
