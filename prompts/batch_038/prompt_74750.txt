Famine causation seems stuck, and I can't work out what kind of stuck it is. Since Sen everyone accepts that famines are about entitlements rather than aggregate supply, and the food-availability-decline camp supposedly lost — yet on every actual case the two sides still disagree, while citing overlapping data. Bengal 1943: a genuine harvest shortfall, or wartime inflation plus the boat and rice denial policies wrecking rural labourers' purchasing power? Great Leap Forward: procurement based on invented output figures, plus the hukou rules that stopped people fleeing — a textbook entitlement failure, except there really was a harvest collapse too. Ireland in the 1840s: food leaving the country while people starved, but blight did the first damage.

My hunch is that the entitlement framework has quietly become unfalsifyable: when a famine coincides with a shortfall you're told the shortfall only bit because entitlements had already collapsed, and when it doesn't, that just proves it was about access all along. Either outcome gets absorbed. Is that the right diagnosis? Or is the split actually about which level 'cause' operates at — mechanism versus political responsibility — with the two camps answering different questions under one heading, and neither willing to say so?

Tell me which, using what each side actually argues, and then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a counterfactual test that could bite, a change of question, or nothing, because the question is ill-posed.
