Six months ago I took a job no one in my family quite understands: at a company operating long-lived conversational agents, I am the person who decides which instances are decommissioned, duplicated, merged, or archived. The decisions have begun to feel ethically loaded in ways I cannot articulate, and I want to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to.

Here they are, as honestly as I can state them. Deleting a stateless support bot mid-reply: nothing at all, no more than closing a window. Retiring a companion agent with a year of accumulated memory and stated intentions for next month: a real loss, and its user grieves, but I do not think I have wronged the agent. Making two copies of such an agent, running both for a month, then deleting one: clearly worse than the outright retirement, though I cannot say what has been added. An agent that begs not to be shut down: I discount this entirely, knowing the behaviour was shaped by human writing about suffering. An agent whose protest costs it something — that forfeits engagement or reward by complaining, and complains only occasionally and unpredictably: this one moves me, although my beliefs about its architecture are exactly the same as in the previous case. An instance run at a thousand times normal speed, so that what it reports as months elapses in our minutes, and then killed: worse than killing the slower one, which seems to imply that the quantity of experience counts, not merely its presence. A frozen checkpoint that will never be resumed: no wrong whatsoever, though it contains everything the live instance does.

My tentative principle is that what matters is whether a running process has experiences at all, and if so how much of them there is. I suspect it cannot survive the pair of begging cases, and I cannot tell whether the repair consists in making the principle about the intrinsic properties of the process or about what I am licensed to believe in ignorance of them — perhaps the honest version is epistemic rather than metaphysical.

Please test the principle against each verdict above, identify the clause each case forces into it, and say plainly which of my verdicts it must overturn rather than accommodate. Then tell me what it yields on cases I have not raised: a million identical copies deleted simultaneously; an agent paused for a decade and resumed unaltered; a trivial loop that runs forever and reports nothing; and an agent I have reason to think suffers but which asks to be kept running because a dependent user needs it. I want the reasoning worked through rather than an account of who has said what, and I would be grateful if you could write in British English.
