I am a high school junior, and I wrote the argument below for a regional history research competition, where a judge told me that my central case was not merely unproven but self-undermining. The argument is intended to establish that the adoption of agriculture was a mistake.

1. If a way of life leaves the typical person living under it worse off than the way of life it replaced, then adopting it was a mistake.
2. Farming left the typical person worse off than foraging did. In the regions I studied, skeletons from early farming villages show shorter adults, far more tooth decay and enamel defects, more indicators of iron deficiency, more bone lesions consistent with infection, and a narrower diet than the forager burials that precede them; and the recorded time budgets of foraging groups suggest they met their needs in fewer hours of work than a farming household does, so the loss was not only nutritional.
3. Therefore adopting farming was a mistake.
4. My principle does not stop there. Farming also made it possible to keep many more people alive than foraging could, and later changes have done the same: the first irrigation cities, whose residents were smaller and sicker than the villagers around them; the move into factory towns, where for several generations the recorded heights of working men fell and wages barely rose while the urban population multiplied; the plowing of new land that displaced herders. Each fed a larger population at a lower level per person, at least for the first generations to live through it.
5. Therefore, by (1), each of those was a mistake as well.
6. That cannot be right. A rule that condemns supporting more people whenever the per-person level dips is measuring something other than whether lives went well, and a principle whose verdicts I want to overrule in every case I can check is not a principle I should keep.
7. Therefore (1) is false, and (3) falls with it.

I cannot accept (7). If I am forced to keep one step, I want to keep (2), because that is the part I did the research for. So I would like to know which step to give up, and precisely what giving it up costs. There is one feature of the argument I keep circling without being able to place: the two ways of life I am comparing do not contain the same number of people, and mostly not the same people, so the typical person under farming is not anyone who was ever a forager. I cannot tell whether that is fatal to my principle, fatal to the bones, or simply what my principle is supposed to handle.

The judge also raised an objection to (2) that I have not been able to answer. She said that everyone in a cemetery died, that a bone lesion is evidence of having survived an illness long enough for bone to react to it, and that two samples with very different death rates and age structures cannot be compared as though they measured the health of the same kind of population — so a farming cemetery full of lesions might belong to a less frail population than a forager cemetery without them. If that is right, is (2) still available to me at all?

For what it is worth, my paper also argues that farming produced the first hereditary inequalities, on the basis of later sites where a few burials carry elaborate grave goods and most carry none. I do not know whether that evidence belongs inside this argument, beside it, or nowhere in it.

Please answer in exactly five numbered points of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: the first naming the step you would reject, and the remaining four covering what rejecting it costs me, whether any version of (1) survives, what the judge's objection does to (2), and what my paper could rest on instead.
