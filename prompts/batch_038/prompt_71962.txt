I'm the deputy editor at a small forecasting shop — fourteen analysts, we publish probability numbers on geopolitical stuff (treaties, sanctions, coups, port strikes), the numbers get scored after the fact, and the score is basically our whole reputation. Twice a month I chair what we call a reconcile: two analysts who filed different numbers on the same question sit down with me for 45 minutes and I try to walk them out of the room with one house number. I run it entirely by vibes right now and its showing. I did enough decision theory in grad school to know Aumann's result — the one saying two rational people who share a prior can't knowingly keep different beliefs about the same thing once each one's belief is common knowledge between them — and the follow-up showing that if they just keep announcing their numbers to each other the disagreement has to collapse after finitely many rounds. What I can't do is turn any of that into something I can actually chair without it turning into a seminar.

So: write me the procedure. I want an ordered set of steps, and for each one, the question I ask out loud, what counts as a sufficient answer, what I do with that answer, and the exit condition — when the step hands me a verdict versus when it just routes to the next step. The core job I want it to do is sort a stubborn disagreement into the kind the theorem says can't be rational and the kinds his assumptions allow: private information one of them can't disclose, priors that genuinely differ, the two of them unknowingly estimating different events, common knowledge failing because each is modeling what the other will *say* instead of what the other believes, and one of them just not updating coherently or sandbagging the number for reputational reasons. It has to survive the actual conditions — 45 minutes, analysts who have never seen a sigma-algebra and go quiet the second I write math on the board, and a number that goes out under the firm's name, so a lazy split-the-difference when one of them is straightforwardly wrong is expensive for me. Also give me a verdict on the move my predecessor used, which was declaring an official shared prior at the top of every session (usually some historical base rate) precisely so the theorem's assumption would be satisfied by fiat: legitimate operational trick, or a cheat that voids the whole diagnosis? And wherever the procedure is really just a judgment call wearing a lab coat, say so plainly — I want to know which steps I can't hand to a junior editor. No reading list or citations, please; nobody here has time to chase papers and I won't be able to look anything up mid-meeting.

Then run it on three cases. (1) "Will the armistice be signed before the end of Q3?" — one analyst filed 20% working purely off public statements; the other filed 60% and is partly going on two back-channel conversations with a source she's contractually forbidden to name or even characterize. They've reconciled four times on related questions and every time landed on a 40-ish compromise that each of them later told me privately they thought was wrong. (2) "Will the central bank hike in June?" — 70% versus 30%, and this is the infuriating one, because both of them swear they're working from the identical packet of speeches and data releases and the identical base rate over the last twenty cycles, neither can point to a single fact the other hasn't seen, and the whole gap seems to come down to whether the governor's last paragraph reads as hawkish signaling or boilerplate hedging. (3) "Will there be a strike before the contract deadline?" — 65% versus 15%, and I only discovered in the second session that one of them is estimating a strike anywhere in the region while the other is estimating one specific union walking out at one specific port. For each: walk the steps, say where it terminates and what I publish, and flag any step where you think the procedure is going to misfire in the room rather than on paper. Make sure at least one of the three ends somewhere I'm going to hate — I already suspect it's the first, where the honest output may be two numbers plus a note saying the disagreement is legitimate, which my boss will read as me not doing my job.
