I am preparing a seminar on the philosophical commitments of mathematical practice, and formalized mathematics keeps striking me as a case where a community's implicit philosophy of foundations is unusually explicit — written into the tooling rather than left as background assumption. I would like a reconstruction of the implicit philosophy behind one particular design decision, not a history of it.

The setup, described as fully as I can so that you will not need outside knowledge. Lean is a proof assistant whose core is a dependent type theory with a proof-irrelevant sort of propositions at the bottom. On top of that type theory the system assumes exactly three further axioms: propositional extensionality; a rule governing quotient types, which brings function extensionality along with it; and a choice principle, which turns a proof that something satisfying a property exists into an actual term denoting such a thing — it manufactures data out of a proposition. Mathlib, the large community-built library developed in this system, covers most of undergraduate mathematics and a good deal of graduate mathematics, and takes all three axioms as background infrastructure.

Two features of the resulting practice interest me, and I suspect they sit uneasily together.

First, axiom dependence is tracked and reportable. Every theorem in the library carries a machine-checked record of which of the three axioms its proof actually used, and any user can ask the system to print that record. Treating a theorem's axiom footprint as a legitimate object of inquiry — something one might ask about, document, or attempt to reduce — is an ordinary part of how the community works.

Second, the choice axiom is nevertheless not economized. Any definition depending on it must be annotated as noncomputable, meaning the system's code generator cannot execute it; the annotation is applied and the definition is then used freely thereafter. Proofs invoke choice-dependent existence results — that every vector space has a basis, Hahn–Banach, Tychonoff, the existence of non-principal ultrafilters — without remark. In practice, a theorem whose footprint includes the choice axiom is not regarded as weaker, less informative, or in any way second-class relative to one whose footprint does not.

There is a further wrinkle. In this foundation, excluded middle is not an independent assumption: by a Diaconescu-style argument it is derivable from the choice principle together with the other two axioms. Classical logic and the capacity to extract witnesses from existential propositions therefore come as a package. Unlike in set theory, where one can accept excluded middle while rejecting choice, here the two cannot be separated — and the axiom-tracking record consequently cannot distinguish a theorem that merely reasoned classically from one that genuinely required a strong existence assumption.

My questions:

1. What view of mathematical existence, and of what a proof is, does the combination of these features presuppose? Is it platonism about a completed set-theoretic universe, with the type theory treated as a convenient syntax for it (the usual relative-consistency arguments read the system off against a set theory with large cardinals)? Is it a separation-of-concerns quietism, on which questions of witness extraction are simply not the library's business? Is it something about proof irrelevance — that once you know a proposition is inhabited, the particular inhabitant is of no interest? Reconstruct the implicit theory as explicitly as you can.

2. Which rival views does the design rule out, as opposed to merely sitting awkwardly with? Consider Bishop-style constructive mathematics, Martin-Löf type theory, Feferman-style predicativism, and the view on which the content of an existence proof is a method for producing witnesses. For each, say whether the practice strictly excludes it or whether the same infrastructure could be licensed from within it — and note that the existence of the noncomputable annotation may make the system look as though it keeps a door open for constructively minded users.

3. Do the two features hang together? Meticulous axiom tracking presupposes that axiom dependence is a philosophically meaningful property of a theorem; the free use of the choice axiom presupposes that at least one such dependence does not matter. Is that an inconsistency in the practice, or is there a coherent implicit theory on which both are correct? If the latter, state the theory and say what else it commits the community to.

4. Is the noncomputable annotation doing any philosophical work at all, or is it a purely engineering marker — a note that a definition cannot be run — that is easily mistaken for a principled concession to constructivism? Does your answer change anything you said in response to 1 through 3?

5. Suppose the community decided to reverse course and treat choice as a scarce resource. What would have to change, and where would the practice break first? I would like a rough triage of the library into results that survive, results that require restatement in a weaker form, and results that are simply gone, together with an assessment of how much of the loss would follow from giving up excluded middle rather than from giving up choice, given that in this foundation the two are bundled. As a probe: how much of the library would remain available in a universe of sets in which dependent choice holds and every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable?

Throughout, please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flag the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same behavior.
