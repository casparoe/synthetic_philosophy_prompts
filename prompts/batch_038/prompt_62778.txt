You are to write as a professional philosopher of mind who also has a working knowledge of the neuropsychology of commissurotomy — someone equally at home in the metaphysics of personal identity and in the philosophy of experimental practice, and willing to think in the vocabulary of paradigms, controls, and artifacts. Please reason carefully rather than reaching quickly for a verdict, and treat the questions below as genuinely open.

I should say something about myself, since it shapes what I am asking for. I am a retired experimental physiologist; for some thirty-five years I worked on sensory transduction in invertebrate preparations, and my professional life consisted of asking whether a given stimulus was detected, by which receptor, with what threshold. I have only recently taken up philosophy in earnest. What disorients me is discovering a dispute in which everyone has the same data, has had them for decades, and yet no result — no measurement, no cleverer control — appears capable of settling anything. I should like a diagnosis of that impasse, not a survey of it.

The phenomenon, as I understand it. In patients whose corpus callosum has been sectioned to control intractable epilepsy, a picture flashed briefly to one visual field, under conditions that prevent the eyes from moving, can be handled by the hemisphere contralateral to it alone. The patient then denies having seen anything, while the hand controlled by the silent hemisphere can retrieve the object from behind a curtain or draw it; a word presented to that hemisphere can govern a choice without entering the patient's verbal report. The speaking hemisphere, meanwhile, produces fluent confabulated explanations of behaviour initiated from the other side, and in some patients the two hands have been observed working at cross purposes. And yet these same people drive, hold jobs, converse, marry, and describe a single continuous life. Outside the testing room there is very little to suggest two minds.

The disagreement, as I read it, has three camps. One holds that the patient really has two streams of consciousness, hence arguably two subjects. A second holds that there is one stream but that it is only partly unified: some experiences are co-conscious with each other and others are not, so that the co-consciousness relation among the patient's experiences may fail to be transitive — one experience unified with a second, the second with a third, the first not with the third. The third holds that consciousness remains fully unified at every moment, the two hemispheres taking turns rather than running in parallel, so that the appearance of simultaneous double experience is an artefact of how the tasks are scored.

Two features of the standoff strike me as suspicious, and I should like them examined.

First, the argument seems to have migrated into a quarrel about which experimental setting is authoritative. The defenders of unity say the disconnection only shows up under contrived conditions — millisecond lateralised flashes, forced-choice responses, restrictions on eye movement — and that in ordinary viewing both hemispheres receive essentially the same information, so the apparatus manufactures the division. The defenders of division reply that ordinary behaviour proves nothing, because natural life is redundant and inferentially rich, gives the patient unlimited time and free eye movements, and never poses a question that the intact hemisphere cannot answer for both; only the controlled presentation tests anything at all. Each side thus treats the other's evidence as contaminated. I have seen this pattern in physiology, where it usually signals that the two groups are measuring different things and have not noticed. Is that what is happening here?

Second, I suspect the camps are not even asking the same question. How many subjects there are, how many streams, how many unified agents, and how many beings whose consent one would need to obtain may come apart; and the debate may quietly slide among them. Relatedly, I have wondered whether the question is simply indeterminate in this case — whether nothing about the facts fixes whether such a system has one mind or two, so that the dispute is about which way of counting we adopt. If so, I understand why neither side wants to say it, since it looks like surrender.
