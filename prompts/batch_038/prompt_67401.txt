I write literary fiction — four novels out, none of them set anywhere near a university — and this year I'm the writer in residence in the philosophy department here. They've roped me into setting one question for the second-year philosophy of mind final, partly as a joke, partly because the head of department reckons an outsider will set something the students can't cram for. I'd like you to help me actually write it, plus the model answer and the marking scheme, since obviously I can't set an exam I couldn't answer myself.

Act as a professional philosopher who has set and marked a lot of undergraduate philosophy of mind papers. Think carefully, and before you draft anything spend five or six lines brainstorming out loud about where the real crux of the question is and what a competent-but-second-class answer will get wrong — I want to see the reasoning, not just the finished product.

Here's the material I want the question built out of. In the book I'm working on there's a small religious community whose members have trained themselves, from childhood, never to describe themselves or anyone else in terms of beliefs, desires, intentions or wants. Their whole idiom is dispositions and goings-on: when a woman's husband dies she says only "the crying comes now, and the going to the window at dusk", and that is accepted by everyone there as a complete account of her state. A philosopher character in the book says this proves folk psychology is optional — here's a whole form of life that gets on without positing propositional attitudes, so the attitudes were never there. I want the exam question set in exactly those terms. Keep the sect and the widow's sentence in it verbatim, please, and don't smooth the whole thing into a generic "state and assess eliminative materialism" question: both because that's a worse question, and because I genuinely need to know how a real philosopher would attack my scene.

The question has to push students onto the crux rather than the summary. What could a difference in *descriptive practice* like this possibly prove about the ontology of the attitudes? I want them to have to work out whether the sect's idiom is a genuine alternative explanation of the same behaviour or a relabelling of folk psychology — note that these people still teach their children, still deceive one another, still get surprised, and that the widow's own sentence is offered as an account of her state and can be corrected by others if they think its wrong. And I want the self-undermining pressure on eliminativism to come up, but not as a slogan: what is the eliminativist *doing* when she says the attitudes aren't there, and is "saying isn't believing" an escape hatch or just the problem restated with the serial numbers filed off? A student who recites the phlogiston analogy and the wait-for-neuroscience argument should get nowhere near full marks.

Constraints: 45 minutes, 25 marks, second-year level. They'll have read a couple of primary papers in class, but I don't want the question turning on remembering who said what in which year. Please give me: the question exactly as the students would see it, including any scene-setting; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme broken into must-hit points with the marks each is worth, totalling 25, plus a one-line note under each saying how students typically fumble it. Then a short paragraph at the end telling me which two or three answers a smart-but-lazy student will be tempted to give and what precisely is wrong with each — that's partly for the marking and partly because I want to know whether my philosopher character's line survives contact with an expert.

No bibliography, no reading list, and please don't name works, papers or dates: I can't check them and I'd much rather you described positions than risk getting an attribution wrong. A philosopher's name is fine only where you're confident they really held the view you're attaching to them.

Write the whole thing in British English, spelling and idiom both — the department is fussy and I've already been corrected once for writing "toward". Oh, and don't seperate the marking scheme into some elaborate grid: plain numbered points are fine.
