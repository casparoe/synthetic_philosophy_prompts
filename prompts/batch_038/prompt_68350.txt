Been keeping a list of my gut calls on when a vote of the people should actualy stick, and I can't get them to line up. Can you find the principle underneath, or just tell me which calls are wrong?

- The Swiss minaret ban: passed with 57.5%, no procedural complaints, and I still think it shouldn't have taken effect.
- A nationwide cap on second homes: passed with 51%, and that one's fine, implement it.
- Leaving a treaty union, 52–48: binding, dont re-run it.
- Making possession of a certain drug a felony, 52–48: not binding, the legislature should feel free to reverse it next session. Same margin, opposite calls, I know.
- Abolishing the constitutional court, 52–48: here I want a supermajority, which flatly contradicts the treaty one.
- The same question asked again three months later hoping for a different answer: illegitimate. The same question asked again four years later, after the deal the first vote was supposed to deliver fell apart in negotiation: legitimate, maybe required.
- A measure that passes 80–20 on 18% turnout: void, no quorum. But a party that wins 35% in a general election governs for four years and I don't blink at that.
- A measure declaring that a vaccine doesn't work, passed with 70%: void — not because it targets anyone, but because you can't vote on a fact.
- Secession: the region's voters decide, but I also think the whole country should get a say, like the Swiss thing where you need a majority of both the people and the cantons. Except if a region can leave a country, why can't a city leave the region? City secession sounds absurd to me and I can't say why.

My tentative rule is that a vote sticks if (1) everyone who has to live under the outcome got to vote on it, (2) the question is one voters could reasonably have an opinion about rather than a technical detail, and (3) losing it doesn't lock the losers out of future votes. Test that against the list — patch it or replace it — and then tell me what it says about cases I haven't listed: a citizens' assembly recommendation put out to a popular vote, a referendum that makes all future referendums binding, and a rule like one in my state where if the official cost estimate turns out to be more than 10% off two years down the line, the measure automatically goes back on the ballot (bad kind of re-run or good kind?). And say plainly which of my verdicts your principle has to give up — if it saves all of them, tell me what the extra machinery is costing me.
