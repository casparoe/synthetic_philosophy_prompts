ok so my jurisprudence reading group has been stuck on the same thing for a month and I want to see whether the two sides can actually be made to talk instead of just shouting "that's not valid!" at each other.

Here's the setup I want you to work inside — don't swap it for a real constitution, the made-up one is the point.

There's a small body, call it the Concord Assembly, operating under a founding charter. Article 12 of the charter says (paraphrase) that the charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the Assembly, and that Article 12 is itself amendable under that procedure. Year one: the Assembly passes Amendment 1 by two-thirds, replacing Article 12 with a new Article 12 that requires only a simple majority — and adding, in the same instrument, that the new Article 12 may not itself be amended. Year two: the Assembly passes Amendment 2 by simple majority, deleting the entrenchment sentence. Year three: by simple majority it passes Amendment 3, which dissolves the charter outright and adopts a new one whose basic rule is just that whatever the Assembly resolves is in force.

Two camps in the group:

Camp A, the derivationists (Ross-flavoured): validity flows down from a basic rule that nothing inside the system can confer. A rule that purports to repeal the very rule that is the source of its own validity either fails outright, or wasn't an amendment at all but a revolution wearing an amendment's clothes, and what "cures" it afterwards is not the old rule but everyone's subsequent going-along. On this view Amendment 1 already strains things, and Amendment 3 is a break in legal continuity, not a change within the system.

Camp B, the practice people (Hart-flavoured): the rule of recognition is a social practice among officials, not an axiom, and legal validity isn't logical deduction from it. There's no more contradiction in a rule being altered by the procedure it authorises than in a game where the players vote to change the voting rule. What makes Amendments 2 and 3 in force is that the officials whose convergent practice constitutes the system treat them as in force — and that treatment is constitutive, not merely evidence.

What I want is the joint statement these two could both put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps accept about the Concord — about what Article 12 said, about what happened in years one through three, about what the Assembly's own dispute body would most likely rule and whether that settles anything, about what continuity and validity are doing here. I want claims that actually constrain what comes later, not throat-clearing like "both sides agree law matters."

2. A disagreements table. Each row: the residual disagreement written twice — once in wording a Camp A person would accept as a fair statement of their own position, once in Camp B's wording — then a third column with the shortest neutral formulation of the thing they're actually disputing.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling one would move at least one side. I suspect one is whether validity is transitive and deductive or procedural, and another is that continuing-versus-self-embracing business about whether a rule-making power can bind its own successors. For each crux, name the concrete episode in the Concord's history — or invent a new one in the same setting — that both camps agree bears on it, and write down each camp's prediction about it in advance, before the argument resumes. Amendment 1's entrenchment sentence looks like a good candiate: a self-embracing reading makes it stick, a continuing reading lets year three walk straight over it, and I have a feeling the two camps don't line up with those readings the way I've been assuming, so if they don't, say so and show me the mismatch.

4. Tell me plainly which parts of the fight are verbal — where "valid" or "amendment" means something different on each side and the dispute would evaporate under an agreed vocabulary — and which parts would survive any vocabulary they both accepted. And if you think there's a real case in this setting where the two camps reach opposite verdicts about whether some particular amendment is currently in force, give it and argue it rather than gesturing at it.

5. At the end, tell me where this framing of mine is most likely to be a bad map. Which argument or consideration would make you redraft the whole document — collapse the camps into one, split off a third camp, or say I've picked the wrong cruxes? And flag the one or two lines in your agreed-claims list that a serious derivationist would most likely refuse to sign, and why.

No bibliography, no reading list, no "see also" — argue it from the Concord. And don't sand the disagreement down to look tidy: if the honest outcome is that they converge on four claims and everything else survives, that's the answer I want.
