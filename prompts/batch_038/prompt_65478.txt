I am the handling editor for methods and theory at a mid-ranking journal of evolutionary biology: roughly eighteen hundred submissions a year, an impact factor in the low single digits, and a referee pool of working empirical biologists who are unpaid, who give us perhaps two evenings per report, and none of whom has any formal training in philosophy of science. A substantial minority of what crosses my desk — perhaps forty manuscripts a year — makes an adaptationist claim of the familiar shape, that some morphological, behavioural or life-history trait exists because it was selected for a particular function, and the reports I receive are almost useless as a check on such claims. They polarise into two failure modes. In one, the referee invokes Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels critique as a kind of rhetorical cudgel, accuses the authors of telling an unfalsifiable story, and then declines to say what evidence would in fact have satisfied them, which makes the report impossible for me to act on and impossible for the authors to revise against. In the other, the referee accepts the claim because the adaptive narrative is internally coherent and fits the natural history, without noticing that no alternative explanation was ever excluded. I want a decision procedure I can circulate to referees alongside our existing submission guidelines: a set of questions to be worked through in a fixed order on any manuscript whose central claim is that a trait is an adaptation for some function.

The procedure should be grounded in what the spandrels critique actually argues rather than in its reputation — the absence of null hypotheses, the practice of responding to a failed adaptive hypothesis with a further adaptive hypothesis rather than with a hypothesis of a different kind, the neglect of architectural by-products, allometric consequences of size, and phylogenetic inheritance as rival explanations of present form — but it should equally be built to survive the standard replies to that critique, and it must not be a device by which no adaptationist claim could ever pass. For each question, state plainly what would count as a sufficient answer and what would count as a fatal gap, and say which questions can be settled from the manuscript and its data alone and which require experiments or comparative analyses the authors may not have done and could not reasonably have done. I also want the procedure to force a referee to specify which of three questions the manuscript is answering, because conflation of these is, in my experience, the single largest source of unfair reports: what explains the origin of the trait, what explains its current maintenance, and whether the trait enhances fitness now irrespective of either of the former. A paper may legitimately answer only the third and be criticised only for the claim it actually makes; and the distinction between a trait that arose by selection for the role it now plays and a trait that merely happens to be useful now must survive into the checklist, since referees routinely treat the demonstration of current utility as though it established historical origin. Mark explicitly the steps where judgement cannot be eliminated and must be returned to the referee as a matter of confidence rather than procedure — how much weight to place on a phylogenetic comparative method whose tree or model of trait evolution is uncertain, for instance — and be candid about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. Finally, flag anywhere the procedure risks degenerating into a demand that the authors disprove every conceivable alternative, which would simply invert the original vice.

Please then run the procedure on three hypothetical manuscripts. The first argues that the direction of shell coiling in a certain intertidal gastropod is an adaptation to heat load at low tide, on the strength of a latitudinal correlation in coiling direction and measurements of heating rates in three-dimensional-printed model shells of reversed chirality. The second combines a phylogenetically controlled comparison across some seventy species, a field manipulation that removes the trait, and an explicitly stated null model of drift and size allometry, and concludes that a colour pattern in a lizard is maintained by selection for crypsis rather than being a by-product of body size; I should tell you that my own published work defends the by-product reading, so I want to see whether the procedure can hand me a verdict I will not welcome and still explain why the claim is adequately supported. The third concerns human social behaviour, arguing that a widespread moral emotion is an adaptation for coalition formation, supported by cross-cultural frequency data and a computational model showing that the trait would be favoured under plausible ancestral conditions; here the comparative and phylogenetic apparatus on which much of the procedure rests is simply unavailable, and I want to see either a principled adaptation of the questions or an honest statement that the procedure cannot adjudicate the claim. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a long email that I could forward to referees more or less unchanged: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold, no tables, and no summary diagram at the end. Signpost the steps in words instead of layout. I am not after a survey of the adaptationism controversy or a history of the debate beyond whatever each step needs by way of justification, and please do not close with suggestions for further reading.
