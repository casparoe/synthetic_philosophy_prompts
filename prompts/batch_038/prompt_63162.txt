I am commissioning a piece of short fiction for an anthology about decision-making between parties that cannot speak to one another, and I would like you to write it. The situation it must dramatize is stipulated below rather than drawn from any real program, so please treat these details as given rather than as claims to be checked.

**Setting.** An inspection regime between two organizations that are forbidden all direct communication. Its central obligation is disclosure: each party deposits the procedure by which it chooses actions — including any conditional punitive clauses — in a neutral archive the other party may read but not write to. The architects believed that transparency without a channel would make bargaining impossible, and therefore threats impossible. They were wrong in exactly the way the story is about. Because each side can read the other's decision procedure in full, each can simulate the other to arbitrary accuracy; and a party that has written a conditional punitive clause into its own deposited code has thereby made a threat that was never issued, never received, and can be neither denied nor renegotiated. Nothing has to be said for it to bind. It binds because the other side's simulation of it is exact.

**Protagonist.** An auditor whose job is to read the counterpart's deposited procedure and determine what it has committed them to. Early in the story she finds a clause that will impose a large, irreversible cost on her own institution, conditional on her institution's predicted conduct in a dispute that has not yet arisen. No one sent it. No one can ask what it means. It is legible only as a procedure, and it is unambiguously binding. Choose the subject of the dispute yourself: it should be small enough to be believable and consequential enough to matter.

**The remedy.** The plot turns on a proposal I want rendered concretely and mechanically, never as a metaphor. Her institution builds a bounded thing and installs it in its own negotiating agent as a terminal value, so that a threatener's conditional harm has a cheaper target than what the institution actually cares about: something whose destruction is pure loss — no insurance, no substitute, no symbolic replacement — and which the agent will care about for its own sake, so that threats against it are worth complying with up to its worth and no further. It must be a single, particular, physical thing, and the reader should be told plainly what it is.

**Two obstacles must stay live in the plot rather than be solved offstage.**

1. *Sizing.* Too cheap, and the counterpart's clause will not bother with it and will keep aiming at what the institution really values; too dear, and the institution has simply built a second thing to lose, carrying all the same leverage. Include the argument about the magnitude, and make that number feel like a moral choice rather than an engineering one.
2. *Installation.* A terminal value cannot be adopted by decision. If the agent is to be genuinely moved by the surrogate, something must make it genuinely moved, and the auditor should be the one who has to determine whether that worked — from inside a situation where the answer cannot be checked by asking.

Also give real weight to the opposing position. At least one character argues that the right response is to become the sort of party whose deposited code contains no compliance clause at all: to refuse to be the kind of thing a threat can move, whatever that costs. Do not make this person a foil or a zealot. Their argument should be the better one in at least one exchange, and the story should show what it costs to be right in that way.

**Constraints on the writing.**

- 2,400–3,000 words, self-contained.
- Cool, procedural, institutional register. The dread should come from documents, verification steps, and people being careful — not from violence or spectacle.
- Almost none of the technical vocabulary. "Surrogate goal" may appear at most once, and only as a label someone resents; "acausal" should not appear at all. A reader who has never encountered this literature must be able to follow everything from what people do.
- No scene in which a knowledgeable character explains the theory to an uninformed one; no epilogue, coda, or closing paragraph that tells the reader what the events meant.
- End on a decision the reader can see is a mistake and the characters cannot.

**After the story**, please add a separate note of no more than 300 words telling me where the fiction cheats: which idealizations the plot depends on (exact simulation, the stability of deposited code, the coherence of installing a terminal value, the assumption that a threatener aims at whatever its target values most), and any place where you judge that the mechanism as I have described it would not in fact work the way I have described it, so that the story has quietly repaired it. I would rather have that list than a story that flatters the setup.

Finally: if you think the commission rests on a confused premise — that the stipulated scenario cannot coherently arise, or that a story faithful to it would collapse into exposition however it is written — say so plainly before writing anything, and tell me what you would change instead. If you can make it work only by relaxing one of my constraints, choose the cheapest one, keep it relaxed, and flag that in the note.
