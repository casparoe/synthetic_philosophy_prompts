I'm 71 and retired three years ago after a career in autonomic pharmacology — most of my working life went into looking at what adrenaline does to rats and to people's hands, which you'd think would be relevant here and mostly isn't. Now I sit in on a philosophy reading group that meets Tuesday nights in the back room of a pub, and we've just spent six weeks on the question of what an emotion actually is, and the labels have done my head in. Judgment theories, perception theories, feeling theories, constructivism, and everyone in the group claims a different one, but when you hand them a case they answer in ways that don't match their label at all. So I want to build a sorting instrument, and I'd like you to write it: a decision tree, call it eight to twelve branch points, that takes a reader from "I have emotions, obviously" to a named position, where every node is a concrete case or a plain yes/no claim they can answer without knowing which view the answer leads to. No node may be phrased in terms of the labels themselves — nothing like "do you think emotions are judgments?" — because that just asks people to pick a team and tells you nothing.

Here are the branch points I care about, and I'd like them in there in some form: whether the state can be wrong about the world the way a perception can, or only inappropriate to it; whether a person can have an emotion they sincerly disavow, like the arachnophobe who tells you calmly that the spider is harmless and then won't go in the room; whether feeling the bodily change is necessary for the emotion, merely typical of it, or beside the point; whether you could in principle assemble the state pharmacologically, with the full peripheral signature and no object at all, and what each position has to say about that specimen; and whether the categories carve nature at the joints or are to some extent local, learned, language-shaped. Use cases like the one where somebody works out from their own behavior that they must have been furious; the bereaved spouse who reports the grief as flat and objectless for months on end; the shaky-bridge sort of situation where the arousal gets recruited by whatever story is handy; and — as best I recall from the spinal cord injury literature — patients whose fear and rage got quieter after the lesion but never went away entirely. That last one is the one I keep bringing up and nobody in the group can answer cleanly, so I'd like the tree to route through it rather than around it.

The leaves should be the actual positions: a strong cognitivist or judgment view, a non-doxastic perceptual view, a Jamesian feeling view, some hybrid that splits the emotion into components, a constructivist or social-kind view, and an eliminativist who thinks "emotion" doesn't pick out a natural kind at all. If one combination of answers turns out to have nobody in it, leave that leaf standing and give me a sentence on why it's empty — is the position incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored? Wherever two leaves answer every node identically, say so out loud and tell me what, if anything, could separate them; my own suspicion is that the perceptual and the feeling views collapse into each other on most of these cases and that this is a real feature of the debate rather than a flaw in my tree, but talk me out of it if you can. And be explicit about where you're least sure: which node do you think is a badly posed question, which placement of a view is genuinely contestable, and what argument or piece of evidence would make you redraw the whole thing? I'd much rather have a shaky map with the shaky parts marked than a confident one.
