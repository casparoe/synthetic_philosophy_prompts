So, context: I'm a platform/SRE engineer, no philosophy background at all, and for the last year I've been volunteering with a distributed group of maybe forty people who do AI-safety-adjacent coordination work — writing and reviewing red-team findings, some of them at labs, most of them not. Six months ago it split roughly down the middle and I've been roped into drafting something both halves could actually sign, apparently because I'm the neutral one (I mostly just maintain the docs and the CI, so nobody hates me yet). The thing that set it off: one member published a detailed writeup of a jailbreak they'd found against a hosted model without telling anyone in the group first, and it got scraped and reused inside a week; then it came out that at almost the same time a different member had been sitting on a separate finding for eight months waiting for consensus, by which point three strangers had rediscovered it and published anyway. Each camp now treats its own incident as the paradigm case and the other side's as a weird edge case, and they've been arguing in circles ever since.

The two camps, in their own preferred labels. Camp A calls itself consultation-first: they think the answer to the unilateralist's curse is a genuine ex ante norm — if your estimate that an action is net-good is more optimistic than your peers', you hold off and ask — and that the strength of the norm has to scale with group size, because it only takes one optimist and the group's action distribution is way worse than any member's private estimate. They think that's close to a moral obligation, and that "I genuinely thought it was fine" is precisely the excuse the curse predicts you'd have. Camp B calls itself the reversibilists, and thinks a blanket consultation norm is status-quo bias wearing a safety costume: it kills positive-expected-value projects, it concentrates power in whoever is slowest to respond, and the variable that should actually key the norm is whether the action is a one-way door and whether the downside lands on people who didn't consent to it. Their stock example is incident response — when prod is on fire you want the person on call to act immediately without convening a committee, and a group that demands consensus before everything will get out-acted by groups that don't. Here's my own idea, which neither camp likes and which I want the document to handle explicitly rather than quietly drop: warning people is itself a one-way door. Publishing "this capability exists and here's roughly how" is a unilateral act with an irreversible, asymmetric payoff, so if you apply camp A's norm to disclosure you just regenerate the same curse one level up — the person who breaks the silence about the silence is now the unilateralist. I've been calling it the second-order curse, and my hunch is it means neither camp's rule can be stated purely in terms of how optimistic you are relative to your peers.

What I want is the joint statement these two camps could both put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, not a summary of the fight. Format: first a numbered list of claims both sides can sign — including claims about the mechanism of the curse itself, which I think they actually agree on more than they admit — then a disagreements table with columns for the crux, camp A's formulation in wording camp A would accept, camp B's formulation in wording camp B would accept, what test or case both sides would agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Then a short section saying which of the disagreements you think are merely verbal and why. Please include my second-order thing somewhere honest: either as an agreed claim, as a crux of its own, or as a confusion that dissolves under scrutiny — I'd rather be told it's a confusion than have it flattered. Two more asks, since I can't evaluate this stuff myself: say which of the cruxes you think is genuinely decidable and which ones will still be being argued about in five years, and flag anywhere you had to soften the wording to make it signable, because that's where the document is lying. And tell me what would change your mind — which argument, or which detail about the two incidents, would make you draft this differently or drop a crux you listed — plus where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to break.
