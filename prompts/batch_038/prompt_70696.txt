The question the candidate took was this.

**Question 3 (30 marks).** Max Black asks us to imagine a universe containing exactly two spheres of chemically pure iron, each a mile in diameter, a mile apart, and nothing else. Explain what the Identity of Indiscernibles claims and why Black thinks this universe refutes it. Then consider the haecceitist reply — that the spheres are distinguished by their primitive thisnesses — together with the objection that this reply rescues the principle only at the cost of making it trivial. In what scope must the principle be stated if it is to be both non-trivial and defensible, and is a principle in that scope worth having? Argue for your answer.

The script, transcribed without alteration other than the paragraph numbers:

(1) The Identity of Indiscernibles (PII) states that there cannot be two numerically distinct things which share all their properties: if x and y have every property in common, then x is identical with y. Leibniz treated it as a basic principle of his system and supported it by the principle of sufficient reason: were two individuals exactly alike, God would have had no reason for making two rather than one, or for placing them differently, and since nothing is without a reason, such a pair cannot exist. PII is the converse of Leibniz's Law, the indiscernibility of identicals, which is a theorem of the logic of identity and is not what is at issue here. The two are worth separating precisely because their statuses differ so sharply: one is a logical truth, the other a substantive metaphysical thesis that might have been false.

(2) Black's universe is meant to show that PII is not a necessary truth. He asks us to conceive a world containing nothing but two spheres of chemically pure iron, each a mile in diameter and a mile from its nearest neighbour, alike in colour, temperature, chemical composition and every other quality. The description involves no contradiction, so the world is possible; and if it is possible then there are two things that share all their properties, which is what PII denies. The force of the example is that Black is not describing anything exotic — nothing about persistence through time, nothing about vague boundaries — just perfect symmetry.

(4) A first response is that the spheres differ after all, in their spatial relations: each is a mile from the other, and each occupies a place the other does not. But this fails as it stands, because in a perfectly symmetric world the relational properties come in matched pairs. The property of being one mile from an iron sphere is had by both, and although it is true that one sphere is a mile from A and the other is a mile from B, that is only a difference in the labels we attach, since nothing in the world fixes which sphere is A. So the counterexample is not dissolved by pointing at relations.

(4) The haecceitist reply goes further and denies that the spheres are indiscernible at all. Adams holds that each individual has a thisness, the property of being that very individual, which is not qualitative and not analysable into anything qualitative. Sphere A has the property of being A and sphere B lacks it, so the two share all their qualitative properties but not all their properties, and PII holds. The standard objection is that this makes PII empty: any two things whatever differ in their thisnesses, so a principle counting thisnesses as properties merely restates numerical diversity in the idiom of property-sharing. That is the same content as Leibniz's Law read in the opposite direction, and it means Black's universe — in which the two spheres are indiscernible yet identical — is a counterexample to Leibniz's Law itself, a far more alarming result than the loss of a contingent principle about the world.

(5) The thisness need not be mysterious. On Adams's account the spheres are individuated by the positions in space they occupy: absolute space does the individuating work that qualities cannot, and thisness is simply the name for that fact, which is why Adams's position is often described as a form of realism about space. Hacking presses in the other direction: if the case really requires individuals of this bare sort, then the world Black describes cannot be coherently described at all, and Black begs the question by stipulating that there are two spheres when the stipulation was supposed to do the work of showing there could be two. On this reading the dispute is not about whether PII is true but about whether a symmetric world is possible.

(6) There is a middle way, which the framing of the question invites. The spheres are not qualitatively discernible, but they are weakly discernible: the relation being one mile from is irreflexive and symmetric and holds between them, and an irreflexive relation suffices to tell two things apart without any appeal to thisness. Stated in that scope — any two distinct things must be discernible by some irreflexive relation — PII is neither empty nor refuted by Black's world, since the two spheres do stand in such a relation. It follows that the exchange between Black and Adams is resolved in Adams's favour: PII is true if thisnesses are counted as properties and false if they are not, and since the version that counts them says nothing, the honest verdict is that Leibniz's principle is false after all. Nor does the collapse stop there. Leibniz's argument from sufficient reason was an argument for the principle, and once we see that the principle admits no non-trivial formulation the argument loses its target; so the triviality objection disposes of the rationalist case for PII as well, whether or not the symmetric world turns out to be possible.
