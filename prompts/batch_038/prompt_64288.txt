Taking a philosophy of biology seminar this term and I have to write a paper on the units of selection debate, but before I commit to a thesis I want to know what my own judgments actually add up to. Here's where I land, case by case:

- Mouse t-haplotype: a heterozygous male passes it on to something like 90%+ of his offspring, homozygotes are sterile or don't develop, and it still hangs around at low frequency in wild mice. Here I say selection is acting on the gene, not on the mouse.
- Wolbachia: maternally inherited bacterium that messes with host reproduction to bias its own transmission, at some cost to the host. This feels structurally identical to the t-haplotype to me — a replicator beating its vehicle — but my gut says no, Wolbachia is just a parasite and the infected insect is still the individual that's selected. I genuinely can't say what the difference is.
- Dictyostelium slugs, where chimeric slugs form and some genotypes shirk the stalk and end up as spores instead: I say the slug isn't an individual. It's genotypes competing, full stop.
- A honeybee colony that swarms: I say this one IS an individual and a real unit of selection, even though it doesn't reproduce by making copies of itself — it makes a queen and some drones who go off and build something new. That's supposed to be different from the slug and I can't articulate why.
- Worker policing, where workers eat other workers' eggs: I want to call this the colony suppressing selection at a lower level, not competition among workers.
- A tumor cell lineage inside somebody: obviously heritable variation with differential reproduction, and I feel like it counts as a unit of selection, but I also want to say nothing is really evolving there since it all dies with the body. Those two thoughts can't both survive.
- The Pseudomonas flourescens microcosm work, where a mat-forming strain takes over the surface of a static flask and then gets eaten from within by non-mat cheaters, and the whole setup only persists if you propagate the mats as units: I call that genuine group selection. But then I also want to say that human cultural group selection — groups with costly norms outlasting or outbreeding other groups — is NOT genuine group selection and is usually just kin selection with sloppy bookkeeping.
- Yeast [PSI+], where the heritable thing is a misfolded protein and not DNA at all: I say it can be a unit of selection, which kind of terrifies me because it means whatever criterion I'm using isn't genetic.

My tentative principle is: X is a unit of selection when differences in fitness among Xs are caused by properties of the Xs themselves and get passed on to the Xs' descendants. But "properties of the Xs themselves" is carrying the entire weight and I can't cash it out.

So: does any existing way of slicing this — replicator vs. vehicle, interactor, reproducer with a bottlenecked life cycle, MLS1 vs. MLS2, or just reading off the covariance terms in a Price equation decomposition — deliver all of these, or most of them? Walk me through the one or two best candidates, show me exactly where each one gets my verdicts right and where it has to overturn one, and tell me whether the overturning is defensible or whether I should keep the verdict and drop the principle. Then give me what the account you land on predicts for cases I haven't listed — something like a mitochondrion, a B chromosome, or a plasmid moving between bacteria by conjugation — because I want to know if it's going to embarrass me in section.
