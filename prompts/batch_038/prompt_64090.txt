In my dissertation I defend the Stalinesque half of Dennett and Kinsbourne's (1992) distinction about the color phi phenomenon reported by Kolers and von Grünau (1976): when a red dot is flashed and, roughly a hundred milliseconds later, a green dot is flashed nearby, and the subject reports smooth motion with the color changing somewhere along the path, the correct description is that the experience of that motion is composed only once the second dot has arrived. There was never a veridical experience of a stationary red dot for memory to edit. What makes my version more than a redescription, I think, is an extra commitment: content is fixed at the time of report, and the report does not merely register conscious content but partly constitutes it. Reportability is not an epistemic bridge to experience; it is where the question of what was experienced gets settled.

I worry that this commitment also insulates the view. Every report of a mid-path change is explained by postdictive composition, and every failure to produce one can be handed to the Orwellian alternative. So I want the exposure map rather than the defense. What would count against the position? In particular:

- Which results, arguments, or intuitions should move me, and how do they rank from fatal to merely awkward? I have in mind at least the possibility that the reported mid-path change is an artifact of how subjects are questioned, and the possibility that postdiction in this display is a claim about judgment rather than about experience at all.
- Is there any non-report evidence that could break the tie — decoding the first dot's color from visual cortex during the interval, a speeded color task cued to fire before the second dot appears, a reaction-time signature of a composition delay? I want to know whether such measures would genuinely discriminate Stalinesque from Orwellian revision, or whether both accounts can absorb them once consciousness is decoupled from availability.
- Does the position stand or fall with Libet's backward-referral results (Libet et al. 1979), and if those results are an artifact of comparing reports across two stimulus sites, how much of my view survives?
- What does the view predict that its rivals do not: Orwellian revision, and the deflationary option on which nothing anomalous happens and subjects simply misdescribe an unremarkable percept?
- Which of these would a committed postdictivist concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss?
- Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that a defect here or an appropriate response to a distinction that may not carve anything?

Please keep what is settled — that subjects do report mid-path changes under suitable conditions, and that the reported order is anomalous relative to the stimulus order — visibly separate from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading of Libet or of postdiction as the consensus.

Format, in this order: "The view, stated without metaphor" (~150 words); "Fatal or near-fatal" (~400 words); "Awkward but survivable" (~300 words); "Distinctive predictions" (~250 words); "Concessions and the unfalsifiability charge" (~300 words); "Settled versus contested" (~150 words).
