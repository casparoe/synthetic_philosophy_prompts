At my uni's effective altruism group two people got into a pretty heated arguement and asked me to referee, which I dodged. Context: a member had just explained why she's taking a consulting job instead of going into global health policy — earn to give, donate half a six-figure salary, buy bednets. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Nadia said the choice is obvious: the consulting route turns into a very large sum over a career, which buys nets and prevents malaria deaths you can actually count, whereas policy work produces reports. (2) Theo said that's measurability bias dressed up as a comparison — she isn't weighing policy against nets, she's weighing whatever survives her measurement tools against whatever doesn't. (3) Theo then gave a sharper version: measurement is endogenous to attention, so the causes that come out looking best-evidenced are exactly the ones that already attracted money and staff, which means an evidence-first filter systematically picks crowded causes with low marginal return per dollar. (4) Nadia said that argument can't be right on its own terms — if he can't measure marginal returns either, he isn't steering toward less crowded causes, he's steering blind, and blindness has no direction; besides, pandemic preparedness was perfectly legible and neglected for years. (5) Theo said he never claimed to know the returns, only that her numbers are false precision, and if the real choice is between confident guessing and admitted guessing the second is more honest. (6) Nadia said honesty isn't a decision procedure.

Can you rate each move (1)–(6) from clearly wrong to clearly right, one or two sentences each? Then say who's ahead at the end and what neither of them got at.
