Help. I set out to make the Lucas–Penrose line respectable enough to teach without embarrassment and instead produced something whose conclusion is that PA proves its own consistency, which is either the greatest result in the history of logic or — much more likely — a howler I can no longer locate. Staring at it has stopped working, so: where does it break?

The setup. Call an agent A arithmetically ideal when

(a) everything A establishes about arithmetic is true;
(b) A's establishings are exactly the theorems of some consistent recursively axiomatisable theory, with a provability predicate Prov_A that A can itself pick out — so for any sentence φ, A can establish φ iff Prov_A(⌜φ⌝);
(c) A can do everything PA can do.

Nothing there mentions flesh, or consciousness, or anything else the mechanist is entitled to balk at. PA read as an agent whose establishings are its theorems satisfies (a)–(c), on the assumption that PA is sound; so does any sound r.e. extension of it; and if the mechanist is right about me, so do I.

Now the argument.

1. Let A be arithmetically ideal.
2. A establishes things only by proof. A proof is a finite object, and A can survey it, checking each step; what the survey shows is that every step preserves truth. So A can see that it never establishes a falsehood, hence never both φ and ¬φ. So A can establish Con(A).
3. By (b), "A can establish Con(A)" just means Prov_A(⌜Con(A)⌝). A proves its own consistency.
4. That is Gödel's second theorem denied, for arbitrary A. So there are no arithmetically ideal agents.
5. But PA is one if it's sound, and I am one if I'm the sort of ideal reasoner Lucas needs. So (4) is not something I can accept.
6. Therefore (2) fails, at least for formal systems — which of course we already knew.
7. But Lucas needs (2) for me, and he needs (b) to convert my establishing into the machine's proving. Without (b) the Gödel sentence I supposedly see isn't a counterexample to the machine capturing me, since the machine might prove it too and I'd just be the machine doing so.
8. So Lucas needs (2) true of me and false of PA, and on the mechanist's own account the only difference between me and PA is which theory I happen to be.
9. So either (2) is false for me as well, and the anti-mechanist argument never gets started; or ideality in the sense of (2) is something flesh has and formal systems cannot, in which case (2) was never a premise about reasoners generally but a premise about humans, and deploying it against the mechanist assumes the mechanist is wrong.

The bit I can't get past is that 9 looks like a dilemma with no decent horn, and yet step 2 sounds perfectly sane read aloud — surveying proofs is what mathematicians do all day, and nobody thinks they're thereby licensed to write down a proof of their own consistency. So why does 2 sound fine to me?

What I want:

- Name the step to drop and say exactly what's wrong with it. If it's an equivocation, tell me between what and what; if it's a suppressed assumption, state it out loud.
- Explain why 2 seemed to survive the transfer from me to PA. I keep feeling that there's a difference between surveying a system from outside and proving within it, but I can't cash that out without already assuming I'm not the system being surveyed, which is precisely what's in dispute.
- Price the alternatives rather than just pointing at them. If I give up 2, what is left of Lucas and Penrose worth defending at all? If I give up (b) — deny that "A can establish φ" ever comes to "Prov_A(⌜φ⌝)" — does that cost me anything I actually care about, or is that just what the anti-mechanist should have said from the start? And what if I give up (a) instead and concede I might be inconsistent?
- A colleague proposes this patch: keep 2 but restrict it to systems A is *presented with* from outside, so that I can establish Con(F) for any F I'm handed — including, if mechanism is true, the F that is me — whereas PA never gets handed itself. Is that principled, or is it engineered solely to block step 4? And does it require me to recognise which F I am? If it does, I dont see how that's possible; if it doesn't, I don't see how the restriction is meant to bite.

I'm obviously not giving up the second incompleteness theorem. And I'd rather keep 2 than concede that the entire debate since 1961 was about nothing. I just can't see how to do both.
