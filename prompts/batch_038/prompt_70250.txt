Consider the Letter Spirit project — the cognitive-science research program, associated with Douglas Hofstadter's group, into what makes every handwritten a recognizably an a. Its working thesis is that the essence of a letterform is not a template, a rule, or a set of necessary and sufficient conditions, but a family of admissible shapes related to one another by small, principled shifts; a model built in this spirit starts from a deliberately impoverished grid alphabet and attempts to generate entire alphabets in one coherent style without ever having been told what any individual letter is.

Please write a joke about this material. I would like it to satisfy the following constraints.

1. The punchline must depend on the substantive content of the position — that letterness is a cloud of possible forms rather than a definition — rather than on the generic observation that categories are hard to pin down or that cognitive science is complicated.

2. The following may not supply the joke's engine: puns or wordplay on type, font, bold, italic, serif, sans, glyph, character, script, or letter; illegible handwriting; and the stock frames of the walks-into-a-bar joke and the academic committee meeting, unless the frame itself is doing real work.

3. The joke should be intelligible and mildly amusing to a reader whose only background is knowing that a typeface is a set of matching letter shapes, and should deliver a second, sharper laugh to a reader who knows that the interesting difficulty lies in generating a whole alphabet coherently while having no definition of any single letter to appeal to.

4. Length: no more than roughly 120 words, with a legible setup and exactly one turn. Dialogue, a one-liner, or a short scene are all acceptable.

After the joke, add three or four sentences identifying precisely which feature of the view the punchline turns on, and stating what the joke would lose if that feature were removed from the setup.
