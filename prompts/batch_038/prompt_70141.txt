I'm prepping a session for a philosophy-of-history reading group and I've ended up with an argument I can't dismantle, mostly becuase half the room will happily accept the conclusion. Here's the arguement.

(1) Browning and Goldhagen agree on what Reserve Police Battalion 101 did — the middle-aged Hamburg reservists, the shootings at Józefów, Trapp telling his men that anyone who didn't feel up to it could step out of the ranks, and fewer than a dozen of roughly five hundred doing so. They disagree only about the explanation. Browning says conformity, obedience to authority, careerism, and the numbing routine of the killing. Goldhagen says a specifically German eliminationist antisemitism that made the killing make sense to the men who did it.

(2) Goldhagen's explanation, if it's right, is also a situational one. Being raised inside a culture that hands you a belief is a feature of the environment you were dropped into, exactly like being handed a uniform, a squad, and a commander's order on a summer morning. Neither is a trait of character.

(3) So whichever side of that historiographical debate wins, the men's behavior is explained by factors outside their characters.

(4) If a behavior is explained by factors outside the agent's character, then it isn't explained by character — there's no explanatory work left for the dispositions to do.

(5) That's precisely the point the situationists in moral psychology pressed from Milgram and Zimbardo: cross-situationally consistent character traits don't earn their keep as explanations, and what doesn't earn its explanatory keep isn't there.

(6) An agent is blameworthy for what she does only if the doing issues from her own stable dispositions, in a way that lets the action say something about her.

(7) So the men of Battalion 101 are not blameworthy for what they did.

(8) Nothing in (1)–(7) actually mentions the Holocaust, or Germans, or 1942. Substitute any act by any agent: everyone's dispositions come from genes and upbringing they didn't choose, so nothing anyone does issues from a character that isn't itself explained entirely from outside.

(9) So nobody is ever blameworthy for anything.

The trouble is that (9) is a live position with serious defenders, so I can't just refuse the conclusion and call it a day, and I can't find the step where things slip. Which one do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you — do you lose the situational explanation of Józefów, or lose the ability to say that Trapp's offer to step out was morally significant, or lose something else? And separately: does the argument come out stronger or weaker if Goldhagen is right about the battalion rather than Browning? I'd really like to keep (1) and (2) if I can. I'm suspicious of (4) but I can't articulate why.
