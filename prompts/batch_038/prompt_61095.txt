I keep running into an accounting rule that seems to contradict the first thing every econ textbook tells you, and I can't work out what it's actually committed to. Under US GAAP a factory or a machine sits on the books at what you paid for it minus depreciation — historical cost — not at what it's worth today. If it turns out to be worth much less, you write it down, and that reduced number becomes its new cost basis permanently; the rules explicitly prohibit writing it back up later, even if the asset recovers and its worth more again. IFRS, covering basically the same assets, does let you reverse the write-down. Then the very same set of financial statements carries a trading portfolio at today's market price, with no reference at all to what was paid for it.

So what picture of value does the American rule presuppose? My hunch is that it treats the past as only allowed to push the number down, never up — history gets a veto but not a vote — and that this isn't really a claim about assets at all but about management's optimism. But I can't tell whether it's a metaphysics of value (what you spent genuinely bears on what the thing is worth) or an epistemology of testimony (a write-down is the kind of thing you can verify and a write-up never is). And is this a case of honoring sunk costs, which we're all told is irrational, or the opposite — an institutional ritual for burying them, after which they can never come back?

Tell me which rival views the rule quietly rules out, whether the path-dependent treatment of the factory and the path-independent treatment of the stock portfolio can be licensed by a single theory or need two, where the practice is just underdetermined so that several theories would fit it equally well, and what would have to change if you rebuilt the whole thing on a strictly forward-looking view where only expected future cash flows count.
