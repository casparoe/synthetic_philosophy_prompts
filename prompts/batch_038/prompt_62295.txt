I've been working part-time for about a year in a university archives, processing collections, and I keep getting wiplash from the ground rules, so I want to figure out what they actually assume. The rules I'm trained on are basically: arrange and describe everything by the office that created it, never by subject and never by person; never mix records from two different creators in one folder even when they're the same kind of document about the same event; keep each creator's records together and separate from everyone else's. Our finding aids list series by administrative function — "Correspondence, 1948–1971," "Case files, 1962–1980," "Subject files, Planning Office" — and there is no name index at all. So if you want to find one specific woman who got evicted in 1969, you can't search for her; you have to already know which office touched her, and then hope she's in that office's papers. Researchers get told some version of "we can't refile it that way, it would destroy the context" and mostly they shrug and go read the annual reports instead.

The thing that finally made me write this down: we just finished processing something like four hundred rent-assistance and relief case files that came to us from a county office. Between the donor agreement and state privacy rules they can only be described in the aggregate — the finding aid gives the date range, says what kinds of forms are inside, notes that some files are restricted, and does not name anybody. So the people in those boxes are, as far as our catalog is concerned, unfindable. Meanwhile the records of the county commissioners who voted on the budget line those files came out of are individually named, indexed, cross-referenced in three other collections, and cited in our exhibit labels. The institution is discoverable down to a single interoffice memo; the people it acted on aren't discoverable at all, and we're told we couldn't fix that with an index anyway because it would break provenance. Then, in the same building, I get put on a community collecting project about a neighborhood that's being torn down, and suddenly we're building "artificial collections" assembled by person and by topic, plus a vertical file of newspaper clippings and flyers we made ourselves, and nobody seems bothered that this is the exact thing we're not allowed to do to the county records. I took a historiography seminar last year where everyone kept saying "history from below" like it was a solved problem, and I can't stop wondering whether the archive's filing rules make it structurally impossible — not because anyone ever decided that, just because of how the boxes are arranged.

So what does this practice presuppose, philosophically? I don't really have the vocabulary, so reconstruct it for me. Is arrange-by-creator an epistemological claim — that a record only means something in the context of its creation, so context is what has to be preserved — or is it a claim about agency, that institutions are the things that act and individuals are only ever what gets documented? Does "no name index" presuppose that a person appearing in records isn't a subject of history but an object of administration, and does "aggregate description only" presuppose that a person is a type rather than an individual (because if a person were an individual, naming her would be the whole point)? Which rival views about who counts as a historical subject does the rule quietly rule out that we'd never notice ruling out? And please separate what the practice strictly needs from what it just happens to fit well with — I can think of at least one totally non-philosophical story, about custody, donor contracts, chain of title, and the fact that it's cheaper to describe a series than 400 people, and I want to know whether that story alone licenses everything, or whether there's a leftover that only some view about historical subjects can explain. Also: is the artificial-collection thing actually inconsistent with the rule, or does it reveal that the rule was never about truth in the first place? Finally, tell me what would have to change — not just in the finding aids but in what counts as a fact and who counts as a source — if we replaced arrange-by-creator with something person-centered, and whether the privacy restriction on those case files should be understood as protecting the people in them or as completing their erasure.
