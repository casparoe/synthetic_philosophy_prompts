Last autumn a general-interest magazine ran a long essay arguing that the whole controversy over deplatforming, public shaming, and people losing their jobs over old posts has been misdiagnosed by both sides. I picked it up because I work in trust and safety at a mid-sized platform, read it on a flight, and afterwards discussed it with a colleague who did some philosophy of law in grad school. I came away persuaded; he was not, and when I pressed him he said only that the essay was "borrowing the machinery of punishment theory without paying for it," which I could not unpack and which he has not explained since. Here is the core of the essay's argument, condensed by me but keeping its formulations as nearly as I can recall them:

> Deplatforming is punishment, and once that is granted the argument is essentially over. To punish someone is to impose a serious harm on them, deliberately, on account of something they did, by an agent who has standing to impose it. Removing a user from a platform, or an employer dismissing a worker for a post, has exactly that structure. The gap between this and a prison sentence is one of degree rather than kind: the state takes your liberty, the internet takes your audience and your livelihood. It follows that whatever conditions legitimate punishment must satisfy, these sanctions must satisfy too. Legitimate punishment must be proportionate to the wrong; must be imposed only after the accused has had a fair opportunity to answer the charge; must be governed by published standards; must be aimed at some good — deterrence, reform, or the community's expression of its own values; and must leave the punished person a way back once the term is served. Informal social sanction, as currently practised, meets none of these conditions. There is no hearing, no published standard, no ceiling on severity, and no expungement. The familiar defence — that nobody is entitled to a platform, that a private firm may set its own terms, that all of this is only more speech answering speech — is therefore not a defence at all, because it answers a question nobody asked. Whether you are permitted to do something is beside the point when what you are doing is sanctioning someone, and sanctioning them badly.

I am still inclined to think the essay is right in spirit, and I do not want you to write me a defence of pile-ons. But I would like an accuracy audit I can actually use in the next conversation with my colleague, and I do not know where the soft spots are. Could you work through the passage claim by claim, separating what is an accurate statement of how punishment is standardly analysed, what is a fair simplification for a general audience, and what is a genuine misrepresentation? I have a vague memory that a well-known mid-century legal philosopher listed several necessary conditions for something to count as punishment, and that the essay's definition quietly drops at least one of them — but I may be confusing this with something else, so please say whether the memory is right and which condition, if any, has gone missing. Then there are the internal problems I noticed but could not evaluate: whether the essay's own definition is even satisfied by its own examples, since it is not clear to me who the punishing agent is when a sanction results from a diffuse crowd plus an employer reacting under pressure; whether "the internet takes your livelihood" collapses two different acts by two different agents with different normative structures; and whether the essay can consistently list the community's expression of its values among the legitimate aims of punishment and then say informal sanction meets none of the conditions, given that social sanction looks like the most expressive kind of sanction there is.

Most of all I would like you to test the last move. The essay treats the private-rights defence as evasive, but it strikes me that the legitimacy conditions it invokes are usually justified by features specific to the state — a monopoly on hard treatment, the absence of any real possibility of exit, the law being authored by the people it binds, the special standing required before one may inflict suffering on another. If deplatforming lacks those features, the analogy may fail for reasons that are substantive rather than evasive, and the essay would be dismissing an answer to a question it very much asked. Tell me what a careful philosopher who accepts the punishment framing would say in its defence, what someone who rejects the framing entirely would say, and which of those two positions the corrected version of the argument can support. Finally, sketch what the strongest version of the essay's case looks like once the errors are repaired, and whether its conclusion — that the debate is essentially over once the framing is granted — survives in any form.
