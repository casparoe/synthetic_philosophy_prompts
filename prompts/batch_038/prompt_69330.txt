I cover archaeology and human-environment stories for a general science magazine, and I've realized that my own desk instincts about the word "collapse" don't add up to a rule. Here are my verdicts, case by case. The Mycenaean palaces around 1200 BCE: collapse, printed without hedging — the administrative system was destroyed, the script went out of use for centuries, and settlement thinned drastically. The Indus cities some centuries earlier: not collapse, but de-urbanization — towns emptied and the Indus script also stopped being used, yet villages, craft traditions, and population continued and shifted eastward. The ninth-century southern Maya lowlands: collapse at the level of the dynastic and monument-raising system, but I'd refuse the headline "the Maya collapsed," since northern centers rose and Maya-speaking peoples persist. Rapa Nui before European contact: not collapse, only the end of statue-carving and a shift in ritual authority, with the demographic trajectory disputed; the nineteenth-century slave raids and epidemics: collapse, flatly. Cahokia in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries: collapse — the largest settlement north of Mexico emptied and the surrounding region stayed thinly occupied for a long time. Chaco and the Mesa Verde region in the same era: not collapse but relocation, because the migrants' descendants are traceable in the Rio Grande pueblos and the Hopi and Zuni towns. Norse Greenland: collapse, since nothing continued there — though I balk at writing that a society collapsed when Inuit communities in the same region carried on. And western Rome: I'd write transformation for Gaul and collapse for Britain, which now strikes me as a verdict about what survives archaeologically rather than about what happened.

My tentative principle is this: call it collapse when a society loses, without replacement, the institutions that made it the particular society it was; call it migration or transformation when the same people become someone else's ancestors. Is there a principle — mine or a better one — that delivers all of these verdicts, or nearly all? State it precisely, say which clause each case motivates, and tell me what it implies for a case I haven't listed, such as the abandonment of Angkor as the court moved south, or the closing of a company town I may have to cover next year. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. And if the only principle that saves every one of them has to make "collapse" turn on how far descendants and institutions can be traced rather than on what occurred, say so and explain whether the word still earns its place in my copy.
