I have no training in logic or in computer science. This is an argument I assembled for myself over a couple of years of reading on my own, and I have now argued my way to a conclusion I do not believe. I would like a diagnosis rather than reassurance.

I began with a question I could not settle: when people say that anything a person can do by following a definite method a machine can also do, is that a discovery about the world or a decision about words? I kept reading that the Church-Turing thesis is confirmed by the fact that every formalism anyone has proposed — recursive functions, the lambda calculus, register machines, cellular automata — marks exactly the same class of functions. I also kept reading that proposed counterexamples (a device that carries out infinitely many steps in a finite time; an analog machine whose dials can be set to arbitrary real numbers) are dismissed on the grounds that they are not genuine step-by-step procedures. Both sides sounded confident, and it struck me that the second side can never lose. So I wrote this down:

(1) A claim is genuinely true or false only if some possible finding could count against it.
(2) The Church-Turing thesis says that every function calculable by a definite, humanly-followable procedure is computable by a Turing machine.
(3) "Definite, humanly-followable procedure" is not a defined term; it is an informal notion, fixed only by examples and by our sense of what belongs to it.
(4) To refute (2) one would have to exhibit a procedure of that kind whose function no Turing machine computes.
(5) Exhibiting a procedure means stating its steps exactly; and steps stated exactly are the specification of a Turing machine, or of something already known to be equivalent to one.
(6) So a candidate counterexample, stated exactly, turns out to be computable after all, and a candidate not stated exactly does not determine any function at all.
(7) Therefore no possible finding could count against (2).
(8) By (1), (2) is neither true nor false; it is a stipulation about how "procedure" is to be used, a definition in the costume of a discovery.
(9) A stipulation is not confirmed by evidence either, so the repeated coincidences among the formalisms are not evidence that (2) is true; they are only the stipulation being obeyed again and again.
(10) So the thesis is empty, and the question I actually care about — whether some method I use, of the sort I use when I do long division, or when I look at a figure and see what to do next, is beyond any machine — is not a real question.

(10) is what I cannot accept, though I have been circling it for months. I would sooner give up (1) than (5), since I cannot picture how (5) could fail: what would a counterexample be, if not an exact specification? But I suspect that concession is doing more work than I realize, and I have no way to check.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable from the inside? Then tell me what I would be committed to after rejecting it — in particular, whether the coincidences among the formalisms can be evidence for anything, and whether a device that performs infinitely many steps in a finite time is a counterexample or a change of subject. And if you think the argument is sound instead, say plainly what (10) costs someone who wants to keep the question about minds open.
