I was an analytical chemist for thirty-five years — mostly trace analysis by mass spectrometry, measuring things at levels where you're never quite certain you're measuring anything at all. Retired four years ago and promptly fell down the philosophy hole, reading after dinner. In six weeks I'm giving a 40-minute talk with Q&A at a philosophy of science research seminar at the university about forty minutes up the road. It came about because I put a short preprint somewhere online and a faculty member there read it and emailed me. I'm equal parts delighted and terrified.

The room, as I understand it, is a dozen or so people. Two committed scientific realists of the structural sort, who I suspect think underdetermination was sorted decades ago and is now a bit of a non-problem. One constructive empiricist in the van Fraassen tradition, who'll probably like where I end up but not how I get there. A Bayesian epistemologist who'll want to know why my puzzle isn't just a story about priors and likelihoods. A historian of chemistry who cares whether actual laboratory practice looked anything like what philosophers say it looked like. And a handful of grad students who know the literature cold and have never in their lives run an instrument.

What I want to argue is that permanent underdetermination isn't primarily a relation between whole rival theories and one shared body of evidence. It sits in the apparatus. I've been calling it instrumental slack: the gap between the raw signal and the reported quantity, filled by a chain of calibration assumptions and judgement calls — matrix effects, interferences, drift, whether this peak really is the analyte — which can't be individually tested inside the local experiment and which get adjusted together with the substantive claim they're supposed to be testing. Two things hang off this. First, the slack doesn't shrink, it relocates: every gain in sensitivity moves the same gap one level down, so a better instrument isn't less underdetermined, it's underdetermined about smaller things. Second, the case studies philosophers reach for are survivorship-biased — we remember the controversies where the slack eventually closed and never hear about the huge number where it didn't, because nobody wrote those up.

Argument in outline: (1) a reported number is always a signal plus an interpretive chain; (2) the chain is holistically adjustable alongside the substantive claim, so a recalcitrant result can always be re-attributed to the chain rather than to the hypothesis; (3) this doesn't get fixed by more data, because more data arrives through the same kind of chain; (4) so for a large class of quantitative claims the choice is underdetermined permanently rather than transiently — but only locally, and only about magnitudes, not about whether the entities are there. That last hedge is doing alot of the work and I know it.
