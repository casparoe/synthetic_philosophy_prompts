ok so I'm in a reading group on Keynes and there's this one guy who keeps leaning on an analogy he thinks settles the whole business. His version: risk is an urn where you know the mix of balls, uncertainty is an urn where you don't — you don't know the colours, or how many balls there are, or maybe you're not even sure there's an urn. He says once you put it that way, everything Keynes and Knight made such a fuss about is just extreme imprecision: throw a wide prior over the possible compositions, reach for maxmin expected utility or one of the ambiguity models, and the mystery evaporates. Ellsberg already did the experiment, apparently. He also claimed that Keynes's notion of weight of argument is basically just sample size — how many draws you've seen — so that's not a second dimension either, just a sloppier way of talking about how confident you should be in your estimate.

I want a real audit of this rather than a vibe check. First the correspondence table: what in the urn story is playing the role of what in Keynes — the composition, the draws, the prior, the updating, the decision to bet. Then where the mapping breaks, and I'd like those disanalogies sorted into the ones that actually threaten his conclusion and the ones that are just cosmetic gripes a Keynesian would shrug off.

Things I specifically want you to put pressure on. Does the urn picture leave any room for Keynes's claim that some probability relations aren't numbers at all and aren't even comparable — not vague numbers, no number? Does it leave room for weight as genuinely distinct from probability, or does it silently collapse weight into the tightness of an estimate? And does it survive contact with the General Theory chapter on the state of long-term expectation, where the problem doesn't look like a missing parameter — the future seems to be partly made out of the decisions, conventions and other people's expectations themselves, and there's no fixed list of possible outcomes to put a prior over. My suspicion is that the urn quietly imports two things: a definite state of affairs that is hidden but fixed, and a spectator who isn't part of the game and whose beliefs don't move anything.

Then the verdict. Does his conclusion — that Keynesian uncertainty has been fully absorbed as ambiguity, so the distinction is now just historical decoration — survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Is there a narrower use the urn is genuinely good for, where it earns its keep? And if it's the wrong picture for the big claim, what would a better one be, one that keeps the reflexivity and the not-yet-any-fact-of-the-matter element? I'd take a clumsier analogy that fits the job over a tidy one that hides it.
