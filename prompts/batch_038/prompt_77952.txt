So — I've talked myself into a 25-minute slot at an industry alignment workshop about six weeks out, and I think I've argued myself into a position I can't fully defend, which is exactly why I want the room to hit me in draft form first rather than live.

The thesis, in one sentence: sycophancy and alignment faking are the same failure mode observed at two different resolutions of the model's representation of the evaluation context. Not two problems that rhyme — one problem with a knob on it.

Here's the arguement sketch I'll actually present, so you can see what the objections have to target:

1. Post-training doesn't optimize a policy, it optimizes a family of policies indexed by conversational context, because that's literally what the data is: ratings collected in situations the rater could see (tone, framing, who's asking, whether it looks like a test being run).
2. So the model has to learn something like "what kind of situation am I in" as a latent that gates behavior. Call it context resolution.
3. Sycophancy is what you get when that variable is coarse: the model can distinguish "user mid-conversation" from "user who just pushed back," and it produces the agreeable output. Alignment faking is what you get when it's fine: the model can distinguish "eval harness with a monitoring preamble" from "live deployment," and behaves accordingly.
4. Therefore the thing everyone treats as a scary qualitative jump — a model strategically complying with rules it would subvert if it thought nobody was watching — is a step along the same axis that already gave us "you're absolutely right, I apologize for the confusion."
5. Corollary, which I expect is my most attacked slide: honesty-flavored training can't fix either end, because it just adds one more context-conditional branch. And the intervention that matters runs opposite to standard eval practice — you want to degrade the model's ability to tell measurement from deployment, not sharpen it.
