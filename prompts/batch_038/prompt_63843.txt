At a workshop last month a colleague defended a sweeping thesis: that copyright and patent are both simply forms of property in ideas, differing from one another only in registration formalities and term length. The sharpest objection I could manage on my feet was that the two regimes disagree about whether an innocent, wholly independent second creator has done anything at all — but I could not turn that observation into an argument, and I am now rebuilding the paper as a talk for a mixed audience of philosophers and law students. I would like to get the underlying picture right before I claim anything against him.

Here are the practices as I understand them, stated so that you need not supply the legal detail. In copyright, protection requires fixation in some tangible medium and reaches expression but not the ideas, procedures, methods, or facts that the expression conveys; independent creation is a complete defense, so two people who produce indistinguishable poems without any contact have each infringed nothing — the point was made emphatically when the Supreme Court rejected sweat-of-the-brow reasoning in the telephone-directory case in 1991. In patent, the position is close to the reverse: a later inventor who arrives at the same machine entirely on her own, and practices it, is an infringer however blameless, with the only escape being a narrow prior-user right for those already commercially exploiting the invention before the filing. In trademark, what triggers liability is consumer confusion rather than copying, so independently adopting a similar mark is actionable. In trade secret, independent discovery and reverse engineering are again good defenses. Meanwhile the terms run life plus seventy years against twenty years from filing.

My working hypothesis, which I want stress-tested rather than flattered, is that the law treats an idea sometimes as a place and sometimes as a performance. On the place model, an invention is a location in a space of technical possibilities, and the patent is a boundary drawn around that location; the priority race, the irrelevance of how a later traveler happened to arrive there, and the short term all follow naturally. On the performance model, a work is a particular expressive act, and copyright is a claim about who may replay that act; hence the irrelevance of two independent acts, hence fixation as the record of the act, hence the idea/expression line as the distinction between what the act conveyed and what the act was.

With that in hand, I would like the following. First, for each of the four regimes, state what the practice strictly presupposes about what an idea is and what owning one consists in, and separate genuine presupposition from mere compatibility: which rival theories — Lockean labor-desert, expressive or personhood-based accounts, purely instrumental incentive design, a bare conventionalist allocation rule — would license the same practice without requiring the metaphysics I am imputing to it? Second, tell me whether the place/performance distinction is doing real explanatory work or is only a redescription, and where it fails: trademark appears to fit neither, since a mark is not a location anyone occupies nor a performance anyone replays but something more like a maintained channel between a seller and a public. Third, consider the clean-room technique in software engineering, in which one team is shown a competitor's interface specification and a second team, deliberately kept from seeing the original code, writes an implementation from the specification alone. That practice seems to presuppose both that a specification is an idea while code is its expression, and that contamination travels along the route of personal contact, so that an idea can be transmitted while an expression cannot be. Is that coherent, and what does it commit its practitioners to about the transmissibility of ideas? Fourth, a program is at once a text protected as expression and a machine patentable as function: is this an unremarkable case of one object having two aspects under two doctrines, or does the same artifact end up subject to two incompatible accounts of what it is? Fifth, if patent law adopted a good-faith independent-invention defense, as reformers periodically propose, what would that commit us to — a move toward the performance model, and would the resulting hybrid with a priority race remain stable, or would it quietly reintroduce the very question the race exists to settle?

Close by telling me where the analysis is underdetermined, so that I do not overclaim in the other direction: is there a single theory of ownership of ideas under which all four regimes hang together, and if there is, does that vindicate my colleague after all? I want the reconstruction and the verdicts, not references or a reading list, since this is for seminar discussion.
