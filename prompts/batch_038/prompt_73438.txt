I am a novelist, and for the past two years I have been circling an essay that began as a craft problem rather than a philosophical one. It is now long enough that I can no longer tell whether its central claim is doing real work or merely redescribing, in borrowed vocabulary, something every working novelist already believes about fiction. I have condensed the draft below. I would like comments of two kinds: first, on the accuracy of the philosophy, which I have learned piecemeal and may have garbled; second, on whether the literary thesis actually follows from it or only sounds as though it does.

**Draft**

Most plots are machines for producing a single ordering. A protagonist faces alternatives, and the novel arranges the world so that one of them turns out to be better, or so that the costs can be settled against the gains at the end. Melodrama, as I am using the word, is what happens when that settlement is made explicit: the reconciliation at the deathbed, the restored estate, the reputation repaired. Suspense, correspondingly, depends on our ignorance of the ordering rather than on there being no ordering to find.

Against this I want to set the kind of scene I am trying to write, for which I have adopted a term of my own: a parity scene. Two options are rendered in sufficient detail that a reader can appreciate each on its own terms; the reader is not, at the end, in a position to rank them; and the character must nevertheless choose. The expressive work is done by the conjunction of those three facts — the reader's failure to rank and the character's inability to abstain. My contention is that such a scene is not the dramatization of a dilemma in the ordinary critical sense, meaning a case in which both options are bad, or in which one obligation collides with another. It is the dramatization of a choice among options between which neither betterness, nor worseness, nor exact equality holds, because the consideration under which we compare them simply runs out.

Two supports for the distinction. The first is structural. Suppose a novel presents a life left behind and a life taken up, and a sequel improves the life taken up by a trivial increment — a little more money, a slightly kinder household. The improved life is plainly better than its unrevised version, and yet it does not thereby become better than the life abandoned. If the two lives had been precisely equal in worth, the improvement ought to have carried across the comparison and broken the tie. It does not. So they were not equal; nor is either better. That is the shape of argument I have in mind, and it seems to me available to narrative in a way it is not available to discursive prose, because a sequel can perform the small improvement in time rather than merely describe it.

The second support concerns what happens afterward. Bernard Williams's discussion of the regret owed by an agent who causes a terrible harm without fault — the driver who kills a child though nothing was wrong with his vehicle or his conduct — suggests that the residue of a choice is not proportional to how badly the agent chose. In my terms, the mark of a parity scene on the page is a character who is not at peace with the option he took and who cannot be shown to have taken the worse one. A character who chooses and is untroubled has, whether or not the author intended it, been written as someone facing comparable alternatives. Sentimentality is the suppression of residue.

**Where I want your comments**

1. Please tell me plainly where I have misdescribed the philosophy. My understanding is that the standard picture admits three comparisons — better, worse, and equally good — and that Ruth Chang argues for a fourth, parity, on which two items are comparable without any of the three holding; that Joseph Raz, by contrast, defends genuine incomparability, on which no comparison holds at all; that John Broome and others treat the appearance of incomparability as an artifact of vagueness; and that a further view treats it as mere ignorance of which comparison in fact obtains. Have I got any of these wrong, flattened a distinction, or credited the wrong person? Do not spare the small errors, since the essay will be read by philosophers as well as critics.

2. My draft slides between parity and incomparability, partly because the longer word reads better in a sentence about novels. Tragedy, as critics use the term, seems to concern irrecoverable loss — that is, incomparability — rather than parity. Is there a version of my claim that survives the correction, or does the claim depend on the slide?

3. The strongest objection I can construct is this: readers do rank. When I put the scene in front of a workshop, someone says the character should have stayed, and gives reasons, and most of the room agrees. On that showing, my parity scene is just suspense with poor information design, and the appearance of unrankability is epistemic. I have two replies — that the reasons offered are post hoc and unstable from reader to reader, and that the same scene looks like a hard choice to one reader and a clear choice to another precisely because the covering consideration is itself contested — but neither convinces me. Do either of them have force? Is there a better reply, or should I concede the point and narrow the claim?

4. If the vagueness account is right, then what I have been calling a parity scene is a scene about a borderline case, which strikes me as a much less interesting thing to be writing. Would adopting that account cost me the thesis, or could the thesis be restated so that vagueness does no damage?

5. Finally, and most bluntly: is my coinage earning its keep, or is a parity scene simply a tragic dilemma wearing a new label? I would rather cut the essay than publish a neologism that names nothing.
