I chair the Wellbeing Measurement Advisory Panel of our national statistics office — a standing body that signs off on the country's headline social and economic indicators and advises the Treasury on how wellbeing evidence enters spending decisions. Next month the panel must return a formal response to a proposal from the Minister of Finance, and I need a draft decision memo to circulate to the eleven members beforehand so that the meeting is spent arguing rather than being briefed.

The Minister's proposal has three parts. First, that we adopt our national average life-evaluation score in the major international happiness ranking — the one built from the 0-to-10 ladder question asked in the Gallup World Poll — as an official headline indicator, published alongside GDP. Second, that the government commit to a target of reaching the top five of that ranking within ten years. Third, that major spending bids be appraised in WELLBYs, using a fixed Treasury exchange rate between money and a tenth of a ladder point.

Please treat the following as established by our own analysis, and reason from them rather than from what you may recall about the ranking:

- In the most recent edition, the second- and third-placed countries are separated by one thousandth of a ladder point, yet the publication prints a strict ordering.
- One of the top five scores more than two ladder points above what the ranking's six explanatory variables predict, and nothing in that model accounts for the gap.
- Our harmonised national survey finds the ladder question yields scores about 0.4 points higher when asked face to face than by telephone in the same month; the international poll switched mode in two of our regions last year.
- Our survey team wants the headline measure to be affect-based — yesterday-style questions on enjoyment, worry, sadness, and feeling that life is worthwhile — on the grounds that the ladder records a verdict on one's life rather than happiness.
- The Treasury's equity adviser objects that a target set on the national mean can be met entirely by improving the lives of people who are already doing well, and observes that some of our lowest-income groups report ladder scores high relative to their circumstances.

The memo must recommend one course: adopt the proposal as drafted, adopt a modified version, or decline it and specify the indicator set we should publish instead.

Please write it with exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Recommendation** (about 150 words): the bottom line, including what we publish and from what date, and precisely what we tell the Minister about the top-five target and about the WELLBY exchange rate.
2. **What the ladder measures, and what it hides** (about 300 words): the difference between evaluating a life and living a happy one, why that difference is substantive rather than technical, and how adaptation and the choice of the mean as the aggregand interact with it.
3. **Why a rank is not a level** (about 250 words): the sampling-error, mode-change, and unexplained-residual problems above; whether a position in an international ordering can serve as a policy target at all; and what happens to the measure once the government is publicly trying to move it.
4. **The strongest case for the proposal** (about 200 words): an honest reconstruction, not a strawman — what a single headline number and a visible international target buy us politically and administratively, and whether any part of the proposal deserves to survive even if the target does not.
5. **Conditions and sequencing** (about 200 words): what would have to be true for our recommendation to be right, what we publish instead or alongside, explicit review triggers, and dates for each step.

The document should be one the eleven members could actually vote on: commit to a position, keep the reasoning concrete, and do not include citations, references, or a reading list.
