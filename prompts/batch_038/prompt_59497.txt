Question. Answer both parts in about 800 words. (a) Hacking distinguishes indifferent kinds from interactive kinds, and describes a looping effect in which the classification of people changes the people classified, which in turn forces a revision of the classification. Explain the distinction and the looping effect, and give at least one example that is not drawn from our readings. (b) Do labels such as "Millennial" and "Gen Z" pick out interactive kinds? If they do, what follows: is a generation thereby less real than, say, a chemical kind, and what would a demographer have to do differently in order to study one? You may draw on Mannheim's account.

Answer submitted by Participant 9. Hacking's point is that the natural sciences mostly deal with kinds that do not care how they are classified. Plutonium does not read the paper explaining what plutonium is, and it does not adjust its behavior in response. He calls these indifferent kinds. Interactive kinds are different, because they are kinds of people, and people can find out how they are classified, take the classification in, and start to conform to it or to resist it. Once they do, the people who devised the classification have to revise it, because the group the classification was meant to pick out is no longer quite the group that now answers to the description. Hacking calls this back-and-forth a looping effect. An example not from the readings: the removal of homosexuality from the DSM in the 1970s. Before that, a person so classified was classified as ill, and the classification shaped what lives were available to that person; once it was withdrawn, new ways of describing and organizing oneself became available, and the category itself had to be rewritten. A live example is "neurodivergent," a term that came out of autism activism rather than out of clinical research, which has since changed what clinicians look for, so that the people now identified are not quite the people the earlier studies were about. Millennials and Gen Z are obviously interactive in this sense. Nobody is a Millennial in the way an atom is an atom. The label was manufactured by market researchers and journalists, pushed at the people it described through advertising, listicles, and workplace training, and those people then began to recognize themselves in it and to hire, date, and spend accordingly — and to complain accordingly as well. The loop is closed. That has two consequences, I think. First, a generation is less real than a natural kind, or real in a weaker way: the boundary of the group is not out there waiting to be found, it is made by the counting. And Hacking's account shows that once a classification loops, there is no fact of the matter about what the group was like before it was classified, so a survey finding that Millennials delay marriage is measuring an artifact of the surveying. Second, Mannheim supports this. He showed that what makes a generation is not merely being born in the same years but sharing a common location in history, and that a generation is really a set of attitudes produced by that shared location. On his view a marketing category and a birth cohort are not so different: both are locations in history, and both become real once people start behaving accordingly. What a demographer would have to do differently is hard to say, since if the looping is total there is nothing stable left to measure. At the least she would have to record the date the label was applied and treat the data before and after it as data about different groups, rather than as one continuous series.
