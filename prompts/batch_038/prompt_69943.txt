Write a short story of roughly 1,500 words about a public agency that has adopted Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as its actual decision procedure: every citizen carries a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function, social value is a weighted sum of them, and the weights are set by officials before anyone learns which citizen they will turn out to be. The agency is dividing a scarce resource that will lengthen some lives and shorten others, and its arithmetic is unimpeachable.

Let the story turn instead on the procedure's soft spot: individual utilities are determined only up to positive affine transformations, so the choice of zero points and scales — made years earlier by a calibration questionnaire — is quietly doing all the normative work the theorem claims to have eliminated. Give the reader one citizen who objects not that the arithmetic is wrong but that her life is being measured in a unit never built to carry it, and one official who defends the procedure in good faith on the ground that every alternative merely hides the same choice inside somebody's intuitions. End by dramatizing the aggregate's indifference between two distributions with the same sum.

Show rather than explain, and let the technical content emerge from the case instead of from anyone lecturing about it.
