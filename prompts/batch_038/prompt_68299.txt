Consider a public health agency that wants to reduce the burden of a disease and settles on a single proxy indicator for that goal. Imagine the setting is idealized in two respects: the indicator is measured exactly, with no instrument error, no sampling noise, and no reporting lag; and the clinicians and administrators whose work it evaluates are scrupulously honest, so there is no scope for gaming, cream-skimming, or misreporting. My understanding is that Goodhart's law only bites when one of those two things goes wrong — either the proxy is a noisy stand-in for the target, or the people being measured respond strategically to being measured — so that in this idealized setting the agency could push the indicator as hard as it liked without undermining its actual aim. Is that right? If not, explain what mechanism still drives the indicator apart from the underlying goal, and say why perfect measurement and honest reporting do nothing to block it.
