I spent forty years as a plant pathologist at a state ag station, retired three years ago, and since then Ive been reading philosophy of science, which turns out to be a lot more fun than I expected. The one who grabbed me is Lakatos: the hard core, the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, the difference between a problem shift that is theoretically progressive and one that is just patching, and the rest of it. Trouble is I've talked my way onto the science panel of a small family foundation and now I chair it. We get maybe thirty-five letters of inquiry a year, we meet on two Saturdays, I have about twenty minutes per proposal and no expertise in the field most of the time. There are five of us: me, two working scientists, a corporate lawyer, and a retired schoolteacher who is sharper than any of us but has never heard of Lakatos. Our decisions come in only three kinds: reject, invite a full proposal, or fund a small workshop to get people in a room. And the board wants the reasons in three sentences a layperson can actually read.

So can you write me the procedure I wish I had? A sequence of questions to put to a proposal, in order, with what counts as a satisfactory answer at each step, the steps where we stop and say no, and the steps where the question is really about values or about betting on people and should be handed to the panel instead of to the rules. Built on Lakatos, but stripped down enough that a lawyer and a schoolteacher can run it. And be explicit about which of his distinctions you had to blur to get there, and which ones absolutely have to survive the trip or the whole thing collapses back into counting citations.

Two things I do by hand that I suspect are too crude. First, novelty: I only count a prediction as novel if the group wrote it down and dated it before they ran the thing, and if the predicted phenomenon wasnt among the ones the theory was originally built to explain. Does that capture what the distinction needs, and where will it misfire on me? Second, ad hoc-ness: I call a new auxiliary hypothesis ad hoc only if it does no work anywhere else, meaning it exists solely to keep this one anomaly off the core. Usable, or am I about to wave through a pile of patches that have independent support in name only?

Then the deadline problem, which bothers me more than the rest. As I recall Lakatos refuses to say how long a degenerating programme gets before you write it off, tell me if Ive got that wrong. Our panel does have a deadline in practice: three funding cycles. How do I write a rule that knows it might just be impatient?

Now run it on three cases. One: a group that has worked on excess heat in metal hydrides for thirty years, with a long tail of explanations for every failed replication, but last year put up a preprint predicting a particular isotope signature in a new material and then measured it. Two: a four-year-old group in insect navigation with a bold core idea, nothing confirmed yet, but it has already changed how three neighbouring labs set up their experiments and has two dated predictions in the works. Three: a big established group asking for money to build an instrument, with no theory I can identify at all, and I want the procedure to tell me whether that even belongs in front of me.

Give me one case, real or invented, where your procedure says no to something I would personally fight for, and say so bluntly. Then end with what the procedure cant do and which steps are just judgment wearing a lab coat. No reading list, please, I cant chase references down and I dont intend to. And write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, even though I know a procedure practically begs for them.
