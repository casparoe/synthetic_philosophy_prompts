I am drafting a section of a paper arguing that the instrumental, competence-based case for epistocracy cannot be repaired by better test design, and I need a worked case that a sympathetic epistocrat could not dismiss as rigged. Please construct the case to the following specifications.

**The scenario.** One paragraph, set in a realistic local-government body — a county board, a regional water authority, a school district, a transit agency — facing a single binary decision. No science fiction, no invented societies, no narrators with privileged access to the truth.

**The two arms.** The same population, the same decision, the same turnout, the same motivation. In arm A, everyone votes. In arm B, the franchise is restricted to those who pass a competence examination, and the examination is not a sham: it is fairly administered, it tests material genuinely relevant to the body's work, and across the full range of decisions that body will make over the following decade, screened voters are strictly and substantially more likely to be individually correct than unscreened voters. If that global advantage is not built into the description, the case is worthless to me.

**The result to be generated.** On this one decision, the screened electorate's majority verdict is less likely to be correct than the unscreened electorate's, and not merely marginally so. This must be an epistemic failure rather than a motivational one: no self-interest, no demagoguery, no misinformation campaign, no captured media, no differential turnout. I want the mechanism isolated entirely to what the voters know and to how their errors depend on one another.

**Two constraints that matter most.** First, the decision must have a fact of the matter about which option is better, fixed by consequences that everyone in the case agrees are the relevant ones; I do not want the outcome to turn on value pluralism or on contested conceptions of the good, because that is a different objection and not the one I am making. Second, the knowledge that lets the unscreened electorate get this decision right must be knowledge the examination could not certify in principle — tacit, distributed, held in fragments by people none of whom can articulate the whole of it — rather than knowledge the examiner merely neglected to test. If the obvious fix is "write a better test," the case fails.

Do not announce the verdict inside the scenario. Describe the setting and the relevant structure; the outcome must be derivable from it, not asserted.

**Then the demonstration.**

1. Supply a toy model with explicit indicative numbers — electorate sizes, individual accuracies, a correlation parameter or its structural equivalent — giving the majority accuracy in each arm on this decision, and giving the screened group's global advantage across the body's ordinary business. The arithmetic may be approximate but must be real.
2. Say which condition of the Condorcet jury theorem fails here, and whether it fails for the screened group, the unscreened group, or both; then explain why the standard appeal to that theorem gets the sign wrong on this particular decision.
3. Identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and run at least two removal tests: what happens if the screened voters' errors are made independent of one another while their individual accuracy on this decision is held fixed, and what happens if the distributed local knowledge is made examinable while the correlation among screened voters is held fixed. Report for each whether the case collapses, reverses, or survives.
4. Say whether this is a special case or a structural one: does any screen that selects for a shared body of examinable knowledge tend to produce this result on the subclass of decisions that turn on distributed local knowledge, and how large must that subclass be before the global advantage required in the second specification is lost altogether?
5. Give the epistocrat's three best replies — for instance, that the screen is not the cause of the failure, that a partly screened or mixed assembly would have caught it, or that the case assumes an implausible distribution of issue types — and price each one: what the epistocrat must concede to make it.
6. Close by stating the minimal modification to the screening rule that would restore its advantage on this decision, and whether the epistocrat can accept that modification without surrendering the reason for screening in the first place.
