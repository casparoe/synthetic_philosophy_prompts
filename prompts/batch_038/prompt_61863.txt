I cover mathematics and machine reasoning for a general-interest magazine, and I am assembling a piece about how working mathematicians talk about their confidence in claims they have not yet proved. While reporting it I have kept running into a pattern I cannot name. I would like to know whether the technical literature has already named it, or whether I am looking at something that has to be defined for the first time.

The pattern shows up in three concrete forms.

First: a researcher will tell me she is about ninety-five percent sure of each lemma in a chain, and separately about ninety percent sure of the theorem those twelve lemmas jointly deliver — and then, when I ask her directly about the conjunction of the twelve as a single proposition, she gives me something nearer sixty. All three answers come from the same person in the same conversation, and she is not embarrassed by any of them. The number appears to depend on where in the derivation she happens to be standing when the question lands.

Second: the same sensitivity runs in the opposite direction, and there it looks less like a defect than like good sense. Tell her the theorem has now been independently verified and she raises her estimate for the individual lemmas, often by more than I would have predicted from her earlier base rates. Evidence travels backward along the chain readily, while the cost of the chain's length is apparently never charged going forward.

Third: seeing more of a proof does not move confidence in a fixed direction. When the additional steps are ones she would genuinely check, more of the proof means more confidence; when they are merely enumerated, more of the proof means less, because every named step is one more place for something to have gone wrong. What seems to govern her credence is not the fraction of the argument that has been established but the fraction she would be willing to audit.

I want the concept rather than the label, so please treat the naming as the smaller half of the job. What I need from you: what this phenomenon precisely is; which established concepts sit nearest to it and how it differs from each — I can already think of a famous puzzle about an author who expects his own book to contain at least one error, and a well-known laboratory finding about people rating a conjunction likelier than its parts, and I suspect neither is the right thing here; whether the standard treatments of reasoning under computational limits have a term for the asymmetry between the forward and backward directions of flow; and if no term exists, what you would call it and where its edges lie — what would count as an instance, and what a merely similar-looking case would be that does not. I am not after references or a reading list, only the concepts and whatever names attach to them.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. I recognize five short items is a tight budget for all of the above; spend them on the distinctions rather than on restating my examples.
