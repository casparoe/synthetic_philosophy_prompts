I am preparing the closing session of a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of quantum theory, and I intend to build it around a single case study rather than a survey. The case is the run of experimental tests of Bell inequalities from the early 1970s through the loophole-free experiments of 2015, together with the 2022 Nobel Prize that effectively canonised that run. What I require is a written case analysis, roughly 1,800 words under headings, that treats the sequence as an episode in which a philosophical thesis was submitted to experimental arbitration, and that then turns the episode back on the thesis.

The salient facts, so that nothing needs retrieving: Bell's 1964 result and the CHSH reformulation of 1969 made local causality — factorisability of the joint probability for distant outcomes given the complete common past — testable in principle. Freedman and Clauser performed the first such test in 1972 on a calcium atomic cascade and found a violation. Aspect and collaborators in 1982 introduced rapidly switched analyser settings to exclude subluminal communication between the wings. The Innsbruck photon work of the late 1990s replaced periodic switching with genuinely random, spacelike-separated setting choices. In 2015 three groups reported violations with both the locality and the detection loopholes closed: Delft, using electron spins at nitrogen-vacancy centres in diamond in two laboratories 1.3 km apart, with heralded entanglement and only a few hundred usable trials, and two photon experiments, at NIST and in Vienna, using high-efficiency detectors. Subsequent cosmic Bell tests took the setting choices from starlight and then from quasar light, pushing any putative common cause far into the past. The 2022 prize went to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger "for experiments with entangled photons, establishing the violation of Bell inequalities and pioneering quantum information science."

Now the analysis, in this order.

1. The mapping. Set out the assumption set of a modern Bell–CHSH argument explicitly and decomposed: Bell locality as factorisability; Shimony's split of that condition into parameter independence and outcome independence, with Jarrett's corresponding talk of locality and completeness; measurement independence; the assumption of a single definite pair of outcomes per trial; the assumption that trials are not memory-laden; and, in the heralded and photon cases, the legitimacy of the analysed sub-ensemble. For each, say what the 2015 experiments actually probed, what they left untouched, and which surviving position — Bohmian mechanics, dynamical collapse, many-worlds, retrocausal accounts, superdeterminism — exploits the gap. A compact table is welcome, but it must be argued around; a list of loopholes with one-line glosses is what my students already have and will not be accepted.

2. The direction of the arrow. The standard telling is that the experiments refuted local hidden variables. Test that sentence. Quantum mechanics itself violates outcome independence while respecting parameter independence, so the nonlocality the theorem forces is compatible with the absence of superluminal signalling: say what an experiment can and cannot bear on that distinction, and whether "nonlocal" in the press release and "nonlocal" in Bell's sense pick out the same property. Mark the places where the theorem is simply silent — above all on which assumption ought to be surrendered.

3. A colleague's claim, which I want you to argue with rather than summarise. He holds that the moral of 2015 is negative and modest: the experiments closed every loophole admitting of empirical treatment and left standing exactly one assumption — measurement independence — whose denial is not falsifiable, since any correlation between settings and hidden variables can be posited after the fact to mimic a local account. On his view the episode demonstrates the indispensability of an untestable assumption in experimental physics generally, and the Nobel citation's talk of "establishing" anything is metaphysical overreach. Is he right that measurement independence is uniquely untestable, or only least tested? Do the cosmic Bell tests alter the epistemic status of the assumption, or merely its rhetorical force? If his conclusion is sound, does it generalise to the other assumptions, or is this one special?

4. A counterfactual discipline check. Suppose Delft had come out within the Bell bound. What would have been concluded, and what had NIST and Vienna disagreed with it? A test whose failure mode nobody has specified is not much of a test, and I want my students to see whether this was a genuinely risky prediction or a confirmation ritual with unusually good public relations.

5. Your own verdict, stated plainly: does the episode confirm Bell's theorem, vindicate it, or expose a limitation in how it was framed — a conceptual result about explanations that could be turned into an experimental programme only by being recast as a claim about probabilities?

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations: the argument is what I want. Write for readers who have the formalism of a first-year graduate course but have never been asked to sort a Bell argument into assumptions an experiment can touch and assumptions it cannot.
