I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, writing a dissertation chapter on the relationship between Keynes's early work on probability and the contemporary literature on non-precise credences. Before it is presented at our department's work-in-progress series, I would like you to act as an expert reader — someone with a research-level command of both the philosophy of probability and early twentieth-century economic methodology, and someone willing to be blunt — and give me critical comments on the argument skeleton below. My discussant is a formal epistemologist who works on imprecise probabilities, so I expect her to press precisely the objection sketched in point 6; I would much rather hear that objection from you now, in its strongest form, than encounter a softened version of it in public.

For orientation, the chapter's target is a very common way of recruiting Keynes into the modern debate. Here is the skeleton, with the labels I have coined for the three candidate readings of Keynes's notion of weight.

1. *Setup.* In the 1921 *Treatise on Probability* Keynes treats probability as an objective logical relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion, denies that all such relations are numerically measurable, and holds that probability relations are in general only partially ordered. In the chapter on the weight of arguments he introduces a second dimension, independent of magnitude: the weight of an argument depends on the quantity of the relevant evidence or knowledge on which the probability relation rests, together with the balance between the favourable and unfavourable portions of it. Two arguments can therefore carry the same probability and differ in weight. On my reading weight is a relation between a conclusion and a body of evidence, not a feature of anyone's doxastic state, and it is monotonically non-decreasing in the arrival of further relevant evidence.

2. *The three readings.* For want of better terminology I call them (a) the **spread reading**, on which weight is nothing over and above the width of an imprecise credal set, so that low weight is maximal imprecision and high weight is precision; (b) the **coverage reading**, on which weight is the amount and internal balance of the relevant evidence actually in hand — a property of the evidential relation, as in point 1; and (c) the **steadiness reading**, on which weight is the expected resistance of a probability estimate to revision under further information, i.e. what Skyrms calls resilience, high weight corresponding to low resilience-sensitivity.

3. *The thesis.* Keynes is committed to (b), and the familiar move of reading him as a forerunner of Levi or Walley trades on a silent substitution of (a) for (b). Of the two modern proxies, (c) is closer to Keynes than (a) is, but it still fails: resilience is defined relative to an agent's own probability assignments and to a partition of possible future information, whereas Keynesian weight is evidence-relative and agent-independent; and resilience is not monotone — you can learn something that leaves your estimate less steady — whereas weight, on my reading, cannot be lowered by relevant evidence.

4. *Why (a) fails specifically.* A credal spread is a summary of a doxastic state, and it is silent on two things the coverage reading makes essential: which further evidence would be relevant, and how much of the relevant evidence is already in. Two bodies of evidence can license exactly the same spread while differing wildly in weight — a wide interval obtained from a great deal of heterogeneous, mutually balancing data is not the same epistemic situation as a wide interval obtained from almost nothing, even though (a) cannot tell them apart.

5. *Continuity with the later work.* I argue that the discussion of long-term expectation and confidence in the *General Theory* presupposes (b) rather than (a): the point there is not that our probability estimates are vague intervals but that in some domains the relevant evidence is structurally thin and no feasible amount of further investigation will thicken it, which is why deliberation is displaced by something more like a spontaneous urge to act. I flag this as the most historically exposed part of the chapter, since a number of commentators have argued that the later notion of weight comes apart from the earlier one.

6. *The objection I expect.* My discussant will say that the imprecise framework absorbs (b) and (c) without residue: the spread encodes coverage, and the agent's own higher-order model of how the spread would collapse on various possible pieces of evidence encodes steadiness, so that adding a separate Keynesian dimension is idle. My reply is that this makes weight agent-relative and model-dependent in exactly the way Keynes's account is not, and that it cannot reproduce the claim in point 5, since within the framework the width is always a function of the evidence one has and can in principle be driven toward zero by more data. I am not confident the reply works.

7. *A loose end.* Keynes seems to say both that weight matters to the rationality of acting on a probability and that in practical affairs we should not let considerations of weight paralyse us. I am not sure whether the chapter needs to resolve this or whether it is orthogonal to my thesis.
