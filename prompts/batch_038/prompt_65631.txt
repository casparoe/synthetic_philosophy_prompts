In June I am giving the commencement address at a medical school whose graduating class numbers 178. Roughly twelve hundred relatives will be in the seats; the faculty will be on the platform behind me. My standing at the school is a little awkward. I am a philosopher who teaches their required second-year course on what counts as a disease, and I also direct a small outpatient clinic. I hold the value-laden view: that functioning cannot be characterized without values already built in, because normal function is always function for some kind of life, and a condition is a disease insofar as it cuts down what a person is able to do, where that ability is a joint product of their biology and their circumstances. Several people sitting behind me hold the opposed view: that disease is a value-free biological matter, a statistically typical departure below normal functioning of some part or system measured against a reference class of the same age and sex, with values entering only afterwards, when we decide which dysfunctions to treat, fund, and worry about. Both camps will hear me describe their position in public.

Please write the address. Six to seven minutes when spoken; 900 to 1,000 words.

The disagreement between those two views should do the structural work of the speech — it should determine what I am able to say and what I must leave alone — rather than appear as a theme I announce and then illustrate. The families must not feel they have wandered into a seminar, and the faculty must not feel they are being served a diluted version of their own field. State each of the two positions in wording its own side would accept as a fair statement of what it holds.

1. Build the middle of the speech around a single case, which I supply and which you should render in plain language, without naming the gene or the enzyme. There is a hereditary variant, common in some parts of the world and rare in others, in the enzyme that clears the toxic product of alcohol metabolism. Carriers flush, turn nauseous, and get a racing heart after one glass of wine. Consider two carriers. The first works in a trade where nothing is settled until it is settled over drinks, and refusal carries a career cost; the second lives somewhere that drinking is a matter of complete indifference. Add, because it belongs in the case, that for a carrier who keeps drinking anyway the risk of certain cancers of the upper digestive tract rises sharply. The two views give different answers about these two people, and I want the graduates to see the difference clearly without my telling them which answer I think is right. Do not resolve the case. Do not resolve it by the familiar move of saying that disease is really both a dysfunction and a harm — that hybrid is where this argument begins, not where it ends. Do not organize anything around the three-way split between disease, illness, and sickness; nobody in the audience has heard of it and it would not help them.

2. There is one thing I cannot say from that platform. Six weeks ago our largest insurer reclassified the treatment my clinic provides as an enhancement rather than a medically necessary treatment, on the ground that the people it serves do not have a disease. We are appealing, some of the families in the audience are affected, and the dean has asked me, reasonably, not to litigate it in the address. The speech must not name the clinic, the insurer, the decision, or the patients, and must not contain anything recognizable as an allusion that a reporter could pick up. But it should be written so that anyone who knows the situation hears afterwards that the address was about it, and so that the graduates leave holding something they can think the classification through with. This is the hardest part of the assignment: the omission must not read as evasion, and the speech must not turn into a disguised grievance.

3. Exactly one joke. It has to land for the relatives as well as the physicians, and it must not be at the expense of patients.

4. No philosopher's name, no book title, no citation, nothing that requires the audience to have read anything. At most one term of art, and if you use it, cash it out in the same breath.

5. One sentence acknowledging the strongest thing that can be said against my own view — that on my account a society can make its members healthy simply by expecting less of them — and then let it stand unanswered.

6. Give them something for the first afternoon they sit down at a terminal and type a code. It should be a practical instruction they can actually follow, and it should not be a restatement of my theory.

7. The last minute belongs to the parents. They paid for this, and they have spent four years listening to their children use the word disease in ways they no longer recognize. It should land for them without being sentimental and without implying that the diagnosis someone once gave their own child was arbitrary.

Write it to be spoken aloud in a large room: sentences that survive being said once. Do not open by thanking the dean or the committee; avoid "as you go forth" and its relatives; close on your own words rather than someone else's; no predictions about medicine in 2050; do not tell them they are going to change the world; and do not offer, as wisdom, that they will simply have to live with ambiguity or hold two things in tension at once.
