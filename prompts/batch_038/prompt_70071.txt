**Draft: Two Names, Two Requests — the White Horse Argument and the Intersection Constraint**

**1.** The White Horse dialogue is usually read in one of two ways. On the sophism reading, the argument trades on the range of the negative copula in the thesis, which can express either non-identity or exclusion from a kind: the Advocate wins only by sliding from "not the same name as" to "not a member of." On the serious-insight reading, the dialogue anticipates a distinction we already have — intension against extension, or the abstract term against the concrete one. I think both readings answer the wrong question, because both assume the exchange is about the copula. My claim is that the load-bearing move in the dialogue is not about the copula at all, but about what counts as correctly answering a request. Once that is seen, the argument has a different shape, and a different weakness, from either of the standard ones.

**2.** There are two moves in the text. The first is the shape-and-color move: "‘Horse’ is what designates shape. ‘White’ is what designates color. What designates color is not what designates shape. Therefore I say: a white horse is not a horse." The second, which I take to do the real work, is the seeking move: "If one seeks a horse, yellow and black horses may all be delivered. If one seeks a white horse, yellow and black horses may not be delivered." The Advocate treats this as decisive rather than merely suggestive, and closes the exchange by announcing that the thesis "is certain." The Objector's counter is the membership intuition stated flatly: "If there is a white horse, one cannot say there is no horse."

**3.** Here is my reconstruction of the seeking move. For any name N, let the response set R(N) be the set of things whose delivery is a correct response to a request framed with N. The seeking move's premise is then that R(horse) properly includes R(white horse): yellow and black horses belong to the first and not to the second. To that add an identity principle for names — if two names were the same name, they would license the same correct responses. Contraposed, divergence of response sets entails non-identity of names. So the conclusion is true when read as a claim about names rather than about animals. This is not an evasion imposed from outside: the dialogue opens by asking whether the thesis can be asserted, and the language of the original does not mark number or definiteness, so the name-reading is available to the Advocate rather than being a rescue devised by commentators.

**4.** The gap is that non-identity of names does not by itself yield non-predication. What blocks the slide is a principle I will call the intersection constraint: for a modifier-plus-noun compound, R(XY) = R(X) ∩ R(Y). Under that constraint, R(white horse) is contained in R(horse), so a divergence in response sets is precisely what the constraint predicts, and containment in the kind follows from it. Under the constraint, the seeking move establishes only that white horse is not synonymous with horse — which no one disputes — and leaves the Objector's membership intuition untouched. So the dialogue's argument is cogent only if the constraint fails, and nothing in the text argues that it fails. The shape-and-color move looks like a candidate argument for its failure, but as it stands it establishes only that white and horse are different names, which is entirely compatible with the compound's response set being their intersection.

**5.** This is the part I most want pressure on. My thesis is that the missing argument is not an oversight: the dialogue is best read as a reductio whose target is the apparent obviousness of the intersection constraint. Two considerations push me that way. First, the later exchanges — I paraphrase rather than quote them, since I am still comparing renderings — press the question whether white and horse can be separated from white horse, and discuss horses with and without color at issue; I read the separability question as asking whether a compound is automatically the sum of its parts, which is just the constraint under another description. Second, the Objector's counter is the constraint asserted from the other direction, and the Advocate's reply to it is about what may be delivered in response to a request, not about membership. On this reading the two speakers are not talking past each other about the copula; they are disagreeing about whether response conditions compose by intersection. If that is right, the dialogue is neither a sophism nor a proto-Fregean discovery but an early statement of a live question: whether a compositional semantics is owed the intersection constraint for free.

**6.** The loose end I have not resolved. If the constraint fails, something must replace it, and any semantics on which R(XY) differs from R(X) ∩ R(Y) owes an account of when compounding narrows and when it does something else. The cost may be steep: such a semantics might undermine the inference from this being a white horse to this being a horse in general, or might make the acceptability of a delivery depend on the request context rather than on the meaning of the compound. I close with a paragraph sketching this, and I suspect the sketch promises more than it delivers.

**What I want from you**

1. On section 3: does the response-set formulation genuinely capture the seeking move, or have I slid from what may correctly be delivered to what is being asked for? A yellow horse might be an acceptable delivery in one context, where the requester is indifferent to color, and unacceptable in another. If R is context-sensitive in that way, the premise that R(horse) properly includes R(white horse) is not clean. Tell me whether the equivocation is mine or the text's, and whether R can be defined so as to avoid it.
2. On section 4: I assert that nothing in the dialogue argues against the intersection constraint. If you think there is such an argument in the material I have summarized, name the move and explain how it is supposed to work. If there is not, say so.
3. On section 5: give me a verdict. Is the reductio reading defensible, or is it a later problematic projected onto the text? Take a position and argue for it rather than weighing considerations on both sides. If your answer is that it is not defensible, propose one replacement thesis for section 5 that the same material would support — one, not a menu of three.
4. Flag any place where I have mis-stated the dialogue's moves, and any place where my paraphrase of the Objector's counter and of the later separability exchanges has smoothed over something that matters to my argument.
5. On section 6: should it stay in a four-thousand-word paper or be cut? A short answer is fine, but commit to one.

Please be blunt and argue for your conclusions rather than hedging them; I would rather receive one confident objection I have to answer than a diplomatic inventory of possibilities. I am not asking you to check sources or to tell me what the secondary literature says — only to judge the argument as it stands on the page, and to say plainly where you think it fails.
