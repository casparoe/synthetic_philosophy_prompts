I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been commissioned to review a forthcoming trade-press book by a working behavioural geneticist — a credentialed researcher with a substantial publication record, not a polemicist. The publisher sent me an early chapter, and the passage below sits near the end of it, where the author pivots from evidence to education policy. My review has to say whether this passage is a fair statement of the science or a piece of motivated overreach, and I am not a specialist: I know roughly what a heritability estimate is supposed to be, but I cannot tell which of these sentences would survive contact with a reviewer at a specialist journal and which are merely loose in the way popular exposition usually is.

Here is the passage, which I have broken into numbered sentences so you can address each one separately. It is quoted as it appears in the manuscript:

1. Heritability is the share of a trait that a person gets from their genes, and for cognitive test performance that share sits near 0.4 in middle childhood and climbs to roughly 0.8 by adulthood as the genome finishes its work.
2. That climb is among the most robust findings in the field, and it matters: genes do not merely set the starting conditions, they increasingly decide where people end up.
3. We know this because identical twins separated in infancy and raised in unrelated families correlate at about 0.7 for IQ — nearly as highly as identical twins raised together — which shows that the family home leaves no detectable mark on adult cognitive ability.
4. Twin designs of this kind deliver narrow-sense heritability, the additive component of genetic variance, which is precisely the component that responds to selection.
5. None of this implies that the trait is fixed: heritability is a property of a population under a particular range of circumstances, and it moves when those circumstances move.
6. It follows that if we were to equalise schooling and nutrition across the population, improving everyone's environment at once, the heritability of test scores would fall — and whatever differences remained would be the ones most within the reach of policy.

Two things about my own reading, offered as data rather than as a request for confirmation. I have seen the substance of sentence 3 repeated in several books and articles I trust, so I am inclined to treat it as settled; my suspicion falls mainly on sentence 2, which sounds to me like an overstatement of what a correlation coefficient can support. I may have this backwards, and part of what I want from you is to find out whether I do.

What I would like, then:

- Go through the passage sentence by sentence. For each, assign one of four verdicts — correct; acceptable simplification for a general audience; misleading but repairable; or false — and give the reason in two or three sentences, in terms of what the technical quantity actually is and what the sentence claims about it.
- Where a sentence is misleading or false, supply a repair: how the same point would have to be put if the author wanted to keep the rhetorical force but stay accurate. If there is a charitable reading on which the sentence is defensible, say so explicitly and tell me whether it is the natural reading a general reader would give it.
- Be scrupulous about separating the author's errors from the field's live disagreements. Sentence 1's numbers, sentence 3's inference, and sentence 4's identification claim are all places where I suspect competent specialists differ among themselves to some degree, and I need to know how much of each sentence is settled, how much is contested, and in which direction the contest runs.
- Address sentence 2 on its own terms: does an increase in a heritability estimate across the lifespan support the claim that genes increasingly determine outcomes, and if not, what is the standard explanation offered for that increase, and what would have to be true for the author's gloss to be right?
- Then take the argument as a whole. Sentence 6 is where the chapter's policy turn rests. Does it follow from sentence 5, which I believe is correct? Work out what would happen to the variance components under the intervention sentence 6 describes, and tell me whether the conclusion is a non sequitur, a claim pointing the wrong way, or something defensible under an assumption the author has not stated.
- Finish by telling me which of the sentences I could quote approvingly in the review, and which one or two errors a diligent general-interest fact-checker would most likely let through, so I know where to spend my scepticism.

Please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I have my own route to the primary literature and I will do the sourcing. I want the reasoning laid out, not the references. Aim for something I could hand to a colleague who has never taken a genetics course and have them come away knowing exactly what this author got right and what they got wrong.
