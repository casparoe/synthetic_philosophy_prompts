So: I cover food, farming and the occasional animal-cognition story for a science desk, and I've finally talked my editor in to letting me run a first-person piece about what I actually eat and why. Which means the reasoning has to survive the comments, two newsletters that quote things back at me, and the academics who email when I get something wrong. My editor read the draft, said something in the middle doesn't cohere, and then wouldn't say what. So I'm writing out the package as it stands and I want a check on it before I file.

1. Wild animals matter as individuals, not as scenery. A vole being eaten alive has a worse time than a cow that drops in a field at twelve, and I don't think "that's just nature" does any work at all as a reply. I've said this in print.

2. Annual cropland is basically a machine for killing small animals. Ploughing, combining, rodenticides, irrigation, and the fact that the whole habitat gets wiped and rebuilt several times a year. Per hectare, a lot of death, ongoing, forever.

3. Good permanent pasture is much gentler per hectare. Not zero, obviously, but it isn't turned over, and — this is the bit I lean on hardest — it doesn't get re-killed every season.

4. So a pound of beef off well-run permanent grassland can come out ahead of a pound of protein from annual crops, all animals counted. I've said this on panels and I'll say it in the piece.

5. Separately, if you want to cut farmed animal suffering, chicken and eggs are where to start: many more individual birds than any other farmed species, and worse conditions per animal than cattle or pigs. My standing advice to readers for years has been drop the chicken, don't nag people about the Sunday roast.

6. I'm publicly pro cultivated meat and I've argued it deserves real research money, not just venture capital and a shrug.

7. Land is the big lever. Livestock soak up most of the world's agricultural land, a mostly-plant food system would free a lot of it, and I'm on record cheering rewilding schemes — including the soft stuff, let-the-garden-go, don't-mow-in-May.

8. On insects I'm honestly uncertain — call it a real but not dominant probability they're sentient — and I've argued that uncertainty should push us toward perenial and low-till systems rather than being waved away.

9. What I will not endorse is deliberate population-level meddling to cut wild suffering. No gene drives to lower fecundity in prey species, no fertility control for deer, none of it. Too much model uncertainty, too many ways to make things worse.

Here's what I want: an audit, not a lecture. Go through these and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other, ranked worst-first — I want severity, not a flat list of everything that could conceivably be quibbled with. For each real tension, say which commitment has to bend, what bending costs me (with the two audiences I care about, animal-welfare people and ecologists, who hate each other and will both be reading), and what the cheapest available fix is. Then separately flag the pairs that look like tensions and aren't, because I strongly suspect I've been losing sleep over the wrong one and I can't tell which from the inside. And if there's a single move — one claim to drop, or one distinction to add — that cleans up most of the mess at once, tell me what it is and what I lose by making it.

No reading list, please, and don't tell me who's made which argument before; I know roughly where these come from. What I want to know is which of my own claims is doing unearned work.
