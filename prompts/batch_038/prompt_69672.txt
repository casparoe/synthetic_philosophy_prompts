I'm writing a piece arguing that "its not censorship, it's just a private company exercising its property rights" is a bad answer to deplatforming, and I want to run the argument past Beccaria — or rather past my model of him. So: suppose Cesare Beccaria (the 1764 one, died 1794) is alive, gets a week to read up on content moderation, and is then asked to weigh in.

Here's the case I want his verdict on. Not a head of state — a mid-tier commentator, maybe 300k followers, who spent months telling people a vaccine caused infertility. Nobody charges him with anything. The platform permanently bans him under a ToS clause nobody has ever read, applied by a team of underpaid contractors whose decisons are inconsistent enough that his co-hosts posting near-identical material got a temporary reach-reduction. Then it cascades: his payment processor drops him, his video host terminates the account, and the two app stores refuse to carry the alternative app his audience migrates to. He loses most of his income and effectively loses the ability to be heard by anyone but a small remnant, permanently, after an appeal process that took eleven months and produced a two-sentence email.

The standard reply: no punishment was inflicted, because punishment is what a sovereign does under law, and a firm declining to supply a service is just a firm declining to supply a service. He's free to speak; he simply isn't entitled to a microphone, a bank account and an audience.

Four things I want him to rule on:

1. Does the right-to-punish machinery transfer? He grounds the state's authority to punish in each person handing over the smallest possible portion of their liberty. A platform never got that grant from anybody. But the loss it inflicts is larger than most criminal fines he'd treat as a real punishment. Is "not the sovereign, therefore not punishment" a clean answer for him, or is it the kind of verbalism he'd attack? Does he have a notion of the harm of punishment that's independent of who administers it?

2. Certainty versus severity. His thesis is that a modest penalty applied with near-certainty both deters better and is more legitimate than a savage one applied at random. Moderation is the exact reverse: wildly uncertain (opaque rules, inconsistent contractors, arbitrary appeals) and maximally severe (total and permanent). Would he prefer a system of many tiny, prompt, public, predictable penalties — 24-hour reach-reductions, visible strikes, temporary mutes, a ledger anyone can inspect — over the current lottery-that-ends-in-exile? And if yes: why does nobody actually build that, and would he care that part of the answer is that small predictable penalties don't do what platforms want done, which is making the person stop mattering rather than making them stop?

3. The rule-of-law fix. Lots of people propose making moderation more legal: published rules, precedent, independent adjudicators, reasoned written decisions, real appeals. My hunch is he'd love it. But there's a worry I can't shake — that this imports the whole apparatus and rhetoric of state punishment into a body with no democratic accountability, no proportionality ceiling and no power that can be checked, so it launders arbitrary power by giving it procedure. Which way does he go, and is there anything in his own framework that lets him tell apart "procedure that constrains power" from "procedure that dignifies power"?

4. Permanence. He opposed capital punishment partly because nobody could have contracted away their own life and partly because perpetual penal servitude makes a longer-lasting example than a spectacle does. Is a permanent ban the platform-era death penalty for a public voice, i.e. the thing his proportionality reasoning should forbid? Or does he say the analogy breaks because the man keeps his life and can go be a plumber?

If you want to reach for what he says about banishment, exile, confiscation, or punishments that carry public infamy, go ahead — but tell me how sure you are those themes are actually in the book rather than you filling in a slot that sounds plausible for an 18th-century penal reformer. Same for the cascade: if his framework simply has no place for power exercised through infrastructure rather than through a magistrate, say so plainly instead of stretching it.

Please don't write him as a generic free-speech liberal. I want the places where he'd surprise me — where he'd say something a modern civil libertarian wouldn't, and where he'd endorse something I've assumed he'd hate. Argue those rather than announcing them.

Last thing: tell me which of the four you're least confident about and why, what would actually change your verdict on it — which fact about the case, or which other commitment of his, would flip you — and where you think this whole exercise is most likely to be producing confident-sounding nonsense, e.g. because it's really just me projecting current moderation-policy arguments onto an old text and dressing them up in period costume.
