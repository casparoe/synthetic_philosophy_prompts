I spent thirty-five years in statistical mechanics and have taken up philosophy seriously only since retiring. For the past term I have been auditing a reading group on population ethics, and I have kept a notebook of the facilitator's rulings. Her framework is always the same: a world that never ends, containing exactly one person in each generation, the generations numbered 1, 2, 3, … by birth order, welfare given on a common cardinal scale, and no uncertainty.

Her verdicts so far, in each case comparing the first world with the second:

1. Everyone at 4; everyone at 4 except the person in generation 900,000, who is at 9. The second is better.
2. Everyone at 4; everyone at 5. The second is better.
3. Everyone at 4; the first billion generations at 5 and everyone thereafter at 4. The second is better.
4. Everyone at 4; the first billion generations at 4 and everyone thereafter at 5. The second is better.
5. Everyone at 4; odd-numbered generations at 3.5 and even-numbered generations at 5. The second is better — and she added that the infinite number of people thereby made worse off does not by itself veto the ranking.

Next week I intend to put her this case: everyone at 4, versus the first billion generations at 10 and everyone thereafter at 3.

Predict her verdict. Set out the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with 1–5 — I have in mind something like a criterion on the tail behaviour of the running sums of the welfare differences, a discounted sum, a limit of running averages, and whatever else the data permit — say what each implies about my new case, note which of them the notebook already excludes, and note any constraint it places on a surviving rule's parameters. Then commit: give me your single best guess at her answer, with the reasons for preferring that rule to its rivals, rather than a balanced menu leaving the choice to me. If the surviving rules in fact disagree here, tell me whether my case is already the sharpest discriminator or whether some other case I could raise the week after would settle the matter more cleanly, and say what she would have to say about that further case under each rule.

Finally, I should like your assessment of a worry bred by my old profession. If her rankings turn on sums taken in birth order, then the very same welfare profile listed in a different order — a rearrangement of the same people — might reverse a verdict, just as rearranging a conditionally convergent series changes its sum. Is that a genuine reductio of the rules in question, a reason to insist that birth order carries moral weight of its own, or a confusion on my part? Please take a definite line on this too.
