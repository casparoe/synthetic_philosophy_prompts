ok so this is kind of a long one but I'm applying for a summer research program (6 weeks, you get a mentor and a laptop and you present at the end) and the application wants a ~2 page proposal for an independent project. My topic is whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer, and specifically whether the reward signal is anything like pleasure or pain for them.

here's the half formed idea I want turned into an actual proposal. Everyone in this debate seems to be arguing about whether the reward signal feels like anything, and I think that route is stuck, because nobody is settling the consciousness question in six weeks or maybe ever. So my angle is to just not ask that. Instead: look at what an agent does to control its own internal states, and at how it reacts to reward it expected and didnt get. Two behaviors I keep coming back to:

(1) information avoidance. If an agent can choose between an option that tells it how its doing and an option that doesn't, both with the same expected reward, and it picks the uninformative one, that looks structurally like an animal avoiding a cue it finds aversive. Its hiding from bad news.

(2) the disappointment thing. I've read that animals who reliably expect a treat and then don't get it often end up worse off than ones who never expected it. In an RL agent, an expected reward that doesn't arrive is just a negative prediction error, which is supposed to be pure information. So the question is whether that state is ever bad *for* the agent in more than an instrumental way, like whether the agent will pay to avoid entering it, or whether being in it degrades things beyond what the numbers predict.

So the project would be: design a test battery for negative valence in RL agents that doesn't assume anything about consciousness, and then actually run those tests on small toy agents (gridworlds, bandits, whatever a beginner can do) to see whether the tests even discriminate. Like, do they come apart from the trivial thing, which is just 'does this agent's value function contain negative numbers'.

That last part is what I'm most worried about and I want the proposal to face it head on instead of hiding it: in RL literally everything is downstream of the value function, so maybe any behavioral test is automatically satisfied by construction and therefore tells you nothing at all. That would be the project failing, and I want the proposal to say clearly what that failure looks like and how I'd know I've hit it.

Write the proposal with: the research question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the positions people actually hold (reward is valence, reward is only an indicator pointing at stuff that matters, machines can't have valence at all because of what they're built from, and the 'we can't know so be cautious anyway' view) all leave my question open rather than settling it; the method week by week for the six weeks, marking which parts are philosophy and which parts are me actually running code; why this method rather than the obvious alternatives, which I guess are just arguing with the consciousness literature, or asking the agent to report on its own states; what a result would look like, since I think the best outcome isn't 'agents suffer' or 'they don't' but a test that either works or provably can't work; an honest scope section on what a high schooler with one laptop and a mentor who does ML but not philosophy can realistically finish, and what I should cut first if it's too much; and a couple of lines on why a program should pick this over a more tractable-sounding project.
