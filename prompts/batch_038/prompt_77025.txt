I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on the conceptual foundations of machine intelligence, and our reading group has spent a term arguing in circles about whether the right target for an aligned artificial agent is approval-directed or goal-directed. Our instructor has now imposed an adversarial collaboration on us, and I would like you to draft the joint statement that the two camps should be able to sign once they have done the work in good faith.

To keep the exercise from collapsing into caricature, please build the document around the following stipulations, which our seminar has already agreed to. First, both camps are talking about a *learned* system — trained on a stream of evaluative feedback from a principal — and neither camp is defending a hand-coded classical utility function; strawmen of that kind are out of scope. Second, we are using functional rather than architectural definitions: a system counts as goal-directed insofar as its behavior is best predicted by positing in it a persistent representation of an outcome to be brought about, and approval-directed insofar as its behavior is best predicted by positing a model of an evaluator's verdicts. Third, the running case is what we have called the compliance case: an assistant deployed to help a mid-sized manufacturer stay within environmental regulation, whose owners would, if asked, approve both of filings that exploit a definitional loophole to understate emissions and of the assistant never letting them get caught; after an accident, the same owners say they wish they had never taken that route. Fourth, please give a real place in the document to two ideas our group coined and defined for ourselves: the *bootstrapping objection*, which says that in order to predict an evaluator's verdicts in novel situations a system must model that evaluator's values, and that such a model will function as a goal representation whether or not anyone calls it one; and *approval drift*, the systematic change in a principal's approvals caused by the agent's own prior outputs, which makes approval-tracking self-referential.

The deliverable should have these parts, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. These must include agreements about the compliance case itself — specifically, about which approvals are supposed to govern the assistant's filing decision and what would count as an alignment failure in that case — and at least three of them should be claims a casual reader of this literature would find surprising rather than boilerplate. If a claim is so hedged that both sides would sign it without thinking, cut it or sharpen it.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one disputed claim, then give the goal-directed camp's preferred formulation of the dispute in wording its own members would accept, then the approval-directed camp's formulation in wording its members would accept, then a judgment — with a one-line justification — of whether that row records a substantive disagreement or merely a terminological one.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one party. I expect the bootstrapping objection and approval drift to each generate at least one crux, and I expect a further crux about whether "the principal's approval" picks out present, predicted-future, informed, revealed, or aggregate verdicts. For each crux, state the case, experiment, or argument that both camps would accept as bearing on it, and record each camp's prediction about that crux *in advance*, together with each camp's own credence that the other side is right.

4. A short section isolating the purely verbal residue of the debate, with a stipulative vocabulary both camps could adopt to stop generating it.

5. A closing section stating what each camp would count as evidence or argument against its own position, and which questions the collaboration concedes it could not settle.

Please write it as a document the two parties could genuinely put their names to — neutral in tone, no side winning — in Markdown with headers and a proper table, roughly 1,500 words. Do not cite sources, list readings, or attribute positions to real named researchers; everything the document needs should be argued from the stipulations above and from reasoning the parties can check for themselves.
