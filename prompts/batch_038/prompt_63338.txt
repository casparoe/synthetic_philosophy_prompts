I'm opening a seminar session on abstract categories with a poem rather than an argument, and I'd like you to write it. The subject is the letter-spirit problem: a designer works inside a strict lettergrid — a fixed inventory of straight segments, uniform weight, no curves at all — and must nevertheless produce a complete alphabet that reads at a glance and coheres as a single style. Write a sestina, six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi, and choose your six end-words so that the permutation itself carries the thesis: the same small handful of elements returning in new arrangements, which is why the letter cannot live in any one arrangement. The poem must stage two failures without explaining either — a case in which a feature everyone calls essential is simply missing (the grid has no curves, and yet the round letters are still read as round), and a case in which every expected part is present and the letter is nevertheless dead. No line may state a definition, a criterion, or a theory of categories, and no philosopher may be named; the word spirit should appear exactly once, in the final line. Please handle the vocabulary of type anatomy accurately — stem, bowl, counter, shoulder, terminal, aperture, spur, ear — and be careful never to give a letterform a part it does not actually have.
