I'm a fourth-year history PhD student working on a chapter about counterfactual reasoning in historiography — the whole "would it have happened anyway?" literature, agency versus structure, that sort of thing — and our reading group finally got round to Carlyle's lectures on heroes. Mostly because basically everyone who invokes "great man theory" as a strawman quotes one sentence from the first lecture and nobody seems to have actually read the thing, and I want to know whether there's an argument in there or just a slogan with good rhythm. I'm reading it off Project Gutenberg (the file says it follows the Sterling Edition of the complete works). Below is the opening of Lecture I, and then a chunk from a couple of paragraphs later in the same lecture. The prose is doing my head in — I can feel that there's a claim being made but I can't get it into a form where I could say what it committs him to:

> We have undertaken to discourse here for a little on Great Men, their manner of appearance in our world's business, how they have shaped themselves in the world's history, what ideas men formed of them, what work they did;—on Heroes, namely, and on their reception and performance; what I call Hero-worship and the Heroic in human affairs. ... For, as I take it, Universal History, the history of what man has accomplished in this world, is at bottom the History of the Great Men who have worked here. They were the leaders of men, these great ones; the modellers, patterns, and in a wide sense creators, of whatsoever the general mass of men contrived to do or to attain; all things that we see standing accomplished in the world are properly the outer material result, the practical realization and embodiment, of Thoughts that dwelt in the Great Men sent into the world: the soul of the whole world's history, it may justly be considered, were the history of these.

> It is well said, in every sense, that a man's religion is the chief fact with regard to him. A man's, or a nation of men's. ... But the thing a man does practically believe (and this is often enough without asserting it even to himself, much less to others); the thing a man does practically lay to heart, and know for certain, concerning his vital relations to this mysterious Universe, and his duty and destiny there, that is in all cases the primary thing for him, and creatively determines all the rest. ... The thoughts they had were the parents of the actions they did; their feelings were parents of their thoughts: it was the unseen and spiritual in them that determined the outward and actual;—their religion, as I say, was the great fact about them.

So, concretely. First, can you reconstruct the argument in plain modern prose, roughly sentence by sentence, saying what claim each one is actually making rather than what it sounds like it's making? Second — and this is the bit I keep going in circles on — where is the explanatory weight located? He says history is "at bottom" the history of great men, but he also says the general mass of men "contrived to do or to attain" all of it, and that the great man is a "modeller" and "pattern" and only "in a wide sense" a creator. That hedging phrase is doing an enormous amount of work and I can't tell what it concedes. Is the hero a cause, a template, or an exponent of something already sitting in the age waiting to be articulated? Related: the very first sentence splits the subject into "their reception and performance" and then into "Hero-worship and the Heroic," and he later talks about a relation "which in all times unites a Great Man to other men" — if the receiving culture is what makes the man effective, isn't that structure sneaking back in through the back door, just relocated from material conditions into collective belief?

Third, a small textual thing that I suspect is bigger than it looks: "the soul of the whole world's history, it may justly be considered, were the history of these." That "were" reads to me as a counterfactual subjunctive — would be — not a plain past tense. If so, is the thesis an identity claim about what history *is*, or a claim about what history *would turn out to be* if you could see it properly? Because that seems to decide whether the thing is falsifiable at all or just a way of describing a perspective. Which brings me to what I actually need it for: try translating Carlyle's thesis into the strongest counterfactual-dependence claim it can support, of the kind you'd deploy in the seminar chestnut about whether National Socialism happens without Hitler. Which parts of the passage survive that translation and which ones collapse into rhetoric? And please flag anywhere I'm being actively misled by the syntax — e.g. "how they have shaped themselves in the world's history" and that slide from "a man's" to "a nation of men's" — I can't tell if that slide is a licensed analogy or a sleight of hand.
