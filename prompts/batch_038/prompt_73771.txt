I have assembled the following argument for a seminar on the ethics of killing and letting die, and I now think it proves considerably more than I am willing to accept. Each premise strikes me as defensible, and I cannot locate the seam. What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey of the landscape.

Two pieces of background, stated as neutrally as I can manage. First, in every U.S. state a competent adult may refuse life-sustaining treatment, and a clinician who honors that refusal by disconnecting a ventilator, halting dialysis, or removing a feeding tube acts lawfully; the death that follows is not legally classified as a killing, and no state permits a physician to administer a lethal dose on request. Second, the standard way these deaths are described is that the underlying disease caused them: the withdrawal is characterized as removing an obstacle to the disease's progress rather than as introducing a new cause of death.

The argument:

(1) A competent adult's refusal of life-sustaining treatment may lawfully be honored by a clinician's act of withdrawal, and that act is not a killing.
(2) The withdrawal is a bodily intervention performed on the patient by the clinician; death follows within hours or days and is foreseen with practical certainty by everyone involved; and the reason for acting is that death will follow, since the object of withdrawing is that the patient die rather than be sustained.
(3) An act that is a bodily intervention, that foreseeably brings about death, and that is undertaken for the sake of the patient's death is a killing, not a letting die.
(4) So the withdrawal cases are cases of consensual killing, and the line the law draws between them and voluntary active euthanasia tracks no moral difference.
(5) If a legal line tracks no moral difference, the law must either prohibit both sides of it or permit both; otherwise it mandates on one side what it punishes on the other, for acts that are by hypothesis identical in every morally relevant respect.
(6) Prohibiting both is not a live option: it would require clinicians to sustain dying patients against their settled wishes, and no legal system has adopted that as a general rule.
(7) Therefore voluntary active euthanasia must be permitted wherever withdrawal is permitted.

Here is what bothers me. Nothing in (1) through (7) mentions terminal illness, irreversibility, unbearable suffering, or the patient's interest in being free of unwanted bodily intrusion; none of those features does any work in the derivation. So the argument runs just as well for a patient who is not dying at all: a person with a chronic but manageable condition, or a competent young adult with treatment-responsive depression, who asks a physician to end her life. If (1)–(7) are sound, consensual physician-administered killing is owed to anyone who requests it, and every regime that confines euthanasia to the dying, or that requires the request to be connected to a medical condition, is drawing a distinction its own logic forbids. At least one jurisdiction that permits physician-administered euthanasia has dropped any requirement that the patient's death be reasonably foreseeable, which I take to be this generalization running its course rather than an aberration. I do not accept that consequence, and I doubt that most people who would happily endorse (1)–(4) accept it either. So something above has gone wrong.

(a) Identify the step you reject, and state the defect as precisely as you can. If it is an equivocation, name the two readings and say at which step the switch occurs; if it is a scope slide, say exactly which proposition is established and which the argument needs.

(b) Explain why that step looks unobjectionable in isolation — what is it about the withdrawal cases, specifically, that makes the premise seem true rather than merely convenient? If your diagnosis turns on the difference between intending death and merely foreseeing it, say so, and explain what work that difference is doing in a case where the foreseeing is certain and the acting is for its sake.

(c) Price the alternatives. For each of (1)–(6) in turn, say what a defender of the equivalence thesis loses by rejecting that premise instead of the one you chose. Three costs interest me especially. If the way out is to deny (2), does that commit one to saying the clinician's act is not among the causes of death, and how is that to be squared with the fact that the identical act performed on a healthy person would be homicide? If the way out is to deny (3), does that require abandoning the doing/allowing distinction altogether, or only some restricted version of it — and is the restricted version stable, or does it collapse on the next case? If the way out is to deny (5), what does one owe by way of an account of why a legal system may enforce lines that its own underlying moral theory calls arbitrary?

(d) Say plainly whether you take the argument to be valid. If it is valid, and the real question is which premise is cheapest to abandon, say that and say which — but do not assume in advance that validity is where the trouble lies.

For the record, the premises I would most like to keep are (1) and (6).
