I've been working my way through Berkeley's Querist lately — the odd little book of numbered queries he wrote as Bishop of Cloyne while agitating for Irish economic development, including a sketch of a national bank — after a long forum thread about the ontology of money. What arrested me is how thoroughly functionalist he is: he asks whether money is to be regarded as having intrinsic value, or as a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, and then suggests that "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter," while elsewhere holding that money is useful only so far as "it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour." He also insists that velocity substitutes for quantity — less money circulating swiftly being equivalent to more circulating slowly — and that the causal chain runs through desire, since "power be not referred to action; and whether action doth not follow appetite or will," with fashion creating appetites. In my reading group we started arguing about what this framework would actually predict about monetary arrangements that did not exist in the 1730s, and I would like your considered verdict rather than a survey. Please approach this as a historian of economic thought who has also worked on Berkeley's metaphysics, and think carefully before answering; begin with a short reconstruction of the load-bearing commitments (functional rather than commodity theory, money as incentive device operating on appetite, quantity-and-velocity, the role of public authority, the developmental aim of an idle nation made industrious), flagging explicitly which of those are textually supported and which you are extrapolating.

Then tell me what Berkeley would say about each of the following, giving for each a predicted position, a confidence level, and the specific place where his theory runs out: (1) Bitcoin and similar fixed-supply, issuerless assets — is a counter with no counter-maker still money on his account, and does his velocity point commit him to the standard critique of hoarding-oriented designs, or might he welcome a token that cannot be debased by the authority issuing it? (2) Central bank digital currencies and programmable money, where the token itself carries conditions on its use; given that he was happy to entertain the legislature interposing in the making of fashions, would he treat conditionality as a feature of good monetary design or as a corruption of the ticket? (3) The modern claim, common in chartalist and MMT circles, that money is a creature of the state and that its value is anchored by the state's demand for it in payment of taxes — would Berkeley recognize himself as an ancestor here, or does his emphasis on industry and on the circulation of wants rather than on fiscal obligation cut against that appropriation? (4) The case I find most interesting and which I suspect the secondary literature has nothing to say about: an economy in which the marginal transactor is not a person but an autonomous software agent — models holding wallets, paying one another for inference and compute in units denominated in GPU-hours or joules, negotiating at millisecond frequency, with no human directly involved in most exchanges. Berkeley's mechanism is that money works on appetite, and that appetites are manufactured by fashion; an agent has no appetite to stir. Does his account then imply that such tokens are not money at all, or only that the motivational work has relocated to the human principals who deploy the agents? Could a purely coordinative token — one that allocates scarce compute among non-desiring processes — create wealth on his criterion that wealth is a nation's industry rather than its specie? And (5) a smaller internal question: he allows that the denominations crown, livre, and pound sterling are "exponents or denominations" of a proportion, yet he is famous for denying that we have abstract general ideas. Is there a tension between an abstract unit of account and his anti-abstractionism, and if so which commitment would he surrender?

Two constraints on the answer. First, no bibliography, no reading lists, and no invented page or query numbers — where editions differ in numbering, just identify the query by its content, and if you are unsure of a textual detail, say so plainly rather than guessing. Second, keep the predictions honest about underdetermination: separate what follows from Berkeley's theory of money as such from what follows from his policy program for a poor, agrarian, import-dependent Ireland, since those two can pull in different directions, and point out at least one modern camp that claims him and would be disappointed by what he would actually say. Close with the single contemporary monetary phenomenon that his framework handles worst, and — as a check on your own reconstruction — write three or four queries in his own interrogative style, addressed to whatever he would take to be the pressing monetary questions of the present day.
