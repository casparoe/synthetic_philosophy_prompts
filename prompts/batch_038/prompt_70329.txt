I'm preparing a session on Tolstoy's *What Is Art?* and want to test how far his theory actually determines his verdicts. His track record, as I read it: Shakespeare is not art at all, despite the technical mastery; late Beethoven and Wagner's operas are the bad art of an idle class; *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, Dickens, Hugo's *Les Misérables*, and Dostoevsky's Siberian prison memoir are good art; village songs sung by illiterate peasants are good art; and he is willing to place much of his own fiction, *Anna Karenina* included, outside good art while keeping two of his short peasant tales.

Now a case he never faced. A warehouse worker, unpaid and untrained, records an a cappella song about a colleague crushed by machinery: plain words, a melody a child can pick up after one hearing, distributed free. Within a year it is sung at funerals and picket lines on four continents, in dozens of languages, always by amateurs. Its final verse has the mourners swear to make the owners answer in kind, and crowds sing it as an act of defiance against the employers of one particular country.

Predict his verdict. Lay out the candidate rules that fit the record above — infectiousness and sincerity; accessibility to the labouring many; the content criterion about feelings that unite rather than divide; his hostility to paid, professional art made for people who do not work — say which of them diverge on this song and why, and give your best guess with reasons, noting how the praise of *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, which is hardly free of indignation, bears on the content criterion. Then specify the altered version of this song that would most sharply separate the two leading candidates. Lead with your verdict in one sentence, and keep the whole answer under 600 words.
