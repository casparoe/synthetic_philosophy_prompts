I am a staff software engineer on the trust-and-safety side of a mid-size online platform. My training is entirely technical, so I will try to describe a pattern I keep running into in policy work without assuming any vocabulary for it, and I would like help identifying it.

The pattern has three stages, and I have now watched it run to completion four or five times.

Stage one. A prohibition is proposed on some category of content or account behavior. Asked what the practice does to anyone, the proponents have nothing to offer, and it is understood in the room that they have nothing to offer. Their reasons are of a different order: this is not the kind of place we are, it makes the product feel cheap, the users who built this community would not recognize it, our standards are themselves the asset. The proposal frequently passes on precisely that basis.

Stage two. Later — typically after an outside critic has observed that no injury to anyone has been identified — an injury is produced. A paper, an anecdote, an internal metric, a claim that the content pushes vulnerable users off the platform. Sometimes the injury is genuine and was simply not what anyone had in mind during the deliberation; sometimes it is not clearly genuine at all. In either case it is now presented as the reason the policy exists, and it migrates into public-facing documents.

Stage three. From then on the original ground has effectively vanished. Question the injury and you are told the policy was never only about the injury, and the standards reasons resurface — but as reminders of something settled rather than as premises open to argument. Question the standards reasons and you are referred to the injury. Neither support is ever on the table at the same time as the other, which is what makes the policy so hard to press against.

Two instances. We banned a genre of joke accounts because they made the site feel like a bad place to be; nine months later, answering a journalist, our stated rationale was that such accounts had coordinated to harass individual users, which was never part of the deliberation and for which we had no internal data at the time, but which now appears in a transparency report while the original reasoning survives only as a background assumption nobody is required to defend. Separately, we required non-anonymous display names on the ground that anonymity was beneath the tone of the product; when the resulting drop in sign-ups became awkward, the documented rationale became impersonation fraud and doxxing. I have since seen the ordering run the other way as well — a rule adopted on a claimed injury that the data failed to bear out, re-grounded a year later on the claim that the behavior was unbecoming of our community.

What I want to know is whether this has a name.

I can guess at neighbors, and I think each of them misses. There is the general observation that people manufacture reasons after the fact for judgments they already hold; but that concerns individuals and their psychology, and it does not involve one kind of justification being replaced by a different kind. There is the debate in legal philosophy over whether a society may prohibit conduct merely because its members find it degrading, when nobody is injured; but that debate appears to be about whether the standards-based reason is admissible in the first place, and it pictures people offering that reason openly, whereas what I am describing turns on the standards-based reason being withdrawn from view and supplanted. A colleague suggested this is just the familiar move of retreating to a defensible claim when attacked and returning to the strong one afterward; I am not convinced, because here the two claims are not a defensible core and an indefensible extension but grounds of different types, and the substitution is one-way and permanent rather than tactical.

So, specifically:

1. What are the nearest established concepts, and precisely how does each fall short of the thing I have described? I want the mismatch stated, not a list of candidates.
2. Has the phenomenon been described in the literature on law and morality, or in whatever literature deals with how institutions justify themselves to outsiders? I am not after references to chase down — I have no library access and no background in the field — only the concepts and the distinctions between them.
3. Where are the edges? Sometimes a genuine injury is discovered after a rule is adopted and the rationale is honestly revised in light of it, and I do not want to classify every such revision as this phenomenon. What separates the two, in terms I could apply to a policy document rather than to a description of people's motives?
4. If there is no name for it, propose one, with a definition tight enough that I could use it to sort real policy rationales into instances and non-instances, and say what the borderline cases look like.

I would also welcome being told that I have conflated two distinct patterns. The cases feel like one pattern to me, but I may be running together a claim about the order in which reasons arrive and a claim about their substitution.
