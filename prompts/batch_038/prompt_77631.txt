I am a junior, and for the past two years I have been the student moderator for my school's Philosophy of Science colloquium series. We bring in one outside speaker a term, and a student asks the questions in front of an audience that is roughly half AP Biology students and half students who have never read any philosophy at all; my biology teacher, who sits in the front row every time, is on record thinking that the vocabulary of codes and blueprints did real damage to how a generation understood the genome. Next term's guest is a philosopher of biology who argues the opposite. Her view, as I understand it from a recorded lecture and an interview, is that when molecular biologists say DNA carries information, contains a code, or specifies a message, they are speaking literally rather than loosely. The mapping from codons to amino acids is, on her account, a genuine sender–receiver system whose content was fixed by natural selection, so a gene really does carry instructions about what will be built, and the ribosomes, the tRNAs, and the synthetases are the receiving machinery rather than co-authors of the message. She is also known for a particular way of ending an exchange: when pressed, she tends to say that people who object to information talk in biology are usually motivated by a fear of genetic determinism rather than by arguments, and that once the anxiety is set aside the objection dissolves.

I do not want to walk in with eight versions of "isn't that just a metaphor," because she has a polished answer to that and my audience would learn nothing from hearing it. Please draft eight to ten questions for me, and for each one give a sentence or two on the answer she is most likely to give followed by a follow-up keyed to that specific answer. Each question needs to be short enough to ask aloud — two sentences at most — and any technical term has to be glossed inside the question itself, since half the room has never met it. I would like at least one question on each of the following, and I am happy to drop the rest of my own ideas to make room for them: the objection that the synthetases, the ribosomal RNA, the chaperones, and the maternal cytoplasm are just as necessary for the mapping as the DNA is, so why is DNA the sender and everything else merely the decoder, together with a follow-up that presses on what could distinguish a decoder from a second source of content; the fact that sender–receiver accounts usually require common interest between the two ends, and what intragenomic conflict — transposons, meiotic drive, elements that copy themselves at the expense of the organism — does to that requirement, including whether she would say such sequences are messages that lie; whether the contingency of the codon assignments cuts for or against her, since a mapping that is a frozen historical accident looks conventional in one breath and merely causal in the next, with a follow-up about the engineered bacteria in which a codon that used to terminate translation now specifies an amino acid — does a genome that works fine under a re-alphabeting show that the content survives the recoding, or that the alphabet was never carrying content to begin with; what test could be applied to any candidate cause to decide whether it carries information or only exerts influence, since an embryo's temperature also makes a systematic difference to what gets built; and where the message lives once alternative splicing, RNA editing, and methylation are on the table, if the message is not determinate until the receiving context is fixed. Beyond those, I want a run of two or three consecutive questions that make her determinism-anxiety deflection progressively harder to deploy — starting from a case where it genuinely might apply and ending somewhere it clearly cannot — and a final question asking what discovery or experiment would, in principle, count against her position and lead her to give up literal information talk. One more question, if it fits, on the practical stakes: whether she can hold that genes literally carry instructions while still rejecting the "a gene for X" phrasing that shows up in popular science coverage.

Since I will be relying on these in front of a room of people who know more biology than I do, please be explicit about your own confidence. Tell me how sure you are that these questions represent the debate as its participants actually understand it rather than a caricature of it, and single out the one question where you are least certain either that the answer you predict is the one she would give, or that the objection has not already been answered in a standard way that I would look foolish for not knowing.
