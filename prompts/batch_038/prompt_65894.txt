I'm putting together a handout for a grad seminar on speech acts and I need a case built, not a lecture delivered. The question I want the class arguing about: does an illocutionary act need uptake to come off, and if so, whose uptake — the addressee's, or the wider audience's?

Build me a matched pair of scenarios. Same company, same two people, same words, same afternoon. In both, a CEO dismisses his COO. He has the authority to do it, he's completely sincere, he intends exactly what he says, and nobody is deceiving anybody, so no sincerity-flavoured infelicity is anywhere in play — I want the pressure to come from uptake alone.

- Case 1: everyone at the company hears it and acts on it within the hour — access revoked, payroll stopped, an interim named, the board emailed — except the COO herself, who has left for a two-week off-grid trek and learns nothing for a fortnight.
- Case 2: the COO hears it, understands it, accepts it, shakes on it, clears her desk that evening — and nobody else ever does. Nothing is recorded, the board isn't told, her access still works, and when she comes back the staff, hearing it for the first time from her, treat her as still in post.

Constraints: realistic, no sci-fi, no exotic devices, no emergencies, no deaths; each case one paragraph; then spell out explicitly what is held fixed across the two and what the single varied feature is. Stipulate that the company's bylaws and the local employment law are both silent on when a dismissal takes effect, so nobody can read the verdict straight off a rule. And nothing in your wording may prejudge the answer — no "the dismissal was thereby complete," no "as yet nothing had happened."

Then run the pair through three accounts: a convention-first Austinian one (accepted procedure, appropriate persons, appropriate circumstances, executed correctly and completely by everyone concerned); one on which uptake by the addressee is constitutive of the act; and one I'll call the ledger view, on which what matters is that the act gets into the shared public record, so uptake by the relevant audience is what does the work. Give the verdict each account returns in each case and why, and say which of the three the pair actually discriminates between — my guess is that it doesn't separate all three equally, and if you agree I want to know which one is left untested and how you'd fix that.

One objection I know is coming from the second row: the cases aren't matched, because in Case 1 the CEO is really addressing the staff rather than the COO, so the addressee shifts along with the uptake and nothing is isolated. Deal with it. Either reword the utterance so the address to her is unmistakable in both cases without breaking anything else, or explain why the objection doesn't in fact collapse the pair.

Finish with the audit: which feature of the cases is doing the real work, what happens to the verdicts if you delete it (say, if she finds out on day one in Case 1, or if in Case 2 the CEO emails HR an hour later), and how the defender of whichever account comes out worst would attack the construction rather than swallow it.

Don't close with a balanced survey of the options and hand the choice back to me. Commit: tell me which of the two scenarios, if either, describes a real dismissal, which of the three accounts you'd bet on, and where you think my setup is simply wrong if you think it is — including if the whole pairing strikes you as rigged in some way I haven't spotted.
