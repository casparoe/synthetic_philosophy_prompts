You are a philosopher who works on Wittgenstein and on Kripke's 1982 book on rules and private language, and who has also spent enough time around software standards bodies to speak their language without condescension in either direction. Think the task through carefully before you answer: for each item below, brainstorm at least two candidate translations into my vocabulary, decide which is more faithful and why, and then show me only the result, not the drafting.

I am a senior technical editor at a standards organization. I write normative specifications and I own the conformance test suites that accompany them. My native vocabulary consists of requirement statements and their keyword levels; the difference between a specification text, a reference implementation, and the behaviour actually observed in the field; test assertions and test oracles; observable behaviour versus internal state; semantic versioning and what counts as a breaking change; errata, interpretation requests, and the maintenance group that adjudicates them; and interoperability testing between independent implementations. I am expert in all of that. In philosophy I am a layperson: I read Kripke's book once about fifteen years ago and I retain the arithmetic example — someone who has never added numbers as large as 57 is asked for 68 plus 57, and answers 5 on the grounds that on every past occasion the function he meant agreed with ordinary addition but was really quus — but I could not now reconstruct the argument that the example is doing work in, or the various responses to it.

Here is the thought I cannot shake, and it is the reason I am asking. In my profession, a specification text together with a finite conformance suite does not determine a unique function from inputs to required outputs. Any implementation that satisfies every assertion we have written is, so far as our tests can tell, an implementation of some other requirement that coincides with ours on the whole suite and diverges elsewhere. Once, two independent vendors shipped conforming implementations of a clause about ordering guarantees and disagreed about whether a retransmitted message counts as a fresh acceptance; both passed every assertion we had, and neither was wrong by any test we could point to. We resolved it not by discovering what the text had always meant but by publishing an interpretation and adding assertions — that is, by fiat plus more tests, with the community of implementers as the arbiter. On my reading that is your quus situation, except that my colleagues and I appear to have solved it and to have done so without embarrassment.

Task. Restate the rule-following debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage. Take the pieces in turn: the skeptical paradox itself; the normativity point, that meaning something by a sign is supposed to involve an obligation about future applications rather than a mere tendency; the dispositionalist reply and whatever you take to be the decisive objection to it; and the move to agreement within a community, together with the distinction between a straight and a skeptical solution, since I suspect that distinction does real work here. For each, give the closest available formulation in the language of specs, assertions, oracles, reference implementations, conformance, and interpretation procedure.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually want. Sort the material explicitly into three classes: claims that translate cleanly; claims that translate only with a distortion you must name and assess; and claims that have no formulation at all in my vocabulary. Two constraints on honesty. Do not smuggle in my framework's assumptions and present them as translation — in particular, do not quietly grant that what a specification means is whatever its conformance suite certifies, because that is precisely one of the things under examination. Then answer the question I care about. Does the residue show that specification-and-test practice is missing something — some resource for fixing meaning that my discipline has no name for and that its practitioners would not recognize if it were pointed out — or does it show that the philosophers' disagreement is less substantive than it looks, so that everyone concerned is simply running the errata process under different terminology? Take a side, argue for it, state your confidence candidly, and tell me what evidence or what counterexample would move you.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning after it, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. A table is welcome for the piece-by-piece mapping; the residue report and the final verdict should be prose.
