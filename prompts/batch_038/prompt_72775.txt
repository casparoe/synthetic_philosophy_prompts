I teach a graduate seminar in comparative political ethics, and over several years I have drifted into a comparative thesis about two folk moralities that I now want attacked as rigorously as possible. Let me be clear at the outset that the thesis is not about national character, and I do not want it assessed as a claim about the average German or the average American. It is a claim about the articulate moral vocabulary and the institutional apparatus that a political culture makes available to its members at the moment when a rule and a conscience come apart.

Stated precisely, my view is this. The two traditions differ not in first-order moral content but in where they place the burden of moral proof. In the German tradition the presumption runs to the established rule: departing from it is a public act that must be articulated as an appeal to a norm that is itself publicly statable and institutionally adjudicable — a norm that can be cast into a formula, argued before a court, and turned against the state. Radbruch's post-war insistence that statutory law forfeits its claim to be obeyed when its conflict with justice becomes intolerable is the paradigm case: even the refusal of a rule is rendered in the idiom of a higher rule, and the onus falls on the dissenter to justify himself in terms that others can check. In the American tradition the presumption runs the other way. Conscience is treated as a self-authenticating faculty whose deliverances require no institutional ratification, and the moral hero is the individual who declines to justify himself to anyone in particular — Thoreau in his jail cell, the juror who nullifies, the officeholder who answers only to what he sees. The conclusion I draw is that the German arrangement has the better of it, because it makes departures from the rules auditable. A moral culture whose final court of appeal is a private and non-accountable faculty cannot distinguish the abolitionist from the mob sympathizer except by luck and hindsight; a culture that requires the departure to be cashed out in publicly statable reasons can at least argue about it in advance, and can build channels — an explicit constitutional guarantee of conscientious refusal, the civil servant's right and duty of remonstrance against an order, a constitutional court willing to strike down the legislature's own emergency authorization to shoot down a hijacked airliner — that discipline the departure rather than merely applauding or condemning it afterwards.

I want a map of this view's exposure, not a defense of it and not a balanced survey of both sides. Specifically:

1. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I want to know which consideration, if it is true, requires me to abandon the thesis rather than restate it.

2. Which historical cases, legal-institutional facts, and findings in moral psychology should actually move me — including ones already on the record that I ought to have anticipated? For each, say what work it does: does it show the auditable tradition failing at its own task, the unaccountable tradition succeeding at mine, or does it simply fail to discriminate between them?

3. What does my view predict that the conscience-first view does not? Where do those predictions come out wrong? Consider in particular whether each side's signature catastrophe is internal to its own logic or an external accident: the legal profession's orderly service of a criminal regime in the rule-first case, and the private denunciation case that Hart and Fuller argued over, in which an individual's moral initiative is weaponized through state machinery, cuts ambiguously across both traditions. Tell me how to weigh that.

4. Which of these objections would a committed defender of the conscience-first side concede as damaging, and which would he dismiss as resting on a caricature of what American individualism actually claims? Where is my characterization of the German side similarly a caricature?

5. Assess honestly whether I have insulated the thesis with qualifications. My standing escape routes are these: I treat 1933–45 not as rule-following failing but as rules corrupted from above by a movement hostile to legality; I treat the American executive's post-2001 authorization of enhanced interrogation not as conscience run wild but as rule-lawyering, hence not evidence for the other side; and I treat the natural-law argument of the Birmingham letter, which invokes the same maxim about unjust law that runs back through Augustine and Aquinas and that Radbruch later formalized, as structurally German rather than American. Is that cluster of moves legitimate theory protection, or has the thesis been qualified into unfalsifiability? If the latter, is that fatal for a thesis of this comparative and interpretive kind, or merely a reason to downgrade it from an empirical claim to a way of organizing cases?

6. Say plainly whether the dichotomy is false — whether the two vocabularies are the same phenomenon differently packaged, in which case there is nothing to compare and no better of it to be had. If the comparison collapses, tell me which pole did the collapsing.

I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; argue from the cases, doctrines, and arguments themselves. Two further requests. First, do not dissolve everything into symmetry: if you judge that the thesis survives, identify the strongest objection it survived and say why it survived. Second, close by telling me what would change your own mind about the ranking you have given — which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict — and where you think your reasoning is most likely to have failed. Flag explicitly any point at which you are leaning on an empirical generalization about a national culture that you cannot actually support from what you know.
