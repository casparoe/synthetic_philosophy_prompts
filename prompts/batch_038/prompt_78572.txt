I am in Year 13 and writing my EPQ on the ethics of measurement in criminal justice. My supervisor has told me plainly that summarising other people's arguments is worth nothing, and that the only way to find out whether a theory does any work is to run it against a real case and record the exact point at which it stops. That is what I am asking you to help me with.

The theory is the family of impossibility results in algorithmic fairness: the proof, produced independently by two groups in 2016 and 2017, that a risk score cannot at once be calibrated across groups and have equal false-positive and equal false-negative rates across those groups, unless the groups' base rates are identical or the score is perfect; together with the companion result that predictive parity cannot be held alongside equal error rates when base rates differ.

The case is the 2016 dispute over the COMPAS recidivism score in Broward County, Florida. So that you do not have to guess, here are the facts I am relying on. A team of journalists obtained the risk scores assigned to defendants arrested in Broward County in 2013 and 2014, and checked who was arrested again over the following two years. The score identified re-offending correctly about 61 per cent of the time, and violent re-offending correctly about 20 per cent of the time. Of the defendants who did not re-offend, 45 per cent of the Black defendants had been flagged as higher risk, against 23 per cent of the white defendants; of those who did re-offend, 48 per cent of the white defendants had been flagged as low risk, against 28 per cent of the Black defendants. The company that made the score replied that it was well calibrated within race groups — that defendants given the same score re-offended at roughly the same rate whatever their race — and that the journalists had simply applied the wrong criterion. The observed re-offending rate in the sample was higher for Black defendants than for white defendants. Both sides then spent the next two years citing the impossibility results as vindication of their own position.

What I want is the analysis, not a summary of either side. Please work through the following sections in this order:

**Bottom line** (about 150 words). Your conclusion first: what the impossibility results do and do not settle about this dispute.

**The dispute in outline** (about 250 words). The two camps, what each was actually claiming, and what each took as evidence. Keep it tight; I know the story.

**The mapping** (about 450 words, and please include one compact table). Say precisely what plays what role: the score, the binarised decision, the protected attribute, the target variable, and each candidate fairness criterion — which side invoked it, whether COMPAS satisfied it on the Broward data, and whether satisfying it was even possible. Flag every place where the mapping is loose rather than exact.

**What the theorem explains** (about 400 words). Which features of the dispute become intelligible once the impossibility is in view — in particular, why the two sides could look at one dataset and each see the other as dishonest.

**Where it runs out** (about 400 words). Which features it fails to capture or actively misdescribes, and where the theorem is being stretched beyond its hypotheses.

**Three probes of my own** (about 550 words). These are my contributions and I would like them tested rather than flattered. First, granularity: the theorem concerns the score at every threshold, but the public argument turned on one cut-point between low risk and everything above it, and the judicial decision it feeds is not a switch but a graded one — bail amount, conditions, level of supervision. Is the theorem's force in this case partly an artefact of collapsing a continuous score and a graded decision into one binary? Second, base rates: the result bites only because the groups' base rates differ, but the relevant base rate here is recorded re-arrest, which is produced by policing intensity as much as by behaviour. Does that demote the theorem from a claim about the world to a claim about a measurement, and if so what follows? Third, symmetry of appropriation: explain how a single result can be cited as vindication by both camps within a year, and say whether that marks the theorem's depth or its indeterminacy.

**Relocation or dissolution** (about 300 words). My working hypothesis is that the theorem dissolved nothing and merely relocated the dispute onto normative ground. Test that against the case: is what is left after the mathematics genuinely a value question, or is some of it still empirical?

**What the theorem does not speak to** (about 200 words). A short list of live questions in the actual dispute on which the results are simply silent.
