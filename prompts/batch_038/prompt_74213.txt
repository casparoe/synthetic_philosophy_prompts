I am a third-year philosophy student, and alongside my ancient ethics lecture I facilitate an ungraded discussion section of twenty students that meets once a week for seventy-five minutes. Next week we cover Aristotle on exchange and money: the reciprocity chapter of the Nicomachean Ethics, with its builder-and-shoemaker case and the crosswise proportion, plus the Politics contrast between household management and money-getting. The students will already have read both. I would rather not spend the hour talking through them; I want an activity in which they run into Aristotle's problem before anyone names it.

What I want them to come away seeing is four things, in this order. First, that the proportionality requirement is not a demand that equal value change hands but a demand for a crosswise ratio between parties of different standing and goods of different kinds. Second, that without a common measure this ratio is practically unfixable: bargaining across incommensurable goods stalls, or collapses into whichever player is most persuasive. Third, that a conventional token measure solves the coordination problem, but only as a stand-in for need, since Aristotle concedes that things so unlike in kind are not commensurable in truth, only well enough for exchange to happen. Fourth, that once the proxy exists, players begin to optimize the proxy rather than the goods it was supposed to track.

Please design the activity so that those four things emerge from the mechanics rather than from anything I say beforehand. I have in mind, though you should feel free to improve on it: an opening round of direct barter in which each player holds a private ranking of needs that cannot be fully expressed in the terms of the trade; a second round in which a token currency is introduced and its scale is fixed by a decision of the group, so that the students experience commensurability as something instituted rather than discovered; and a closing round in which tokens may be lent at interest, so that money begets money with no goods changing hands at all. Give me full rules, a materials list limited to things I can assemble from a stationery cupboard and a printer, a payoff scheme that uses no real money and no grades, the text of any role cards or handouts, and a minute-by-minute timeline that fits the seventy-five minutes including the debrief.

The debrief should map particular moments in play onto particular claims in the texts, and say what each moment is supposed to have made visible. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: a student who dominates the haggling, a group that just divides the tokens evenly to be agreeable, someone who pre-solves the ratio with arithmetic and deflates the puzzle. For each, give me an intervention that repairs the session without announcing the point I wanted them to discover. Be frank about which feature of Aristotle's account the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in words. My own suspicion is that it is the claim that the proportion is a requirement of justice rather than of prudence: a game will make it feel like a coordination problem, not a normative one. Tell me whether you agree, or whether the design can be adjusted to resist that drift.

Finally, please be explicit about where your design rests on a contested reading. There is a live interpretive dispute over whether Aristotle holds that need is the real measure and money a mere proxy for it, or that the institution of money is itself what makes otherwise incommensurable goods comparable, with no deeper measure behind it. State which side your mechanics implicitly take, and what would have to change in the activity to stage the other side instead. And tell me what would change your mind about the plan: which element you think most likely fails in practice or teaches something false, and what consideration would lead you to redesign it.
