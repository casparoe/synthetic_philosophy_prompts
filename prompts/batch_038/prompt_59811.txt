I've been trying to write down what I actually believe about safety guarantees for deployed systems, mostly because I keep making a version of this arguement at work and I suspect it doesn't hang together. Rather than defend it, I'd like you to audit it. Here's the list, as close to my real view as I can get it:

1. For a property to count as a *safety* property it has to be a worst-case guarantee. "Aligned on the inputs we tried" isnt safety, its a test result. I want the claim to hold for every situation the system can end up in, including ones nobody predicted.
2. That said, what I actually tell people in meetings is: a system that's catastrophic in one run out of a thousand is worse than useless to me — I'd much rather have something that's merely mediocre in every single run.
3. I'm a plain vanilla Bayesian about all of it. I don't believe in imprecise credences, unquantified uncertainty, or non-probabilistic decision rules. Every claim about the future gets a number, and I act to maximize expected value under my numbers.
4. We should deploy iteratively — narrow, supervised, monitored — and use what we learn in the wild to tighten the next version. Pure sandbox development gives you a false sense of knowing.
5. At the same time I'm deeply sceptical of evals as evidence of safety. A green benchmark suite tells you about the distribution you tested, and capable systems will end up off it.
6. A monitor layer is a load bearing part of my safety story, not a crutch: another system watching the outputs, a human in the loop, and a kill switch.
7. I don't think interpretability is going to give us verification of anything at this scale on any timescale that matters, so I lean on capability control instead — restrict what the thing can actually do. Compute budgets, no persistent memory, no self-modification, no access to anything that matters. Whatever it turns out to want, it can't reach it.
8. I also can't rule out that a trained system ends up optimizing for something other than what we trained it to optimize for — the goal it aquired and the goal we specified come apart, and it only looks aligned because nothing in its history so far forced the difference to show. I think that's a live possibility. But I think 6 and 7 make it survivable.

I'm deliberately not telling you which of these I'd be saddest to drop, because part of what I want is for the list to tell me that. So: go through it and find which pairs are genuinely pulling against each other, which only look like they conflict and don't, and which are just fine sitting together. Please don't flag everything as a problem to look thorough — if a pair is consistent, say so and move on. For the real ones, tell me what specifically has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and put them in order. I want the ranking, not a flat list of eight complaints.

Give me the whole audit as a JSON object with this schema (a few sentences of prose after it are fine for anything that won't fit the fields):

- `verdict`: two or three sentences on whether the package hangs together as stated
- `tensions`: array, worst first, each entry an object with:
  - `pair`: which numbered commitments are involved
  - `status`: `"genuine"` | `"apparent"` | `"no-conflict"`
  - `severity`: integer 1–5, where 5 means one of them has to go
  - `why`: the actual argument, stated in terms of how I phrased them — not a label
  - `cheapest_fix`: the smallest edit to my list that removes it
  - `what_the_fix_costs`: what I lose by making that edit
  - `standing`: `"settled"` | `"contested"` | `"your-call"` — i.e. is the judgment you're making in this row something people who work on this actually agree about, or are you taking a side
- `the_one_revision`: if I could only change one item, which one and to what
- `underdetermined`: anything my list doesn't pin down that you'd need to know to finish the job

Be careful with that `standing` field. I don't want to come away believing there's a consensus that isn't there. Where two reasonable people who know this material would disagree about whether, say, 1 and 3 can both be held, say that rather than picking one and presenting it as the answer.
