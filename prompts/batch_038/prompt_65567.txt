For nearly a year I have been arguing with a neighbor about whether our town should repeal an almost century-old ordinance requiring every new house on our street to provide two off-street parking spaces. He argues on the traditionalist side and always reaches for Chesterton's fence, in its popular paraphrase about not taking a fence down until you know the reason why it was put up. Curious, I tracked down the actual passage, and two things in it struck me. The demand Chesterton's "more intelligent type of reformer" makes is not about origins at all: he will allow the fence destroyed only "when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it." The origin talk appears separately, as an assertion — that the gate or fence "did not grow there," that "some person had some reason for thinking it would be a good thing for somebody."

Our ordinance looks to me like precisely the case Chesterton excludes by stipulation. It was lifted from a model code no one on the council wrote, amended three times by people with three different motives, and it now stands partly on reasons nobody ever had — it keeps certain lot sizes profitable, and it saves the assessor a category of disputes.

So I would like an audit of the analogy applied to a practice that genuinely did grow. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the fence, the road, the reformer, the person who had a reason, and the going-away-and-thinking. Then the places the mapping fails, separated into the failures that defeat the conservative conclusion and those that do not. Specifically: does the origin premise do any work of its own once the use premise is present, or can the whole demand be restated as a question about why a practice persists rather than why it began? And if a fence is the wrong image for accreted cases, which source domain would carry the argument better — a footpath worn across a field, a coral reef, an old codebase full of patches no one dares delete — and does it point where Chesterton points, or the other way?
