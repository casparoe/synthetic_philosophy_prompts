I am in my final year of high school, and in a few weeks I am giving a talk to our school's philosophy society. I constructed an argument leading to Parfit's repugnant conclusion, planning to reject the conclusion at the end and show the room where the reasoning breaks down. The difficulty is that I can no longer locate the break, and I would much rather discover that now than discover it in front of an audience. What I want from you is an identification of the step that does not follow — or, if every step does follow, an account of which premise is cheapest to surrender and what I inherit by surrendering it.

The setup. Begin with a population, A: ten billion people, every one of whom enjoys an excellent life — full of the relationships, projects, and pleasures that make existence worth having. Now consider A+: everything in A exactly as before, plus ten billion further people whose lives are worth living but clearly less good than the lives of the first group. Suppose the two groups are wholly cut off from one another. They never meet, never learn of one another, and cannot affect one another in any way.

My argument is this.

1. A+ is not worse than A. Nobody who exists in A loses anything, because the groups cannot interact; and the added people have lives worth living, which is a good thing for them. If nothing is taken from anyone and something is given to some, I do not see how the result can be a worse state of affairs.

2. Now consider Alpha: a population of the same total size as A+ in which everyone enjoys a very high quality of life — slightly below the level of the best-off group in A+, far above the level of the other group, and equal for all. Alpha is better than A+. Nearly everyone in it is better off than the corresponding person in A+, only some are slightly worse off, and a large inequality has disappeared. A change that is good for almost everyone, seriously bad for no one, and that removes a gross disparity in how well people's lives go must be an improvement.

3. From Alpha I can repeat both moves: add another group with lives worth living but lower than Alpha's level, producing Alpha+, and then equalize upward to just below the best level in Alpha+, producing Beta. Nothing stops me from continuing, lowering the level a little at each round.

4. Every step in the series is therefore, by 1 and 2, either not worse than the step before it or strictly better than it. Nothing in the series is ever a decline.

5. I take "at least as good as" to be transitive across such a series. I am applying one and the same standard of comparison to whole populations at every step, and a standard that failed here would leave me unable to compare distant populations at all.

6. So the last population in the series is at least as good as A; and since many of the steps are strictly better and none is worse, the last population is better than A.

7. Carried far enough, the last population is Z: an unimaginably vast population in which every life is barely worth living — worth continuing, but stripped of nearly everything that makes life in A good.

8. Therefore Z is better than A.

I cannot accept 8. Whenever I picture Z and picture A side by side, A seems plainly better, and I do not think that is merely a failure of imagination or an inability to hold very large numbers in my head. At the same time I am attached to the thought behind 1: that bringing into existence further people whose lives are worth living cannot make the world worse. If I were forced to choose, I would give up 5 before I gave up 1 — but I am aware that this is a preference and not a reason, so please do not simply report my preferences back to me. Tell me which premise is actually untenable.

Three things would help most. First, name the step that fails and explain why it seemed to hold when I wrote it, since I wrote each of them thinking it was obvious. Second, if you think the argument survives, tell me which premise I should concede to a room of skeptical seventeen-year-olds and state precisely which conclusion I then have to accept instead. Third, tell me whether the stipulation that the two groups in A+ cannot interact is doing any real work in the argument, or whether I added it for comfort and it changes nothing.
