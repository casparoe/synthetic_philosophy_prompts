A friend and I argued for two hours last night about whether counterfactual history is coherent — whether it makes sense to ask what would have happened had some individual decided otherwise — and we both ended up claiming the same text as our witness. Her line was that outcomes are the product of many individual wills in conflict, so any one of them could have been different and the event with it; mine was that this is empty, because each will is itself fixed by prior conditions, so the resultant is as necessary as the forces that produce it. We both pointed to this passage from Engels's letter to Joseph Bloch, London, 21 September 1890 (Progress Publishers translation):

> In the second place, however, history is made in such a way that the final result always arises from conflicts between many individual wills, of which each in turn has been made what it is by a host of particular conditions of life. Thus there are innumerable intersecting forces, an infinite series of parallelograms of forces which give rise to one resultant — the historical event. This may again itself be viewed as the product of a power which works as a whole unconsciously and without volition. For what each individual wills is obstructed by everyone else, and what emerges is something that no one willed. Thus history has proceeded hitherto in the manner of a natural process and is essentially subject to the same laws of motion. But from the fact that the wills of individuals … do not attain what they want, but are merged into an aggregate mean, a common resultant, it must not be concluded that they are equal to zero. On the contrary, each contributes to the resultant and is to this extent included in it.

Could you help me work through it? First, set out plainly, step by step, what the argument of the passage is and what the mechanics of vector composition is being made to do. Second: in the composition picture, altering one force alters the resultant, so where does any necessity come from — and does it matter that in the preceding paragraph Engels defines an accident as something whose "inner interconnection is so remote or so impossible of proof that we can regard it as non-existent, as negligible," only to add that the economic movement "finally asserts itself as necessary"? Is that distinction between accident and necessity metaphysical, or merely a claim about what a historian can trace? Third, does the closing insistence that individual wills are not "equal to zero" actually answer the objection that no one's choices matter, or does it restate the problem? Please mark clearly where the passage underdetermines the reading instead of settling our dispute for us.
