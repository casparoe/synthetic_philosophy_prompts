Before retiring I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, and for the past three years I have been reading moral philosophy seriously. Next month I give a talk in a university ethics seminar defending a version of moral particularism, argued from a direction I have not encountered in that literature: I claim the generalists' insistence on exceptionless principles rests on a mistaken picture of what a genuine law must look like, since the fundamental generalisations of physics are hedged by idealising conditions and supplemented by correction terms, and they count as laws not because they hold without exception but because the departures are systematically tractable. Moral principles, on my view, are zeroth-order approximations whose correction terms are real but not computable, which is why every principle yet proposed has had counterexamples manufactured for it.

The audience is about a dozen moral philosophers — Kantians, rule-consequentialists, and virtue ethicists who take principles to be indispensable to deliberation and moral education — together with one or two graduate students already sympathetic to particularism. I would be grateful for a preparation sheet for the question period: the objections this audience will actually press, in roughly the order they will come and in the vocabulary they would use, each with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is softest. Please separate the objections that follow from their substantive commitments from the methodological ones, say which of them I should concede in my opening rather than fight, and identify the single reply that must land if the talk is not to fail. Finally, tell me which question I should most hope nobody asks — the one that would expose the weakest joint in the analogy with physics.
