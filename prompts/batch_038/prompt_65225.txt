so I run a small seminar thing at work — mostly analysts, a couple of historians seconded from a university, one guy who's basically a Bayesian — and for two years we've been going in circles about whether historical analogy is usable in policy work. Munich, Vietnam, the Thucydides trap business. Nobody has moved an inch and what I want is a diagnosis of the argument rather than a verdict on it.

Here's roughly the two camps as they actually talk. The skeptics say an analogy is never chosen on its antecedent conditions, it's chosen after we already know the outcome, so what looks like disciplined inference is a story fitted backwards; and when we reach for Munich we aren't learning from 1938, we're reusing the one case that survived in memory precisely because it has a clean moral shape. They want mechanisms and local detail and they think the analogists are just laundering a prior. The analogists say there is no non-analogical route to a judgment about a one-off macro event — you cant run the experiment, n=1, and 'reason from first principles about the mechanism' is a demand nothing could ever satisfy — so the job is to discipline the analogy instead: name the disanalogies, state the prediction it implies, score it later. Each side thinks the other is either naive or unfalsifiable, and they talk past each other inside the same meeting.

My own hypothesis, which may well be wrong: the real disagreement sits one level down, in the archive. The pool of analogies anyone can draw on is itself selected on the dependent variable — we remember the cases with reconstructable antecedents and vivid outcomes and forget the rest — so the base rate the analogists want to appeal to doesn't exist to be estimated, and the skeptics' 'fitted backwards' complaint isn't a sin of the individual reasoner, it's a property of the memory store everyone's drawing from. If that's right, both camps are arguing about a method while the actual problem is a corrupted sample, and that's why neither can land a hit.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Verbal — is 'analogy' doing double duty, a private inference heuristic for one camp and a public persuasion device for the other, so each is attacking the other's sense? Two seperate questions under one heading, one about whether single cases license inference and one about how deliberation actually goes in a room? A hidden empirical question that could in principle be measured — do decisions framed by analogy turn out worse than decisions framed by explicit forecasts? Or bedrock stuff about whether one-off historical events can support generalisation at all, where neither side can argue for its starting intuition?

Diagnose it using what each side actually says, not with a tour of famous analogies — we all know them — and no reading list please. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, some clever natural experiment, a change of question. And if your answer is 'none of those, the stalemate is the correct resting place', I want that argued for too rather than asserted.
