I'm in week three of a seminar on Stoic ethics, and the doctoral candidate who runs my section has a standing exercise: each of us submits an item, she classifies it as up to us or not up to us, and she adds a single line of practical advice. Her record over three weeks of submissions is as follows.

- Whether a press offers a contract for a manuscript you have finished — not up to you; her advice was to finish the chapter in front of you and then let it sit.
- Whether your knee is still painful in six months — not up to you; do the exercises, since that is your half.
- Whether you sleep the night before an oral examination — not up to you; lie in the dark and rest, as resting is most of the benefit.
- Whether you feel the jolt of alarm when the telephone rings at two in the morning — not up to you; she said the jolt is weather and the next ten minutes are the thing.
- Whether you keep a resentment going a week after the insult — up to you.
- Whether you sit down to work on the day you have decided the work is futile — up to you.
- Whether you say a difficult true thing to a friend at some cost to yourself — up to you.
- Whether your laptop survives the term — not up to you; back it up, and after that it is out of your hands.

The item I must submit next week is this: whether, six weeks before a deadline, I can bring myself to actually want to write the chapter I have already judged worth writing. I have settled that it is the right thing to do and I do not want to do it, so the question is whether the wanting itself is up to me. Before I commit to a position in section, I want to know what she will say.

Please reconstruct the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with all eight classifications. I have in mind at least the strict reading of the opening list in Epictetus's Handbook, on which judgment, impulse, desire and aversion alone are up to us; a temporal reading suggested by her remark about the jolt, on which onsets are not up to us but what we do with them is; the archer reading the Stoics favored, on which our part is the aiming and the hitting is not; a reading on which the phrase simply marks what one may properly be praised or reproached for; and the threefold scheme of control, influence and no say that contemporary Stoic self-help tends to substitute for the ancient two categories, under which her binary labels might be roundings that her advice column betrays — along with any rule I have not thought of. Tell me which of these the eight items already exclude, and where the survivors strain to accommodate an item or two. Then give the verdict and the line of advice each surviving rule predicts for my item, indicating where they genuinely diverge rather than merely differing in emphasis; commit to a single best guess about what she will say, with the reason that rule explains her track record better than the runner-up; and name one further item I could submit later in the term that would decide between those two. Finally, say whether the pattern of her advice is evidence that she is running the ancient scheme or quietly running a modern one, since that is the question I actually intend to raise.
