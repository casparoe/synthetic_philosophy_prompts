You are a philosopher who specializes in collective judgment and voting procedure, and you have advised real selection committees. Think the case through carefully before answering.

I chair the nine-member committee that awards our learned society's annual prize. The bylaws say a work qualifies only if it meets all three criteria: technical originality, likely practical impact within a decade, and fit with the prize's stated subject area. This year we voted criterion by criterion on each of the two finalists and then voted on the award itself. Both finalists carried every criterion, 6–3 — but never by the same six members, and the defecting three were different each time. On the final vote both finalists were rejected 7–2, so we gave no prize at all. Four members have since written to me saying that, personally, they thought one of the two clearly deserved it.

It gets worse. Last year we ran the votes in the opposite order, deciding the award first and recording the criteria afterwards; the prize then went to a work that failed one criterion 5–4. That year's runner-up has now appealed, and the society's director has asked me for a written position before she responds.

So: which order is the right one, or is the choice arbitrary given what our bylaws actually say? Would a different rule — members ranking the finalists, a two-thirds threshold, letting me break ties, or splitting us into three subpanels of one criterion each — avoid the trouble, or will every rule generate some version of it? And what, concretely, should I tell the appellant?

Reply with exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, and nothing else — no preamble, no closing summary.
