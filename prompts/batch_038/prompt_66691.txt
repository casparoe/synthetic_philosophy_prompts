I'm the instructor for an upper-level seminar on the governance of advanced technology and I'm marking the short-answer section of the final. 40 students, and there's a second reader who has to sign off on my marks, so whatever I write has to be able to stand on its own if she disagrees with me. This question was worth 20 points.

Here's the question as it appeared on the exam:

"State Bostrom's singleton hypothesis. Explain what a singleton is, and why a technologically mature civilisation would tend to end up with one rather than with a system of competing powers. Give one argument that a singleton would be a good thing and one that it would be a bad thing. Finally, say whether the hypothesis is a prediction, and what would count as evidence for or against it."

And here is the student's answer, transcribed from the blue book:

"A singleton is a political arrangement in which there is one decision-making agency at the highest level, so nothing sits above it and it has no external rivals. Bostrom is careful that this doesn't have to be a world government in the constitutional sense with a parliament and a bill of rights; it could be a machine intelligence, or a very tight coalition that acts as one. The point is that there's a single locus of final authority.

The hypothesis says that a civilisation that reaches technological maturity will form a singleton, and that once formed it will be stable. The mechanism is the singularity. If intelligence can be amplified recursively, then whoever gets there first pulls away from everyone else, and because the gap keeps widening there's no way for the others to catch up or to balance against it. So the singleton is basically what the world looks like after the singularity — it's the political name for the post-singularity condition, and it's inevitable in the same way the singularity itself is.

The good thing about a singleton is coordination. Under anarchy, states face prisoner's dilemmas: they overfish, they emit, they each build the dangerous technology a bit faster than is safe because they're afraid the other side will get there first. A single agency internalises all of that. It can ban a line of research everywhere at once, price externalities globally, and spend on projects with thousand-year payoffs instead of election-cycle payoffs, rather than every power duplicating the same R&D. Of course these benefits only actually arrive if the singleton is benevolent, which is why the value-alignment problem has to be solved first.

The bad thing is lock-in. A singleton has no competitors, so if its values are bad they stay bad forever — there's no rival society doing better that people can compare themselves to or defect to, and no external force that can overthrow it. Historically empires decayed partly because dissidents could emigrate or because a neighbouring power took over; with a true singleton there's nowhere to go. The other danger is that without peer competition innovation stalls, which is the price of the coordination gains.

Is it a prediction? Yes, straightforwardly. It says something will happen in the future and it could fail to happen. We would test it by watching whether today's international institutions keep consolidating — the EU, the UN, the WTO — into something with real enforcement power. So far the evidence is mixed to bad: two hundred years of great-power rivalry, a Security Council that can't act, and no move at all toward a world state. That counts against the hypothesis, unless the consolidation only kicks in once the technology arrives, in which case the hypothesis is safe but also unfalsifiable for now."
