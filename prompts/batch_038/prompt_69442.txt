I'm prepping next week's session for our philosophy of language reading group and instead of assigning a paper I want to hand people a short dialogue. Topic: whether indicative conditionals state facts at all, and what that does to counterfactuals.

Set it in a small betting shop, ten minutes after the fourth race, at the counter where the bookie is willing to lay a novelty bet. Two customers, both philosophers, both competent, arguing about whether the bet makes sense:

- one's a Lewis-style possible-worlds theorist: both kinds of conditional have truth conditions, a counterfactual is true or false according to what happens in the nearest worlds where the antecedent holds, and indicatives are every bit as factual;
- the other's an Adams/de Finetti type: an indicative conditional doesn't report a fact at all, you assert it by supposing the antecedent and seeing how things look from there, and the betting analogue is that a conditional bet is a bet on the consequent that gets called off, stake back, if the antecedent fails — so it comes out true, false, or void.

The bet on the board is "If the favourite won the fourth, the stewards missed the interference in the stretch." Nobody knows yet, the photo's still up. The Lewisian wants to know what happens when the second customer negates that sentence, or when somebody says "either that or the track was too soft" — the void option has to survive being embedded inside other things and he thinks it cant. The de Finetti type thinks that's a cheap trick and says so. Then the result goes up: favourite lost by a nose. The bookie, annoyed, offers to lay the second bet too — "If the favourite had won the fourth, the stewards would have missed the interference." Same content, subjunctive, antecedent now known false, so the void clause has nothing left to bite on and the Adams man has to say whether that bet is even a coherent thing to lay. That's the pivot I want the piece to turn on, and he also owes some account of what became of the first bet, which under his own view nobody can now assert.

Make them answer each other rather than trade speeches. I want the triviality result on the table at some point — why the Lewisian can't have both his truth conditions and the equation between the probability of a conditional and the corresponding conditional probability — and I want the counterfactual side of it too, since his own view has those two coming apart, so the Adams man should press him on why that isnt just as bad for him. No walkovers, nobody converts, and it ends at a genuine impasse.

Then step outside the dialogue for three or four sentences and tell me flat out which of the two you think is right about indicatives and why, and whether you'd let that bookie lay the second bet. Commit on that part, don't lay the options out and leave it to me. No quotes from anybody, no references, and keep the whole thing, closing note included, under 700 words.
