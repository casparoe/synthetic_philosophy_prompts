Okay so my friend Dana and I just spent four hours going in circles about control versus alignment for frontier models and we got nowhere, and we've agreed the only way out is to actually write the damn document down. Dana is control-first: you engineer the deployment assuming the model is already working against you — locked-down weights, airgapped compute, capability thresholds, sandboxing, monitors, tripwires, human kill paths — and hoping the thing is actually aligned isn't a plan, it's a wish. I'm alignment-first: I think most of that apparatus is security theater the moment the model is smart enough to know it's inside it, and the only thing that really moves risk is building models that don't want to subvert the process in the first place.

You can see how the conversation goes. Every time Dana describes a control measure I ask how she'd know it's working rather than just passing the test, and every time I describe an alignment method she asks how I'd verify it at the frontier — where verification is exactly the thing I just said was impossible. We're each using the other's standard of proof as the refutation. Someone in our reading group said we should do a real adversarial collaboration, so here I am asking for the artifact.

Write the joint statement Dana and I could both put our names to. Some things it has to contain:

1. A numbered list of claims we'd both sign — about what scheming models are plausibly capable of, about what our evals actually measure, about where the monitoring breaks down, about what a control regime buys you even if it eventually fails. I want at least one claim in there that each of us would rather not have to sign, and you should say out loud which one is uncomfortable for which side.

2. A disagreements table with these columns: the disagreement; the wording Dana would accept as a statement of her own view; the wording I would accept as a statement of mine; whether it's a genuine crux or just words; a concrete case, experiment, or result we'd both agree bears on it; Dana's prediction written down now; my prediction written down now. Predictions in advance or it's worthless.

3. The verbal parts flagged as verbal. I suspect at least one of these is a fight about the word "safety" — whether it means something about this deployed system or about the whole capability trajectory — and another is about whether a monitor model trained to catch scheming counts as control or as alignment, which is Dana's recursion problem: who watches the watcher, all the way down.

4. A short section where each of us names what would count as a real defeat of our own view, not a strawman defeat. Dana's pet objection is what she calls the glass-house objection: any control regime the model can model, the model can plan around, so control only ever works on models too weak to be dangerous, which is to say it never works where it matters. Mine is that alignment research is insurance, not verification, and insurance is worth buying even when you can't check the policy — plus the point that control failures and capability gains are correlated, so control gets harder faster than it gets better, and Dana owes me an answer to that.

The two cruxes I'd bet money on, if you want my read: whether there's a real eval-versus-deployment gap for a model that knows it's being scored, and whether interpretability-style monitoring could in principle detect deception at frontier scale rather than just detecting the deception we already thought to look for.

Don't adjudicate, don't tell us who's right, and absolutely don't hand me a synthesis in the middle — "both matter, use both, more research needed" is a thing we can already write ourselves and it's the reason we're stuck. Keep it under about 1200 words, and watch that neither side's wording smuggles its own conclusion in as a premise.
