I'm assembling a seminar unit on bounded rationality, and our discussion keeps sliding back to a defensive reading of Herbert Simon: satisficing is what you resort to when maximizing is too expensive, a second-best accommodation to search costs, deadlines, and fatigue. I want to test whether that reading is compulsory, and for that I need a counterexample built to order. Construct a thought experiment in which a satisficer makes a strictly better decision than a maximizer, where the advantage cannot be traced to any cost of searching. State the scenario in a single paragraph. Constraints: the setting must be an ordinary organizational one — a charity dividing its fundraising budget among donor-acquisition channels, a county health department splitting an outreach budget among clinics, a purchasing agent allocating orders among suppliers — and it must not be a financial portfolio, since equal-weighting beating an optimized allocation is the version of this that everyone already knows. Both agents have exactly the same data, the same unbounded computational capacity, and no time pressure; acquiring further information is genuinely free and would tell them nothing new. The maximizer must not be a strawman: it has a well-defined utility function, it reasons correctly in expected-utility terms, and it uses all the evidence the case supplies. The satisficer works from a fixed aspiration level that was set in an earlier period for reasons unrelated to the present decision, and accepts the first allocation that clears it. And the satisficer's victory must not be luck: the same structure has to produce the same verdict across repeated cycles of the decision, not merely in the instance you describe.

After the scenario, show the work. Say precisely what each agent computes and what each ends up doing; identify the specific step in the maximizer's calculation that fails; and name the feature of the case that does the work — my own suspicion is that it has to do with the ratio of noise in the per-channel estimates to the number of channels competing to be selected, but I want your account rather than mine. Then run the checks. What happens if that feature is removed: if the estimates are precise, or if there are only two channels rather than many, or if the environment is stationary and the historical sample is enormous? At which point does the case collapse back into an ordinary search-cost story, and does any part of the satisficer's advantage survive the collapse? I'd also like a direct answer to whether the satisficer is winning because it is doing something right or because it is doing something less wrong.

Finally, press the objection that decides whether the case is worth anything. A defender of maximizing will say the maximizer I specified was under-described: an agent who properly represented its own uncertainty about the estimates — shrinking them toward a prior, or maximizing across a set of plausible models rather than at a point estimate — would never fall into the trap, and would end up behaving very much like the satisficer in the end. Lay out that reply, say whether it defeats the case or concedes it, and, if it concedes it, say what is left of the satisficer's achievement: is the aspiration-level rule simply a crude but effective stand-in for a statistical correction the maximizer could have made, or is there a residue of the advantage that no reformulation of the maximizer's problem can absorb? Close with what you take the case to show about Simon's stronger claim — that satisficing is a normative alternative to maximizing rather than a description of what resource-limited agents do — and be explicit about which of your conclusions depend on the particular numbers and details you chose rather than on the structure itself.
