I am four years into a novel and have arrived at the scene I have been dreading, because it must carry the argument of the whole book without ever sounding like a seminar. The setting is an invented country: a small mountainous republic, Vessar, administered as a highland province of a composite empire until independence in the 1920s. Its national text is the Cycle of the Nine Winters, a body of heroic verse said to have been sung by transhumant herders in the high valleys and taken down from oral performance by missionaries; it is taught in schools, sung at funerals, quoted in parliament. Late in the novel an archivist at the national library, Emil Brann, finds a notebook in the hand of a draughtsman who worked for the imperial survey office in the early 1870s — drafts, false starts, metrical calculations, and beside one passage the marginal note "what the herdsmen would say." It establishes beyond dispute, and my characters do not dispute it, that the Cycle was composed in that survey office by a lowland man who never travelled above the foothills, working from a phrasebook of highland idioms compiled for surveyors and from some forty lines of genuine herder song that he kept almost verbatim. There is one further forensic detail I want used: the Cycle names a surveying instrument unknown in the high valleys until the imperial survey brought it, and Brann was the scholar who first flagged the anachronism, decades ago, when he still believed the text was old.

What I want from you is the scene itself. Brann meets Vera Ott, a historian who chairs the Centenary Commission and who is by temperament and conviction the opposite of a nationalist mystic, in the commission's offices after hours on the evening before the discovery is announced; the anniversary of the first printed edition falls in nine days, and the two highland provinces — where the Cycle is supposed to have originated, and which are pressing a demand for autonomy under its banner — have already begun their own commemorations. Their positions are these. Ott holds that the notebook changes nothing that ever did any work: the Cycle bound people together not because it was old but because it let strangers who would never meet conceive of themselves as participants in a single horizontal life, and its claimed antiquity was at most decorative scaffolding, so a republic that has known the truth for a fortnight can perfectly well go on being a republic. Brann holds that the notebook is fatal precisely because he understands the mechanism better than she does: the Cycle could perform that work only because it was received rather than made, taken as a transmission from ancestors rather than as a composition addressed to the present, and once a people know that the voice they have been answering all these years belongs to a clerk writing for the power that administered them, whatever made identification possible is gone and what remains is state cult. I want both of them right about the facts and arguing in good faith at full strength; I do not want a convert at the end, and I do not want the quarrel defused into a disagreement about how the word tradition is being used.

Now the constraints, since this is where I am usually cheated. Roughly nine hundred words of dialogue, no turn longer than a hundred and fifty, at least one real interruption, and a line or two of stage direction at most — I will write the room myself. Neither character may take the two easy exits: no one is to say that all traditions are invented to some degree and therefore nothing has been lost, and no one is to retreat to the claim that the poetry is beautiful regardless of who made it. At least one turn must hinge on the anachronism and at least one on the forty genuine lines still standing inside the fabrication, because I want to know whether either of them can make anything of the fact that this forgery is partly not a forgery. Nobody may name a scholar or a theory inside the dialogue; Ott and Brann argue in their own idiom, as two people who have spent their lives with one text. And if you feel moved to invoke the distinction between a tradition that is invented and a community that is merely imagined, you must show me what that distinction is doing in this particular case rather than playing it as a trump.

After the scene, step out of it and give me the analysis I cannot write from the inside: where the substantive hinge between Ott and Brann actually lies once the parts of the disagreement that are only about words have been separated off; what each of them is committed to, empirically or counterfactually, that events in my novel could test — I am considering having the centenary go ahead regardless and having the highland separatists rather than the central state be the ones who profit from the revelation, and I want to know which of my two characters that outcome would vindicate and why; what premise about what makes a community real each of them leans on without ever arguing for it; and which of the two, if either, can explain why a discovery of this kind tends to detonate in one political direction rather than another. I have read enough Hobsbawm and Anderson to recognize the standard moves, so please do not give me a survey of them, and no references or reading list — the argument, and nothing else.
