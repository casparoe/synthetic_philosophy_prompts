I've been reading around on what actually counts as corruption — clientelism, gift-giving, the whole argument about whether the international anti-corruption consensus is just a Western export — and the abstract definitions never line up with my own gut reactions, so I'm trying to work backwards from the verdicts instead. Here they are, roughly in the order I wrote them down.

- A small-town mayor appoints his brother head of public works. The brother is genuinely the most qualified person in the county and the town is 4,000 people, so anyone competent knows everyone. Not fine. But:
- The same mayor appoints a stranger who is *less* qualified, from the other side of town, purely to neutralize the opposition caucus. Fine? Actually kind of admirable statecraft? I can't make myself condemn this one and I don't know why.
- A construction contractor pays for a district officer's daughter's wedding. Obviously not fine.
- Village women give the district officer a goat at the harvest festival, every year, everyone gives something, nothing is pending. Fine.
- Same goat, same people, same officer, one week before he rules on a land dispute involving their relatives. Not fine.
- After eight months of waiting on a passport the law says you get in thirty days, you slip the clerk the "speed money." Fine — he's the corrupt one here, you're the mark.
- You pay a nurse to move your father up the list for a hip replacement, ahead of four people who've waited longer. Not fine. These last two keep bugging me because they look structurally identical — pay an official to get you something the rules would otherwise allocate differently — and my verdicts split.
- Same hip replacement, but you learn the four ahead of him all have private coverage and are getting theirs done at a private clinic next month, so the queue is basically fictional. Now I flip. I don't know which way.
- Big campaign donors getting ambassadorships. Not corruption, it's just how the place runs, and saying otherwise feels like a gotcha. But:
- A head of state appoints a donor's son-in-law as ambassador to a country where he'll be negotiating a trade deal that benefits the donor's company. That IS corruption. So the line is proximity to a decision, which feels arbitrary to me.
- A professor writes a glowing letter for a student she's barely taught because the kid's father is an old friend from grad school. Mildly not fine, but nobody would call it corrupt, and I can't say what's missing compared to the wedding case.
- A congressman steering a highway interchange into his own district. Totally fine, that's literally the job. Steering one into a colleague's district in exchange for the colleague's vote: fine, logrolling. Steering one into a colleague's district in exchange for the colleague's brother landing the paving contract: not fine. The first two and the third differ only in who pockets the return.

My working principle so far is something like: an office gets corrupted when the officeholder lets a relationship he has in his private capacity displace the standards attached to the office. It's not holding up. It can't handle the festival goat (no decision pending, so nothing is being displaced), it can't handle the passport money (the payer isn't an officeholder at all, but I do think there's corruption in that transaction, just not mine), and it seems to condemn the congressman's interchange, which I refuse to accept. Worse, every time I test it against cases from places where the state is thin — the goat, the clan hiring, the wedding — it either condemns them and I have to say the people living inside the practice are simply wrong about what they're doing, or I add a clause about what the office means in that context and the principle stops being universal, which was half the reason I wanted a principle in the first place. I also don't want to bail out into "it's all culturally relative," because that reads to me like letting real predation off the hook by describing it politely.

So: give me the principle, or the two or three real candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case neither of us has thought of — with a note on which of my cases motivated each clause. Then run it on three I haven't listed: a judge who accepts an unpaid honorary teaching post at a law school bankrolled by a firm with cases in her courtroom; an NGO that pays a village chief a "coordination stipend" straight out of the project budget; and a company that takes a regulator to a football match and logs it in its public gift register. And tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up — name one, just one, and argue it, including the hip-replacement pair, where I want to be told which way to land and why. Don't hand me a menu of options with pros and cons or a both-sides shrug. I want a committed answer, especially on the universality question: does your principle end up condemning some practices that the people inside them accept, and if it does, say why that's the correct result rather than a sign that you've just handed me my own political culture with the local color filed off.
