You are an expert in judgment aggregation and social choice theory — in particular the discursive dilemma or doctrinal paradox tradition, including its legal applications to collegial courts — who also has substantial experience designing participatory activities for advanced undergraduates. Please think carefully before answering, and treat this as a genuine design problem rather than a request for the standard illustration. The failure mode I want to avoid is an activity that quietly re-stages the familiar three-juror table from the textbooks and then supplies the moral in the debrief; my students should collide with the divergence between premise-wise and conclusion-wise aggregation in their own committee, under their own hand, before anyone names it.

Context. I teach an upper-division seminar on collective rationality: 24 students, one 90-minute session, six round tables, whiteboard, index cards, no laptops, and no budget for materials beyond paper. Roughly half the class has had an introductory logic course; none has encountered judgment aggregation. No lecture is permitted before the activity, and the terms "discursive dilemma" and "doctrinal paradox" must not appear until the reveal.

The seed case I would like you to build on — adjust the details freely, but preserve the conjunctive charter and the three separately contestable premises — is this. A fictional city's Contractor Integrity Panel must decide whether to debar a small concrete firm from municipal bidding for three years. Under the panel's charter, debarment requires an affirmative finding on all three of the following: (P) the firm's certified payroll understated hours worked on the Riverside culvert project; (Q) the understatement was made knowingly rather than arising from clerical error; (R) the understatement was material, in the specific sense that correcting it would have changed whether the firm met the contract's local-hiring threshold. If any finding fails, there is no debarment. Give the case some moral texture — a foreman who will lose work, a school project the city needs the firm for, a complainant who was passed over — so that the stakes are felt without turning the exercise into a values debate.

Requirements for the design:

1. Divergence must be generated by evidence, not by instruction. No card may tell a student what to find. Instead, each of the three panel members receives a slightly different slice of the record — a shared dossier that everyone reads, plus a private insert (one member has the raw timesheets the payroll clerk never reconciled; one has an ambiguous internal email; one has the arithmetic on the local-hiring threshold, including a disputed pair of workers) — such that sincere, defensible disagreement about P, Q, and R arises naturally. Write these inserts. Specify how much overlap there is between them and why that amount.

2. The panels are three members each, giving eight panels from my 24 students. Arrange the distribution of inserts so that six panels are likely to land in the divergent pattern, and two panels receive a "control" distribution in which the premise-wise and conclusion-wise procedures agree. The point of the control panels is that students discover the phenomenon is profile-dependent rather than an inevitable trick of the room. Explain how you make the intended profiles likely without scripting beliefs, and what to do about the real possibility that five minutes of discussion moves someone off their dossier.

3. Include a stakes-and-scorekeeping mechanism that preserves sincerity. Monetary or grade payoffs tied to a particular verdict would corrupt exactly the feature the paradox depends on, namely that members vote their actual factual beliefs. My provisional idea is a reason-coherence score: after voting, each panel must publish a short written justification, and a different panel scores whether that justification runs from the recorded findings to the verdict without invoking anything the scoring panel did not vote on. Evaluate this idea, improve on it or replace it, and say why the mechanism you choose keeps members honest about what they believe.

4. Build in the sequencing wrinkle explicitly. The chair is instructed to put the findings to a vote in the charter's fixed order, and a negative finding on an earlier premise raises the live question of whether the later premises are still voted on, recorded as unresolved, or dropped. Write the chair's instruction so that this question is forced to arise in at least some panels, and tell me what I should say when a panel asks me to rule on it.

5. Include a second round in which at least one member is quietly placed in a position where reporting a premise belief they do not hold would secure the verdict they think correct, and in which the chair is told something about the order of voting that the other members are not. I want students to discover from the inside that premise-wise voting is open to this sort of play. Do not signal this to them in the instructions they read.

6. Give me a minute-by-minute timeline for the full 90 minutes, including where the reveal happens and what exactly I say at that moment.

7. Provide the complete printable texts: the one-page charter, the shared dossier (short enough to read in under five minutes), the three private inserts, the tally sheet, and the published-justification form.

8. Write the debrief so that it maps identified moments in the activity onto the conceptual content, in this order: premise-based versus conclusion-based aggregation; the fact that the group's set of findings can be inconsistent even though every member's set is consistent; the epistemic case for voting premise by premise, given that members hold different slices of the record, and the price that case exacts; the general flavor of the impossibility-theorem result, with an explicit and honest flag that one witnessed instance is not a theorem; the consequences for holding a group responsible when no member's reasoning supports its verdict; and the parallel situation of an appellate court deciding a multi-element claim, including the possibility that its members agree on the outcome while disagreeing about which route got them there. Close the debrief by having the class vote on which procedure the charter should adopt going forward — and tell me whether that meta-vote is itself exposed to the same problem, and whether I should point that out or let them find it.

9. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, with a response I can use in the moment. At minimum, cover: a panel that reaches agreement through discussion and thereby destroys the intended profile; a student who refuses to record a finding contrary to what they privately believe; a panel that diagnoses the setup early and announces it will simply vote on the conclusion; the student who objects that the whole thing is an artifact of how the questions were worded; and the panel that invents a fourth procedure on the spot, such as pooling everyone's reasons into one composite justification or deferring to the chair.

10. Finish with a frank section on what this activity cannot reproduce and what I must therefore supply by talking. I expect at least: the persistence of a group's commitments across a series of decisions over time, which is where the inconsistency really bites; the generality of the impossibility result, which no single playthrough exhibits; the fact that real deliberation can change what individuals believe, so the dilemma may be dissolved rather than solved, and the question whether that dissolution counts as an answer; and the absence of anyone whose livelihood actually turns on the panel's verdict.

Two further constraints. Do not cite sources, list references, or attribute claims to named papers or books — I cannot verify them and neither can you here; if you want to gesture at a result, describe the result. Do not place anything in quotation marks as though quoting a published text. And keep the student-facing materials lean: the conceptual load belongs in the debrief, not in what they read at the table.

Before the design itself, give me a short section of no more than three paragraphs in which you sketch two or three candidate setups you considered, identify the one you have chosen, and justify the choice — with particular attention to the hardest part of the brief, which is seeding sincere disagreement through evidence without scripting anyone's beliefs.
