I've been assembling a little list of cases for a population ethics seminar and I've noticed my own verdicts don't hang together, so I'm outsourcing the reflective equilibrium. Welfare levels below are on a 0–100 scale where 0 is a life exactly worth neither living nor not living and 100 is the best a life can get, and I'm stipulating away every measurement worry, so please don't spend any time on whether the scale is meaningful. I'm granting that it is.

1. Ten billion people whose lives are barely worth living, level 1 throughout, versus a hundred million at level 100 throughout, with total welfare equal in the two. The second is better, and not slightly.

2. A hundred million people at level 100 versus a hundred fifty million at level 70, everyone living the same length of life in both. The second is better — a bit better. Half again as many decent lives beats fewer excellent ones when the arithmetic is that close.

3. A hundred people at level 100. Option one: one newcomer at level 90, and the cost of supporting them drops each of the hundred to level 99, so a hundred units of loss against ninety of gain. Option two: one newcomer at level minus 1, nobody else affected at all. I take option one. I'd rather make one happy person and nick a point off everybody than make one person whose life isn't worth living, costless though that is. My grad students think this is incoherent and I can't see it.

4. A thousand people who each live eighty years at level 100 versus a thousand who each live a hundred twenty years at level 70. Total welfare is five percent higher in the second. The first is better. Longer-but-flatter doesn't beat shorter-but-better, even with more of it.

5. The same thousand people, eighty years at level 100, versus the very same lives with forty extra years at level 70 tacked on the end. Second better, obviously, no hesitation.

6. Two lives with exactly the same integral of momentary welfare: one flat at level 50 for a hundred years, one that starts at 20, climbs to 80, and comes back down, with the profile adjusted so the totals match. The climbing one is better. Same integral, better life.

7. Adding a person at level 5 to a population with nothing else changing is not an improvement — neutral, or at least I can't bring myself to call it better. Adding a person at level 55 is an improvement. And not adding someone who would have been at level minus 5 is definitely an improvement.

Notice what 2 and 4 do to each other. They're the same arithmetic — half again as much quantity at thirty percent less quality, total up five percent — and I answer them in opposite directions. The only difference is whether the extra quantity comes from more people or from more time inside a life. And 5 and 6 tell me it isn't just that I'm squeamish about length, since I add length happily when nothing is traded away for it.

My tentative principle, which I suspect is a mess: population value is the sum over persons of some function of that person's life, where the function is not the integral of momentary welfare but is sensitive to trajectory and to whether the momentary level clears a threshold, with sub-threshold lives contributing nothing rather than something small. That gets me 4, 6 and 7 and its clearly straining on 2, where the added people have ordinary-length lives at 70 and ought to count for something.

So: is there a principle, or a small family of them, that delivers all seven? State it precisely enough that I can run it on the cases myself — I want the functional form, not a mood — and say which verdict each clause is there to capture and which ones it's costing you. Then tell me which of the seven you'd overturn if none of them fit; I want your best guess at which one is the illusion, and I'd rather you commit than hedge. After that, run your principle on a case I haven't given you: ten to the twelfth people who each live one ecstatic minute, versus a billion who each live eighty years at level 70. Which is better, and how do you know your principle is giving the right answer rather than the answer it was built to give?

No lit review, please — I've read my Parfit and the critical-level crowd, and I don't want references. I want the principle and its costs. And end by telling me where you're most likely to be wrong: which of my verdicts you're treating as data that shouldn't count as data, and which step in your reconstruction would collapse if I leaned on it. If you think 3 is simply confused, say so, and say why I keep finding it compelling anyway.
