For a philosophy-of-biology seminar I reconstructed Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels critique as an explicit argument, and it now seems to prove considerably more than its authors intended.

1. A trait is an adaptation only if selection is the reason that trait is there rather than absent.
2. Every organism develops within an architecture — a phylogenetic and developmental arrangement that fixes which forms are buildable — and any particular feature is realized only as part of that arrangement.
3. For any trait T, T can be described in such a way that a feature so described follows necessarily from the architecture, in the manner of the triangular surfaces that appear once a builder raises a dome on four arches.
4. Therefore, for every trait there is an explanation of its presence that invokes no selection.
5. Where such a non-selectionist explanation of a trait's presence is available and adequate, it displaces the selectionist explanation of that same trait, since the two compete for the same explanatory role.
6. So no trait is an adaptation: every trait is a spandrel, and adaptationism is not so much false as unfalsifiable, because a confirming story can always be had by redescribing the trait.

I find the conclusion unacceptable twice over. It implies that industrial melanism in the peppered moth and the echolocation apparatus of bats are not adaptations. And it is self-undermining: Gould and Lewontin treat spandrels as consequences of a primary decision to build, so if nothing is an adaptation, there is no architecture left for anything to be a byproduct of.

Which step fails? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to surrender? For each candidate, explain why the step looked innocent and state what the resulting position on biology must concede. I should like to keep both the thesis that development genuinely restricts what selection can produce and the thesis that melanism in the peppered moth is an adaptation; tell me plainly whether I can keep both. Please write your answer in American English.
