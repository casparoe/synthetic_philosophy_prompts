Just spent about two hours arguing with a friend about the 1976 swine flu thing and I want a third opinion, because I'm pretty sure we were talking past each other the whole time.

His position: the Ford mass vaccination program is the reductio of the precautionary principle. One dead soldier at Fort Dix, a strain loosely related to the 1918 flu, a CDC memo, and within weeks the president is on TV promising to vaccinate every man, woman and child in America. Something like 43 million people get the shot, the pandemic never shows up anywhere outside the base, and then the vaccine itself gets linked to Guillain-Barré and a couple dozen deaths, and the program is pulled in December. His moral: PP tells you to act on uncertain catastrophe risk, and when you act on uncertain catastrophe risk you create real harms to prevent imaginary ones. Precaution is never free.

My position: that's too fast. The story isn't "PP said vaccinate everyone." There were intermediate options on the table -- surveillance plus standby production, targeted shots for the elderly and high-risk groups, military first, waiting to see what the strain did in the Southern Hemisphere winter. And the uncertainty in play wasn't the usual PP uncertainty about some novel mechanism with unknown effects; it was ordinary uncertainty about how likely an already-identified virus was to spread beyond one army post. Plus the blame structure is lopsided: do nothing and 1918 repeats and you're finished, act and people get GBS and you're also finished. I suspect PP is getting blamed for a decision that was political from the start.

Here's what I want: take the precautionary principle seriously as a decision rule and run the episode through it properly. Spell out the components a decent formulation actually needs -- threat condition, uncertainty condition, action condition, burden-of-proof shift, whatever you think the best version requires -- then map each one onto March 1976. Who exactly is the agent (Ford? Sencer? the whole apparatus acting as one)? What was the real option set, not the binary my friend is arguing against? What is the uncertainty uncertainty *about*, and does it satisfy the condition PP needs? What counts as the precaution and what counts as the risk, and does any version of PP actually pick out universal vaccination over the intermediate options? Then tell me honestly where the theory goes silent instead of making it say something.

I specifically want to know whether the GBS blowback is fatal in the way he thinks, or whether it only shows that PP can't seperate two precautions from each other when both are responsive to a threat. And whether the case is better read as PP failing, or as nobody having applied PP at all -- just ad hoc political judgment that both sides now dress up.

Don't hedge it into mush and don't write me a history of influenza; assume I know the events. 700-1000 words. If you think I've got a fact wrong above, flag it in one line and move on, nothing in my argument depends on the exact numbers.

Last thing: does the verdict change if you evaluate the decision ex ante, on only what was knowable in March 1976, rather than with the December outcome in hand? His argument seems to need the bad ending, and PP isn't supposed to be scored on outcomes. But there's an ugly asymmetry there -- nobody can ever point at the pandemic that didn't happen because a precaution worked -- so tell me whether the ex ante framing is a genuine escape for the principle or just a dodge that makes it unfalsifiable.
