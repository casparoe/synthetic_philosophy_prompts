I serve on the five-member board of a small rural electric cooperative in the mountain West, and at our vote on the twelfth of next month we must decide a single line item: whether to commit $14.2 million — roughly four percent of annual revenue, funded by a rate increase of about nine dollars per member per month over twenty years — to hardening one substation against a geomagnetic disturbance of a severity the grid has never encountered in its operating history. The engineering is not speculative. The physics of induced currents in long transmission lines is well understood, and a severe event of this kind occurred in the middle of the nineteenth century, before there was a grid to damage. What is speculative is the rate. Our consultant puts the annual probability somewhere between one in five hundred and one in ten thousand, derived by extrapolating from a handful of historical and proxy records; two of the five board members treat those figures as though they were meter readings, and a third says the entire exercise is a way of dressing up a guess in decimals. If the event occurs, the substation's large transformer fails, the replacement lead time is twelve to eighteen months, and roughly eighty thousand people go without power for much of that period. Staff's cost-benefit analysis puts the quantified damage at $1.2 billion. Our emergency manager has separately written to the board arguing that the analysis omits the only number he thinks matters: excess deaths in the hundreds, from cold, from powered medical equipment, from the failure of water and fuel distribution. No one will put that figure in the model, and no one can say whether it belongs there.

The options before us are these. (1) Full hardening at $14.2 million, which — apart from a premium reduction of about $90,000 a year offered by our insurance carrier, a figure the proposal's cover letter leads with and which I mention only so you can see how little work it can do — yields essentially nothing in any year the extreme event fails to occur, and slightly reduces throughput while requiring specialized maintenance. (2) A partial measure at $3.5 million: a spare transformer, mobile substations, and strengthened mutual-aid agreements, which staff believe would not prevent the damage but would cut restoration from eighteen months to something under six. (3) A portfolio spending the same $14.2 million on ordinary reliability and wildfire-mitigation projects with well-supported probability estimates, which staff value at about $40 million in avoided cost over thirty years. It matters to the decision, and not only as a budgetary fact, that three other tail-risk proposals are queued behind this one — a seismic retrofit, a cyber-hardening package, a flood diversion — each with its own advocate and each insisting that its catastrophe is precisely the one that cannot be traded off against ordinary benefits. If the logic wins here, it wins four times, and the co-op cannot fund four. Our general counsel observed that the hardening proposal has the structure of Pascal's wager: an unbounded bad outcome multiplied by a probability no one can estimate, set against a certain and modest cost, so that any finite price begins to look small. He took this to be a reason for suspicion. I think he has hold of something real but has not said what it is, and I do not want the board to reject his analogy or embrace it without knowing which.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the other four members ahead of the vote. It should take a position — approve, reject, or approve one of the alternatives — and its reasons should be ones that the two members who trust the consultant's numbers and the one who distrusts all of them can each engage with, rather than arguments that only go through if you already accept one side's account of what a probability is. I want it to face two questions head-on instead of gesturing at them: whether an estimate of this kind can bear decisional weight at all, or whether collapsing a range like one-in-five-hundred-to-one-in-ten-thousand into a single multiplier is a category error that the subsequent arithmetic then hides; and whether the emergency manager's unquantified mortality claim can be excluded from the analysis without that exclusion silently deciding the case for the quantified side. Do not send me references or a reading list — work only from the figures and options above, and flag explicitly any additional number you introduce.

Structure the opinion with the bottom line first, using these sections in this order: (1) Recommendation — the option, the dollar figure, and the motion I should put on the floor, in under 150 words; (2) What the arithmetic can and cannot do — run the comparison at both ends of the stated probability range and identify which assumptions the ranking turns on, about 350 words; (3) The standing of the estimate — in what sense, if any, these are probabilities of the sort that can be multiplied against outcomes, about 300 words; (4) The unquantified harm — how the board ought to treat the mortality claim, about 300 words; (5) The strongest case against this recommendation — the best argument a thoughtful member could make for the option you rejected, stated in terms its proponent would accept as fair, about 250 words; (6) Conditions, triggers, and the queue — what conditions attach to the recommendation, what would cause the board to revisit it and by when, and how it should handle the three tail-risk proposals waiting behind this one, about 250 words.
