Last month our consciousness reading group spent two hours on the 2024 multicenter fMRI/EEG study of disorders of consciousness led by Bodien and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine. Of 241 patients who were unresponsive to commands at the bedside, 60 — about a quarter — showed repeatable brain signatures of covertly following instructions such as imagining opening and closing a hand, sustained across minutes; separately, of 112 patients who *did* follow commands behaviorally, 62 percent showed no such signature on the imaging tasks. Dana, a philosopher of mind, and Ravi, a computational neuroscientist, turned those results into an argument about Block's access/phenomenal distinction and global workspace theory, and they asked me to referee. Here is the exchange as it actually went:

(1) **Ravi:** Those 60 patients are what global workspace theory predicts and what the access/phenomenal distinction cannot accommodate. Holding a task set online for minutes, switching motor imagery on command, and stopping it on a stop instruction requires global broadcast — ignition — and on GNT ignition just is consciousness. Nowhere in this data set do access and phenomenology come apart, so the distinction is doing no explanatory work.

(2) **Dana:** Every marker the study uses is an access marker: comprehension, sustained attention, repeatable volitional modulation. Availability of information is not evidence about what it is like to be the patient, and inferring one from the other is exactly the conflation Block warned against. Worse for you, the study's own negatives show that access is not a single global property: nearly two thirds of patients who plainly follow commands behaviorally fail the imaging task, so "availability" fragments into local, task-specific capacities.

(3) **Ravi:** That fragmentation is a sensitivity artifact, not a metaphysics — patients scanned days after injury, often sedated, on noisy single-trial paradigms. Absence of signal is not absence of broadcast. But note the asymmetry: no local module can maintain a task set across minutes and toggle it on verbal instruction. The positives demand a workspace; the negatives only demand a better experiment.

(4) **Dana:** Grant the workspace, and the covert-command-following patients become the anomaly for GNT rather than its confirmation. On the theory, a globally available representation is available to *all* the consumer systems — report, decision, motor control. In these patients it is available to exactly one route and none other. Either GNT holds to its criterion and must say the content is not available to the reporting system, which is not what anyone believes about them, or the workspace is domain-specific, which drains the theory of content. The cleaner description is phenomenal consciousness with access reduced to a single channel — P without full A — the very dissociation you said the data lacked.

(5) **Ravi:** Then the distinction is idle precisely where it should matter. However you label those 60 patients, they understand language, remember instructions, and sustain attention, so they should be spoken to, given communication interfaces, presumed to feel pain, and screened before any conversation about withdrawing life support. A distinction that licenses no difference in care is a distinction without a difference.

(6) **Dana:** The care follows, but from the opposite premise. Your own underpowering argument says a negative imaging result licenses no conclusion, so experience cannot be ruled out in any of the 241 — including the 181 who tested negative. The precautionary obligation to treat all of them as possibly sentient rests on access and phenomenality coming apart; if they were identical, a negative access test would license withdrawing support.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, and tell me who is right about what and who is more right overall.
