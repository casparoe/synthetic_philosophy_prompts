You're a philosopher of language who's taught Frege on sense and reference to undergrads a bunch of times, so I want your read on something small and fiddly rather than a survey. Think it through carefully and do the brainstorming out loud before you commit to anything -- I want to see the candidate rules you generate, not just the final call.

Here's the situation. I'm in a reading group that's been working through Frege's two famous papers, the sense/reference one and the one on the thought. Our convener Nadia keeps a ledger on the whiteboard. Whenever we throw a sentence or a pair of sentences at her, she rules one of three ways: SAME (they express one and the same thought), DIFF (two distinct thoughts), or NONE (no thought at all, just a mock thought in Frege's sense of that phrase -- nothing apt for being true or false). She maintains she's doing nothing but applying Frege's own apparatus. Half the group thinks she's quietly bolting something else on and can't say what.

The ledger after nine weeks:

1. "Hesperus is visible after sunset" vs "Phosphorus is visible after sunset" -- same speaker, knows both names, has never put it together that they name one planet. DIFF. Her reason: each name gives the planet in a different way.
2. "Hesperus is visible after sunset", said by an amateur astronomer who got the name off a star chart, vs "the brightest thing in the evening sky is visible after sunset", said by her neighbour who's never heard of Hesperus. SAME. No reason offered.
3. "It rained yesterday" said on a Tuesday vs "It rained today" said on the Wednesday. SAME. Her reason: both sentences put you onto Monday, and a thought is what's true or false, not the words.
4. "It rained yesterday" said Tuesday vs the identical sentence said Wednesday. DIFF. Reason: the sense of "yesterday" only pins down a thought once you know the day of utterance.
5. One of us says "I have a headache"; another, told about it ten minutes later, says "Nadia has a headache". DIFF. Reason: that first-person thought is one nobody else can have, not even in principle.
6. "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street", read aloud as a line of the story. NONE.
7. "In the Conan Doyle stories, Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" -- its a real thought, and DIFF from anything you might say about London.
8. "The tenth planet lies beyond the orbit of Neptune", written in 1905 by an astronomer who is seriously hypothesizing. A real thought with no truth value, and explicitly *not* a mock thought. Reason: "he was trying to say something true."
9. "Nadia believes that Hesperus is visible after sunset" vs "Nadia believes that Phosphorus is visible after sunset", where Nadia doesn't know they're one planet. DIFF. Reason: inside the that-clause the names stand for their ordinary senses, so swapping them swaps what gets attributed.

Next week's case is mine to set up. A fantasy novel contains the line "Vesh is the last of the sunken kings." Seperately, an 1891 diary entry by a spiritualist medium reads "Vesh warned me the tide would turn at midnight" -- she took Vesh to be a real discarnate spirit, used the name in flat assertion, and there was and is no such spirit. And a present-day historian writes "the spirit the medium called Vesh warned her that the tide would turn at midnight."
