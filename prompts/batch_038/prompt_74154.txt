I run a four-person shop that builds annual giving plans for people who aren't rich — households in the $80k–$400k range, mostly professionals, one or two small business owners. Around forty intakes a year, each one a 45-minute conversation and then a written plan the client keeps and we both sign. The percentage in that plan is the thing they remember, and if we tell somebody 15% and they later run into an argument saying 15% is a moral failure, we've done them a disservice. Our founder, who retired last year, left behind exactly one rule that everyone treats as the whole theory: never recommend an annual amount the client couldn't realistically repeat every year for the rest of their working life without falling below a floor they've agreed to (emergency savings, insurance, retirement contributions, debt service).

I don't think that's the whole theory. I think it's a smuggled-in answer to a question we've never actually asked, which is what baseline "enough" gets measured against — what you'd owe if everyone else were doing their share, or what you owe given that they demonstrably aren't.

So: build me a decision procedure we can run at intake. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the arithmetic that turns those answers into a recommended percentage, where the procedure stops and hands the thing back to the client because its a values question rather than a fact question, and what a junior adviser with no philosophy background does when a step can't be completed — variable freelance income, no idea what they'll earn in five years, a client who won't give real numbers. Ground it in whatever the demandingness debate has actually established that's usable. I know the standard map already (the marginal-utility and drowning-child style arguments, the integrity objection, the nonideal/partial-compliance response, the line that says the demandingness worry is a bug rather than a refutation) — I don't want a tour of it, I want you to say which of those positions each step silently commits us to, pick a baseline explicitly, and defend the pick. The durability rule stays, but tell me what work it's really doing and whether it survives contact with the partial-compliance point or whether it's just the demandingness objection with a spreadsheet.

Then run it on three clients:

- 34, single, no dependents, rents, $145k salary, $40k of student debt at 5%, gets about a 4% raise a year, wants to sign a public pledge today and wants the fraction to ratchet up automatically with every raise.
- 58, surgeon, $610k, two adult children still in grad school and partly supported, spouse with a chronic illness, essentially no retirement savings because of a divorce settlement ten years back, asks whether 20% now is enough or whether they ought to be at 50%.
- 26, second-year PhD student on a $31k stipend, no debt, lives with a partner who covers the rent, wants to give 10% and also donate blood and do pro-bono statistics work for a legal aid outfit.

For at least one of them the procedure should land somewhere that annoys me — I'd rather see the output that makes the firm uncomfortable than three comfortable ones. Give each case a recommended percentage of after-tax income for year one and say whether it escalates and on what trigger.

And be honest about where the thing misfires: which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat, what it can't handle at all (I suspect it has nothing to say about whether to take the higher-paying job in the first place, or about obligations to family that aren't just general beneficence in disguise), and where two clients with identical numbers should get different answers because of something the procedure doesn't measure.

End with a last line in exactly this format, nothing after it:
Verdict: <client 1 %>, <client 2 %>, <client 3 %>
