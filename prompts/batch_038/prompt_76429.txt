My uncle Ray, 78, was diagnosed with something in the dementia family about three years ago and he's now at the stage where he doesn't reliably recognize my aunt, though he still gets visibly agitated if the radio is switched off mid-broadcast and still laughs at exactly the right moments in old Westerns. The family is splitting apart over what to do — my sister is a hospice nurse and knows far more than me about the practical side, my mom knows nothing and has strong opinions — and I keep noticing that I don't actually have a view about what Ray *is* anymore, which is embarrassing because I'm three years into a philosophy PhD and this is nominally my area. What I've been reading lately is the relational-personhood material out of African philosophy, mainly Menkiti's account as I've absorbed it, where personhood isn't something you're born with but something achieved and maintained by being woven into a community, and therefore something that can be degraded or forfeited; plus Metz's repair job on that, where the thing grounding personhood is a capacity, a dispositional/modal property, so that it survives the loss of its exercise. My worry is that the repair job saves the nice intuition by smuggling personhood back inside the individual as a hidden mechanism — i.e. the individualist picture with an African label on it — while the Menkiti version is honest but brutal about Ray.

So here's the half-baked thing I want pressure tested, I've been calling it "escrow" in my notes. Suppose that when someone's relational capacities go dormant, the personhood doesn't just subtract itself from the world; suppose it gets *held*. The network of people who are still in a relationship-shaped stance toward Ray is what carries his personhood in the meantime, so personhood is a property of a relational system with an individual as its current locus, rather than a property of the individual outright. That buys you some things for free — Ray's status no longer depends on a brain doing work it can't do anymore; my aunt setting two places at the table isn't sentimental denial, it's literally part of what constitutes him as a person — and it creates some strange new obligations and hazards: a community can be negligent about the escrow, or can quietly stop holding it without anyone noticing, or hold it for the wrong reasons, and there's no obvious procedure for checking whether it's still funded. I'm not asking you to endorse this and I'm not asking for a defense of it either.

What I want is the implications, and specifically implications for what my family should actually do over the next six months or so. The live questions are: whether to keep him at home or move him to a facility, whether talking to him as though he follows us is a form of lying, whether we should keep bringing the grandkids (5 and 8) when he mostly doesn't place them, whether to keep his room and his chair and his radio routine intact or clear the space out, and whether anyone in the family is entitled to say we're allowed to stop now. Give me exactly five of the most important implications as a numbered list, one or two sentences per item, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should say something about what changes in the decision rather than restating the escrow metaphor back at me, and at least one of the five should be genuinely bad news for the idea — a place where escrow makes the situation worse, or less decidable, than the boring individualist default where Ray either has full moral status or doesnt.
