So my roommate and I went like three hours last night over who should get to vote in city elections, and I swear by the end we weren't even arguing about the same question anymore. It started with the incinerator the council approved — most of the emissions land on a neighborhood where something like a third of the adults aren't citizens, so they eat the cost and have zero say. His move was that anyone whose interests the decision touches should have a voice, full stop. Mine was that this dissolves the whole idea of a demos: does everyone downstream of the river get a vote, does anyone who might one day buy a house in the state, do people not born yet? And then he hit me with the thing I still haven't got an answer to, which was that my alternative is just letting the people who already count decide who counts, which is precisely the question we were supposed to be settling — so the boundary can't be fixed by a vote without going in circles. That's the argument I want to turn into something.

What I'd like you to build is a sorting instrument, not an essay about it: a decision tree, ten questions at most so it fits on a handout for the reading group I'm in (mixed bag, one poli sci guy, rest of us are not). Ground rules that matter to me. Every node has to be a concrete case or a plain claim someone can answer without already knowing which branch it sends them down — nothing like 'do you accept the all-affected principle,' that just sorts people by which label they like. Build it out of the logical space first, then hang the positions on it at the end. I think the real fork points are something like: what property does the work of including you — your interests being affected, being subject to the rules' coercion, belonging to the people whose self-government it is, residing inside the territory, or some combination; whether the boundary has to be justifiable to the people it excludes or only to the people it includes; whether the demos can be constituted democratically at all or whether that's the circularity my roommate threw at me; whether affectedness comes in degrees that could be operationalized (he floated, half joking, giving everyone on earth a fractional vote weighted by how much the decision touches them — I want to know if that's a real leaf or a nonsense one); whether the unit has to be fixed in advance or can be redrawn issue by issue; and whether 'history drew the line, we just live with it' is a genuine position or an admission of defeat wearing a costume. Correct me if those are the wrong forks or if I'm missing one that actually does the sorting.

Then at the leaves: give each a name (use the standard one where there is one, coin one where there isn't), one sentence stating the constituency principle it's committed to, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the case it handles worst. Also mark the cells in the space that come out coherent from the branch structure but where nobody is standing, and say why: incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. And flag any two leaves your tree can't actually seperate, because my suspicion going in is that affectedness and subjection-to-coercion collapse on almost every realistic case and only come apart in contrived ones — if that's true I want the tree to show me exactly where they part company rather than pretend they're rivals throughout. For cases, pick the ones that bite and don't feel obliged to use all of these: the incinerator and its non-citizen neighbors; a river that carries one country's pollution into another's drinking water; a secession referendum, where the question is whether only the region's residents vote or the whole country does; a city that sells its water utility to a firm headquartered somewhere else; citizens who emigrated decades ago and are untouched by anything the country does now; a data center that drinks a whole region's electricity and groundwater; 16-year-olds; a fifty-year infrastructure bond the borrowers won't be alive to pay; an overseas military base whose host-country neighbors can't touch it with a vote; a company town where the single employer sets all the practical rules but nobody is legally coerced by anyone. Oh and don't bother trying to sort people on whether animals or AIs belong in the demos, that's a different handout, and I'm not after sources or a reading list, just the instrument and the notes on where it's weak.
