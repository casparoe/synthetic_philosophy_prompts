You're a philosopher of history with a background in the metaphysics of causation — basically the person a history department calls in when its members are fighting about whether their explanations are actually causal at all. Before you commit to a thesis, spend a little time brainstorming: sketch two or three possible lines of attack privately, then pick the strongest one and defend it properly rather than giving me a survey.

Here's the case I want you to work with. It's invented, but it's meant to be the sort of thing that genuinely shows up in the archives. In 1911 a mid-sized agrarian empire's currency collapsed — total loss of confidence, runaway inflation of the paper notes, a state that could no longer pay its soldiers. Two things had happened over the preceding three years, and they were completely independent of one another: no shared upstream cause, no coordination, no common agent. First, a run of bad harvests forced the treasury to buy grain abroad and pay for it in silver, which drained the metallic reserve that backed the notes. Second, a reformist finance minister rewrote the tax code so that provincial collectors could retain a much larger share of what they gathered, which quietly starved central revenue. Good economic modeling of the period says either change on it's own would have broken the currency within roughly eighteen months.

Now the wrinkle I actually care about. The two causes aren't symmetrical in the way I want to name. I'll call a pair *load-bearing redundancies* when neither passes the but-for test — remove either one and the collapse still happens, maybe a bit later, maybe a bit less dramatically — but each of them is doing real work in fixing the pace and the shape of the collapse, and each is doing that work on behalf of the other. The harvest is what made the crash fast and visible; the tax reform is also what made the crash fast and visible. Neither one is a mere backup that stood around doing nothing while the other did the work.

Here's what I want you to argue for. In cases of load-bearing redundancy, the historian is entitled — not just permitted as a matter of rhetorical convenience or narrative tidiness, but actually correct — to single out the human, agential cause and call it *the* cause of the outcome. So here: the minister's tax reform caused the currency collapse, full stop, and the harvest belongs in the background conditions rather then among the causes, even though the but-for test rates the two as exactly equal. I want you to argue this without conceding that historians simply have a bias toward blaming people, and without admitting that "cause" is being used in some watered-down moralized sense that isn't really causation. If you think the thesis is false, argue it as hard as you can anyway — I'd much rather get the best version of it than a lukewarm one with hedges.
