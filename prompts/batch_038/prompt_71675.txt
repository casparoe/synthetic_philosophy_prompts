My aunt Vera Lindqvist died in March at eighty-one, after fifty years as a répétiteur and accompanist at a regional opera house — a life spent entirely in the service of other people's illusions, which she considered the only sensible profession available. Somewhere around her sixty-second year, not long after her husband died, she found a translation of Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness in a secondhand bookshop and adopted it the way other people adopt a religion. She read it every January. She owned three copies, all of them annotated, and she would press it on anyone recovering from a breakup, a dismissal, or a bereavement, with the same instruction each time: don't get over it too quickly. Taped inside her purse, in her own handwriting, was a single line she had been carrying for twenty years: Don't let anyone make me sensible. The service is in ten days at the crematorium chapel: ninety or a hundred people, my cousins and their children, four decades of colleagues from the house including two singers she coached and a conductor she once threw a score at, and — this is the part I keep circling — my uncle's daughter by his first marriage, who never forgave Vera for a set of choices I will not relitigate here and who has already told me she wants something dignified and short that acknowledges what her father put up with. Barely a third of the room will have heard of du Châtelet. I am a civil engineer and not remotely an academic, and I have been arguing about this for the better part of three hours with a friend of mine who teaches philosophy and thinks the entire commission is conceptually hopeless: that du Châtelet's position defeats itself (if happiness runs on illusions, then anyone who has genuinely understood that fact has lost the capacity for it, and Vera had certainly understood it); that a eulogy is a consolatory genre by definition, so a eulogy forbidden from consoling either fails or ceases to be one; and that an instruction like don't make me sensible is a category error, since it asks mourners to sustain an illusion about a woman whose entire absence is the point of the occasion. I don't fully accept his case, but I have not managed to put my objection into words either, so I would rather you settle the question by attempting the thing.

What I want is a draft eulogy of roughly eight to nine hundred words, in a voice I could plausibly deliver — plain, occasionally dry, no oratory, nothing that sounds like a philosophy lecture. It has to be entirely comprehensible to the sixty people who have never heard of du Châtelet, which means her name should appear once at most and her ideas should arrive as Vera's own convictions, embodied in her habits and opinions: what she thought about the cello passage in the second act, why she maintained a thirty-year grudge against a conductor and refused all invitations to drop it, why she insisted that nobody in her family be permitted to eat dinner alone, why she made each of her nieces and nephews learn to dance badly. It must not say anything she would have counted as false, and on her view that rules out most of the available furniture: no watching over us, no she is at peace, no she is no longer suffering, no she would want you to be happy, no treating her death as a lesson or an occasion for improvement. It should engage honestly with the substance of the position as she held it — that happiness runs on illusions which reason ought to manage rather than dissolve, that the passions are what make a life move and are to be kept alive rather than moderated into safety, that tastes require deliberate cultivation and variety, and that a present mortgaged to a future which may never arrive is simply squandered — without ever turning into an exposition of it.

The hardest requirement is this. Something like fifteen people in that chapel are currently living through what du Châtelet took to be the great misfortune: they have been disabused, they see Vera and the marriage and the whole shape of that life plainly and without any of the agreeable colouring, and they are not going to get it back by Tuesday. The speech has to be for them too, and it cannot do it by telling them their clear sight is a mistake or a lesser condition, because she did not think the disabused were fools — only unlucky. Give the daughter one honest passage: not a capitulation, and not a silence so pointed that the room hears it. Then, after the draft and clearly separated from it, add a short note in your own voice saying where you have stretched or softened du Châtelet for the occasion, which of my friend's three objections you think actually bites, and whether the task as I have posed it can be done well at all. That last part is a real request and not a formality: if you think a eulogy that refuses every consolatory illusion while still consoling cannot be written without bad faith, say so plainly and tell me what the nearest honest substitute would be, rather than handing me something that only looks like it succeeds.
