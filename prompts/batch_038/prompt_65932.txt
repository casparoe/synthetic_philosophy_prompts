I sit in on a graduate reading group in value theory at a university near where I work — I do statistics for a living and go mostly to watch how the arguments get built — and last week's session on spectrum arguments, the torture-and-hangnail style cases associated with Rachels and Temkin, spilled over into a dispute between two of the regulars that the rest of us followed but nobody could referee. They are Nadia, who works on decision theory, and Owen, who works on the metaphysics of value. Because most of what they disagreed about turns on the details of a case they invented together in the room, I will reproduce that case as they stated it before giving the exchange.

The case: a machine with three smooth dials, controlling the intensity of a painful stimulus delivered to a subject, its duration, and the number of subjects receiving it. Every combination of settings is a possible outcome; the dials are continuous in the strict sense, so between any two settings there is another and there is no such thing as the next setting over; and for the sake of argument both of them grant that for any two settings there is a determinate fact about whether one is worse than the other, or whether neither is worse. Three settings matter. The Ordeal: maximum intensity, one hour, one subject. The Long Itch: the mildest irritation the machine can produce, roughly hangnail-level, for a billion years, one subject. The Mass Itch: that same irritation, for one hour, for an astronomically large number of subjects. Everyone present agreed that the Ordeal is worse than a single hour of hangnail for one person, and that the Long Itch is worse than that too; the fight was about the ordering among the three large cases.

Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Owen opened with three pairwise judgments, each of which he claimed is secure when the pair is considered on its own. The Long Itch is worse than the Ordeal, because badness accrues over time and a billion years of irritation contains unthinkably many hours of it; to deny this is to hold that no amount of a lesser bad ever outweighs a fixed greater one. The Ordeal is worse than the Mass Itch, because the Mass Itch contains no ordeal at all: every one of its subjects is merely briefly irritated, and a vast number of separate trifles does not assemble into a single ordeal, since none of those lives is made much worse by it. And the Mass Itch is worse than the Long Itch, because when those two are placed side by side both are nothing but irritation, and the Mass Itch makes an astronomical number of distinct lives worse while the Long Itch spoils only one. Three judgments, each defensible in its own context, running in a circle. So, he said, worse-than is not transitive, and this is not an exotic result but the ordinary shape of comparisons that move across intensity, duration, and number at once.

(2) Nadia said the circle is a product of the order in which he presents the pairs rather than a discovery about value. Shown the Ordeal beside the Long Itch, people reach for a principle about intensity; shown the Mass Itch beside the Long Itch, they reach for a principle about numbers; he has simply harvested whichever principle each framing happens to elicit. A set of intuitions that reverses systematically with presentation order is noise, not evidence, and once you force all three settings into a single comparative context — which is precisely what a machine with three dials lets you do — at least one of the judgments gives way. He has no account of which, and no method for finding out.

(3) Owen replied that this is the finding and not a defect of it. He cannot say which judgment gives way because none of them does: the pairwise verdicts are all correct and jointly unsatisfiable, and the right conclusion is that worse-than is not a single global ordering but a family of local comparisons made within contexts. Nothing in the idea of a comparison requires that it extend to a ranking of everything; the demand for a global ranking is a bookkeeping convenience that Nadia has mistaken for a constraint on the concept.

(4) Nadia said the machine defeats him rather than helping him. The settings form a continuum, the relation is determinate by stipulation, and a determinate total ordering of a continuum that varies smoothly with the settings is representable by a real number — and anything representable by a real number is transitive automatically, with no further argument needed. So on his own case intransitivity is not available. The only escape is a discontinuity, a place on the path where the ordering jumps; but that is exactly what he said in his first move he could neither locate nor believe in.

(5) Owen said the smoothness of the machine's settings licenses no conclusion about the smoothness of the ordering, and that Nadia is smuggling in a continuity assumption she has not argued for: there is no reason why badness must vary continuously with a physical dial, and there is positive reason to think it does not, since whether a life contains an ordeal at all looks like a difference in kind rather than in degree. If she wants continuity she owes an argument; if she instead allows a jump somewhere, she is in the same position he is, committed to a boundary she cannot point to.

(6) Nadia said then take the practical test. Suppose an agent's judgments have the circular shape he described and that agent is offered a sequence of trades, each of which is an improvement by that agent's own lights. The agent accepts each one, and arrives back at the outcome they started with, having paid a fee at every step. That is not a complaint about bookkeeping; it is a demonstration that the view licenses a course of action that is self-defeating from the agent's own point of view at every stage of it. A comparative relation that can be pumped in this way is not tracking goodness.

(7) Owen said the pump requires three things she has not supplied: a chooser, a sequence of trades actually on offer, and myopia. In the spectrum case nobody is choosing between the Ordeal and the Mass Itch — these are impersonal comparisons between outcomes, and it is unclear what being handed one would even amount to. And even where trades are on offer, the pump bites only against a naive agent who takes each trade as it comes; an agent who can see the circle coming refuses the first one, so a view on which the sophisticated agent is safe is not refuted by the existence of the naive one. Her argument shows that a certain choice procedure is bad, not that a certain relation is defective.

(8) Nadia said there is a cheaper exit that keeps everything worth keeping. Somewhere along the smooth path from the Ordeal down to an hour of hangnail there is a stretch in which neither setting is worse than the other and the two are not equally bad either: they are on a par, in the sense of the fourth relation Ruth Chang argues for. Parity blocks the chain without requiring a cutoff, because a chain of worse-than steps cannot pass through a stretch where that relation does not obtain. Transitivity is preserved, the determinacy stipulation is respected, and she need not say where the stretch begins or ends.

(9) Owen said the stretch has two problems. First, an interval of parity laid across a continuous dial is a cutoff with its edges rubbed off: she is still committed to there being settings on either side of a boundary such that everything on one side is worse than everything on the other, and has only declined to locate it. Second, parity is supposed to be detectable, and hers is not — the small-improvement argument that motivates parity says that if two things are on a par, then a third that improves on one of them by a hair should come out better than the other, whereas on her stretch it does not, because she also insists the improvement is too small to register. So her parity is incomparability in disguise, and it fails the very test that earned parity its place. He added that he is not claiming victory: to escape her continuum argument in move five he had to give up continuity, which means he owes a discontinuity he cannot locate, so each of them is committed to something they cannot point at. The difference is only that hers is a bounded band of unrankable pairs and his is a circle passing through the three largest cases in the room.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each numbered move I want a verdict — clearly right, partly right, or clearly wrong — together with the reasoning behind it rather than a bare label. Then, separately: (a) is the circle in (1) genuine, or is it manufactured by the setup, and if manufactured, which feature of the setup does the manufacturing; (b) does Nadia's representation argument in (4) carry the weight she puts on it, given that determinacy was granted for the sake of argument rather than on conviction, and given that Owen's three judgments concern settings far apart rather than adjacent ones; (c) do the three replies in (7) succeed individually, or does he need all of them at once to escape the pump; (d) does the parity stretch in (8) survive the objection in (9), and if it can be repaired, what does the repair cost; and (e) who is more right overall, and about which specific question — I do not think the answer is the same for every issue they touched. Finally, tell me what neither of them raised. I came away with the sense that something both were treating as fixed throughout, either one of the stipulations they agreed to at the outset or a property of the relation they each assumed without argument, is in fact the crux, and that undoing it makes the dispute look different from how it looked to them; I would like that identified and worked out. Please do not tell me that everything depends on how worse-than is defined, and please do not summarize their positions back to me.
