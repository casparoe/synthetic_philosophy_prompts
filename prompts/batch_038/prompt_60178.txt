I chair a nine-member professional licensing tribunal that hears appeals against disciplinary findings, and I have been asked to produce a written defence of the voting rule we use before the membership reappoints me in the spring. Almost every case we hear has the same shape: a finding of professional misconduct requires that three elements each be made out — that the respondent owed the complainant a duty defined by our code, that the respondent's conduct fell below the standard that duty imposes, and that the shortfall caused the harm complained of — and if any one of the three fails, the appeal succeeds. We vote separately on each element and then on the overall outcome, by secret ballot, and we publish a reasoned decision that states which elements the tribunal found. Last month the arrangement broke. Of nine members, four found duty and causation but not breach, three found breach and causation but not duty, and two found all three. Taking each element in turn, breach carried five to four and duty carried five to four, so the published reasoning would have to say both elements were made out and that misconduct followed; taking the outcome as a whole, only two of nine would have found misconduct, so the appeal plainly succeeds. Someone at the table said we had simply to choose whether the panel answers the questions or answers the case, and I have since learned that this structure has names — the doctrinal paradox in the legal literature, the discursive dilemma in social choice theory — and that it sits at the entrance to an impossibility literature descended from Arrow's theorem, complete with conditions I can recite but not really weigh.

What I want before I read another paper on the subject is the specification: what any adequate aggregation rule for a body like ours has to deliver. Give me the requirements list — the phenomena to explain, the cases to get right, the distinctions not to collapse, the formal properties to satisfy — with the motivating case or consideration behind each one. I expect it to include something like universal domain, preservation of unanimity, some consistency requirement on the collective set of findings taken as a whole, the independence-and-neutrality bundle that goes under the name of systematicity, anonymity, monotonicity or responsiveness, determinacy of outcome without a casting vote, and insensitivity to the order in which the chair puts the questions. For each, tell me whether it is a genuine requirement of a rule that must yield a publishable, internally coherent reasoned decision, or an artefact of importing Arrow's framework into a setting with a different purpose — and say plainly where my own characterisations above are loose. Then the part I most want: identify which subsets of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and say exactly what the agenda has to look like for that to bite. I understand the impossibility turns on some connectedness or non-simplicity property of the proposition set, and that dropping anonymity may not rescue you once the agenda has it. How much work is that agenda condition doing? Our docket is overwhelmingly conjunctive in the way I described, so is non-simplicity unavoidable for us, or can we design the question set to escape it the way restricted domains such as single-peakedness are supposed to escape Arrow? Which single requirement, in your judgement, eliminates the most otherwise attractive candidates?

Two further constraints of ours should be kept in view, because I suspect they change the answer. The panel's size varies: members recuse themselves mid-deliberation, sometimes leaving seven or even six voting, and a rule that behaves differently at different sizes worries me. And the chair currently controls the sequence of questions, which colleagues assure me is a form of power we should not be handing to one person. Finally, tell me briefly and in prose how the obvious candidates fare against your list — premise-based aggregation, conclusion-based aggregation, majority rule with the chair breaking ties, supermajority or quota-style rules, sequential or priority procedures, and whatever you would propose instead for a body that must publish reasons. I do not need a full scorecard, just the upshot. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and open with the single requirement you think does the most exclusionary work before you go on to justify that choice.
