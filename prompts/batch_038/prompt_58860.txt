I am a retired condensed-matter physicist, and for two years I have been reading decision theory with a small group of former colleagues: all of us at ease with series and with measure theory, none of us trained in philosophy. We are working on the Pasadena game — a fair coin tossed until the first head, paying (−1)^(n−1)·2^n/n dollars if that head falls on toss n — whose expectation series, 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, converges conditionally to ln 2 but not absolutely.

Acting as a decision theorist and philosopher of probability who teaches mathematically literate non-specialists, build us a decision tree with exactly five nodes that starts from the single shared premise "this series is conditionally convergent; what should decision theory say?" and ends at a position. Think carefully about the logical space first — which assumptions actually do the separating work — but hand me only the finished instrument, not your reasoning and not a survey of the literature.

Two requirements shape the tree. First, it must use a second gamble I constructed for us: Game B, played with the same coin, with prizes assigned to toss numbers so that its expectation series is a rearrangement of Game A's terms and diverges to +∞. One node should force a choice about whether A and B are one gamble seen under two orderings or two different gambles, and about whether the order of realization has decision-theoretic standing. In an infinite electrostatic lattice sum the value depends on the shape of the region you let grow; we do not conclude the energy is undefined, only that a boundary condition must be specified. Say where that analogy holds and where it misleads. Second, the leaves must include: the view that the expectation strictly does not exist, since E|X| = ∞; the view that bounding utility dissolves the puzzle; the view that such gambles admit only of ranking relative to a benchmark gamble; and the view that no real agent ever faces this decision problem. Name a position where the literature has a name for it and describe it where it has none; do not invent attributions, and give me no references or reading list.

Format: a numbered list of exactly five items, one per node, in the order a reader meets them. Each item states the question — framed as a concrete case about a coin, a payoff schedule, or a bookie's asking price, so it can be answered without knowing any labels — then the answer options with the node or leaf each leads to, then one sentence flagging either a pair of leaves this node cannot separate or a combination of answers nobody defends, with a word on whether the vacancy reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Nothing before item 1, nothing after item 5.
