I write a weekly-ish column for the ideas desk of a general-interest magazine — mostly tech-and-mind stuff, sometimes straight philosophy — and I'm currently wrestling with a piece about chatbots that apologize, make promises, name ships, and otherwise "hereby" do things they arguably have no standing to do. My editor is a firm believer that every feature needs one genuine laugh, ideally in the kicker, and she's rejected three straight attempts because, her words, they'd be funny if you swapped the chatbot for a parrot or a toaster. Which is fair: right now I have a piece-shaped hole and a bunch of jokes that don't depend on any of the actual material.

So I want to try the reverse. Write me a joke that only works because of speech act theory — specifically the Austin apparatus in How to Do Things with Words (the 1955 Harvard lectures, published 1962, edited from notes). By that I mean the punchline has to turn on something inside the theory rather than on the ambient folk idea that "saying it doesn't make it so." Good raw material, if you want it: the distinction between an infelicity that voids the act outright and one that leaves the act in place but hollow, i.e. misfires versus abuses; the fact that insincerity is only available as a failure mode to creatures who could have had the relevant thoughts or feelings; the requirement that the audience actually take up the act for it to land; the way Austin spends the first two lectures building a clean performative/constative split and then dismantles it by the end. A joke whose gag is that a chatbot can't be insincere because it has no sincerity slot to violate, versus one that just voids the whole procedure — that's the kind of thing I'm after, though I'd rather you found your own angle than used my half-formed one. And please don't lean entirely on "I hereby," I've seen that gag twice already.

Constraints: keep the main joke under about 120 words, self-contained, no footnotes, and it should work two ways — a reader who's never heard of Austin gets a real laugh out of the human situation, and someone who has gets a second, sharper one from the machinery underneath. Give me that one, then two alternate swings that attack different parts of the theory (I don't want three versions of the same gag), and finally a sentence or two on which of the three you'd bet your name on and which segment of my readership each one lands with. Heads up that my copy desk is brutal about terminology, so if you use the technical words, use them right — if a term is doing load-bearing work in the punchline and you're not certain it means what you think it means, cut around it.
