I chair the heritage and naming committee of a small college. Our founding charter requires that all interpretive material "present the past without presentist distortion" — a phrase nobody has ever defined, and one we must now apply to a live case. Our archivist's work on the ledgers of the founder, an eighteenth-century merchant, has established that a substantial part of his bequest derived from the Atlantic slave trade. A student petition asks that his name be removed from the main quadrangle; the alumni association asks instead for a contextual panel written by a professional historian, and objects that any panel naming slavery at all is itself presentist, since the founder's own contemporaries saw nothing remarkable in his investments and he was unexceptional by the standards of his time. Both sides insist that they are the ones taking history seriously.

Please draft the short opinion I shall circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — what happens to the name, what, if anything, goes on the wall, and by when — and it should say what the charter's anti-presentism clause is to be taken to mean in practice, with a criterion the committee could apply to the next case as well. It must engage the alumni objection on its own terms rather than dismiss it, and it must confront the reflexive difficulty that the rule forbidding us to judge the past by our standards is itself a rule we inherited from a particular moment and will look like presentism to readers later. Say too whether naming a quadrangle is an act of commemoration or a piece of interpretation, and what turns on the difference. I have read enough Butterfield and Carr to know the standard moves; I do not want a summary of them.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, the first of which states the recommendation outright, with no preamble and no closing remarks. Write in British English.
