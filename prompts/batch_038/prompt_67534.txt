So I sat in on a reading group last Thursday that isn't really my area, and afterwards two of the regulars — Ravi and Nora — got into an argument about panpsychism that went on for maybe 40 minutes in the parking lot while the rest of us stood there holding our coats. Neither of them gave an inch. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Ravi opened by saying panpsychism's whole pitch is that it solves the hard problem by getting consciousness out of consciousness instead of out of nothing — but it doesn't actually get anything, because you can't make one unified subject out of a lot of tiny ones, and a brain is a lot of tiny ones. So it hasn't solved the problem, it's relocated it: instead of asking why matter has experience you now ask why a heap of micro-experiences adds up to *mine*, and that's two mysteries instead of one.

(2) Nora said he was assuming combination means addition, and it doesn't. Nobody thinks hydrogen's properties plus oxygen's properties add up to wetness; water is grounded in its parts plus their arrangement. Same here: your experience is grounded in the micro-experiences plus some further bonding relation. Grounding isn't summing.

(3) Ravi said fine, but with water he can say *how* — the arrangement determines dispositions you can measure and check. With the bonding relation you can't say anything at all. There's no rule for which collections get a bonded subject and which ones don't. And a view that won't say what it predicts isn't a solution, it's an IOU.

(4) Nora said okay, here's a rule people actually use: integration. Whatever forms a single causal nexus that can't be broken up into parts that don't talk to each other is a candidate for being one subject. Which answers him directly — brains yes, a pile of sand no, because the sand grains are basically causally independant.

(5) Ravi had two replies. First, "can't be broken into parts that don't talk to each other" comes in degrees, so the rule hands you a sorites: wire up ten million little robots each with one dull grey pulse of experience and at some point your supposed to have a swarm that has a single experience. Which point? And what is the swarm's experience like — is it grey, is it loud, is it anything at all? Second, and he clearly thought this was the bigger problem: the rule makes the existence of the big subject a fact about *arrangement*, so the micro-experiences aren't doing any work. Keep the whole arrangement story, delete the tiny feelings, and not one prediction changes. They're decoration.

(6) Nora said that only bites if the micro-experiences have to earn their keep by predicting something extra. The physicalist still has to say what mass and charge *are* over and above what they do, and what they do is exhausted by their relations to one another. Something has to be the stuff standing in the relations. Micro-experience is her candidate for that stuff. It isn't extra causal machinery, it's what the machinery is made of.

(7) Ravi said then notice what she's conceded: if the only thing that fixes what it's like to be an electron is its causal-dispositional role in the structure, then "what it's like" is just a label for a structural role, and she's back to physicalism with fancier vocabulary.

(8) Nora said no, she's back to the point that a structure has to be structure *of* something and he's never said what. Pick one: intrinsic natures are experiential, or they're something you can't characterize at all, or there aren't any — and the third is what he's actually committed to, which she doesn't think is a coherent option.

By the end I honestly couldn't tell whether they were still arguing about the same claim. Can you go through the eight moves one at a time and say who's right at each — not just who wins overall — and then tell me what neither of them brought up that they should have?
