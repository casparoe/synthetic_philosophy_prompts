I run a six-week reading group on non-Western epistemology for a local philosophy society — about 25 people, mostly retired teachers plus a handful of undergrads, and maybe a third of them have ever read any Indian philosophy. For the last 20 minutes of the session on the pramāṇas I want to hand out a self-sorting quiz: 'which pramanaa theory do you actually hold?' Nine questions, each a concrete case with two to four options, plus a scoring key at the back that maps answer patterns onto named positions.

The thing I care about most: the questions have to be about cases, not doctrines. Nobody should be able to answer by already knowing the labels, and nobody should be blocked by not knowing them. So no item may ask anything like 'do you think postulation is its own source?' — that's just asking people to pick a team. The shape should be: here's how you ended up believing this, what did the actual work?

Leaves I need the key to reach: the perception-only view (Cārvāka-ish), the two-pramāṇa Buddhist reduction, the Nyāya four, Prabhākara's five, and the six of Kumārila and the Advaitins. I also want you to point out the combinations the answer-space permits that nobody occupies — someone who grants non-apprehension but refuses postulation, say, or someone who grants comparison but denies testimony any independent standing — and for each, one line on whether the slot is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered.

On cases: you may use two of the stock illustrations (the wild ox, Devadatta who doesn't eat by day, smoke on the mountain, the missing jar) so people recognize them, but at least five of the nine have to be new ones you invent in the same shapes — modern, mundane, and built so a reductionist and a pluralist genuinely answer differently. That's the real test of whether an item is any good.

Also flag at least two pairs of leaves the quiz can't actually separate. I suspect one will be the Buddhist two-pramāṇa view and some non-Buddhist two-pramāṇa view: same answers on every case, completely different reasons. Say what extra question would have been needed to pull them apart.

If you can smuggle in whether perception is concept-laden or bare, without turning it into a jargon test, do it as a bonus item that doesn't count toward the score. If you can't do it cleanly, leave it out and tell me why in one line.

Scoring should be pattern-based, not point totals, and most of the room will land in an unstable mix — so build in a tiebreaker (if you've got four Nyāya answers and two six-pramāṇa ones, go back to the item where you flip-flopped). And warn me which question is going to produce the 'but I picked both!' protest and how you'd word it to head that off.

You're a specialist in classical Indian epistemology who's also taught years of intro analytic epistemology, so you know how to make these cases bite for people whose intuitions are Gettier-shaped. Think hard and brainstorm maybe twenty candidate cases first, then keep the nine that discriminate best — but do that privately, I don't want to see the rejects.

Format: begin with the handout itself, question 1 on the very first line. No preamble, no 'here's your quiz', no restating what I asked for. Questions first, then the scoring key, then the design notes (empty slots, inseparable pairs, tiebreaker, protest warning) under one heading — still no meta-commentary. Define each Sanskrit term once, in a single clause, at the point it's revealed in the key. No bibliography and no further-reading list.
