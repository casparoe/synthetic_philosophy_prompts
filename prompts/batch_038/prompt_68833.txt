**My thesis.** The standard objections to cliodynamics — that history has no laws, that societies are not closed systems, that human agents are not particles — are weak, because the programme does not in fact require any of those things. The interesting difficulty lies elsewhere, in the model-to-data interface. I argue that the structural-demographic model of secular cycles faces what I call the *phase-locking problem*: for an oscillatory model fitted to a non-stationary system, period, amplitude, and phase are each independently available to absorb error, so the model can be simultaneously right about the cycle and wrong about every quantity an outside observer could check. I then argue that the programme's own methodological defences, which are otherwise excellent, systematically fail to close this gap, and I propose a discipline that would.

**Outline.**

*Section 1: The programme, stated charitably.* Structural-demographic theory, originating with Goldstone and developed by Turchin and collaborators, decomposes a complex agrarian or modern society into four interacting blocks — demographic, elite, state-fiscal, and an instability block — with feedback among them. Elite overproduction, the claim that instability rises when the supply of elite aspirants outruns the supply of elite positions, is the mechanism that does most of the distinctive work, because it is the block that moves independently of mass immiseration. "Secular cycles" are the resulting multi-generational oscillations in political order. I want to insist here, against the cheap critics, that this is a mechanistic model in the sense that matters: the equations are written down, the feedbacks are named, and the model generates trajectories rather than correlations.

*Section 2: Three objections I reject, and why.* (a) The no-laws objection: the model does not claim exceptionless regularities, only that a certain dynamical structure tends to produce oscillation; that is the same epistemic standing as a Lotka–Volterra model, and nobody thinks ecology is unscientific for lacking laws. (b) The reductionism objection: the model is perfectly happy to have agency enter through the elite block. (c) The uniqueness objection — that each society is a one-off and no two cycles are comparable: this does bite against crude cross-national regressions, but not against a model whose claim is about the sign and order of feedbacks within a single trajectory. I say all three fail. This section exists to buy credibility for Section 3.

*Section 5: A constructive proposal.* I want to argue that an oscillatory macrohistorical forecast is admissible only if at least two of the three parameters — period, amplitude, phase — are pinned by sources independent of the series the model is fitted to and independent of the instability record that is being forecast. Concretely: chronology of dynastic turnover and office-holding pins phase; census, wage, price, and land-record evidence pins amplitude; and only then should the period be free to fit the instability series. Where all three are read off the same composite, the exercise is curve description, and its later "success" is not evidence of predictive skill. I also want a *prediction ledger* norm: before a window opens, publish the indicator, the threshold, the region, the window, and, crucially, the outcome that would count as failure. My contention — which I am least sure of — is that the much-discussed 2010 forecast of growing instability in the United States and western Europe over the following decade satisfied the first four conditions and not the fifth, which is why the subsequent decade could be and was scored as a hit without anybody having to say what a miss would have looked like.

*Section 6: Scope.* Whether the phase-locking diagnosis generalizes to other long-horizon oscillatory or trend-plus-tipping-point forecasting programmes, and whether saying so makes my chapter too big. My instinct is that it does generalize, and equally that Section 6 should be cut to two paragraphs and a conjecture.

**Section 3, near-final prose.**

> The dynamical-systems defence of the secular-cycle model is stronger than its critics realize, and it is worth stating exactly how strong. Population and internal warfare are not two variables that should co-move at lag zero. If population pressure drives conflict, and conflict in turn reduces population, the two will oscillate with a common period and a phase offset; a correlation computed contemporaneously can then be near zero even when the causal story is exactly right. This is a real and non-trivial result, and it means the absence of an unlagged association is no evidence against the model at all.
>
> But the same manoeuvre, applied without constraint, is what generates the phase-locking problem. Once the lag is free, the model has acquired a degree of freedom that is not paid for out of the data. A peak in instability before the demographic peak, contemporaneous with it, or after it can each be accommodated by adjusting the offset; a peak that fails to appear can be attributed to an amplitude that was too small to cross a threshold; a peak that appears at the wrong time can be absorbed into the period. Each of these three adjustments is individually defensible, and there is no single one of them that a practitioner would concede to be ad hoc. The difficulty is that they are jointly sufficient to insulate the qualitative claim — *there is a cycle* — from any quantitative failure, and the qualitative claim is the only one the model is ever scored on.
>
> This is not the Duhem–Quine problem, and I want to be careful not to dress it up as such. Duhem–Quine says that a failed prediction can be deflected onto auxiliary hypotheses. Here the deflection targets are not auxiliaries: period, amplitude, and phase are the three intrinsic parameters of the very hypothesis under test. A model that can move all three is not being rescued by its surroundings; it is being rescued by itself. That is why I think the problem is specific to oscillatory modelling of non-stationary systems rather than a generic feature of all theory testing, and why I do not want the chapter to collapse into a lament about holism.

**Section 4, near-final prose.**

> Underneath the phase-locking problem sits a measurement problem, and I suspect that naming it is the most useful thing this chapter does. The instability block is not observed; it is constructed. The composite is assembled from counts of events — political assassinations, riots and general strikes, terrorist incidents, coups and coup attempts, guerrilla campaigns, episodes of state collapse — and these counts come from sources whose coverage density is itself a function of time. Newspaper reporting, and later digitized and machine-extracted reporting, does not sample the nineteenth century at anything like the rate at which it samples the twenty-first.
>
> It is tempting to reach for the measurement-theory distinction between reflective and formative indicators and say that the composite is being treated as reflective when it is formative. A reflective indicator set is a set of noisy manifestations of one underlying quantity, which is why its members must intercorrelate and why internal-consistency arguments apply to it; a formative index is a weighted recipe, whose members need not intercorrelate at all, which cannot be validated by coherence among the items, and whose meaning changes when items are added or dropped. I want to argue that the instability composite is formative in construction — it is a recipe for scoring event types — but is consumed as reflective, both in the reliability talk surrounding it and in the way it is used as the single dependent variable the whole four-block system is fitted to. If that is right, the composite has no claim to be measuring one thing across periods, and the phase-locking problem is worse than I described in Section 3, because the target the parameters are chasing is drifting too.
>
> I should note the obvious rejoinder in my own text rather than let a reader find it: reporting-intensity bias is not a secret, it is well known to the people who build these databases, and there are standard remedies — normalizing by source volume, restricting to event types with stable coverage, using ratios rather than counts, anchoring on series with administrative rather than journalistic provenance. My claim has to be narrower than "the data are biased." It has to be that the remedies, where applied, are applied to make the series internally usable, and are not then propagated into the forecast scoring, so that a model fitted to a stabilized subseries is publicly credited with predicting the raw one.
