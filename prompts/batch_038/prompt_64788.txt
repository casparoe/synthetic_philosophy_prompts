I sit on the historical commission of a small river town, and next April we mark the 150th anniversary of our free library. The story every longtime resident knows, and the wording on our 1974 plaque, is that the mill owner Asher Vance deeded the land and building to the workers in 1876 in gratitude after the mill fire. A graduate student working through the county deed books and one surviving subscription ledger has found that the purchase price came out of roughly ten years of wage deductions from the mill hands themselves, and that Vance's name stands on the deed only because the trustees needed a solvent signatory; a retired county archivist cautions that the same ledger could record a voluntary subscription, so the revision is not beyond doubt. Our own 1974 survey is the source of the plaque's language, which means correcting the story means correcting the commission. The Vance family has pledged $380,000 toward restoring the reading room conditional on the anniversary proceeding as planned, and several descendants of the mill hands tell me the story they were raised on is the Vance story and they do not want it taken from them at a celebration. Our enabling ordinance charges the commission with preserving and interpreting the town's heritage, and defines neither word.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the October meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — what the plaque should say, what the ceremony should do, and in what sequence over the next six months; give the strongest honest case for letting the town's memory stand even if the record is against it; and propose a standard the commission could apply the next time a living memory and the documentary record come apart.
