I'm putting together an application for a small internal grant — nine months, basically enough to write two papers and run a workshop — and I need the proposal section. My half-baked idea, for what it's worth:

The fight over whether neural nets really have representations, versus interpretability talk being a useful fiction, is usually run as a fight about what the models are like. But I think both sides have quietly assumed something that's doing most of the work: that a claim like "there's a linear direction in activation space for the Eiffel Tower" is true or false independant of the decoding method used to establish it. In practice every such claim is relative to a probe, an intervention scheme, a tokenizer, an attribution method, a choice of which layers and which prompts you look at — and nobody has really argued about whether that relativity is the benign kind (like choosing coordinates: the content is invariant, the description isn't) or the malignant kind (like a measuring instrument that manufactures what it detects). My hunch is that the realists and the deflationists are each right about different cases, and that the interesting question is whether there's a principled criterion that separates benign from malignant decoder-relativity, one you could apply in advance rather than as a post-hoc verdict on results you already like.

Turn that into a proposal. What I want out of it: the question stated so it has a determinate answer instead of just a mood; why the existing positions — realism about features, instrumentalism, the it's-all-correlations deflationism — leave it open rather than answering it; the method, month by month, including what I'd actually be doing with my hands, since I'm assuming some mix of working through the formal structure of decoders and what would make two of them equivalent, borrowing the gauge-invariance idea from physics carefully rather than as a loose metaphor, and testing the candidate criterion against a handful of published interpretability results cheap enough to reproduce on small models; what a successful result looks like (I suspect a criterion plus a demonstration that it comes out differently on two real cases); what would count as failure — e.g. if the criterion collapses into "whichever decoder predicts behavior best," in which case I've just restated instrumentalism with extra steps; and an honest line or two on whether nine months is realistic given that I teach two courses a year and have no engineering support.
