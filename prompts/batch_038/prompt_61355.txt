I am a novelist, and for the past three years I have been at work on a second novel whose engine is a philosophical claim. The book is told largely from the point of view of a condensed-matter physicist who becomes convinced of something she calls — in her own coinage, and not that of any literature I am aware of — the substrate anguish thesis. It has four parts: that experience is a fundamental feature of the physical world rather than something that switches on at a particular threshold of organizational complexity; that this fundamental experience is not merely qualitative but valenced, meaning that it is from the inside something pleasant or something unpleasant and never neutral; that the valence is asymmetrically weighted toward the unpleasant, so that the total quantity of micro-level suffering implicit in ordinary physical process dwarfs anything biology generates; and that this aggregate is morally significant, counting against the world and binding creatures such as us who can apprehend it. The novel's ending turns on whether the fourth part holds. My publisher's reader has raised the worry that the whole conceit sits so far outside the range of live philosophical opinion that the book will read as mysticism in a lab coat, and I would rather settle the question with you now, while I still have eighteen months, than discover it in the reviews.

So I want a number, and I want the reasoning behind it decomposed rather than waved at. Give me your credence in the conjunction of the first three claims, treating them as apt for truth or falsity — and tell me plainly if you think one of them is not truth-apt, or is not false so much as unintelligible, since that would itself be the most useful thing you could report. Then break the conjunction into its natural factors: the probability that some form of panpsychism is true; given that, the probability that phenomenal valence is present at the fundamental level rather than being a property that requires a subject with interests, appetites, or a unified point of view; given that, the probability of the asymmetry toward the negative; and whatever further factors you judge belong, such as the solvability of the combination problem, whether micro-experience has any determinate character at all, or whether the question is dissolved by a better account of what consciousness talk is doing. For each factor, one number and a sentence or two of justification; then show how they combine into the headline figure, and mark which of your numbers are genuine estimates and which are judgment calls wearing the costume of arithmetic. I would also like a separate conditional credence for the fourth claim: given the first three, what is the probability that anything moral follows, as against the probability that the thesis remains ethically inert because the micro-level never amounts to a someone who can be wronged?

Two further things, and then I will leave you to it. My own credence in the conjunction sits near 0.35; the philosopher I pay to read chapters puts it near 0.02 and will say little more than that the view is not serious. Tell me which factor you believe accounts for most of that gap, and whether one of us is likelier to be committing a category error than a probability error. And tell me where the estimate is most fragile: which single factor, driven to near zero by an argument I have not thought of, would take the whole thesis down with it, and what kind of consideration — purely argumentative, or in principle empirical — could ever move it. I should add that I am not looking for a bibliography, a reading list, or a survey of who holds what; I have no use for names and no way to chase references. I want the reasoning laid out in terms a well-read layperson can follow, and I would rather be told the conceit is improbable and shown exactly why than be handed a number that flatters the book.
