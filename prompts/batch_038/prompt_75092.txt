Next term I am running a forty-five-minute seminar for first-years on a joint philosophy and economics programme. They have met Arrow's theorem informally in a lecture, and each has been given a two-page summary of Sen's result to the effect that minimal liberalism, the Pareto principle, and an unrestricted domain cannot all hold together. I have a whiteboard and no handouts. What I want is not an exposition of the theorem but a plan for teaching it: the sequence, the carrying example, and the misconceptions, with a reason for each placement.

1. Set out the order in which the pieces must arrive and what depends on what. In particular: where the notion of being decisive over a pair of alternatives should be introduced; whether it should be handed over as a definition or earned through some voting procedure; and whether the idea of a social ranking — as distinct from a chosen outcome — must be in place before anything else can be said. Give rough minute allocations.

2. Test the example I had in mind, and replace it if it fails. Mine: two residents of a shared flat, each with exclusive say over one two-valued feature of their own end of it — whether the radiator in A's room is on, whether music plays in B's room — with preferences arranged so that A's exercise of her right, B's exercise of his, and a single unanimous pairwise preference between two states that differ in both features close a cycle. My worry is that the unanimous preference doing the third leg of the work will read as a conjured trick, and that the class will conclude the theorem rests on people having absurdly nosy preferences. Tell me whether that worry is fatal, state what any example carrying this idea must have and must lack, and, if you reject mine, propose another — on the condition that it is not Sen's own illustration involving the novel, which they will already have read.

3. Give me the misconception map: what they will arrive believing or form on the spot, and the precise point in the sequence at which each must be headed off. I expect at least these — that this is Arrow's theorem under new labels; that "liberal" carries its everyday political sense; that the result reports a manageable trade-off between rights and efficiency rather than an outright inconsistency; and that the fix is simply to rule out meddlesome preferences. For that last one, decide whether I should concede it, and what I must then say the concession costs. Two students have warned me they intend to argue within the first ten minutes that economists only generate a paradox because they model rights as preferences over outcomes; advise me whether to front-load that objection or let it surface where it will.

4. Close with a single check I can run in the last five minutes that would show the idea has landed rather than been recognised — preferably a fresh configuration of the same kind in which nothing goes wrong, so that the task is to identify which condition is doing the work.

Finally, tell me what I should refuse to cover at this level, and how to announce the refusal. No readings, no references, and nothing that would require the students to look anything up afterwards.
