I coordinate peer review for a mid-size program that funds clinical and translational research, and I have become preoccupied by a pair of institutional habits in our field that seem to pull in opposite directions. I would like to know what each presupposes about how the world is layered, and about what it takes to understand it.

The first habit is the demand for mechanism. Proposals and manuscripts are routinely faulted for being "merely correlative," for leaving a "black box," or for reporting an effect without saying how it is produced; a finding is treated as finally secured once someone exhibits a chain of parts and activities at a lower level — cells, molecules, pathways — carrying the effect from cause to outcome. Reviewers write things to the effect that we do not yet understand a result, because we do not know what makes it work.

The second habit is our reverence for the randomized trial. In the evidence hierarchies our guidelines invoke, systematic reviews of randomized trials sit at the top, while in several standard versions of the pyramid reasoning from mechanism or pathophysiology sits at the very bottom, on the same tier as expert opinion. The trial's authority derives precisely from its ability to license a causal claim while remaining silent about how the effect arises: allocation to comparison groups, not an account of the underlying machinery, is what is supposed to make the result trustworthy. Our own archive is full of interventions used effectively for years before anyone could say how they worked, and of interventions with elegant mechanistic stories that failed or caused harm when tested.

A recent funding round brought the tension home. One applicant offered a model that predicted patient deterioration with excellent accuracy but could not say why, and was criticized for supplying prediction without understanding. Another offered a well-powered trial of a behavioral intervention with no account of how it produced its effect, and the panel treated the absent mechanism as a caveat worth noting rather than a defect. Both proposals lacked the same thing.

So, first: reconstruct the theory of explanation, and of the relation among levels of description, that the mechanism demand presupposes. Does it commit an institution to the view that higher-level regularities — disease, behavior, recovery — are explanatorily derivative, carrying warrant only insofar as they can be cashed out in lower-level processes? Or can the demand be discharged by a purely practical reading, on which mechanism is a proxy for something else we care about — robustness across populations, resistance to confounding, confidence that an effect will travel outside the study conditions — a reading that makes no claim about which level is really doing the explaining? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several quite different pictures of levels and of explanation would license the same review form.

Second: can the two habits be held together? Is there a principled division of labor — say, between explaining why an effect occurs and justifying the claim that it does — that dissolves the conflict, or does one commitment, if pressed consistently, erode the other? In particular, if mechanism is what makes a causal claim credible, what is the status of a trial result whose mechanism is unknown; and if a trial result can be fully credible without a mechanism, what work is the mechanism demand actually doing in our reviews?

Third: suppose the institution dropped the implicit view behind that demand and took seriously the possibility that some higher-level properties are not exhaustively accounted for by their lower-level realizers. What would concretely have to change in the wording of our reviewer guidance, in how we weigh proposals against one another, and in how we rank kinds of evidence — and what would we forfeit by making those changes?
