Consider the vending machine Dennett presses into service when he makes his two-bitser case: a state in the machine that was reliably caused by quarters in the environment where it was built comes to be caused by slugs once the machine is shipped to a country where the slugs are the same weight and size, so that the identical physical state, playing the identical functional role, is either a detection of a quarter or a detection of a slug in virtue of facts that are nowhere inside the machine. Searle's standing reply to this family of examples is that observer-relative aboutness of precisely that kind is what needs explaining, that the machine's state is about quarters only for someone who reads it that way, and that human belief is about what it is about intrinsically, in virtue of the biology of a conscious brain. Dennett's standing reply is that intrinsic aboutness is either an unexplained primitive or a homunculus in disguise, and that the only difference between the vending machine and a believer is that one interpretation of the believer earns its keep by predicting vastly more.

Neither has budged in forty-odd years, and I have become suspicious that they are not having the same argument. My hypothesis is that at least three separable disputes are braided together, and each side keeps answering one while the other presses another: a dispute about whether aboutness is a property a system has independently of anyone's reading of it; a dispute about whether semantic indeterminacy of the sort the wandering two-bitser dramatizes is a local defect of artifacts or a global feature of all attribution, human included; and a dispute about what predictive and explanatory success entitles you to assert, so that Dennett's realism about the intentional stance and Searle's intrinsic intentionality may not be competing answers to a single question at all. Tell me whether that decomposition is right, wrong, or incomplete, and locate the load-bearing disagreement: which of the three, if any, would settle the others, and which are merely verbal or merely the same question asked twice.

I also want to know what Fodor's position does to the structure. He grants Searle that content is underived and original but tries to earn it with a causal-informational account rather than with consciousness or biology, and he is at least as much a target of the vending machine case as Searle is. Does the availability of that third position show that consciousness does no real work in Searle's argument, that Dennett's indeterminacy argument proves too much, or that the two have been talking past each other about a premise Fodor made explicit?

Argue the diagnosis from what each side actually needs in order to hold its view, not from a summary of the literature, and do not adjudicate who is right about intentionality; that is not what I am after. Then say what progress would look like, and specify what kind of thing it would have to be: a system whose attribution is forced rather than chosen, one on which two competent interpreters genuinely disagree about what it is about and one can be shown to be wrong on grounds other than predictive success; a definition both parties could accept; a formal result about the determinacy of content; an empirical finding about some actual artifact or organism; or a dissolution establishing that one side's question has no answer to receive. If you conclude that no such thing is available and the impasse is stable, argue for that explicitly and say what the debate should be replaced with.

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning after it, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
