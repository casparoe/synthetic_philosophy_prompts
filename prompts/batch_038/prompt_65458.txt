Right, bit of context first. I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for going on fifteen years now, and one of my longest-running arguments with a friend of mine (we both haunt the same couple of epistemology threads) is about Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and whether it genuinely takes the KK principle down with it. I can recite the shape of the thing back in my sleep: a condition is luminous if, whenever it obtains, you're in a position to know that it obtains; knowledge needs some kind of safety margin, so if you build a slow sorites-style series of cases — dawn coming up, the temperature creeping — then at every point your belief has to stay true in the neighbouring cases too, and the buffer gets eaten until somewhere in the middle you're in the condition without being in a position to know it; and since "knows that p" is itself a condition, KK looks like just luminosity applied to that particular condition, so it inherits the failure. My friend thinks there are escape routes — deny the margin premise, or go for a beefed-up Hintikka-style notion of knowledge on which knowing means further enquiry would be pointless, so the borderline cases simply aren't knowledge — and I think most of those moves cost you more than they save. We've been round this loop maybe a dozen times and neither of us has budged, and honestly the problem is that I can state the argument but I don't *feel* it. So I want to try feeling it through fiction instead.

Here's the ask: write me a short story, roughly 1,800 to 2,400 words, that dramatises this mess without ever lecturing about it. The setting I'd like is a flooded tidal/methane-drainage station off a North Sea coast, a few decades out, where the crew's legal job is "attesting" — signing rolling logs that record whether they currently know the state of some slowly moving quantity in the shaft. After an earlier disaster the regulator made everyone wear a gimbal, a cortical readout that's supposed to make your own epistemic state transparent to you: when you know something, the gimbal says so, and the station's entire safety case is built on it never speaking falsely and never staying silent when it should speak — in effect, on the idea that if you know, you'll know that you know. The catch is that the gimbal reports on a lag, and the thing being tracked (pick your own: the waterline, the gas concentration, whether the attester herself has drifted into hypoxia) moves continuously, so the log entries come out as one long borderline series. I want at least three beats in it. First, a scene where a character is squarely *in* the condition and demonstrably not in a position to know it, and where the reader can see this isn't a gadget malfunction a better engineer could fix — a perfectly working gimbal would still fail, for structural reasons. Second, the station's defender of the principle gets the strongest case you can give them, not a strawman who loses by smirking; if you can make me half-sympathise with them, all the better. Third, somebody proposes narrowing the principle to a special class of states so the counterexamples get definately-defined away, and the story has to show what the station actually loses by adopting that fix, rather than treating it as a cheat code. And the ending should turn on the logic of all this, not on a storm or a heroic sacrifice.

Constraints, and then I'll shut up. Also, don't have the story secretly resolve the philosophical dispute by fiat; it's fine to leave the reader unsure which side was right, so long as the pressure is vivid.
