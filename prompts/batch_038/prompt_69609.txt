I'm co-teaching a graduate seminar on philosophy of history this term, and one week is built around a collision I want to handle carefully rather than glibly: Popper's *The Poverty of Historicism* on one side, and Francis Fukuyama's 1989 essay "The End of History?" and the 1992 book on the other. I'd like a case analysis of the end-of-history thesis — specifically its reception and afterlife, from the summer 1989 essay through the 1990s consensus that liberal democracy was where the world was converging, to the way that consensus was read as licensing the 2003 invasion of Iraq, and then to Fukuyama's public break with neoconservatism in the mid-2000s and his later attempt to rebuild the argument on the foundations of political order — treated throughout as an episode in Popper's terms. Don't concern yourself with whether Popper ever read Fukuyama; the *Poverty* arguments were published decades before the essay, and I want the critique reconstructed from the framework itself. And no references or reading lists, please: argue from the ideas, and I'll bring the history.

The part I most want done with discipline is the mapping. Begin by deciding whether Fukuyama is a historicist as Popper defines the term — an approach to the social sciences that takes historical prediction as its principal aim — or whether the definition has to be stretched or narrowed to accommodate a theory that claims a direction for history while explicitly disclaiming dates, inevitability, and determinism. Then press each of Popper's tools against the two mechanisms Fukuyama actually offers: the logic of modern natural science as an economic and military driver of convergence, and the struggle for recognition, read through Kojève's Hegel, with liberal democracy as the regime that finally satisfies the desire for recognition universally. The relevant tools are the law/trend distinction (Popper's point that a statement asserting the existence of a trend at a certain time and place is a singular historical statement rather than a universal law), the separation of conditional scientific prediction from unconditional prophecy, the argument that the course of history depends on the future growth of knowledge and therefore cannot be known in advance, and the anti-holism objection that we cannot describe the whole of society and so cannot predict its future. Say which of the two mechanisms is the more exposed and why. Note that the first is double-edged in an interesting way: Popper's knowledge-growth argument looks less like a refutation of Fukuyama's scientific mechanism than like a restatement of it, since both make the direction of history depend on the accumulation of knowledge — and I want you to say whether you think that reversal actually works or whether it smuggles in an assumption Popper would refuse (that an acknowledged driver of change yields conditional prediction rather than a claim about where history as a whole is going).

Second, run the analysis in the other direction, because the episode tests Popper as much as it tests Fukuyama. If the end-of-history consensus did help make large-scale utopian engineering thinkable — regime change justified by a theory of where history was already headed — does that vindicate Popper's claim that historicism is politically dangerous, or does it merely show that his polemic was aimed at Marx, Comte, and Hegel and cannot get traction on a fallibilist, mechanism-based, non-determinist theory of historical direction? Where does Popper's idea of the Oedipus effect fit, the influence of a prediction on the event predicted, given that widespread belief in the thesis changed the policies that subsequently seemed to confirm or refute it? And be even-handed about Popper's own exposure: *The Open Society* carries its own directional narrative about the transition from the closed or tribal society to the open one, and situational analysis is itself a theory of social change, so tell me how much of the pressure Fukuyama is under also applies to Popper. Close with a verdict — whether the end-of-history thesis is historicist in the sense that matters for Popper's argument, and whether the 1990s and 2000s falsified it, merely complicated it, or left it untouched — and then tell me what would change your mind: which of my framings above you judge the shakiest, what consideration or piece of the record would move you to the opposite verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
