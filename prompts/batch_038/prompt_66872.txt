The assignment question was:

> Appiah holds that dueling in England, footbinding in China, and the British Atlantic slave trade each came to an end not because people were won over by moral argument but because the practices got rewired into the honor codes of the societies in question. Explain why Appiah thinks the relevant arguments were already around, and already accepted, long before the practices stopped. Then say what his account implies for Bernard Williams's thesis that moral change is not best understood as a growth in moral knowledge: does the honor mechanism support Williams, or does it quietly assume the kind of moral knowledge Williams thinks we don't have?

Here's what the student wrote:

> Appiah's point about all three cases is that the moral arguments weren't new. By the time dueling actually died out in England in the middle of the nineteenth century, preachers, judges and pamphleteers had been saying for something like two hundred years that a duel was a killing and that no code of personal standing could justify it, and almost nobody disagreed, including the men who fought. The letters of duellists are full of men saying they thought the whole business wrong and went out anyway, because refusing a challenge would have cost them their position among their peers. So the belief was in place and the practice carried on regardless. What changed was the honor code itself: honor stopped being the property of a narrow hereditary caste and got folded into a broader idea of what a gentleman and an officer was, and once that happened the duel stopped being the way you defended your standing, and refusing one stopped being the shameful thing. Appiah thinks the same shape explains footbinding, which went from being a mark of a marriageable woman to a source of national shame once reformers tied it to China's standing in the world, and the abolition of the British slave trade in 1807, which came after British national honor had been redefined around opposition to the trade rather than around its profits.
>
> This lines up with what Williams says about moral change. Williams denies that ethics is like science, where inquiry converges on a representation of the world that would look the same from any point of view, and so he denies that the end of slavery is a case of people finally finding out something they'd been ignorant of. On his view there was no moral fact the slaveholder had missed. He's a relativist about it: the slaveholder was right by the standards of his own ethical outlook and the abolitionist is right by the standards of hers, and that's why Williams thinks we can't criticize past societies at all.
>
> Appiah gives Williams the mechanism for how that works in practice. Honor is basically a delivery system for moral truth that runs on shame instead of reasoning: once a practice becomes shameful rather than honorable, people finally see that it was wrong all along. On this picture the reformers' arguments were causally irrelevant, since they'd been made for centuries with no effect and what did the work was social pressure. So Appiah does support Williams — the revolutions happened because the social environment changed, not because anybody came to know anything, and if arguments couldn't move people before then they can hardly be what moved them afterwards.
