You are a metaphysician who also does serious, non-dabbler work on literature and philosophy, and you are being asked to produce publishable-grade criticism rather than a study-guide summary. Please think carefully and treat both sides of the assignment as having their own integrity.

I am preparing a week of a graduate seminar on material constitution. The week's reading is Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde (1886), and I want to use it as the literary case study against which the seminar tests the standard apparatus. The seminar will already have covered the canonical statue-and-clay material: two things in the same place at the same time, the indiscernibility of identicals, the argument from differing modal properties, and the familiar exits — constitution without identity, sortal-relative or relative identity, the four-dimensionalist reply on which the coinciding things share temporal parts, eliminativism about artifacts, and mereological essentialism. I would like your piece to bring that toolkit to the novella and then to say honestly how much of it the novella can carry.

Some specific hooks I want you to work through, described rather than quoted; please do not reproduce Stevenson's prose, and if you are unsure of a textual detail, say so instead of filling the gap.

1. The house. Jekyll's residence presents a handsome, respectable front on the square while a second, windowless, neglected door gives onto a by-street, and the two are joined inside. Enfield's story begins at that second door, and Hyde uses it. Consider this as a spatial figure for the coincidence problem: one building, two faces, two addresses, two social persons. Does the figure do any real work, or is it merely atmospheric?

2. The transformation is also a transformation of matter. Hyde is repeatedly marked as smaller and slighter than Jekyll, and in the final chapter Jekyll offers his own explanation, roughly that the darker side of his nature had been less exercised and so was less developed. Hyde also gives observers an impression of deformity without any identifiable malformation. Ask what this does to any account on which the same lump constitutes whichever person is present: is matter gained and lost, and if so does the novella owe us an explanation it never supplies? What does the changed body do to the dominant-kind or sortal answers, and to the animalist thought that one organism is there throughout, with persons as phases of it?

3. The will. Utterson's alarm begins with the terms of Jekyll's will, under which Hyde inherits everything and, if Jekyll disappears or is absent without explanation for more than three months, takes over the estate immediately and without further formality. This is, in effect, a legal instrument that encodes a criterion of persistence: it specifies conditions under which Jekyll is to be counted as gone while something else continues. Read the will as the novella's own theory of survival conditions. Does it treat Hyde as a successor, as a co-tenant, or as what remains when Jekyll has ceased? Is the legal person here tracking a biological object, a psychological continuer, or neither?

4. The cabinet scene at the end, where the door is broken in and a body is found dead, dressed in Jekyll's clothes, which hang far too large for it, while Jekyll is nowhere present. Consider what, on each of the metaphysical options, has ceased to exist in that room, and what the image of a body that no longer fits its own clothes contributes to the question of what constitutes a person at a time.

5. Jekyll's own reflective claim in the final chapter, to the effect that man is not truly one but truly two — and his further reflection that this plurality may in truth be far greater than two. Note that this sounds compositional, as though a person were a sum of parts, but that everything else Jekyll says about the two sides concerns motives, desires, conscience, and reputation rather than matter. Decide whether Stevenson is making a claim about material constitution, a claim about divided agency, or is sliding between the two, and whether the novella knows the difference. If it does not know the difference, is that a defect the critic should name, or a resource?

Then the harder, more general question, which I want you to face rather than sidestep: the statue-and-clay case is synchronic. Jekyll and Hyde, on the most natural reading of the plot, are never in the room at the same time; they alternate. So it may be that the novella is not a coincidence case at all but a succession case, and belongs to personal identity rather than material constitution. Tell me whether the synchronic frame can be repaired — for instance by treating Jekyll's body, his household, his name and his estate as the persisting thing and Jekyll-and-Hyde as the alternating persons it constitutes — or whether the repair distorts either the metaphysics or the story. An honest critic must be willing to conclude that the reading fails, and to say at what point it fails.

I also want you to guard against the two standard ways this kind of assignment goes wrong: reducing the novella to an illustration of a thesis it happens to resemble, and bending the thesis until it fits. The book is also about Victorian respectability, blackmail, reputation, medical professional boundaries, and the machinery of law and servants that lets a double life run; if the metaphysical frame makes that subject matter disappear, say so and tell me what the frame costs.

Finally, close by saying what the novella contributes to the debate on material constitution that argument alone does not — whether, for example, it exhibits the felt difference between asking which object is present and asking which person is answerable, or whether it makes vivid something about the horror of a dominant-kind question asked of a living body — and be prepared to answer that nothing survives, if that is your view.

Process and format. Begin with a short, clearly marked brainstorming section in which you sketch the competing readings and their costs before committing to one; keep it to a few hundred words. Then write the criticism under the headings House, Matter, Will, Cabinet, Composition, Objection, and Contribution. Aim for roughly two thousand words of prose in total, argued rather than listed, with the textual description woven in rather than recapped scene by scene. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, page numbers, or publication details, and do not attribute any specific view to a named contemporary philosopher unless you are confident of it; work from your own understanding of the positions.
