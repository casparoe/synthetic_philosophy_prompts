Six weeks ago I took up a seat on the ethics committee of a shipping and trading house founded by my great-grandfather in the 1840s, in which my family retains a controlling interest. While preparing for our first meeting I worked through the firm's nineteenth-century ledgers, and they record, in the clerks' matter-of-fact hand, that the company's vessels carried indentured labourers at mortality rates the captains logged season by season, and that a considerable portion of the capital from which the present business grew derives from that trade.

A senior colleague on the committee has drafted what he calls an attribution schedule. It assigns each current director a stated percentage of the firm's inherited guilt, weighted by length of service and by shareholding, and it names a monetary sum at which each percentage is to be regarded as discharged. He supports the device by appeal to Jaspers's fourfold distinction among criminal, political, moral and metaphysical guilt, arguing that since the political category attaches to a community rather than to the individuals who happen to compose it at a given moment, the firm's guilt survives the turnover of its personnel and may be apportioned among them in proportion to their standing.

Accepting that political guilt of this kind passes to those who join a community after the deeds were done, how ought we to set the weights: should tenure and shareholding count equally, or is one of them the more defensible basis for apportionment? And is a lump sum discharging a fixed percentage the right instrument for the firm's contribution, or would a graduated schedule, under which longer-serving directors carry a larger share, be more faithful to the reasoning Jaspers himself gives?
