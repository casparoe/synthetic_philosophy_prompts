Our philosophy-of-AI reading group is devoting its final meeting to the last section of Turing's 1950 paper — not the imitation game, but the proposal to program a child's mind rather than an adult's and let an education do what the original construction cannot, together with his answer to the objection from the informality of behaviour: the claim that, because human conduct cannot be reduced to rules, no rule-governed machine could reproduce it, which he meets by pointing out that we cannot keep track of a machine's states either, and cannot foresee all the consequences of a slight change in its instructions. I would like to open the discussion with a poem in a strict form that carries some of that argument rather than announcing it.

Please write a pantoum of five quatrains in the voice of the teacher-programmer addressing the child machine.

Formal requirements:

- Observe the recurrence exactly: lines 2 and 4 of each quatrain return word for word as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the fifth quatrain closes the circle in the traditional manner by taking lines 1 and 3 of the first quatrain as its own lines 2 and 4.
- Rhyme ABAB within each quatrain, slant rhyme permitted, in lines of roughly ten syllables. Because the inherited lines always land in the first and third slots, they keep rhyming with one another automatically while each pair of fresh lines must find a new rhyme of its own; every stanza is thus half settled and half unsettled, and I would like that to be audible.
- Exploit the positional shift as well. A line that answered at the end of one stanza opens the next, so let the returning lines change grammatical and rhetorical function as they come back — instruction turning into report, reward turning into residue, question turning into verdict — rather than simply repeating.

Content. The poem should get four things right without explaining any of them:

1. The object being built is a child, not an adult: small, cheap, largely unformed, with the expectation that the education will supply what the original programming did not.
2. Rewards and punishments enter as signals rather than as reasons. The pupil does not grasp why it is being marked down; the signal merely alters what it does next time. That asymmetry is the part of the picture most readers find uncomfortable, and the poem should let it sting a little instead of smoothing it over.
3. There is a random element in the search for a workable structure — noise, trial, variation nobody chose — and the teacher's contribution is best understood as making that search cheaper than blind variation alone would make it, not as installing content from outside.
4. The teacher cannot keep track. Even in principle the educator cannot derive the pupil's behaviour from what he put in, and a small change may have consequences he cannot foresee; the education counts as a success only if it outruns the educator. I want the closing circle to enact exactly this: the opening lines return at the end, but they now arrive from the pupil's side of the exchange and no longer mean what the teacher gave them to mean.

Diction: do not use the words Turing, test, artificial intelligence, algorithm, robot, consciousness, or singularity, and do not quote from the paper. The result should be readable aloud in about a minute by someone who has never read the paper, and identifiable in its particulars by someone who has.

Please give the poem first, followed by a single section titled Note on the construction, of roughly 200 words, in which you identify stanza by stanza which lines are inherited and where each signal of reward or punishment enters, and say how you intended the final reversal to land.
