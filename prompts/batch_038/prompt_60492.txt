I am putting together a graduate seminar session on the value problem in epistemology — why knowledge should be worth more than mere true belief — and I want to build it around a historical case rather than around intuitions about Gettier-style scenarios. The case I have chosen is the reception of Semmelweis and the subsequent uptake of Listerism. I know the outline; what I need is a rigorous analysis of it as a test of the Meno's account, run in both directions.

Let me state the facts I am relying on, so that the analysis does not have to hedge about them. At the Vienna General Hospital in the 1840s there were two maternity clinics: the First, staffed by physicians and medical students who also worked in the post-mortem room, and the Second, staffed by midwives who did not. Puerperal fever mortality in the First ran roughly three to four times that of the Second — in 1846, 459 deaths among 4,010 patients against 105 among 3,754. In March 1847 Semmelweis's colleague Jakob Kolletschka died after a wound sustained during an autopsy, and the lesions at his own post-mortem resembled those of the women dying in the clinic. In late May 1847 Semmelweis required hand disinfection in a chlorine preparation before ward entry; after further clusters in the autumn he extended it to every examination and recognized sources other than corpses. In 1848, the first full year under the rule, the First Clinic recorded 45 deaths among 3,556 patients, about 1.3 percent. His proposed mechanism was the transfer of decomposing cadaverous matter, not microorganisms. His appointment was not renewed; he left Vienna in 1850, worked in Budapest, published a full treatise in 1861, and died in 1865 after being committed to an asylum. Meanwhile Oliver Wendell Holmes had argued in 1843, from published case clusters, that practitioners carried puerperal fever between patients, and Alexander Gordon had connected an Aberdeen outbreak to particular attendants in 1795; neither changed practice. Lister, working in Glasgow from the mid-1860s and publishing in The Lancet in 1867, used carbolic acid on the explicit basis of Pasteur's work on fermentation and putrefaction, and antiseptic practice spread. The specific pathogen of puerperal sepsis was not identified until Pasteur found haemolytic streptococci in 1879, twelve years after Lister's publication.

With those facts fixed, I want the following. First, the mapping, done carefully: whose belief-state is at issue in each of the three cases (Holmes, Semmelweis, Lister), what exactly its content is, what would count as the tether that the Meno describes when it says true opinions must be tied down by working out the reason why, and in which dimension stability is being measured — persistence in the individual believer, survival of the practice across a change of personnel, resistance to institutional attack, or extendability to new cases the original evidence did not cover. Please say explicitly which dimension the episode actually turns on, since I suspect the Meno's image conflates them.

Second, the two-way test. Which features of the episode does the tethering account explain, and which does it misdescribe? I am particularly interested in the awkwardness that Semmelweis looks like a case of knowledge rather than of true belief: he had a standing comparison between matched clinics, a pathological analogy, a deliberate intervention, and reproducible outcome data. If so, the binary the Meno offers cannot place him, and the episode yields a gradient rather than a pair. Say whether that is a real anomaly for the value problem or a sign that I am using it too literally.

Third, I have a proposal of my own that I want pushed on rather than flattered. My thought is that what tethering actually tracks is not psychological stability in the agent but public auditability: the capacity of a third party to reconstruct why the belief is true, and hence to defend it against objection, extend it to new circumstances, and know when it ceases to apply. Semmelweis's results were in principle reproducible by anyone, yet no one outside his own clinics could reconstruct his reasons in a form that survived contact with existing theory. Tell me whether this refinement is already contained in Plato's account, whether it answers a different question than the Meno's — the value of a belief-state to a community rather than to its possessor — or whether it is simply wrong.

Fourth, the anomaly I cannot resolve. Lister's tether was a mechanism that was, in detail, false at the time: no organism had been identified as the cause of the fever his practice prevented. If a partially false account can perform the tethering function, then tethering is not a matter of grasping the truth about why one's belief is true, and the value it confers looks like inferential fertility rather than accuracy. Work out what that does to the Meno's argument, and to the related worry that the value of knowledge is swamped by the value of the true belief it entails. If the episode relocates the extra value from success-in-action to durability-and-transfer, does that rescue the value problem or quietly change the subject?

Finally, be disciplined about silence: where the Meno's framework simply has nothing to say about the episode — about professional offense, about the politics of the Viennese school, about the difference between a ward rule and a published mechanism — say so rather than stretching the theory to cover it. Correct any of my facts that are wrong. I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of the secondary literature; I want the case analysis.
