I write about science and ideas for a monthly magazine, and I'm deep in reporting a feature on the fertility collapse in several middle-income countries and on the commissions that have been convened to reverse it — tax credits, housing reform, the whole pronatalist toolkit. Half my sources are demographers and economists who treat a shrinking population as an engineering problem with an obvious direction of fix; the other half are philosophers who think the question "is more better?" is far less tractable than the commissions assume, because of the argument that walks you from a modest population of very good lives, one step at a time, to an enormous population of lives that are still worth living but contain almost nothing worth having. I want an original poem to run at the head of the piece. My editor's condition is that it work as a poem first: no terms of art, no thesis sentences, nothing that reads like a digest of an encyclopedia entry. Please approach this as a poet who has genuinely absorbed that argument rather than skimmed it, and think the structure through before you draft — work out the schedule in advance, because it is very easy to break it halfway down the page. If you find a constraint impossible at some stanza, revise the schedule at the outset rather than quietly violating it later.

Here is the form, which I'd like you to treat as binding. Five or six stanzas, roughly twenty-eight to forty-two lines in total. Each stanza has exactly one more line than the one before it — the population grows. Every line in a stanza is shorter, in syllables, than any line in the stanza before it — each individual life gets smaller. And yet each stanza contains more syllables in total than the one before it — the aggregate still rises, which is precisely the intuition the whole argument exploits. Count syllables, not stresses; approximate counts are fine so long as both inequalities hold stanza to stanza. Give the piece a title, and let the title be the only place the world of policy, commissions, and birth rates appears — inside the poem I want images, not institutions, and the argument should be carried entirely by the shape rather than stated. The final line should be a single word, and it should be capable of reading either as an entreaty or as a verdict; I'll leave the choice to you. Avoid the stock image of a life reduced to background music and starch, which is the first thing anyone reaches for here. No rhyme scheme is required, but I'd like some sonic recurrence — assonance, a returned consonant cluster, a phrase that comes back altered — so that the sequence feels walked rather than listed. You may letter the stanzas and let a run of ellipses stand for the worlds you skip, though that's optional and I'd rather you didn't lean on it as a gimmick.

What a philosopher should recognize without being told: the opening world of few and very good lives; the step that adds people whose lives are worth living without touching anyone already there, and the seductive sense that such a step cannot make things worse; the long undifferentiated run of further steps, each barely distinguishable from its predecessor; the terminal world of vast numbers and thin lives; and the fact that arriving there forces you to break something — a step, a comparison, or the transitivity that carries you down the chain. The poem should not say which of these has to go, and it should not console the reader or indict him; it should make the impossibility of choosing feel physically present in the reading. Please give me the poem first, under its title, and then a single section titled **Notes on the Form**, running about three hundred to four hundred words, in which you include: a short stanza-by-stanza audit of lines per stanza and syllables per line so I can check the inequalities held; an account of which specific structural choice corresponds to which step in the argument; the two or three alternative forms you considered and why you rejected them; and, frankly, any place where the poem compresses or slightly bends the argument for the sake of the verse — I need to know where the art costs the philosophy, since the piece will be fact-checked.
