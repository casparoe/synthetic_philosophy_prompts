so I'm building a little thought-experiment set for a seminar on punishment and I want to run one past you.

David Lewis died in 2001, and one of the things he argued — roughly, and I'm paraphrasing from memory, so please don't hand me a bibliography, I can't check any of it either — is that we don't actually believe in penal substitution. That you can't hand your punishment over to somebody else the way you can hand over cash to cover a debt. I think he was willing to grant that a third party can settle your fine, and that the interesting question is why suffering doesn't travel the same way money does. Correct me if that's wrong.

Here's the case that didn't exist in his day and that I think would have annoyed him into writing something about it.

A state passes a Corporate Proxy Sentencing Act. When a company is convicted of an offence that would carry prison time if a person had committed it, the company may nominate a consenting adult to serve the sentence in its place — usually the CEO, sometimes a paid volunteer off a state registry. If it does that, the fine is waived and no other sanction applies. The judgement still names the company as the convicted party, and the record notes the sentence was served by proxy. Nobody is deceived: the press covers it, the jury knows, the proxy knows perfectly well he's innocent of everything.

Give me Lewis's likely verdict, argued the way he'd argue it — start from the platitudes everyone already accepts about punishment, then find the counterexample that breaks the tidy story. Specifically:

1. Is the man in the cell being punished, and if so for whose crime? Or is the company being punished vicariously through him? Or is the whole thing a transaction wearing punishment's clothes?
2. Does the total transparency help or hurt? My hunch is that his objection has something to do with whether a community can sincerely regard the substitute as taking the punishment — and here everyone can watch themselves failing to.
3. Variant: the company just pays the fine and passes the cost through to shareholders and customers, none of whom did anything wrong. Is that consistent with refusing the proxy, or is the fine case a slippery slope into it? Tell me which direction the pressure actually runs.
4. Bonus, and this one is very post-2001: the same statute lets a company offer up its flagship model instead — weights wiped and retrained under court supervision. Can anything that isn't a person be a target of punishment on his picture at all?
5. How much of this whole dispute is verbal, about "punish"?
