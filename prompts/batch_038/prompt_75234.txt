I would like a short story — 2,400 to 3,000 words — that inhabits the problem of constitutional precommitment, and specifically its hardest version. Not the familiar one, in which a man lashes himself to something and has his companions stop their ears: I want the case in which a polity binds its successors not by constraining their options but by controlling what they are able to know, and in which the person who took the oath is not, in any legally or practically recognizable sense, the person who would have to break it. The intellectual territory is entrenchment and the dead-hand objection (Jefferson's 1789 claim that the earth belongs in usufruct to the living is the polestar here), the difference between removing an option and removing the desire or the information that would make anyone want it, and the reductionist view of persons, on which the swearer and the actor are only loosely connected.

The following apparatus is stipulated. Use it as given; you may add texture but not substitute your own machinery.

- The polity is the League of the Three Reaches, a river federation of perhaps ninety thousand people. One hundred and eighteen years ago it ratified the Second Instrument, which built the reservoir called the Standing Water: the drowning of a fertile upper valley in exchange for the permanent flood-safety of the lower reaches.
- The Standing Clause forbids any alteration of the sluice regime for one hundred and twenty years from ratification. Two years remain.
- A blight has taken the lower harvests. There is grain for forty days. The drowned valley, if drained and planted in spring, could feed the League for a generation. Draining it removes the flood buffer through one melt season.
- The founders' engineering survey of the drowned valley — including a revised set of figures completed after ratification, which revises the odds of a great flood in the unprotected year upward rather than downward — was sealed rather than published, and all ordinary records of the valley were ordered destroyed. The Clause is entrenched by ignorance: citizens cannot want what they cannot conceive of.
- Only three officers, the Keepers of the Seal, may read the sealed sheets. They live in the Seal House and do not leave it. Appointment has two rites: the Striking, in which the Keeper is declared civilly dead — name struck from the roll, property divided among heirs, mourning performed by the family — and the Apprising, in which the sheets are read. The Instrument says a Keeper is not made until both are done.
- Hence the Clause of the Unmade: the Seal may be opened only on the joint petition of the three Keepers, and a petition is valid only if filed by one not yet apprised. Since the Apprising normally follows the Striking within days, the window is shut forever in practice. It was left in as a form of words.
- Eight days ago the second Keeper died mid-term. His replacement has been Struck — her sister performed the mourning — but the Apprising has not been performed, because the confessor died too. She is nineteen. She is legally dead, her share of her father's mill has already been sold, and she alone in the League has standing to petition for release from a bond whose content she does not know.

The senior Keeper, fourteen years in office, narrates the story in the first person and addresses it to this girl across one night in the Seal House. She is telling her everything, because telling her is the only way to get a valid petition filed — and telling her is also what destroys her standing to file it. The narration must therefore turn out to be the decisive act of the plot, and the decisive figure in the sealed sheets must be withheld until the final third.

Requirements:

1. The third Keeper — the dying surveyor's colleague who has been in the office twenty-six years — must argue for holding the bond, and must be given the strongest case available: that a people unable to bind themselves can undertake nothing longer than a mood; that the famine was foreseeable and the League chose the works anyway with the price known; that releasing the bond now teaches every later generation that the bond was a threat rather than a promise; and that the alternative to keeping it is not that the living decide but that the loudest decide. He must not be a zealot, a coward, a bureaucrat protecting his office, or a villain. A reader should be able to side with him and say why.
2. The narrator's case must be equally argued and must not rest on the suffering of the starving. It must engage standing: whose oath is this, given that the woman who swore has been struck from the roll, her property sold, and her family already consoled for her death.
3. The hinge must be the Clause of the Unmade. The story must make the reader feel simultaneously that a release-clause operable only by someone who does not know what she is releasing is the only design that could ever work, and that it is intolerable.
4. The reductionist material about persons is to be carried entirely by the rites — the Striking, the divided mill, the mourning, the girl's own changed status overnight — and by the characters' ways of speaking about who they used to be. Do not use the vocabulary of the literature: no occurrence of precommitment, entrenchment, social contract, autonomy, paternalism, personal identity, psychological continuity, or future self as a term of art.
5. No nautical or Homeric imagery of any kind: no ships, masts, ropes, sirens, wax, or islands; no character named after a figure in the Odyssey; no retelling or transposing of that episode. The concept must be present with the vocabulary entirely the polity's own.
6. The blight must be real, the numbers must be what they appear to be, and there may be no revelation that any of it is a dream, a simulation, a test, or a hoax. The cost of each course must remain exactly as grave as it looks.
7. The ending must be a thing the narrator does before dawn, rendered as scene rather than summary, and interpretable in two incompatible ways. She must not tell the reader how to take it, and the story must not close on reflection, resolve, or an image of the valley.

After the story, and outside it, add a note of no more than 150 words: name the single argument the story is built to test, say which side the structure of the story itself favors and by what device, and identify the point at which you think the story is weakest as argument. No citations, no bibliography.
