I'm auditing a philosophy of biology seminar this term and we're on adaptationism. Our prof handed out a couple of pages from some trade book by a science writer who's clearly been in a lot of fights with the "Darwinian fundamentalists," and this is the passage he wants us to react to:

> "Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper destroyed the adaptationist program. They pointed out that if you put a dome on four arches you get four triangular spaces at the corners, and the builders of San Marco in Venice filled those spaces with gorgeous mosaics of the four evangelists — so a tourist, seeing how perfectly the pictures fit the space, concludes the space was designed for them. It wasn't; it was an unavoidable leftover of the dome. Gould and Lewontin showed that this is what supposedly adaptive features usually are: leftovers with no function of their own. The male nipple, the human chin, the redness of blood — none of these were selected for anything, and any biologist who says otherwise is telling just-so stories. Their conclusion was that natural selection explains far less than its champions admit and that most of the machinery of an organism is architectural accident. Gould later had to admit that the San Marco surfaces aren't spandrels at all in the architect's sense — they're pendentives — which rather shows the analogy was doing all the work, and that when it goes, so does the argument."

My own instinct is that the last sentence is at least half right, because I do remember reading somewhere that Gould conceded the pendentive point, and that concession seems to me like the kind of thing you can't walk back. A classmate of mine who's a serious adaptationist says the entire paragraph is a strawman and that nobody who's actually read the paper talks that way, but she wouldn't say which bits bother her. So can you go through it claim by claim and tell me what a working evolutionary biologist or a philosopher of biology would sign off on, what's a fair-but-loose popular simplification, and what's just wrong? The things I specifically want settled: does the paper actually argue that selection explains *less*, i.e. that it's making a quantitative claim about how much of the phenotype is adaptive, or is the complaint methodological — about how you're allowed to argue — and if so, what exactly is the fallacy it names? Does it anywhere license treating a trait as a spandrel just because nobody has demonstrated an optimality result for it? Where does "spandrel" stop and "exaptation" start, and does the paragraph keep those apart or run them together — and are those three examples really the paper's examples, or are they from Gould's later popular essays, and does that matter for the audit? Finally, on the architecture: is the concession real, and does it actually undermine the concept the way the book says, or can the concept survive the example? If there's a *stronger* version of the objection to the San Marco illustration than the terminological one the book trots out, I'd like to hear it, because I suspect there is and I'd rather hand in the good objection than the easy one.
