A team is preparing to deploy a learned controller that will dispatch generation and shed load across a high-voltage transmission network. Two of the team's researchers disagree about what sort of guarantee the system must carry before it is switched on, and the disagreement is now holding up the specification document.

Dr Adaeze Okonjo holds that only a worst-case guarantee is worth having: a bound that holds for every input sequence and every operating condition the controller might encounter, resting on no assumption about how frequently particular situations arise. She does not claim such bounds are easy to obtain — she concedes that over an unrestricted class of environments they are either vacuous or simply false — but she holds that the network's harm function is severely non-linear, that one cascading failure outweighs a million well-handled dispatches, and that taking an expectation over a distribution is precisely the operation that hides the single event which matters.

Dr Emil Vasquez holds that an average-case guarantee under a well-characterised deployment distribution is both the only attainable target and the only relevant one. He does not claim that tails are unimportant — he agrees that they are — but he holds that a worst case is always a worst case over some specified set, that the choice of that set is a modelling judgement with no better epistemic standing than an estimate of a distribution, and that a demand for distribution-free certification will yield a controller so conservative that it declines to act under ordinary conditions. The harm caused by that refusal, on his view, is real, measurable, and lands on the average rather than on the tail.

Write a dialogue between them, set during a working session in which the two are trying to draft the certification clause itself. Observe the following:

1. Both are equally competent. Neither may be a strawman, and each must be allowed at least one argument that the other cannot dissolve within the exchange.

2. The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth: every turn should answer the turn immediately before it rather than restating a position, and each character should make explicit concessions where concessions are due.

3. It should run to at least seven exchanges per speaker and roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words.

4. Among other things, the exchange must engage with (a) whether distributionally robust optimisation dissolves the dichotomy altogether, given that the supremum of a loss over a moment-constrained ambiguity set can often be rewritten as a penalty on the mean and the dispersion of that loss, which would reduce the dispute to a quarrel about the choice of a radius; (b) whether such a radius can be estimated at all, given that the controller selects which parts of the network to bring under its own supervision and shapes the load profile against which it will later be evaluated, so that the deployment distribution is partly a fixed point of the policy rather than an exogenous given; and (c) what is left of the notion of a worst case once one grants that it must always be relativised to a set.

5. Introduce at least one further substantive objection of your own devising, not derivable from the three listed above.

6. Let the argument rest on reasoning. No appeals to authority, and no named results, papers, or reading lists.

7. Do not settle the matter by having either side capitulate, and do not append a narrator's summary of who was right. End instead with each stating, in a sentence or two, the consideration that would most move them.

Write in British English, in a formal but plain spoken register appropriate to two colleagues working late over a document.
