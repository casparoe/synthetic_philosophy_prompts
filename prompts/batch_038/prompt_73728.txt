I am a fifth-year doctoral candidate in political science, working at the boundary between formal theory and normative democratic theory, and in three weeks I am giving the job-talk version of my paper at a departmental colloquium. I would like you to prepare me for the question period.

The paper's thesis, stated compactly: the standard repair for the Downsian paradox of voting — replacing the private benefit in pB − C with a social benefit, as in the Edlin–Gelman–Kaplan line, where a voter trades an astronomically small pivot probability against the aggregate welfare of millions — does not answer the swing voter's curse, and in fact aggravates it. Consequently the puzzle posed by real turnout is not that so many people vote, but that the wrong people vote, and at rates the curse says they should not.

The argument runs in four steps, and I would like the objections aimed at these steps specifically.

1. Downs's observation is about the magnitude of the stakes: p is so small that pB cannot cover C for a self-interested voter.
2. The standard reply rescales B, not p. What is at stake on my ballot is not my own welfare but that of the whole polity, so pB_social can exceed C even when p is tiny. This move concerns how much is riding on the election. It says nothing about how good my decision is likely to be.
3. The swing voter's curse is a different objection, about conditional probabilities rather than magnitudes. In a large binary election in which some voters receive informative private signals about which alternative is better and others receive nothing, an uninformed voter who turns out is likelier to be decisive precisely in those states of the world where the signal-holders are split or evenly balanced — that is, where the election is close because the informed are in disagreement. Conditioning on being pivotal therefore degrades the probability that the uninformed vote selects the better outcome, and below a threshold the uninformed voter's expected contribution is negative even when voting is costless. Abstention is then the equilibrium response, and the Feddersen–Pesendorfer result together with the subsequent literature on information aggregation is the canonical statement of the point.
4. My move: step 2 cannot repair step 3. Rescaling the stakes multiplies a quantity whose sign has already turned negative. Attaching a larger social benefit to a ballot that is worse than a coin flip conditional on its being decisive increases expected harm rather than offsetting it. The two lines are not rival solutions to a single puzzle; they address different terms in different expressions, and the literature's habit of treating the altruistic-voting reply as a general answer to voting paradoxes conflates them.

The upshot I press in the final section is that this makes turnout data harder to rationalize, not easier. Political knowledge is among the strongest known predictors of voting, yet the turnout gap between the most and least knowledgeable citizens is far narrower than the curse would require if the least knowledgeable were behaving as the model says they should. And the altruistically motivated voter — the one who internalizes the welfare of the polity — is exactly the voter for whom casting a curse-affected ballot is socially costly rather than merely privately futile. The reply meant to rescue mass voting identifies a class of participants who should be more careful about participating, not less.

The room is roughly thirty people. About a third are formal theorists who know the Feddersen–Pesendorfer setup cold, along with the costly-voting and abstention-equilibrium extensions, Poisson-game formulations, and the later results on when information aggregation does and does not succeed; they will be alert to whether I have confused an equilibrium-selection claim with a dominance argument, and to whether my step 4 is really just a restatement of the standard comparative statics. About a third are empirical voting-behavior researchers who work with knowledge batteries and turnout models; their reflex will be that "uninformed" is a continuum rather than a type, that information acquisition is endogenous and bound up with partisanship, education, and mobilization, and that a stylized common-value binary election is carrying more weight than it can bear. The remainder are methodologists plus two visiting democratic theorists who will go after the normative frame: whether a model that values citizens only for their truth-tracking reliability is the right way to think about voting at all, and whether my conclusion quietly licenses an epistocratic inference I am not prepared to defend. There are no decision theorists present, so I do not expect objections from the axioms of expected utility, and I do not expect the expressivist or civic-duty literature to be the main line of attack, since my target is narrower.
