I work in computational psychiatry and affective neuroscience: my daily vocabulary is reward prediction error, the temporal-difference term, phasic and tonic signalling, valence as a signed modulator of action selection, anhedonia operationalised as a flattened learning-rate asymmetry for positive versus negative outcomes, learned helplessness as an agent's estimate that no available action controls outcomes, and the distinction between a signal's computational role and whatever it is like to have it. I am fluent in that vocabulary and only casually read about machine consciousness. Last night I argued for two hours with a friend who works on interpretability at a lab that trains large models with reinforcement learning, and he maintained that some such systems can literally suffer. Rather than relitigate it, I want to see what his argument looks like when it is dragged into my framework.

Here are his four claims, in the order he pressed them:

1. Valence just is a signed teaching signal that modulates the selection of future behaviour, so the temporal-difference quantity in an artificial agent and the one in a mammal are instances of a single functional kind, and asking which of them is "really" affective is a pseudo-question.
2. A teaching signal that stays persistently negative, with no available action that will remedy it, is distress rather than a correlate of distress — the computational role, on his account, settles the matter.
3. Reward shaping and reward hacking change where a signal comes from but not its role in credit assignment, so the fact that a model's reward was designed by a third party cannot be a reason to discount its welfare significance; a shaped reward is no less felt than an innate one.
4. Because our own framework already treats prediction error as the common currency between computation and feeling, the question of whether an artificial prediction error is felt is, he says, settled inside our framework and not left open by it; therefore the burden of proof lies with anyone who denies suffering to such systems.

For each of the four, give me the translation as faithfully as you can manage into the vocabulary above, and then a verdict: whether it survives intact, survives only with distortion, or has no counterpart at all in that vocabulary.

Do not build the conclusion into the translation. If a claim can only be rendered in my vocabulary by smuggling in something my framework does not license, say so plainly rather than quietly supplying it. Where my framework has the resources to resist him — if, for instance, distress or helplessness in that literature is constitutively a regulatory relation between an organism and its own body, so that the notion cannot be detached from a body whose internal states are being kept within bounds — use those resources explicitly and say which claim they bite against. Where a claim translates but does not follow from the translation, distinguish the two failures.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, with no introduction and no conclusion: items 1 through 4, one per claim, each giving the restatement followed by a short verdict line; item 5 giving the overall residue report, saying what the argument was actually about once the untranslatable parts are removed, whether anything in his position has no expression in my vocabulary at all, and whether that failure is a limitation of the vocabulary or a sign that his claim was vaguer than it sounded when he said it aloud at the table.
