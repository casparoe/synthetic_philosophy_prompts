Ten years ago I left an academic job in philosophy for health policy, and this spring I was appointed to the value assessment committee of a mid-sized regional health system: the body that decides which high-cost drugs, devices, and services our formulary will cover. Sitting at that table has unsettled something I was taught as a graduate student. The committee's ordinary business — assigning a number to a health state, multiplying by expected years, dividing by cost, and comparing the result to a threshold — is an institutionalized machinery for making interpersonal comparisons of wellbeing, and it operates with an assurance that the philosophical literature on the impossibility, or at least the merely evaluative status, of such comparisons would not license. My brother has used a wheelchair since an accident five years ago, so the questions the committee settles are not abstract for me, and I have drafted an essay about them for an interdisciplinary workshop this fall.

What follows is a condensed version of the draft, roughly 1,100 words drawn from a 2,400-word essay, with the examples trimmed. I would like detailed comments on it, of the specific kinds listed afterward.

---

**Anchored at Full Health: Interpersonal Utility Comparison as a Coverage Decision**

*1. A stalemate that policy declined to observe.*

Robbins's Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science turned interpersonal comparisons of utility into value judgments rather than positive claims: utility is ordinal, only rankings within a person are meaningful, and a comparison across persons is neither true nor false but an expression of the comparer's ethical sympathies. Harsanyi's mid-1950s article on cardinal welfare tried to buy the comparisons back. An impartial observer who knows each person's tastes and beliefs and can imagine occupying any of their positions will have extended preferences over those positions; those preferences yield a common cardinal scale, unique up to a positive affine transformation once a zero and a unit have been fixed. Sen's reply is roughly two-pronged. First, the observer is not a neutral instrument but a person, so the resulting numbers inherit the observer's own tastes: what looked like an objective ratio of welfare turns out to be a projection. Second, subjective satisfaction is the wrong target anyway, because the chronically deprived recalibrate their expectations downward, and satisfaction can be high where wellbeing is low; one should compare instead on some more objective basis, such as capability. What strikes me about this stalemate is that policy never waited for it to be resolved.

*2. What the committee actually does.*

In every coverage decision, a health state is assigned a number and multiplied by years. The numbers come from preference elicitation: time trade-off (surrender some lifetime for perfect health), standard gamble (accept a risk of death for a chance of perfect health), visual analogue rating, or standardized instruments such as EQ-5D, whose five dimensions — mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain and discomfort, anxiety and depression — are valued using representative samples of the general public. Death is zero. "Full health" is one. Some states score below zero, worse than death. We then compute cost per quality-adjusted life year gained and compare it against a threshold range. The threshold is where the interpersonal comparison becomes explicit: it asserts that a QALY gained by one patient is worth what a QALY gained by another is worth, and that we will refuse treatment to this patient in order to treat five of those.

My central claim is that the anchoring convention does the work Harsanyi's extended preferences were supposed to do. Cardinal utility is unique only up to a positive affine transformation, so any cross-person aggregation requires a fixed zero and a fixed unit. The QALY system supplies both by stipulation, and the stipulation is what makes the numbers commensurable across persons. That is a real achievement, but it is a normalization and not a discovery, and it carries content: it says that everyone's "full health" is the same state, that health-related quality of life is the relevant metric, and that the state sitting at one is a meaningful reference point for every human being.

*3. Where the anchor fails.*

(a) Whose rating counts. People who have lived with a permanent impairment for years consistently rate their own health states higher than members of the general public rate the same states. Elicitation practice has largely resolved this by using lay preferences — the "societal perspective" — on the ground that patients' own ratings have adapted and are therefore unreliable measures of the state itself. My brother rates his own health near the top of the scale; the lay value for his health-state profile sits considerably lower in the value set our committee uses, and both numbers are in the dossier. This resolution reinstates exactly the structure Sen objected to: the observer's values correct the subject's, and the number that governs a real person's access to treatment is a projection. If adaptation licenses discounting the patient, then Sen's adaptive-preferences argument — advanced to protect the deprived from the evidence of their own recalibrated contentment — is here deployed against them. If adaptation does not license it, then the lay ratings underwriting our threshold are arbitrary.

(b) The counterfactual reference state. For a person whose baseline is a permanent impairment, "full health" is not a state they occupy or could occupy; it is a counterfactual about a different body. Each of their years is scored as a fraction of a life unavailable to them. The denominator is not shared across persons in the way aggregation assumes. The QALY presents itself as one common scale but is a family of scales indexed to a reference point that only some people can reach.

(c) When Oregon proposed to rank Medicaid services by a cost-effectiveness-style list in the early 1990s, the federal government declined to approve the plan, and the Secretary of Health and Human Services objected that it valued the life of a person with a disability less than the life of a person without one. That objection is usually filed under distributive justice. I read it instead as an objection to the anchoring convention: the plan did not compare lives but compared life-years against a reference state that disabled people could not attain, and then let the resulting ratios set priorities.

*4. The reply I expect, and my response.*

A colleague on the committee will say that the QALY measures health, not wellbeing; that no one is committed to claiming disabled people are worse off as persons; and that standard correctives exist — separating health from disability, severity weights, prioritarian adjustments, and excluding non-health benefits from the metric. Two responses. First, if the metric measures health alone, the utilitarian justification for summing, and hence for the threshold, no longer applies: the threshold's credibility depends on an implicit claim that we are maximizing something that matters across persons, and if that something is health alone, the apparatus needs an ethical foundation other than the one it borrows. Second, the narrowing is not stable in practice, because the instruments include usual activities — work, study, housework, family, leisure — together with mobility and self-care, which is precisely the terrain where impairment and disability resist separation. Severity weights, offered to fix the distributional worry, are themselves defined as shortfalls in QALYs from full health: the same anchor, reintroduced one level up.

*5. A deliberately modest proposal.*

(i) Report lay and experienced ratings side by side in every coverage dossier, and treat divergence as evidence about the metric rather than noise to be averaged away. (ii) State the anchoring convention explicitly — death at zero, full health at one — in the documents that justify a denial, so that the value judgment is visible where it does its work. (iii) For permanent conditions, adopt a within-condition reference point, the best attainable health for people living with that condition, and disclose that this weakens cross-condition comparability. (iv) Stop describing the threshold as though it reported a fact about wellbeing. I concede that (iii) partly surrenders the common scale on which aggregation depends, so it is not a solution but a disclosure regime. The honest summary: we should make our interpersonal comparisons in public.

I would be grateful for comments organized under the following headings. Please be blunt rather than encouraging; I have already committed to giving this talk.

1. **Accuracy of the intellectual history in section 1.** Have I characterized Harsanyi correctly in attributing to him the claim that extended preferences supply a common cardinal scale once a zero and a unit are fixed? And have I conflated two Sen arguments — the projection objection and the adaptive-preferences objection — that a specialist would insist on keeping distinct? Tell me where I have compressed unfairly and how to compress fairly.

2. **Whether section 2's central claim is an insight or a category mistake.** A shared zero and a shared unit do not by themselves establish interpersonal comparability of level; one still needs equal intervals to mean equal amounts for different people. Does my argument smuggle that further premise in? If it does, can section 2 be repaired, or does the analogy between the anchoring convention and the empathy postulate collapse?

3. **The steelman I have not given in section 3(a).** State the best actual defense of using lay rather than patient preferences — the one a health economist would make, not the one a philosopher imagines an economist making — and tell me whether my argument survives it. If it does not survive, say so plainly and tell me what section 3 should become instead.

4. **Terminology.** I have used several expressions from the measurement literature from memory, including the general-public-versus-patient divergence and the idea of a "response shift." Flag anything I have labeled loosely or wrongly and give me the correct term. Do not send me to a bibliography for this; I want your judgment about whether I am using the vocabulary competently.

5. **Whether my opponent in section 1 is a straw man.** I wrote the history as though economists and philosophers still treat interpersonal comparison as blocked. If the live situation is more complicated than that, tell me what to say in place of the sentence about policy never waiting for the stalemate to be resolved.

6. **The coherence of section 5.** Does item (iii) collapse into item (i)? Is "disclosure regime" an honest description or a dignified way of dressing up a retreat? If you were reviewing this, which of the four items would you cut, and does the concession in the final paragraph undercut the essay's point or complete it?

7. **Audience and interest.** The workshop is clinicians and bioethicists with a handful of economists present. Which section loses them, which sentence is doing the least work and could go, and is "we should make our interpersonal comparisons in public" a thesis I can defend at the podium or merely a slogan?

Please do not include a reference list or suggest further readings; I can look things up myself. Where you believe I have got something wrong, tell me why rather than pointing me toward a source.
