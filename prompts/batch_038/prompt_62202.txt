I am a second-year economics PhD student and I run a small Thursday reading group on social choice; everyone in the room has had the first-year micro sequence and is fluent in von Neumann–Morgenstern utility, but nobody has taken a proper social choice course, so the exposition has fallen to me. Last week I walked them through Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: each individual's preferences over prospects satisfy the vNM axioms, the social preference does too, a Pareto indifference condition holds, and therefore the social utility function is an affine combination of the individual utility functions, with non-negative coefficients once the Pareto condition is strengthened appropriately. The proof took fifteen minutes and everyone found it easy. The discussion afterwards took two hours and went nowhere, because the room split into two camps that could not agree on what had just been shown — one saying the theorem derives utilitarianism from Pareto plus expected utility theory, the other saying it derives nothing of the sort, that it is a bare representation result, and that all the ethical content was smuggled in with the assumptions. Since I have to referee this next week, I want the conceptual issue explained properly. Start by saying precisely what the theorem does and does not deliver. Why are the coefficients not pinned down to anything resembling equal weights, given that each individual utility function is itself only defined up to positive affine transformation, and what has to be added before a claim about equal weights is even meaningful? What work does the choice between Pareto indifference and the stronger condition do — why does it fix the sign of the coefficients but not their magnitude? Then explain what the separate veil-of-ignorance, impartial-observer style argument contributes on top of the representation theorem: which assumption in it does the identification work, whether that assumption concerns beliefs or tastes, and whether it is best read as a normative argument or as an interpretation of what the theorem's coefficients mean. I would also like to know why prioritarianism is usually described as being in tension with the theorem, and whether that tension survives if one declines to read the individual utility functions as measures of well-being rather than as preference representations.

Second, the part that actually derailed us and the part I understand least well. The theorem is silent about learning: it represents a social ordering over a fixed menu of prospects and says nothing about what the aggregate does when new information arrives. Someone pointed out that the linear pool of the individuals' probability measures is not preserved by conditionalization — pool first and update on a piece of common news, and you generally do not get what you would have got had everyone updated first and you pooled afterwards — whereas the logarithmic pool does commute with updating. I want this worked out rather than asserted. Give a small explicit numerical example: two people, two states of the world, a common utility function over outcomes, differing subjective probabilities; compute the social ranking of two prospects before and after a piece of common news, and show exactly where the two procedures diverge. Then identify the general mechanism. My own algebra suggests that the updated pool is still a weighted average of the individuals' posteriors, but with weights proportional to each person's coefficient times that person's prior probability of the news, so that the aggregate quietly redistributes weight toward whoever found the news less surprising; tell me whether that is right and what it implies. Then say what the logarithmic pool costs: does it still respect unanimity, and can a preference formed by log-pooling the beliefs and summing the utilities still be presented as a Harsanyi-style weighted sum of individual utilities? Finally, the question I actually care about: does this dynamic problem embarrass the utilitarian reading of the theorem in particular, or does it embarrass any reading that treats the aggregate as a single decision maker — should we conclude that the theorem's society is not one agent but a sequence of different agents indexed by the information set?

A few constraints on the answer. Aim it at people who can do expected utility calculations unaided but have never encountered the opinion-pooling or social-choice literature, so define terms such as Pareto indifference, extended preferences, and the property of commuting with conditionalization (often called external Bayesianism) as you introduce them. No bibliography and no reading list: I cannot chase references, and I want the reasoning rather than the provenance; where both camps in my reading group have a defensible point, locate the disagreement instead of settling it by appeal to authority. Roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, with the numerical example set out as explicit arithmetic or a small table, and with claims that follow from the mathematics kept visibly separate from claims that are interpretive. End with a single last line beginning "Bottom line:" that states in one sentence which assumption or assumptions supply the specifically utilitarian content that the representation theorem itself does not.
