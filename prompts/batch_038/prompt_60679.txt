I have been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias era, and the strand of it that has stuck with me longest is the insistence that a disagreement is usually a fact about information structure rather than a fact about stupidity. I now work in computational social science, and in about three weeks I give a thirty-minute invited talk with a long question period at an interdisciplinary workshop on belief formation online. I should describe the room precisely, because I expect the objections to be a function of the room rather than of the material. Roughly forty people: mostly computational social scientists who fit contagion-style diffusion models to platform event logs and think in terms of adoption curves, exposure distributions, and whether anything is identified at all; three or four social psychologists who work on motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition, and the experimental literature on accuracy prompts; one philosopher of social epistemology who writes about testimony and epistemic dependence; two former trust-and-safety leads from large platforms, who care about what can actually be shipped under legal and moderation-load constraints; and essentially nobody who works on herding models formally — a couple of people have read the popular expositions, and that is the extent of it. I would rather be prepared for the questions this room asks than for the ones a game theorist would ask, since I can field the latter in my sleep.

The thesis is deliberately provocative, so here is the sketch the objections should be aimed at. I want to argue that the fast spread of false content is mostly rational herding — an informational cascade — rather than the operation of motivated belief: each sharer's private evidence is genuinely weak because nobody verified anything, so the visible actions of earlier sharers carry more weight than their own signal, and the aggregate diffusion curve looks like persuasion while functioning like an urn problem. Two consequences I intend to defend on stage. First, a diagnostic claim: the cascade story and the motivated-reasoning story are not yet joined in the data, because both fit the same adoption curves, and the variable that actually separates them is sensitivity to signal precision — cascade models predict that adoption should respond far more to the count of prior adopters than to the quality of any one person's private evidence, and content-level interventions such as fact-checks, prebunking, and accuracy nudges should only bite where private signals are strong. Second, a design claim: platforms should stop trying to improve the content users see and start changing what is visible about other users' evidence. My concrete proposal is what I have been calling adoption-provenance disclosure — surfacing not merely that four hundred people shared an item but the order in which they did and, where the data permits, whether each share followed an independent source or merely followed share number three. The aim is to convert observed actions back into observed signals, which is exactly the move that the theory says undoes a wrong cascade. I will concede two things myself: that textbook cascades are famously fragile, resting on the first few actions and shattering under a credible public revelation, which I want to reframe as a feature rather than an embarrassment, since it would explain why a fact-check sometimes dissolves a rumor and sometimes does nothing; and that if private signals were sufficiently informative, cascades would not form at all, so my proposal amounts to manufacturing informative public signals by fiat. I will also flag the reflexive worry in advance, since I expect someone to reach for it: once users internalize that most shares are information-free, they may discount even the genuinely independent ones, and the intervention degenerates into blanket skepticism — a second-order cascade running the other way.

Please build me the prep sheet rather than another defense of the view; I have already committed to it. I want the eight or nine objections this specific room will raise, ranked by how much of the talk each one destroys if I handle it badly, and phrased in the register of the subgroup that would raise it — the psychologists talking about directional goals and identity, the diffusion modelers talking about confounded adoption hazards and unmeasured exposure, the philosopher talking about testimonial entitlement and the epistemic burdens I am handing to users, the ex-moderators talking about surface area, label accuracy, and how any new interface affordance gets gamed within a week. For each objection: a reply I can deliver standing up in under two minutes with no equations on the slide; a candid note on where that reply is soft, or where it silently rests on an assumption I could not defend without math I have no intention of showing; and a flag for whether it is aimed at the mechanism claim, the identifiability claim, or the design proposal, because I suspect the same sentence does different work depending on who says it. Then tell me what to give away in the first three minutes so that nothing has to be extracted from me later; which single objection, fumbled, costs me the design proposal and reduces the talk to a literature review; what the weakest version of the thesis is that survives everything the room can throw, in case I have to retreat to it live; and the question I am privately dreading — the one from somebody who has actually tried to measure how much independent evidence a given share carries and concluded that it cannot be done at scale.
