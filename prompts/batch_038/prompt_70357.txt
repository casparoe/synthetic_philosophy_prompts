I'm not academic at all — left school at 16, spent most of my working life in a print shop — but for about four years I've run a Thursday night discussion group in the back room of our library. Ten or twelve of us turn up. Two of the regulars have been having the same arguement since March and I've decided the only way to stop it is to make them both sign something, so I want you to write that something.

Ray is a semi-retired photographer. His line is: the eye and brain produce the picture, and what you know or want or expect never gets inside that. Expectations change what you point the camera at, what you bother to look at, what you say afterwards and what you remember — but all of that is downstream of the seeing. He'll bring out that picture where two squares on a checkerboard are printed in exactly the same shade of grey, and if you cover up the rest of it with a bit of card they measure identical, and they still look completely different to everyone in the room. Knowing it doesn't budge the experience a millimetre. That's what seeing is like, he says: dumb and waterproof.

Priya is training to be a counsellor. Her line is that this is a distinction with no difference. A therapist who's been told beforehand that a client is hostile starts to actually see hostility in a face that isn't doing anything — she's watched it happen to herself and been embarrassed by it. And a photographer with thirty years of looking at light doesn't just have more to say about a scene than the novice standing next to him, he takes in a different scene. She also turns his picture round on him: the reason those two identical squares look different is that the visual system has already made up its mind about where the light is coming from and shaded them accordingly, before Ray's "knowing" ever arrives. So the dumb waterproof picture is itself a machine stuffed with expectations.

They both claimed the same viral dress photo in the same week, which tells you something about how this is going.

I'm pretty sure they're using "see" in two different senses and neither will admit it. There's also a problem Ray keeps walking into that I can't put properly: he says you can't trust what people report, because reports are downstream of the seeing — but reports are the only window anyone has ever had into anyone else's seeing, so how would he ever find out that his own view is right? And there's a third thing: whose expectations even count? Ray's beliefs and Priya's visual system's built-in assumptions about lighting are not obviously the same kind of thing, and I think half the fight is about that. I did eventually find out there's a technical name for this row, something about cognitive penetration, but I don't want the seminar version. I want a version two stubborn people in a library could put their names to.

Please write the joint statement. Sections in this order:

1. **What Ray and Priya can both sign.** Numbered, eight to twelve items, a line or two each. Nothing slanted — if either of them would need to add a caveat to an item, it doesn't belong here.

2. **Where they still differ.** A table, five or six rows: the topic, then how Ray would put it in words he's happy with, then how Priya would put it in words she's happy with, then a column saying whether that row is a genuine disagreement or only a disagreement about words.

3. **The cruxes worth settling.** Three or four of them, about 150 words each, and for each one say why cracking it would actually move somebody and which of the two it would move. A crux that neither side would care about the answer to isn't a crux.

4. **Thursday night tests.** One per crux, about 200 words each. What we'd actually do, what we'd write down, what each side predicts beforehand in their own terms, and what result would come out ambiguous however it landed. These have to be runnable with a phone, a printer, a tape measure, a notebook and ten people in a room — no lab, no equipment we havent got. And don't invent studies and hand them to me as if they were real, and no reading list, I can't chase any of that up and wouldn't.

5. **Giving in.** About 100 words. What each of them would have to be shown to abandon their own side, and whether the other one accepts that this really would count.

6. **What the document can't settle.** About 150 words on what's left over after all of the above, and whether that residue is interesting or just noise.

Whole thing somewhere between 1,200 and 1,600 words, and in British English please.
