Spent three hours last night in an arguement with a friend about path dependence, and I still have to give a speech on Saturday, so I figure I'll combine the two problems.

His line was that the whole lock-in story is survivorship bias with a mechanism bolted on afterward — you only ever hear it from the winner, and if you've got enough people tinkering they'll converge on the same solution regardless of who got there first. I said fine, then explain why nobody ever re-threads an entire county once the tooling is sunk and everybody's bought the matching fittings. He didn't have an answer, or he did and I wasn't listening.

Saturday I'm doing the opening remarks at our family firm's 75th anniversary. We make the couplings that join four-inch hose; because my grandfather's thread pattern got picked up by the regional co-op in '53, three counties of fire departments, grain driers and irrigation crews are still too expensive to convert away from it, and that's the business. In the room: three generations of family, about forty employees, two local historians who know precisely how lucky that bet was, and a cousin who's an economist and will be listening for hand-waving.

Write me roughly four minutes. The founders' choice has to come across as a choice, not destiny — but I can't tell that room their life's work rests on a coin flip, and I'm not saying everything happened for a reason either. If one line in there would strike my cousin as honest rather than flattering, so much the better.

After the speech, tell me how confident you are in the framing overall, and single out the line you're least sure of — anywhere you suspect you're smuggling in a contested claim about lock-in (his side, not mine) just to make something sound wise.
