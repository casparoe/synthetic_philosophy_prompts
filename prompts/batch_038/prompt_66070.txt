Tuesday I've got 75 minutes on Lewis's account of convention, and the room is about half philosophy majors and half econ majors straight out of a game theory unit — everyone can do Nash equilibrium, iterated dominance, mixed strategies, they've all played coordination games and battle of the sexes. I've taught this session twice and both times it went sideways in the same place, and I've concluded the problem is my ordering, not the content. Help me rebuild the plan.

The example I've settled on is the door convention in our corridor: shut means don't disturb, open means walk in, ajar means knock. It's arbitrary in the way I want — the whole thing could be reversed and work just as well — it answers a problem that recurs every single day, and it's small enough that nobody has to be told what a coordination problem is before we start. But tell me straight whether it's actually the right vehicle, because I can already hear one of the econ students saying it's just a signaling game with cheap talk, and I want to know before Tuesday whether that's a real objection I need to answer or a confusion I should be able to head off by construction. If it's the wrong example, tell me what the right one has to have and what it has to lack.

The sequencing is the main thing. Lewis's conditions arn't independent and I keep putting it's pieces down in an order that lets the class collapse the whole account into 'any equilibrium is a convention.' Which of the pieces has to be in place before which — the recurrence of the situation, the near-coincidence of interests, the conditional preference to conform, common knowledge, the fact that some alternative regularity would have served equally well, the role of precedent in generating the expectations? And at which points does their game theory help me and at which points does it actively work against me? I don't want to teach around the machinery they already have, I want to know where to spend it and where to switch it off.

Misconceptions. My guess at the big one is the collapse I just described. The other one I've heard twice is a student insisting that a convention has to be something people agreed to, and then reasoning from 'but nobody ever sat down and agreed to shut their doors' to the conclusion that Lewis is describing a fiction. Give me the rest of the map — what the econ half will bring in from game theory, what the philosophy half will bring in from thinking of norms as things we follow because they're right — and tell me the exact point in the sequence to defuse each one rather than collecting them all at the end.

There's also a hard case that comes up every year and I never know when to take it: somebody points out that plenty of regularities nobody chose are bad for everybody involved — replying-all to department mail, say, or the way everyone in the building takes the stairs to the second floor even though the elevator is faster — and asks how that can be a convention if the whole point is that conventions solve coordination problems. I have an answer that turns on the difference between a regularity failing to be an equilibrium and the interests not actually coinciding, but I'm not sure that's right, and I don't know where in 75 minutes it belongs or whether bringing it up early will blow up the build.

Last five minutes: one question I can put to the room that would tell me whether they've actually got the difference between an equilibrium and a convention, not just whether they can recite the definition. And a short list of what I should explicitly refuse to get into at this level and say so out loud — I'm assuming the Lewis–Sugden stuff about whether conventions get there by reasoning or by selection belongs in that pile, but tell me if I've got it backwards and it's the one thing they can't do without.
