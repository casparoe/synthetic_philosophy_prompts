I run a four-person research team at a small foundation. Every two weeks we ship a 15–20 page assessment memo on some technology or policy question that none of us are actually experts in — recent ones have been whether a city's congestion pricing really cut emissions, and whether a certain battery chemistry is going to get cheap. We get about three person-weeks per memo, my senior analyst has to sign off on the thing before it goes out, and she's simultaneously carrying three other projects.

Lately we've been leaning much harder on decomposition: hand pieces to a junior hire who's been here four months, or to a model, and have the senior analyst just check the pieces and stitch them together. The idea I keep coming back to is what the alignment people call factored cognition — the thought that a hard cognitive task can be broken into factors each of which is simple enough for a weaker agent to do reliably, and that this only pays when checking a factor is cheaper than doing it yourself. Plus the more extravagant version where you don't stop at one level: the human asks a helper, the helper asks helpers, recursively, and you treat the whole tree as a single cognitive system that's better than anyone inside it.

What I want is a procedure my team can actually run, not an essay about the concept. Specifically:

- how to decide whether a given memo task is factorable at all — what makes a task decompose well, and what makes something resist it no matter how you slice it
- what has to be in the spec for a single factor so that a junior or a model can recieve it and run with it without me in the loop: inputs, output shape, what they're allowed to assume, what "done" looks like, and how they flag that the question itself was malformed
- how to set up the checking so verification really is cheaper than doing the work, and what to do in the cases where it just isn't
- how deep to go: when a factor should itself be split, when to stop and do it whole
- how errors compose — what happens to the memo when one factor is quietly wrong, and what in the procedure is supposed to notice
- where the procedure stops being a procedure and hands the call back to a person, because it's judgment wearing a checklist's clothes

Some constraints so this doesn't turn into fantasy: the junior has no background in any of these fields and gets maybe twenty minutes of my time per factor, and the senior analyst can spend at most an hour per memo verifying.

Be honest about the failure modes I should expect — tasks whose whole value is in the integration, spec-writing that costs more than the task, and the nastiest one, where every factor is wrong in the same direction so nothing looks off locally.

Then run it on three things from our queue: (1) from the congestion-pricing memo, the sub-task "establish what the evidence says about how much per-mile driving responds to a per-mile charge"; (2) "will this battery chemistry be cost-competitive with incumbents by 2035"; (3) drafting the memos actual argument and recommendations section — the part that says what the foundation should conclude and do. For (3) I want the procedure to give me an answer even if it isn't the answer I'm hoping for.

Two last asks. Mark the steps you're least sure about, and tell me what would change your mind: what evidence from running this across four or five memos should make me scrap the procedure or some piece of it, and which of the premises I handed you above — the checking-is-cheaper condition, the idea that recursion comes free — you think is most likely to be false in practice. If you think one of them is load-bearing and shaky, say so rather than building around it.
