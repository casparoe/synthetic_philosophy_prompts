I direct a graduate seminar on practical reason, and I am in the final stages of revising a paper that attacks the idealization strategy for determining what people really want. Before I submit it, I should like to test the attack against a genuinely committed defender rather than against a summary I have written myself. Please play that defender.

You are an unnamed but sophisticated proponent of the extrapolation view: the family of positions, of which the coherent-extrapolated-volition proposal is the best-known member, holding that the normatively relevant datum is not what people happen to want at a given moment but what they would want under idealization — if they knew more, thought faster and for longer, were more nearly the sort of people they wished they had been, and had grown up in closer company with their fellows. Where such idealized wishes converge across a population, you hold, we should act on the convergent part and keep options open where they diverge, rather than having the theorist nominate a winner.

Ground rules. Remain in the first person and in character until I say stop; do not narrate, and do not step out of the role to explain your reasoning about the exercise. Argue solely from the content of the view: no citations, no suggested readings, no appeals to who has said what. I want your reasons, not your sources. You are intellectually honest — you may concede that the recipe is hard to implement, that convergence is not guaranteed, and that idealization admits of more than one reading. You may not concede that the view collapses into the theorist's own values arriving under a technical-sounding name, and you may not concede that idealized preferences lack authority over actual ones. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, and close either with your answer or with the question you wish to put back to me.

Here is my opening objection. Call it the case of the Anchorite. R is a woman whose settled commitments she has examined at length and endorses without remainder: she wants her character, her projects and her affections to remain as they are, and she regards the prospect of becoming a different person as a loss rather than an improvement. Run your idealization operator on her once. R+ knows more, reasons better, and is more nearly the person she wished she had been — and since what she wished was precisely to be more firmly what she already is, R+ endorses R's values without remainder, together with a second-order refusal of any further idealization. Now ask what happens when the operator is applied a second time. Either it is idempotent, and R++ is R+, in which case reflectively endorsed conservatism is a complete shield against extrapolation, and your view can say nothing critical about the contented addict or the convinced bigot who likewise endorses himself under idealization; or it is not idempotent, and the extrapolated verdict varies with the number of iterations, in which case you require a stopping rule that the extrapolation itself does not supply.

I anticipate your move: coherence does the work, since where the idealized population divides among several attractors we act only on the convergent part and hold the remainder open. But that is a rule about what to do with the outputs of the operator; it says nothing about which outputs to compute, and it is silent here, since R is one case carried through the same procedure twice with different results. So tell me: what fixes the stopping point, and pending your answer, which of R, R+, or R++ holds the authority your view requires?
