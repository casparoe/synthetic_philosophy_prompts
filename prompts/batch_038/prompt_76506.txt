**The apparatus as given to the students**

1. *The documentary floor.* A novelist may invent utterances, thoughts, and unwitnessed scenes for a named person in the record only where the invention is continuous with what the record establishes about that person. Where the record establishes nothing, invention is permitted, but it must be disclosed in an afterword. The floor binds named persons in the record; it does not bind unnamed crowds, and it does not bind wholly invented characters.
2. *The composite license.* A novelist may fuse several minor real persons into a single character, provided that the composite does not assign to any of its sources an act that none of them is recorded to have done.
3. *The silence obligation.* Where the archive is silent because of a structural exclusion — the illiterate, the enslaved, women whose letters were not kept, the laboring dead generally — the novelist is obliged not merely to note the silence but to imagine into it. This obligation is owed rather than earned, and it does not depend on whether the imagining is verifiable.
4. *Retroactive consent.* Because the dead cannot consent, the novelist must model the consent that a reasonable person in the subject's position would have given, taking the subject's own values as the standard rather than the novelist's or the descendants'. The standard is the subject's, and the clause applies whether or not descendants survive.

**The question as set**

Consider the following case, drawn from a novel of my own that I abandoned. It concerns a woman who kept a toll house on an English canal in the 1840s. Her name appears eleven times in the canal company's ledger, each entry recording tolls collected and remitted; no letter of hers survives, and no census return for her has been located. In the novel she is given a continuous interiority, a miscarriage she tells no one about, and a theft of toll money she conceals for two years. Three boatmen, who appear in the company's tonnage books only as initials against crew counts, are fused into a single character who kills a child at a lock. An afterword discloses the fusion, the miscarriage, and the theft.

Using the four clauses above, evaluate the novelist's procedure. Does it clear the documentary floor? Where do the composite license and the silence obligation come into conflict, and what, if anything, does retroactive consent add to the other three clauses? Finally, say whether the apparatus is best understood as a set of permissions constrained by duties or as a set of duties constrained by permissions, and defend your reading.

**The student's answer**

The apparatus is best read as a set of duties constrained by permissions, since three of its four clauses state what the novelist owes and only the composite license states what she may do. I will take the clauses in turn and then apply them to the toll house.

The documentary floor governs named persons in the record. The tollkeeper is therefore inside its scope, while the boatmen, who appear only as initials against crew counts, are outside it. That distinction does the work one expects of it. It means the floor is breached, if it is breached at all, by the invented miscarriage and the invented theft, both of them attributed to a named woman about whom the ledger records eleven payments and nothing else. Because the record establishes nothing about her inner life, both inventions fall into the clause's second limb: permitted, provided they are disclosed. They are disclosed.

The general defense of such invention is Aristotle's. Poetry is more philosophical than history because it treats of what a person of a given kind would probably or necessarily do, whereas history treats of particulars. The tollkeeper is not a particular in the historian's sense but a kind — the provincial woman of the 1840s who could sign her own name — and invention is licensed by that generality. This is also why the silence obligation cannot be unlimited. The clause says the obligation is owed rather than earned and independent of verifiability, but a duty to imagine into a silence presupposes that the silence has a shape. Where the archive is not merely silent but silent about someone who left no trace at all — the illiterate laborer, the unregistered child — there is nothing to imagine into, and the obligation lapses for want of an object. In such cases the novelist owes only the acknowledgment that someone was there.

The fusion of the three boatmen into one character who kills a child at the lock is a use of the composite license, and in my view the license's proviso is satisfied. The proviso forbids assigning to a source an act none of the sources is recorded to have done. The murder is assigned to the composite, which is not a person in the record but a device, and the afterword says so plainly. No individual boatman is thereby said to have killed anyone, so nothing has been assigned to any source.

Retroactive consent adds the least, and it is where the apparatus most needs revision. Since the dead cannot be wronged — there is no subject remaining who could be in a worse condition — consent modeled on the subject's own values has no bearer, and what is left is the interest of the living: descendants, the community the subject belonged to, and readers whose understanding of that community the novel shapes. Retroactive consent is therefore best read as a duty owed to survivors, which means it binds only where there are identifiable survivors to bind to. The tollkeeper's line is traceable to two living households, so the clause applies to her. It does not apply to the boatmen, whose names are unrecoverable and whose descendants cannot be identified; their treatment is governed by the composite license and by nothing more demanding than the ordinary norms of the craft.

So: duties constrained by permissions, with one qualification. The duties that survive scrutiny are those owed to the living, and the documentary floor and the silence obligation are best understood as instruments for discharging them.
