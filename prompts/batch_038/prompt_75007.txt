I write literary fiction and there's a pattern in my drafts I've never had a word for.

Two instances. In the current book there's a harbour pilot whose gender isn't given for forty pages — my agent, both beta readers, and a book club that read a early excerpt all pictured a man, and nobody treated that as a choice worth mentioning. Then I changed one pronoun in chapter three and suddenly people wanted to know what the book was "saying," whether she was angry on purpose, whether she was there to make a point. Same thing with a minor character in a wheelchair in my last novel: completely invisible until I named it, and then every line he had got re-read as a position on disability.

What I can't name is this: leaving a trait out reads as neutral and costs nothing, but stating it retroactively turns everything the character already did into evidence about the whole category. It runs backwards through the text — the earlier scenes change meaning after the fact. And I want to keep that seperate from the plain observation that some traits get marked and others don't, which is not the interesting part for me.

I know markedness is somewhere in the neighborhood, and there's an old Hofstadter satire, seventies I think, about how absurd it is that language has a default person. But neither seems to cover the backwards-looking bit, and I'm not sure the essay is doing what I need it to do anyway.

Is there an established concept for this? If not, name it and define it properly, then tell me where it stops applying — if I state a trait the reader would have assumed anyway? if there's no likely assumption either way? if the character isn't human? I want the edges drawn, not just a word to scribble in a notebook.
