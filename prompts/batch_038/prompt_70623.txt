Last week two people in my reading group, Dana and Owen, got into it about the view that what's good for me is fixed by what a fully informed, fully vivid, clear-thinking version of me would want for my life. Roughly how it went:

(1) Dana: the view can't handle goods that depend on not knowing. My idealized self already knows how every movie ends and how every bet turns out, so it can't want suspense for me — and some things are only good if you're ignorant of them.
(2) Owen: no problem. Idealized-me isn't after suspense for itself, it wants suspense for the ignorant me. Desires can be about other people's states, and here the informed self is just advising the uninformed one.
(3) Dana: fine, but then the idealization isn't doing any normative work. If the informed advisor recommends suspense, that's because suspense is already good for me, and the advisor is only a way of finding that out. And a discovery tool that distorts — vivid imagining makes risks loom bigger than they are — isn't even a good tool.
(4) Owen: you've got it backwards. There's no prior list of good-for-me facts sitting there to be tracked; the advisor's wanting X is what makes X good for me. So "distortion" doesn't mean anything here. Distortion relative to what?
(5) Dana: then which advisor? Calm, detached me wants me to take the quiet teaching job. Vividly imaginative me wants me to go photograph war zones. If the facts are constituted by whichever idealization gets picked, the view just licenses picking the one that delivers the answer you wanted anyway.
(6) Owen: it doesn't get picked, it's settled by what full information and full rationality actually amount to. If the two versions disagree, that only shows we haven't finished specifying the ideal.
