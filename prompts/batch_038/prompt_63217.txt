I spent thirty-seven years as a comparative physiologist, most of them recording from the nervous systems of crustaceans and insects, and I have come to philosophy late and from the bench end rather than the library end. A local philosophical society has asked me for a paper on whether invertebrates have moral standing, and before I put my name to a position I would like it audited. What follows is what I have actually arrived at, hedged in the way that suggests to me it may not be coherent.

(1) Sentience admits of degrees. I do not believe there is a non-arbitrary line separating subjects from non-subjects, and I regard any account that produces such a line as failing on that ground alone; standing ought to vary continuously with the underlying capacity.

(2) What confers standing is not intelligence, language, self-modeling, or behavioral flexibility, but the capacity for valenced experience: the capacity for states that are, for the subject, bad or good. Everything else is at best instrumentally relevant.

(3) The indicator of valence I trust is architectural rather than behavioral. I look for what I have taken to calling an arbitration station: a locus at which competing drives — feeding, escape, mating, thermoregulation — are resolved into a single global output that commands the animal as a whole. Where there is arbitration I am willing to say there is a subject; where there are only parallel reflex loops under local modulation, I am not. Learning and behavioral richness count as evidence for me only insofar as they cannot be implemented without arbitration. I note, as a physiologist, that mammalian preparations with the cortex removed still mount organized avoidance and what look to me like states of distress, which is one reason I distrust criteria that require much in the way of integrative machinery.

(4) Intensity alone seems to leave out something important about death and about a life going badly, so I add a factor of my own devising. I call it the stake interval: the timespan over which a system's states are bound into one continuing subject, such that what happens now matters to what happens later for that same subject. I hold that standing is proportional to the intensity of the valence multiplied by the length of the stake interval, and that a system with no appreciable stake interval is a succession of momentary subjects rather than one subject with a life.

(5) Since none of this can be verified, I think one should weight one's estimate of a system's standing by one's confidence that it is sentient at all, rather than treating the matter as settled in either direction.

My verdicts on cases, such as they are: a lobster boiled alive is a serious wrong; the honeybee — which navigates, learns, recruits nestmates, shows state-dependent modulation of its responses and, in the judgment-bias preparations my colleagues ran, what look like pessimistic expectations — has negligible standing and does not enter any practical calculus I would defend; the adult mayfly, which lives a day or two on vestigial mouthparts and does not feed, is morally weightless, its destruction no loss whatever; a colony of forty thousand bees has no standing as a colony, only whatever its members severally have; a pain-asymbolia patient who reports discriminating a noxious stimulus yet is entirely indifferent to it is not suffering and is not wronged by that stimulus; and a hypothetical machine possessing both an arbitration architecture and a long stake interval, which I am confident has no valence whatever, has no standing.

I cannot resolve where the trouble is. My mayfly verdict strikes me as smuggling in the very line I disown in (1); (2) and (5) worry me as a way of having things both ways, since I insist that only valence matters and then admit I do not know where it is; and I half suspect (3) is a cognitivist criterion dressed in hedonist clothing. When I try to pin any of these down I end up defending my verdicts rather than my principles, which is why I want a third party.

So: act as an auditor, not as a lecturer. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely conflict and which only appear to, and rank the real conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as a list of equal complaints. For each, identify precisely what would have to be modified or dropped, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision would cost me — in particular, which of my stated verdicts I would be obliged to reverse. Where one of my own diagnoses above is simply mistaken, say so and explain the mistake; I would rather learn that a tension I have been fretting over is illusory than have it confirmed for politeness. Note also anything in the case list that my stated principles cannot deliver even though I have not flagged it as a problem. Finally, tell me which single revision, if I were permitted only one, would eliminate the most inconsistency at the least cost, and whether a physiologist could defend that revision in public without embarrassment.
