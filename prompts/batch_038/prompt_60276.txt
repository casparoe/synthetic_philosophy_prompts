I run interpretation at a county historical society in the midwest — one curator, two part-timers, a rotating pile of volunteers, and a director who has the history training but spends basically all her time on grants. We approve or rewrite maybe ten to fifteen labels and panel texts a month, and every single one turns into the same argument at our Thursday meeting: is this presentism, or is this just being honest about what happened? The argument never gets anywhere because people keep sliding between two different things. One is choosing what goes in the gallery at all — we spent two years on an 1830s plantation house and which story we decided to foreground is obviously a choice made from where we stand, but nobody in the building thinks that's a sin. The other is judging the people in the story by standards they couldn't have recognized — calling an 1840s physician a thief, saying flatly that a family owned human beings, explaining to visitors why the founder's racial views were wrong. Our new state humanities grant makes this worse: it requires every panel to end with a relevance line tying the content to a live present-day issue, which feels like a funder-mandated presentism, and half my volunteers are quietly refusing to write them.

I want a procedure my team can actually run on a draft label in that meeting, not a survey of anachronism. Ground it in whatever the historiography debate really gives you that's usable — Butterfield's attack on the whig reading of the past, Skinner's insistence that you not go hunting in old texts for contributions to our own questions — but tell me which parts of that survive contact with a fifty-word label read standing up by a family on a Saturday, and which parts you're throwing out to make it fit, and why.

The procedure needs to handle these, because they're the ones that eat our Thursdays:
- the funder's relevance line: is it forced presentism, and if not, what's a version of it that isn't?
- picking versus judging, and whether a label can be clean on one and dirty on the other
- institutions rather than persons — a poor farm, a boarding school, a slave quarter — where there's no individual who could have recognized the standard being applied
- the case where the presentist-sounding words are the historical actor's own, quoted straight, and we're just the ones choosing to let them stand
- what I'd call the mirror move: a line that reminds visitors that people two centuries from now will find our practices monstrous. Is that the cure for presentism or the most presentist thing on the wall?

Run it on two drafts. First: a physician-anatomist panel where the school paid men to dig up bodies, and the current text says he built his career on stealing the dead of the poor. Second: a Gilded Age mill panel that ends by comparing the company store to a modern gig worker's expense report — a relevance line I personally want to kill. Include one case where the procedure's verdict goes against me, either defending that line or striking something I'm proud of, and say plainly which of the gates are really judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.

Format: the procedure itself as exactly five numbered gates, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no wrap-up paragraph; then the two drafts as a compact pass/fail line per gate; then a single sentence on what would make you revise the gates — which argument or piece of evidence would flip your mind about one of them, or where you think this thing is most likely to misfire in practice. American English, please.
