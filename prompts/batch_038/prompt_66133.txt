I want a thought experiment built to specifications. Purpose: I am trying to determine whether Chesterton's fence is a substantive argument or merely a dressed-up preference for the status quo, so what I need is a case in which the fence's own logic — first find out why the thing is there — issues in removal rather than preservation. A case that simply stipulates that the discovered function is better served by some alternative will count as cheating; the verdict must fall out of what the function turns out to be.

Specifications:

- Setting: a realistic modern organization (a firm, a hospital, a university, a city agency). No literal fences, no culture-war topics, no science fiction.
- The practice is long-standing, and its rationale is recoverable only by inference from what the practice actually does: no surviving memo, no retired colleague who remembers the original decision.
- Obsolescence is barred: the problem the practice was meant to solve must still exist, and the organization must still endorse the goal.
- Value continuity: the reformers share the founders' values, so nothing may turn on moral or political standards having changed.
- Scenario no longer than two paragraphs.

Then: identify the function and lay out the evidence that pins it down; explain why a Chestertonian conservative who accepts that diagnosis should favor removal; and give the strongest Burkean rejoinder — appealing to prescription, prejudice, or the latent wisdom of inherited arrangements rather than to mere risk aversion — noting whether it depends on functions your scenario left unstated. Finish by naming the single feature of the case that does the work, and say what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed.
