For eleven years I have taught the philosophy of the emotions and of fiction at a university that shares a campus with a teaching hospital, and since 2019 I have co-directed, with the palliative care service, a small program that offers curated films, recorded readings, and short works of literature to inpatients, their families, and ward staff. Roughly a dozen proposals reach me each term — from chaplains, social workers, bereavement facilitators, clinicians, and the patient and family advisory council — asking that we add a work to the catalogue or program an existing one for a particular audience at a particular moment. I have been settling these by intuition, and my own research has not helped: I know the literature on why audiences seek out and value depictions of suffering, but that literature is an argument about mechanism, not a decision procedure, and the committee that will run what you write includes two nurses, a chaplain, and a social worker, none of whom has read any of it. Decisions are made three weeks before a screening date, the hospital's counsel must sign off on anything involving identifiable real patients, and there is usually no way to ask the people most affected what they want, because many of them are too ill to be consulted and their families disagree with one another.

What I want is the translation. Write a screening procedure my committee can run on a proposal in about thirty minutes, grounded in the accounts of painful art that are actually on the table: Hume's claim that the artistry and eloquence of a depiction convert a painful passion into a pleasing one; catharsis; the make-believe account on which what we feel for a fictional character is a quasi-emotion rather than its real-world counterpart; psychical distance as a condition of aesthetic response; the curiosity-and-fascination account developed for horror; and the views that locate the value in the audience's own responsive capacity, or in what the experience makes available to understand, rather than in the feeling's hedonic tone. A survey of the field sorts these into six families — conversion, control, compensation, meta-response, catharsis, and rich experience — and you may use that as your organizing frame, but each question in your procedure should say which family it is testing, because different mechanisms license different design decisions. There is also a body of work on sad music and film suggesting that pleasant being-moved and flat distress are different outcomes of the same stimulus, and that the difference tracks trait empathy, familiarity with the idiom, and what is going on in the listener's own life; use it if it earns its place, but do not let it do work it cannot support.

The procedure should state its questions in the order they are to be asked, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the points at which it terminates in a refusal rather than passing the proposal along, and mark the points at which the question has stopped being one of fact and must be handed to a named person. Tell me which distinctions from the theory must survive the simplification and which may be dropped, and where you expect the procedure to misfire. Address squarely a difficulty I cannot resolve: this literature was developed almost entirely for audiences of fiction, at a distance from the events depicted, while most of my material is documentary or testimonial and depicts precisely the condition the audience is in. Does that break the transfer of these theories, or only change which of them applies? Build your answer into the procedure rather than appending it as a caveat.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. (1) A bereavement facilitator wants us to screen, in the inpatient family room, with an open invitation and no discussion afterward, a documentary in which a father films the final year of his daughter, who dies of the disease most of our inpatients have; she is confident it is exactly what the group needs. (2) A group of ward clinicians wants a monthly staff session of deliberately bleak, unresolved works — no consolation, no redemptive framing — on the ground that their work requires them to stop reaching for redemptive framings. (3) The advisory council wants us to withdraw from the catalogue, for everyone rather than merely flagging it, a stage adaptation I programmed myself three years ago and consider the best thing we hold, because one family reported that it made a relative's final week worse. At least one of these should come out against my preference; I have already told the council I will resist the third, and I want to know whether the procedure can show that my resistance is attachment rather than judgment.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot decide at all, what would have to be settled by values I hold rather than by any theory of the emotions, and what would count as evidence, gathered over a few terms of use, that the procedure is systematically failing — that is, what pattern of complaints or refusals should make me throw it out.
