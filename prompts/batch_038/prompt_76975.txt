I am a novelist by trade — four books published, all of them character-driven literary fiction — and I have no formal training in philosophy or the history of science beyond what I have stolen from library books over the last two years. The book in progress centres on a physicist who settles a disputed question in her own head, at a desk, without running anything, and I have been reading in the philosophy of science so that I do not embarrass myself. I have got stuck on the debate about how a thought experiment can yield knowledge of the world at all. As I understand it, one camp — Norton is its most persistent advocate — holds that a thought experiment is nothing but an argument in picturesque dress: whatever it concludes was already latent in premises drawn from experience, and any competent instance (Galileo's two falling bodies lashed together, say) can be rewritten as an ordinary reductio, at which point the imagining is doing no epistemic work that the argument was not already doing. The opposing camp — Brown's Platonism, roughly — holds that the reconstruction always leaves something out, and that in the good cases we seem to acquire a genuine a priori insight into the laws of nature, a kind of seeing that is not a matter of unpacking premises. Between them sit various mediating views: that thought experiments are experiments performed on models rather than on the world; that they exploit tacit or instinctive know-how; that they are chiefly a species of counterexample-hunting; that their real work is rhetorical, moving a scientific community to abandon a paradigm.

Here is what I want. Please act as a philosopher of science who is also, unusually, fluent in the working vocabulary of fiction, and restate that whole debate for me in the language I actually think in: draft and revision, scene and summary, point of view and focalization, character motivation and the question of what this person would plausibly do next, verisimilitude, the offstage event the reader is left to infer, the difference between a story that merely illustrates a thesis and one that discovers something, and the way a finished chapter constrains what may happen afterwards. Take the debate piece by piece rather than summarising it wholesale. For each central claim — the reconstructibility thesis, the Platonist reply about the irreducible moment of insight, and each of the mediating positions above — give me three things: what translates cleanly into my craft vocabulary, what translates only with distortion (saying explicitly what you had to bend to make it fit), and what has no expression in that vocabulary whatsoever. Be scrupulous about this: do not smuggle the assumptions of narrative theory in as though they were the philosophers' claims, and do not flatter my discipline by declaring that a novelist's imagination and a physicist's are the same faculty. I am completely fluent in the target vocabulary and not at all fluent in the source, so the burden of accuracy falls entirely on the source side.

The residue is the part I actually care about, and I would like you to think hardest about it before you write anything: work through the mapping privately, claim by claim, and only then compose the answer. Where the philosophers say something my vocabulary cannot say, tell me whether that is a defect of the vocabulary — a real gap in how novelists understand their own craft, for instance the absence of any word for a warrant that is neither empirical nor merely coherent within the story — or evidence that the disagreement is less substantive than it sounds, two descriptions of one psychological process quarrelling over labels. There is a specific test case I want addressed head-on rather than waved at: when I write a scene in order to find out what a character will do, and she surprises me, is there anything in that surprise structurally analogous to the Platonist's intuition of a law of nature, or is the analogy purely decorative? Please give me no bibliography, no reading list, and no citation I would have to look up; I have the books, and what I want from you is reasoning, not references. Keep the whole thing to roughly twelve hundred words.
