I cover physics and ideas for a fortnightly magazine and I'm trying to write a piece on many-worlds that doesn't just reheat the usual stuff. At a panel last week one of the speakers — a cosmologist, not a philosopher — leaned on "everything that can happen, does happen" about four times, and each time it seemed to be doing slightly different work. Once it was to swat away a question from the floor about why we should expect to see Born-rule frequencies if every outcome happens somewhere. Once it was to argue that Everett can't be tested at all, on the grounds that a theory which predicts everything predicts nothing. And once, more gaily, to tell the audience there's a branch where I've finished the novel I keep saying I'm writing. He was also very firm, five minutes later, that there's no fact of the matter about how many worlds there are — they're more like clouds than like planets, was his phrase.

Before I put any of that in print I want to know what the slogan could actually be claiming. Can you lay the readings out as a menu rather than picking your favourite one: what sense of "can" is in play (dynamically allowed by the Schrödinger equation? consistent with the actual initial state? merely logically possible?), what sense of "does happen" (in some branch with non-zero amplitude? in a branch of any particular measure? in infinitely many?), what "everything" is quantifying over, and where the set of branches is supposed to come from. Sort them from the ones that are near-trivially true of unitary quantum mechanics up to the wild plenitude claims nobody who works on this would sign up to, and give each a verdict — true, false, or not well-formed — with a line of reasoning, not just a label.

Then tell me which reading the cosmologist would have needed for each of his three uses, and whether he was entitled to it. I especially want the falsifiability move looked at hard: is there any reading on which Everett genuinely cannot come out wrong, and if so, does it follow from anything Everettians actually hold, or from a claim they'd reject? And flag anywhere the slogan quietly assumes there's a definite, countable-ish set of worlds to quantify over in the first place — that seems to sit badly with his clouds remark, and I suspect thats where the dodgy work is being done. One other thing: if the honest answer is that the second and third uses need readings that are flatly false rather than merely contentious, say so plainly, I'd rather have it straight than have my speaker let off.
