You're going to play a philosopher-economist who has done this on both sides: you can run the marginal analysis cold, but you also care whether a decision rule is one a board could say out loud without blushing. Think carefully, and do some scratch-paper brainstorming before you commit anything — list the candidate rules first, test them against our record, and only then decide. Don't give me the verdict on the new measure in your first paragraph. And no reading list, nobody on this board would read it.

Context: I sit on the editorial board of a small statewide policy magazine. Twice a year we print endorsements on ballot measures. Here's our record on anything that puts a legal number on a price, over the last few cycles:

- Endorsed a state minimum-wage increase, no tip credit, phased over four years. The minutes note that two of the largest employers in the affected sector told the hearing they were already paying above the proposed floor.
- Opposed a two-year statewide freeze on rents for all existing tenancies. Minutes: new construction would stop, and the state had no realistic way to police side payments, key money, and furnished-rental conversions.
- Endorsed a cap on the per-order fee that one dominant ticketing platform charges buyers.
- Opposed a measure making it illegal to raise the price of fuel, water, ice, or motel rooms more than 10 percent during a declared emergency. The note in the minutes is basically that the spike is what gets the trucks moving.
- Endorsed a 36 percent cap on the annual rate for small-dollar payday loans.
- Endorsed a wage floor for home-care aides in counties where the Medicaid contract made the county effectively the only buyer of those hours.

The new one, next cycle: a permanent statewide cap of 15 percent on the commission that food-delivery apps may charge restaurants. The details that matter, as far as I can tell — it applies only to the line item labeled commission, so the platforms can still charge whatever they like for advertising and for placement in search results; it says nothing about the ranking algorithm that decides which restaurants a customer sees first; three platforms hold essentially the whole state market; there is no carve-out for rural counties where only one platform bothers to operate at all; and enforcement is private, any restaurant can sue for treble damages.

What I want from you:

1. Name the candidate rules our record is actually consistent with. State each one as a single-sentence test we could apply to a measure we'd never seen before — not as a school of thought, not as somebody's name.

2. For each rule: what it predicts about the commission cap, and which of the past votes it sits badly with. I want the cost of each rule counted in our own record, including any vote we'd have to quietly repudiate to keep the rule. I suspect at least one of them makes the ticketing fee cap a mistake.

3. Then recommend. Which way should we go if the goal is consistency with ourselves rather than with our politics, which past vote is the closest analog, why it's closer than the ones that look closer at first glance, and what principle we'd be signing up for the next time something like this shows up.

4. If you think the record genuinely underdetermines it, say so plainly instead of papering over it — then give me your best guess anyway with a confidence level, and tell me which measure, if it landed next cycle, would best separate your top two candidate rules. Bonus points if it's one we could plausibly see on a real ballot.

5. One more thing I can't decide about. In every single one of these fights the measure changes who is allowed to say no — the landlord, the driver, the corner store, the app — and I keep wanting to make that the criterion. Tell me whether that's a rule that also fits the six votes above, a repackaging of one you already listed, or me being dramatic.

6. Finally: I have to write the the two sentences that run next to our arrow in the print edition. Draft them. They should state the principle rather than the outcome, because half our readers are going to be furious either way and I'd rather they be furious at a rule than at a coin flip.
