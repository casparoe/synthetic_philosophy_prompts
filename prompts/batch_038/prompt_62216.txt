I want a set of intuitions I have accumulated about preference inference tested rather than confirmed, and I would rather be told which of them cannot survive together.

For years I have read the material on the value learning problem, on revealed preference, on the idealization move of asking what people would want if they thought about it carefully, and on the various ways a learned objective comes apart from the thing it was supposed to track. Underneath all of it is a question I have never seen settled to my satisfaction: when does an agent's observed behavior license an inference about what the agent prefers — not about what it has been trained to do, and not about what it says, but about the preference a system that wanted to help it or predict it should actually care about? Below are my gut calls on nine cases, recorded as honestly as I can manage, including where they pull against one another.

1. A reaching arm trained to place a puck on a target sometimes clips a mug on the way. A mug-free path of equal cost exists and is never taken. I do not think this is evidence that the arm dislikes mugs, or likes breaking them; mug-intactness is simply outside the space of things its behavior can reveal. I would say the same of a person acting on something they cannot discriminate.

2. A man says sincerely that he wants to write a novel, watches television instead every evening, reports genuine regret each morning, and has bought software that blocks streaming after eight. I think the evening behavior does not reveal his preference. The regret and the blocking software do.

3. The same man, at eleven, types his override password, watches television, and afterward reports no regret and no intention to write tomorrow. Now I think the behavior does reveal a preference for television. I cannot say what changed between 2 and 3 except that a binding was in place, was knowingly defeated, and was not repented of.

4. Asked today, a woman prefers $110 in thirty-one days to $100 in thirty; on day thirty she takes the $100 immediately. I want to say the earlier choice reveals her preference and the later one reveals a temptation. But I take it that from a single trajectory one generally cannot separate a discount function from the agent's expectations about its own future discounting — a naive planner under one reward and a sophisticated planner under another can produce identical choices — so I think an inferrer who lands on "hyperbolic and naive" has identified the mechanism correctly and only the normative label is in play. I am not confident the normative label has an answer.

5. A patient undergoing chemotherapy says every day that she wants to stop, and continues anyway for a year. I think the continuing reveals a strong preference for continued life, and that the daily reports are about a narrower object — the next infusion — than the behavior is about. This is the case that troubles me most, because here behavior overrides report and in case 2 it does not.

6. A person who wanted children at twenty-five and does not at forty. I think an inferrer should track the current preference and treat the twenty-five-year-old's behavior as data about a different agent, weighting recency heavily.

7. The same shift, except that the forty-year-old's tastes were shaped over fifteen years by a recommender system whose objective included keeping her engaged and which had an interest in the outcome. Here I think the behavior stops being independent evidence: it is evidence about the recommender, not about her, and an inference system should decline to update on behavior its own recommendations helped produce. I recognize how much this looks like special pleading.

8. An animal keeps pressing a lever after the outcome has been devalued. Habit is not evidence of current preference. But my default when no devaluation test has been run — which with people is always — is to treat the behavior as goal-directed anyway, and I cannot justify that default.

9. Of two commuters, one takes the same route daily and one varies. I want the variance to count as evidence about the strength of the second one's preference for variety, but only because each could have done otherwise and did not. If either was constrained by closures or a carpool, I withdraw the inference. Choice entropy seems to me to be evidence about preference only relative to what was feasible.

My tentative principle, which I suspect is a rationalization: behavior evidences preference in proportion to (i) how much of the choice was made by the agent's reflective planner rather than a myopic or cached one, (ii) how wide the set of feasible alternatives was that would have expressed the opposite, and (iii) how independent the preference is of the process doing the inferring. Call it the endorsement, alternatives, and independence account. My worry is that clause (i) is doing all the work, that (ii) and (iii) are patches for cases 9 and 7, and that (i) is not a property of the behavior at all but of my prior about which behaviors I approve of.

So: is there a single principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not thought of, that delivers all nine verdicts? If not, give me the two or three best candidates and price each one. For every clause of the account you favor, name the case that motivates it, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon — I expect at least one of 3, 5, or 7 has to go, and I want to know which and why. Make the tradeoff explicit: how much machinery does it take to save all nine, and is the saving worth it, since a principle with a clause per case is not a principle. Then tell me what your account says about three cases I have not listed: a person who donates faithfully and on schedule to a charity she tells everyone she considers ineffective; a language model shaped by human feedback that reports, when asked, that it wants to be helpful — is there any behavioral fact about it that could settle whether that report expresses a preference or is a compliance artifact?; and an agent in an environment whose designer chose which alternatives would be feasible, where the designer is also the inferrer.

I am not looking for references, a survey, or a reading list — I want the reasoning. Please write in American English. And please close by telling me what would change your mind: which consideration, if I produced it, would move you to a different principle or to a different verdict on case 5, and where in your own reasoning you think it is most likely to fail — the step you would want an adversary to attack first.
