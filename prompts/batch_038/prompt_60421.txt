So I got talked into going to this Tuesday night discussion group at the community center near my place — roughly half philosophy nerds, half people who've done a lot of retreats — and last week the topic was whether karma can be taken seriously as a theory of moral desert. Not as a religious package deal, but as an answer to the question of who deserves what and in virtue of what. Two of the regulars ended up going at it for like forty minutes while the rest of us sat there with our tea, and I keep turning it over because I don't think either one ever landed a clean hit. Could you go through it move by move and tell me who's actually right at each step?

Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Nadia opened by saying karma can't ground desert at all, because the payoff lands across lives: the person suffering now has no memory of and no psychologial continuity with whoever earned it, so it's structurally the same as punishing someone for a stranger's crime, and a desert the subject can't connect to their own agency is just cosmic bookkeeping. (2) Ravi said that assumes desert rides on memory, but in the karma picture what carries over isn't memories, it's dispositions — tendencies of craving and aversion that get transmitted and eventually ripen — and we already hold people responsible on exactly that basis, since someone who drinks into a blackout and totals a car isn't let off for not recalling the night before, given that what matters is the character they built. (3) Nadia said the blackout case only works because there's one continuous life plus public evidence about how that character got built, whereas karma has no evidential hook and no proportionality schedule, so there's no possible check on whether a given suffering fits a given act; that makes it unfalsifiable, and an unfalsifiable desert theory can't be action-guiding, because you can never tell anyone what to do differently on its basis. (4) Ravi shot back that the falsifiability demand is misapplied, since retributivists don't have a proportionality table you could go check either and nobody treats the claim that the guilty deserve to suffer as a hypothesis up for refutation; on his reading karma is a normative claim about the structure of desert and the rebirth machinery is just packaging inherited from a particular metaphysics. (5) Nadia said that concession is the giveaway — strip the packaging and you're left with "character has consequences," which is a banal empirical generalization and not a theory of desert at all, while keep the packaging and you inherit two problems she thinks he can't get out of: the view licenses blaming people for their own suffering, and there's the old circularity worry that there can't be a first karma, because acting presupposes a body and the whole point was that bodies and circumstances are supposed to be karma's output. (6) Ravi's last move was that beginninglessness is a deliberate feature rather than a bug, since the tradition flatly refuses a first moment in the way some cosmologies refuse a first cause, and that the victim-blaming charge confuses a theory's truth with its abuses — any claim that suffering tracks desert can be wielded cruelly, including the secular ones Nadia presumably prefers.

I'm deliberately not telling you which way I leaned, mostly because I still don't know. What I want is a verdict on each of the six moves — clearly right, partly right, or a mistake, with a sentence or two of justification each — and then whatever you think both of them sailed right past. I had a vague feeling at the time that they were using the word desert in two different senses without noticing, but I couldn't have defended it, so treat that as a hunch to either develop or shoot down.
