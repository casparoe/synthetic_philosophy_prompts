Take the 1924–25 origin of Bose–Einstein statistics and read it as a case study in the identity of indiscernibles. Here's the physics I'm working from, so correct me if I've garbled any of it: Bose got Planck's law by counting photons in little phase-space cells in a way that treats swapping two of them as the same arrangement rather than a new one, a step that looks like a straightforward blunder by classical lights but happens to give the right answer; Einstein picked it up, applied the same counting to atoms, and got a condensation prediction out of it; and Gibbs had already been dividing by N! to keep the entropy extensive and to get rid of the discontinuity where the mixing entropy jumps to zero the moment two different gases become the same gas.

Now map the metaphysics onto that, carefully. Who are the players and what were they actually committed to? Black's two spheres are supposed to be a counterexample to the principle: two things alike in every intrinsic and relational respect, distinguished by nothing at all. Does a gas of identical bosons instantiate that scenario, approximate it, or is it something structurally different? I want the mapping done properly rather than by analogy -- what counts as a property here, whether permutation-invariant relational properties do any discerning work, and at what point the label-swapping bookkeeping stops being a claim about how many objects there are and becomes a choice of redundency in the description, something like a gauge.

Then run it the other direction. One reason people shrug at the two-sphere world is that theres no way to tell the difference, so the principle's failure would be empirically idle. But the two counting rules don't give the same thermodynamics: different entropys, and in one case a phase transition you can actually cool a gas into. So does that mean physics weighed in on an argument philosophers had been trading for two and a half centuries -- is the empirical success of Bose counting evidence for or against the identity of indiscernibles? Or is that a straight confusion, since a counting rule is about which arrangements are distinct states and not about the furniture of the world, so you could be a haecceitist about primitive thisness and a Bose counter all the same? Give me your honest read on whether this episode confirms the principle, is an anomaly for it, or is a case the debate never anticipated, and say plainly where the theory is silent instead of making it talk.
