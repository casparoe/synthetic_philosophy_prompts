I spent thirty-four years in observational astronomy — deep galaxy counts, photometric calibration, the tedious business of knowing what your instrument can and cannot register — and retired four years ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy of cosmology seriously, and next month I present a paper at a regional philosophy-of-science colloquium. The room will hold perhaps two dozen people: Bayesian confirmation theorists, generalists in philosophy of physics, one metaphysician who works on haecceitism and modality, and two graduate students who know the Sleeping Beauty literature far better than I do. Nobody there works on anthropic reasoning as a specialty, and several regard the whole enterprise as an evasion of ordinary confirmation theory rather than a contribution to it.

My thesis is that the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption are both wrong about the quantity that matters: what enters anthropic reasoning should not be a headcount but an information-theoretic magnitude. The argument I will give runs roughly as follows. First, in my own discipline the number of objects you report is a function of your limiting magnitude; there is no instrument-independent number of galaxies, only a number conditional on what can leave a durable record. Second, by analogy, a system should count as an observer for anthropic purposes only insofar as its states can carry stable, decoherence-resistant records about the rest of the world. Third, such systems should therefore be weighted not by one each but by the information their records carry about their surroundings — and since duplicates carry redundant information, the weighting must be joint rather than additive, so that a universe containing two trillion mutually predictable copies of the same record-bearing structure receives approximately the anthropic weight of a universe containing one. Fourth, the payoff: this defuses the standard reductio against self-indication, on which a theory becomes overwhelmingly confirmed merely by postulating vast numbers of observers, because in the thought experiments used to run that reductio the extra observers are stipulated to be indistinguishable from one another, and hence redundant. I also claim, more briefly, that this handles fine-tuning evidence without committing the inverse gambler's fallacy.

I expect to be pressed hard, and I would rather be pressed by you beforehand than by them afterwards. Please prepare a question-and-answer sheet, not a defense of the view, and keep it under 700 words in total. Open with one sentence naming the objection you judge most damaging and why. Then give me six to eight objections in roughly the order they will arrive, each with three parts: (a) the objection phrased in the idiom this particular audience would use — how a confirmation theorist or a metaphysician would put it, not how an online forum would; (b) a reply of two or three sentences that I could plausibly deliver from a podium without notes; (c) a one-line flag stating where that reply is soft, or where it leans on a premise I have merely asserted. Close with the single question I am hoping nobody raises, and one sentence on what I should say if it is raised anyway.
