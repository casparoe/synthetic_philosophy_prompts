I serve as a lay trustee on the board of a regional youth orchestra's charitable trust, and I sit on the panel that appoints our salaried teaching staff and, every few years, a permanent player for the orchestra itself. Our head of recruitment has an argument I have not managed to answer. She observes that from the 1970s onwards most major American orchestras came to audition candidates behind a screen, so that the panel could hear the playing without seeing the player, and that the share of women taken on rose markedly in the orchestras that adopted screens. She treats this as the paradigm of a meritocracy: the merit is audible, the irrelevant features are concealed, and whoever wins the chair therefore deserves it. From that she draws two conclusions at once — that we should anonymise applications for every post we advertise, and that once we have done so, any remaining gap between those we appoint and those we do not is a question of desert rather than of justice, so that nothing further (outreach work, bursaries, monitoring of who applies at all, or of who is able to) is called for. I have heard people say that life is a race and that the answer is to level the playing field, and I have always suspected that this way of talking is confused, but I cannot tell where her orchestral version goes wrong, given that in that instance the screen really did appear to do some good.

Could you audit the analogy for me? I should like a mapping of the audition onto an ordinary recruitment process — what plays the part of the screen, the written score, the chair, the audition panel, the years of lessons that lie behind the few minutes of playing — and then the disanalogies, separated into those that actually bear on the two conclusions she draws and those that are merely picturesque. In particular, I want to know whether the weight of her argument rests on the second move: does screening the inputs to a competition imply anything whatever about whether the output is deserved, or is the screen doing no work at all there? And if the analogy can carry anonymised applications but cannot carry the desert claim, what is the least misleading thing to say about those who lose out — is there a better image, whether drawn from music or from elsewhere, for a contest that is conducted fairly and yet leaves its winners with no title to their winnings? Please also tell me how much I should trust the empirical premise, since I gather the blind-audition result has been reanalysed and disputed; I would rather understand what turns on it than see the whole argument propped up or demolished by a single study, and I would prefer you to be candid about what you are and are not confident of here.

Some further context, which may or may not be relevant. I am not a philosopher, though our trustees' reading group has lately been working through material on desert, and I have come away with the vague sense that a claim about desert is supposed to look backwards — a claim about what someone has already done or undergone — whereas almost everything my colleague says sounds forward-looking, a claim about what will produce the better orchestra. I may be garbling that distinction, and I should be grateful if you would set it out properly and say whether it is doing any work in her case. Assume no familiarity with the literature on my part, and no citations, please: I want the distinctions themselves, in terms I could repeat at a board meeting, together with an honest account of what the analogy cannot express and would have to be abandoned for.
