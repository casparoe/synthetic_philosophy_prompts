I serve on my town's charter review commission — nine residents, none of us lawyers or professors. I've owned a hardware store here for thirty years, and I've been keeping careful notes on how we vote, because I want to know what we've actually committed ourselves to rather than what we think we believe. Every proposal we've taken up so far has concerned choosing public decision-makers by lot instead of by election or appointment. Our record:

1. We endorsed unanimously a standing residents' oversight board for policing: twelve people drawn by stratified lot from the municipal water and sewer billing list, one non-renewable eighteen-month term, empowered to subpoena records, take sworn statements, and publish findings — with no authority to discipline anyone or to change a policy.

2. We rejected, 6–3, filling two of the seven seats on the planning commission by lot, though the selection method proposed was identical to the one above. The majority's stated reason was that planning votes settle particular people's land and are final.

3. We endorsed, with conditions, a lot-selected panel to draw the town's own council district lines: its map had to go to a townwide referendum, and anyone who had held town office in the previous ten years, or whose spouse or parent had, was ineligible to serve.

4. We rejected drawing the treasurer and the assessor by lot from all registered voters. One member said we would not let a lottery pick whoever signs the payroll, and nobody argued with him.

5. We endorsed, with conditions, a lot-selected panel to decide how to spend a fixed $500,000 carve-out from the annual budget: members could decline without giving a reason, were paid a stipend, were offered childcare and evening sessions, and the pool was weighted so that renters appeared at roughly twice their share of the electorate.

Next month's item is the one I cannot predict. A petition proposes a standing citizens' veto jury: twenty-four residents drawn by lot from the voter roll each January for a single one-year term, paid, free to decline. Within sixty days of the council passing any ordinance, two hundred signatures send it to the jury, and a two-thirds vote blocks it for the remainder of the year; the council may reintroduce it the following January. No referendum.

Tell me what we will do, and separately what we should do if we want the decision to cohere with our own record rather than with our instincts. Work out which of the five precedents is the closest analog and defend that choice. Then lay out the candidate principles that all five verdicts are equally consistent with — I suspect one of them has to do with who has a stake in the question being decided, and another with whether a body may say no but not yes, though I want the set you can actually defend from the record, including any principle that fits four of our votes and strains on the fifth. Say what each predicts for the veto jury, and then commit: pick our verdict and argue for it, and tell me plainly which principle we would be adopting on the town's behalf if you turn out to be wrong. Finally, describe one further proposal, in a sentence or two, that would split your two best candidate principles, so we could table it and watch which way the room leans. Please do not hand me a menu and leave me to choose. I want your settled view and the reasons for it, including at the points where our record is thin.
