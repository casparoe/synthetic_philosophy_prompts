The student had Hegel's *Philosophy of Right* (1821) in Knox's translation and secondary material on reserve, and was expected to have read the sections of the third chapter of Part III dealing with the state's external relations. I have not yet formed a view of the answer's quality and would rather not be led toward one, so please assess it on its merits rather than on its polish.

The question was:

> **Question 3.** Hegel's *Philosophy of Right* treats war not merely as a misfortune to be avoided but as philosophically significant. In no more than 1,200 words, explain:
> (a) the grounds on which Hegel derives the possibility of war from the concept of the state;
> (b) his criticism of the proposal for a league of states that would secure perpetual peace;
> (c) the function of the claim that war preserves the ethical health of peoples; and
> (d) whether, on Hegel's account, victory in war settles the justice of the victor's cause.
> Answer all four parts. Marks are awarded for accuracy about the argument, not for agreeing with it. (40 marks)

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

> Hegel's discussion of war is usually the part of the *Philosophy of Right* that makes readers most uncomfortable, and it is also the part where his method is most clearly visible. He does not begin from the observation that wars occur and ask how they might be prevented; he begins from the concept of the state and asks what follows from it.
>
> The state, for Hegel, is an actual individual. Individuality, he holds, essentially involves negation: to be a determinate individual is to be not-something-else, and this applies to states as much as to persons. A state that stood wholly alone would not be an actual individual at all, since there would be nothing against which its individuality was determined. Because states are individuals in this sense, they necessarily stand in relation to one another, and where those relations conflict there is no power standing above them capable of settling the conflict. His remark that there is no praetor over the state captures the point: international law, unlike law within a state, is never backed by a will able to enforce it. Treaties accordingly retain the character of an ought; each state's compliance depends on its own continuing will, and there is no contradiction in a state setting a treaty aside where its own welfare, which is its highest law, requires it. From this Hegel draws his conclusion: war is not an absolute evil, nor a mere contingency produced by the passions of rulers, but is entailed by the very concept of the state.
>
> This is also why Hegel rejects the Kantian proposal for a league of states securing perpetual peace. Such a league would presuppose an agreement among states, and an agreement of that kind is only as durable as the wills that produced it, so the guarantor of peace would be precisely the sort of thing incapable of guaranteeing anything. Peace on this scale would not be a state of rest; it would have to be continually produced and maintained by the same contingent wills that make war possible. Worse, it would be corrosive. Just as the wind keeps the sea from stagnating and rotting, so war keeps peoples from settling into a condition in which their ethical life ossifies and citizens come to regard the undisturbed enjoyment of private property as the whole of their existence. War, then, is functional: it recalls citizens to the fact that the particular ends they pursue in civil society are ideal moments of a whole which can demand their sacrifice, and in the readiness to risk property and life the substantiality of the state becomes actual rather than merely presumed. This is the real force of the ethical-health passage, and it is the reason the derivation in the previous section cannot be dismissed as a verbal trick.
>
> The position becomes more radical when one reaches world history. Since no tribunal stands above states, war is itself the mechanism by which the question of right between them is decided, and the outcome shows which of the two was in the right. That is what Hegel means in saying that world history is the world's court of judgment. Between states, might and right coincide in a way they never can within a single state, and every war is a moment at which the world spirit supersedes a people that has exhausted its principle with one carrying the next stage of its development — the schema Hegel works out in his lectures on the philosophy of history, where each world-historical people rises once and sets once, and where world-historical individuals such as Alexander and Caesar serve a purpose they do not themselves grasp. Sovereignty here has two faces. Internally it is the gathering of the moments of the constitution into the decision of the monarch; externally it is the state's independence over against other states. Only the second generates war, and it generates it necessarily.
>
> What follows is that Hegel is not, as his critics suppose, simply an apologist for militarism; he is committed to something more interesting and more uncomfortable. At the level of relations between states, the question of who was right has no answer other than the answer given by events. Anyone who wants to resist that conclusion must resist the derivation of war from state-individuality itself, and Hegel would reply that such a person is thinking not about actual states but about a fiction.
