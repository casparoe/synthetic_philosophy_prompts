I work on infrastructure at a mid-sized software company and have no philosophy training, so I'm asking this as an outsider who has noticed a pattern and cannot find a word for it.

First instance, from my own job. We had a change freeze in effect, and an engineer on call broke it to roll back a deploy that was degrading a service used by emergency dispatchers. Everyone agreed it was the correct call: the freeze existed to protect a different and smaller risk, and letting the degradation continue would have been worse on any measure we could actually produce. What struck me was the aftermath. The engineer wrote the incident report so that it stated plainly which rule had been broken and by whom, declined an offer to have the wording softened, and told me in substance that he would do it again and was not asking anyone to call it fine. His manager ratified the action retroactively and still required a formal review — not as punishment, but on the grounds that if violating the freeze costs the violator nothing, the freeze stops being a rule and becomes a suggestion.

Second instance, from some Buddhist material I have been reading on the side. There is a well-known Mahāyāna story about a ship's captain who learns that one of his passengers intends to kill the several hundred others aboard, kills that passenger himself to prevent it, and deliberately takes the karmic consequences of the killing onto himself. Some of the commentaries I have come across go further and hold that, because the motive was pure compassion, the act generated merit rather than demerit. That last move bothers me, because it seems to erase the very feature of the story I found striking — the captain's willingness to be charged for what he did. I do not know whether that erasure is essential to the tradition or a later smoothing-over, and I would like the answer to say which.

The pattern I cannot name: an agent violates a rule they themselves endorse, in a situation with no genuine dilemma, where one option was clearly best on the merits and the violation is therefore justified — and yet the justification does not wash off. Something is still owed on account of the violation; the agent accepts that it is owed; and that acceptance is treated as part of doing the thing correctly rather than as a failure to see that it was correct. Being absolved, whether by oneself or by others, counts as a further wrong.

This is not the ends justifying the means, since nobody involved is at ease with it. It is not a tragic choice between two bad options, since one option was better. It is not regret about an outcome that happened to go badly, and it is not my industry's standard blameless-postmortem posture, which is closer to the opposite.

Does this have an established name — in Buddhist ethics, in the literature on consequentialism, or in ethics generally? Please do not stop at a label. I want the nearest existing concepts set out one at a time, with the precise difference between each and what I have described; your judgment about which, if any, actually covers it; whether the view is even coherent for someone who thinks consequences settle what is right, or whether it smuggles in a non-consequentialist element; and where the edges of the phenomenon lie. Does a state-imposed fine for a justified violation count? Does an apology count? Does it survive if someone else volunteers to pay, or if the agent pays only because he was caught? If nothing established fits, say so plainly and coin a term, with a definition and boundary cases that keep it distinct from each of the near-misses above.
