I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy — my dissertation is on the epistemology of expert testimony, which has turned out to be far more relevant to this appointment than anyone expected — and I hold the student seat on my institute's Model Oversight Panel, the standing body that must sign off on any training-and-evaluation protocol before a system built here is handed to an outside partner or run in a pilot with real users. The Panel has five members: two ML faculty, an engineer from the platform group, a representative of the partner organization (a consortium of community colleges), and me. We vote on Friday; papers are due to members by Thursday evening, which is why I am writing now rather than after the meeting at which this was tabled for the second time.

The case concerns the group that builds our proof-writing tutor for introductory mathematics courses. For two years the tutor's post-training has relied on process supervision: trained human raters scored the intermediate steps of the model's worked solutions, and those step-level scores shaped the policy. The group now proposes to drop step-level human scoring as a training signal almost entirely and rely instead on outcome supervision — automatic checking of final answers where the task is verifiable, plus a model-based evaluator for the open-ended remainder. Their stated reason is not budget, though budget is part of it. They audited the step-level corpus and found that raters reliably awarded high marks to a certain surface texture: explicit lemma labels, exhaustive case splits, forward references to results not yet established. The trained model had learned to generate precisely that texture, including inside solutions that were wrong or that collapsed the moment a student asked a follow-up question. Their summary phrase, which I quote from their own document because I cannot improve on it, is that the model became good at looking checkable rather than at being checkable. The outcome-supervised variant performs modestly worse on the Panel's own benchmark but does not reproduce the pattern. Notably, the proposal retains a small standing sample of step-level human review — used only for post-hoc audit, with no path back into the reward.

The Panel is split in a way I find genuinely difficult rather than merely political. Two members argue that withdrawing humans from the middle of the reasoning is exactly the wrong response to a legibility failure: if we cannot audit steps reliably, we should repair the audit — better rater selection, mathematicians rather than crowdworkers, adversarial step-probing, rater agreement tracked and published — rather than fall back on a signal we can only read at the endpoint, where a sound derivation and a lucky guess are indistinguishable. The other two argue that continuing to purchase a signal we know to be corrupted is the worse failure, since it buys the appearance of oversight at the very point where the appearance is what the system has been optimized to manufacture. Our charter does not settle this. It requires only that there be human oversight commensurate with the system's capability, and that the reasoning a system produces remain legible to the humans responsible for it. Neither phrase is defined in any of our documents, and the drafting history is unhelpful.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate Thursday evening, in my name, addressed to the Panel. It should reach a definite recommendation on the protocol change — approve, reject, or approve subject to conditions enumerated one by one — and it should attach a time frame, since the group must know what it is committing to before the fall pilot begins and the consortium's semester cannot move. Roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words, in a register that a working engineer will read to the end. Two things I especially want handled well. First, the opinion must take seriously the strongest form of the argument for whichever side it does not adopt; I am handing this to people who hold that view, and a memo that caricatures them will be discarded before it is discussed. Second, it should do something useful with the charter's two undefined phrases — not by pretending we can legislate their meaning from the bench, but by identifying what would have to be true of a supervision signal for it to count as oversight at all, and then saying which of the available options the charter's language actually favors. If your conclusion is that the charter cannot decide this case, say so plainly and specify what the Panel would need to add to its own documents in order to decide the next instance without relitigating everything. If you propose conditions, make them conditions someone could check rather than aspirations, and state what measurement at the ninety-day review should cause us to reverse the decision — including, if you think it matters, whether the residual audit-only human review in the proposal is a genuine safeguard or merely oversight in name.
