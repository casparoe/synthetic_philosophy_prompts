**Four pramāṇas, four defeasibility regimes**

Nyāya admits four reliable means of knowledge: perception, inference, comparison (upamāna, the gavaya-in-the-forest kind of case), and testimony (śabda). Its opponents trim the list. The Buddhists keep only perception and inference, and argue that both comparison and testimony are just inference in disguise — you hear a sentence, you infer a state of affairs from the fact that a competent speaker produced it. Vaiśeṣika trims similarly, Mīmāṃsā and Advaita go the other way and inflate to six by adding postulation and non-apprehension. The debate is normally narrated as a debate about *processes*: is there a distinctive operation by which testimony generates knowledge, or is it the same operation you already find in inference, applied to a sentence?

My claim is that the interesting content of the Nyāya insistence is not process-talk at all. It is about default entitlement and about where the burden of defeating sits. A belief formed on the word of an āpta — a competent, non-deceptive speaker — is entitled by default. The hearer owes nothing: no reconstruction of a reason, no check of the speaker's track record. The entitlement is lost only when a defeater shows up (known unreliability, conflict with perception, incoherence in the sentence). Inferential belief is the mirror image. It is entitled only once pervasion is in place and no fallacious-reason diagnosis applies, and the work of establishing that is on you, up front, before the belief counts. So when Nyāya says śabda is a pramāṇa in its own right, on my reading it is saying testimony has a different default-and-defeater structure, not that there is a testimonial faculty or a sui generis causal route. "Pramāṇa" is doing taxonomic work over regimes of justification, not over mechanisms.

A case that I think makes this vivid. A man in a village has tracked a certain comet for thirty years. He tells you it will be back next spring. He cannot give you a syllogism, cannot state the pervasion, has never written anything down, and would fail if you asked him to defend his claim in a formal debate. Meanwhile a clever newcomer hands you a complete five-membered derivation with a beautiful example-step — which is quietly built on too narrow a sample. On the process reading, the astronomer looks epistemically worse off: nothing recognisable is happening in him or in you beyond uptake of a sentence. On the entitlement reading the ordering flips. The astronomer's assertion carries default entitlement, because the entitlement-conferring fact is out there in the social world — thirty years of tracking, no motive to deceive — and the newcomer's derivation carries none until you check it, and in fact carries a hidden defeater you are obliged to hunt for. The thing that decides which of the two you should believe is not which one is an instance of the right kind of mental event.

That reading buys something, but it costs two things, and I want to be upfront about both.

First, it underdetermines the number four. If a pramāṇa is a defeasibility regime, memory and instrument-readings look like candidate regimes too, and so does postulation. Nyāya's actual move is to refuse memory on the grounds that it yields nothing novel, and to deny that postulation and non-apprehension are irreducible. But "yields nothing novel" is a criterion about the content of the cognition, not about who bears the burden of defeat — so my own gloss doesn't explain why the list stops at four. The best repair I have is: a regime counts as separate only if its characteristic defeaters are not just the defeaters of an existing regime in new clothes. Testimony's defeaters (deception, incompetence, ambiguity) really aren't inference's defeaters (unestablished reason, contradictory reason, too-narrow pervasion). But that looks cheap, and it's obviously reverse-engineered from the answer I want.

Second, and worse: Nyāya holds that the truth of a cognition is known extrinsically, not from within the cognition itself — you find out whether your cognition was veridical by whether acting on it succeeds. If all four pramāṇas are *validated* in the same way, then whatever individuates them has to live on the generation side: the distinctive instrumental cause that produces the cognition. That is precisely the mechanism-talk I was trying to displace. So a Naiyāyika can say my reframing is not a rival account but a redescription of what falls out of their causal account, and it's not clear I have any answer. Relatedly, Nyāya secures the authority of the Veda by arguing for a supremely knowledgeable, non-deceptive divine author — which grounds testimony in a reliability fact about the source, i.e. in something inference-shaped. That is exactly the wedge the reductionist wants, and it's the tradition itself handing it over.

So I'm left with a fork. Either the Nyāya/Buddhist dispute is partly verbal — both sides agree about when testimony-based belief is justified, and disagree only about whether the enabling condition deserves to be called a distinct instrument — or my entitlement reading can't survive the extrinsic-validity objection. I lean toward the first, uneasily.
