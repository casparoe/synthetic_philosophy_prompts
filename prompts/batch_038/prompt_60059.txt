So, no philosophy background here beyond one required ethics class I mostly slept through — I write backend code for a living. I'm on a six-month stint at our company's office in another country, and the local conventions are way more load-bearing than I expected. Two that keep coming up: nobody jaywalks, ever, even at 2am on a totally empty street (I did it once in week one and got stared at like I'd kicked a dog), and there's this rotating thing where whoever makes coffee also washes everyone else's cups, which is in zero handbooks but everybody just does it and there's real social pressure if you don't.

Anyway, our EM killed an entire Slack thread about whether us visiting Americans have to go along with stuff like that by dropping "when in Rome, do as the Romans do," like that settles it. Then one of the local engineers pushed back with what I think is a pretty good dilemma. Either the proverb is just practical advice — blend in so people like you and the office runs smoothly — in which case it isn't a moral claim at all and it can't tell us we *have* to do anything. Or it IS a moral claim, in which case it makes the exact same act (crossing an empty street against the light) wrong here and completely fine back home, which seems insane, and it also commits us to following local practice when local practice is bad — his example was the office-wide norm of "don't file bugs against a partner team's code, just ping them on chat," which everyone here does and which has definately burned us twice. So he thinks the saying is either toothless or false, and the EM thinks he's overthinking it.

I lean toward the dilemma being right, but I have this nagging feeling there's a third option neither of them is considering, and I can't articulate it.

Can you break the slogan apart into the seperate precise claims it could be making? I want a numbered menu, and for each reading: what exactly is being claimed (who's obligated, who counts as "the Romans," what "do as they do" is supposed to cover — every local habit, or only the deliberate cooperative ones? — and whether the force is prudence, etiquette, respect, or morality), plus a verdict on each one, i.e. truism nobody could deny, substantive but defensible, or actually false. Then tell me which of those readings would genuinely have backed up what the EM was trying to do in that thread, whether any of them survives the dilemma, and which one people who say the proverb casually are most likely to have in their heads. Last bit: which reading, if any, explains why the locals get morally indignant about the jaywalking rather than just finding it weird — that's the part that still doesn't compute for me.
