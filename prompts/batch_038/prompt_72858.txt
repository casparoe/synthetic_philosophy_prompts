I am the technical authority for life-safety and emergency-response equipment across a multi-site hospital group: smoke detection, fire dampers, sprinkler heads, emergency lighting and generator start circuits, medical gas cylinder valves, escape respirators, and the fall-arrest lanyards our maintenance crews work in. Every quarter I sign a schedule asserting that each item is in serviceable condition and will operate when required, and our accreditation surveyors have begun pushing back on that wording. Their objection, as I understand it, is that we treat a passing result on a scheduled test as though it established a claim about what the equipment would do under conditions we have never reproduced. The surveyor pointed to a photoelectric head in a service corridor that had passed its monthly push-button test thirty-six times running and failed to sound during a smouldering cable fault; her diagnosis, which I think is right, was that the button exercises the horn, the battery, and the supervisory circuit but not the sensing chamber, so the thing we had been testing for three years was not the thing we had been certifying. Our quality manager's response was to propose that we define "serviceable" extensionally and stop worrying: an item is serviceable just in case it responds correctly to every stimulus on the approved test list, and nothing further need be meant by the claim. I do not believe that definition is coherent, but I have not yet been able to say why in a form my engineers will accept, and I would rather hand them an instrument than an argument.

What I want from you is a reusable decision procedure, runnable by a technician with no philosophical training, for designing or reviewing an acceptance test protocol for any claim of the form: this item will produce response R when stimulus condition S obtains. I have read enough of the literature on dispositions to know that the obvious move — treat the claim as a conditional and then go test the conditional — is contested, and that the counterexamples come in families I want the procedure to name and screen for explicitly. First, tests that remove or destroy the very disposition they were meant to detect: proof loads that fatigue webbing, heat tests that consume a fusible element, repeated actuation that drifts a calibration or empties a reservoir. Second, tests whose background conditions prevent a genuinely disposed item from manifesting: dust packed into a sensing chamber, a protective cap left in place, a battery at low state of charge, ambient temperature outside the rating, a panel left in test or isolate mode. Third, tests that an item passes without having the disposition at all, because something extrinsic supplies the work: a head that sounds only because the loop controller drives it, a valve that opens because a fitter's wrench made up for seized internals. And fourth, the structural problem that no finite list of tested conditionals is equivalent to the disposition, since there is always another stimulus condition not on the list and a ceteris paribus clause nobody can fully write down. The procedure should be built so that these four failure modes are checked in order, with a stated sufficiency criterion at each step — what answer lets the technician proceed, what answer forces a downgrade of the claim, and what answer stops the process at "cannot be certified on the evidence available."

Beyond that ordering, I need the following components. An evidence taxonomy that distinguishes a direct stimulus test from a surrogate measurement of the item's own structure (chamber geometry and contamination reading, source activity, spring force, gasket condition, webbing tensile residual strength, current draw signature) and from calculation, analysis, or in-service observation — and for each class, an explicit statement of the inference risk carried by relying on it, since a surrogate is evidence about the grounds of the disposition rather than about its manifestation. A required field in which the protocol records the gap between the test stimulus and the field stimulus it stands in for (aerosol from a can versus real smouldering combustion products, a proof load versus a live fall, a bench cycle versus installed conditions) and a rule for when that gap is too wide for the test to bear the weight of the certification. A graded rather than binary output vocabulary, so that we assert a likelihood and a validity interval instead of "will operate," together with a defensible rule for setting retest intervals that accounts both for decay of the disposition over time and for degradation caused by the test itself. Clear stopping points, and clear handoff points where the remaining question is one of acceptable risk rather than of fact, so that the technician knows when to stop reasoning and put the file in front of me. Candour about which steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing; I would rather see those flagged than see them hidden inside a threshold. Please format the core as a table with columns for the claim being certified, the field stimulus, the test stimulus, which failure mode the test is exposed to and how the protocol guards against it, what observation would falsify the claim, the evidence class, and the retest interval with its justification.

Then run the procedure on three protocols of ours, one of which must come out against us. First, the monthly push-button test on photoelectric corridor heads: tell me what that test is genuinely evidence of, whether it earns a place in the schedule at all, and what has to be added — and at what interval — before the quarterly signature is warranted. Second, lot-sample destructive testing of sprinkler heads, where we heat a sample from each batch until it operates, destroying those heads and learning nothing directly about the thousands already installed: state exactly what the sample result licenses, what would have to be measured on the installed heads instead, and whether a long-interval in-service examination can substitute. Third, proof-load testing of fall-arrest lanyards already in service, where the test plausibly leaves the lanyard weaker than it found it and where the honest conclusion may be that we should stop testing and start retiring equipment the crews are attached to and for which the budget has already allocated testing hours. If the procedure gives me an answer I do not like on any of the three, say so plainly and show which step produced it. Close with a short section stating what the procedure cannot do — the classes of claim it will never license, and the point at which it should be abandoned in favour of replacement or redundancy — and answer my quality manager's extensional definition directly, by locating precisely where "responds correctly to every stimulus on our approved list" and "will operate when required" come apart, and by saying what each of our existing tests is therefore entitled to support. I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a survey of the debate; I want the instrument itself, written so that a surveyor can follow the reasoning and a technician can execute it in about an hour per protocol.
