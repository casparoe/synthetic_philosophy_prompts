I'm a postdoc in philosophy and next Thursday I'm giving a 30-minute talk plus discussion at a hospital consciousness journal club — so not a philosophy seminar, the room is about a dozen people: an epilepsy surgeon who does the commissurotomies, two neuropsychologists who actually run the lateralized testing battery on these patients, an anesthesiologist who works on depth-of-anesthesia monitoring, a computational neuroscientist who models integration in networks, and one philosopher of personal identity who's there mostly for the clinical ethics side. Only the last one reads philosophy of mind regularly. The rest will have heard of split-brain stuff, mostly from pop science, and several of them I think have a private view that the two-minds story is overblown but won't say why.

My thesis, and I need you to take it as given rather than argue with it: after complete callosotomy there is still exactly one subject of experience, not two, and what the standard disconnection data show is not a failure of unity but a failure of *routing*. I've been calling it the channel-restriction view. Roughly the argument I'll give is:

1. What makes something one subject is not that all of its conscious contents are mutually available to each other. It's that there is a single perspective the world shows up from and a single stream of action selection answerable to it.
2. In these patients both of those survive: one body, one behavioral repertoire with ordinary priority structure, no report of a doubled world, no experience of competing narratives the way you'd expect if two subjects were sharing a skull — and integration improves over months as they learn cross-cueing strategies.
3. What's genuinely lost is that some contents are only ever available to the channel that carries speech, so the subject's self-ascriptions get generated from a restricted sample of its own mental life. That's an epistemic defect in the subject, not a second subject hiding behind it.
4. So the interpreter cases — the fluent reason produced for an action the speech-competent channel didn't initiate — aren't confabulation in the usual sense. They're honest reports from a channel that was never given the whole file. I want to say the two-minds reading commits a scope error: it treats unavailable-to-me as not-mine.

I know this view has real soft spots and I'd rather find them in prep than in the room. So: build me the Q&A prep sheet. The eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do to me, each phrased the way *they* would phrase it — the surgeon's and the neuropsychologists' vocabulary, not the way a metaphysician would put the same point — because I expect at least three of them to come at me clinically rather than conceptually: the testing conditions, what's left intact after surgery, how few patients actually show the full syndrome, whether the old cohort data can carry any of this weight. For each one give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes out loud, and a blunt note on where that reply is soft or where it quietly depends on a premise I haven't argued for. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes to buy credibility, what I absolutely cannot give up without the whole view collapsing, and then the one question I'm praying nobody asks — the one where my honest answer is that I don't know.

Also flag anything in my four-step argument that a computational neuroscientist who thinks about integration for a living is going to spot as a hidden assumption, since I suspect that's where I'm most exposed and I can't tell from the inside.
