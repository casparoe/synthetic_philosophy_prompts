I am presenting a paper next month at a two-day workshop on religion and public finance, and what I need is a question-period prep sheet rather than another defense of the view.

The paper defends a proposal I call designation funding. Any taxpayer may direct a fixed fraction of their income tax liability — I use one-half of one percent — to a religious or non-religious belief organization of their choice, with the state acting purely as collection agent for a flat administrative fee. Three features do the work. Designation is opt-in with no default: shares nobody directs remain with the treasury instead of being redistributed in proportion to the choices that were made, which is where my scheme parts company with the continental percentage-of-tax arrangements I discuss in an earlier section. Eligibility turns on content-neutral criteria — durable organization, published governance, audited accounts, an undertaking as to the use of funds — and is open to humanist and ethical bodies on exactly the terms offered to churches. And I argue that this serves anti-establishment values better than the American status quo of property tax exemption combined with the charitable deduction, because the existing subsidy is invisible, allocated by wealth and endowment rather than by adherents, and never subjected to public reason-giving about which bodies count as religious in the first place.

The argument runs as follows. (1) A subsidy whose incidence no one can observe cannot be politically checked. (2) Designation does not make the state a supporter of religion, since the state is merely a conduit for money the taxpayer would otherwise owe; the familiar Madisonian objection — that a government able to compel even a trivial contribution toward one establishment can compel conformity in everything — gets no purchase on funds the taxpayer himself directs. (3) Because allocation follows adherents rather than property, designation is more neutral among faiths than exemption is. (4) Therefore the proposal sits on the right side of the anti-establishment value rather than against it.

The room holds about fifteen people. Six are American First Amendment scholars, split roughly evenly between those who read the Establishment Clause as barring any state channeling of money toward religious bodies and those who think it forbids only coercion or denominational preference, with history and tradition settling the rest. One is a German professor of public law who works on the church tax and will not grant the analogy to it. One is a French political scientist working on the 1905 separation regime and its recent legislative reinforcement. Two are political theorists who write on accommodation for religious minorities. One is a public economist who studies nonprofit finance. There is no tax lawyer present, and no one in the room has read the paper beforehand.

Please give me: the eight to ten objections I will actually face, ordered by how much damage each would do, weighted by how likely it is to be raised; for each, who raises it and phrased in that person's vocabulary and in the register of someone speaking from a chair rather than writing an article; a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes, with a candid note on where the reply is soft — which premise it concedes, what it commits me to elsewhere in the paper, and whether it depends on a distinction that objector has already refused; a mark on each objection as to whether it engages the proposal as specified or attacks a scheme I am not advancing, and if the latter, what has been misread and whether my own framing invited the misreading; the two objections I should concede during the talk itself, before Q&A, because fighting them costs more than granting them; the single reply that has to land if the proposal is to survive the session, and the shortest route to it; and the question I am hoping nobody asks.

No bibliography, and no lists of cases or statutes. Where an objection turns on a point of law, describe the point and tell me to verify the authority rather than naming one you are not certain of.
