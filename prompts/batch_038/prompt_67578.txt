So bit of context, because this is not an abstract interest for me. My employer started running one of those internal forecasting leagues a few months back — everyone in the analytics team had to put credences on things like whether a supplier would hit a deadline, whether a project would go over budget, whether a given client would renew — properly scored, league table, the lot. I came 41st out of 44. Which is humiliating specifically because I'm the one who's actually read the philosophy: I'd spent the previous year working through the accuracy-first stuff, the whole line where accuracy is supposed to be the one fundamental epistemic good and everything else (coherence, conditionalisation, the rest of it) falls out of it as a consequence, and I was definately convinced that meant I'd at least come out middling. Instead I was the person in the room who felt most careful and scored worst, and when I asked a colleague what had gone wrong he said, without any malice at all, that I hedge like a coward and then get unlucky. I've been chewing on whether he was right ever since, and I've half talked myself into thinking he wasn't — that what actually happened is a mismatch between a truth-relative standard and an evidence-relative performance, plus a small sample. But I can't get a clean grip on it, and I don't want to lecture anyone about it either.

So: I co-run a fortnightly reading group, 14 of us, back room of a pub, 90 minutes, gloriously mixed — three philosophy postgrads, several software people, a retired accountant who keeps a spreadsheet of everything including the crisps, and one guy who does forecasting tournaments competitively and will absolutely dominate the evening if you let him. Design me a runnable activity, a real game with choices and a token payoff rather than a discussion, that gets them to feel the force of the accuracy-first package in the right order and only hear the names of the pieces afterwards. Three things I want them to run into for themselves. First, why sliding everything to 50/50 feels safe and isn't — and then the ambush, which is that this only holds for some scoring rules and not others: I'd love a round where you quietly hand them an improper rule, watch the entire table turn into overconfident monsters within about five minutes, and then show them what they did and why the choice of measure is doing all the normative work. Second, why incoherent credences are self-punishing, ideally by having a bookie take the actual numbers off their sheets and build a guaranteed loss live on the whiteboard while everyone watches, rather than me writing down an inequality. Third, the immodesty point, which I think is the bit I actually didn't understand about my own humiliation: if the measure is proper, every single one of them, reporting honestly, should expect to come first. Get them to write down their own predicted total and their predicted rank before any reveal, so they get caught out by it, and then let the results land.

Practical stuff. No real money — butter beans or choccy buttons or a tally on the board is fine. Critically, no on-the-spot arithmetic: one of the group genuinely loathes maths and I've already lost her twice this year, so please include a pre-printed score grid or lookup table they just read off, not a formula on a card. I want the minute-by-minute timeline, the exact question cards to prepare and how to choose questions so people genuinely have partial information instead of the whole thing degenerating into a coin toss, the materials list, the debrief that maps each moment of the game onto the argument it's standing in for, and an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I've never facilitated anything like this and I'm fairly nervous about the competitive forecaster, about someone sulking when they come last, and about the thing collapsing into noise. Be straight with me, too, about which feature of the view the game simply can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand: my guess is something about the tokens turning epistemic reasons into pragmatic ones, and something about variance over a handful of questions versus the idealised long run the arguments assume, but say it properly and tell me how to patch it. And please write the whole thing in American English — three of the group are American and it's easier if the handout isn't littered with words like 'maths' and 'practise'; I'll translate it back for my own notes.
