Last meeting of my social epistemology seminar next thursday is on judgment aggregation, and instead of a wrap-up lecture I want to hand the students a poem and have somebody read it out loud (wine involved, it's the end of term). Can you write it? Here's the whole setup, so the poem can just assume it rather than explain it:

Three-person departmental search committee. The rule they've agreed to: recommend the candidate iff she's strong on research AND strong on teaching.

- Ana: research yes, teaching no. Votes against.
- Bo: research no, teaching yes. Votes against.
- Chen: both yes. Votes for.

Count issue by issue and the committee recommends her (research carries on Ana and Chen, teaching carries on Bo and Chen). Count verdict by verdict and it rejects her (Ana and Bo). Same three people, same three sincere ballots, two incompatible committees.

The form is the point, so please follow this closely:

Three stanzas of four lines, one per member, with each member's two premises and their own verdict all present in their stanza. Then two more stanzas that are centos — every line in them lifted out of the first three stanzas. You may bend a lifted line's grammar or its tense to make it fit the new slot, and I actually want the bending to be noticeable, because that's precisely what a committee does to its reasons. First cento: the issue-by-issue count, which hires. Second cento: the vote count, which doesn't. They should contradict each other using nothing that wasn't already on the page. Then exactly two closing lines, which do not resolve anything.

A few things I want in there:

- Nobody in the poem is confused, careless, or voting insincerely. Make it unmistakable that the mess is in the group and not in anyone's head.
- The bit that actually keeps me up: on the issue count, the recommendation isn't backed by anybody's reasons — no single member holds both premises. The committee arrives at a conclusion with no author. I'd love that to land somewhere in the fourth stanza.
- Don't let it slide into either easy out. Not a "we should design a better voting rule" poem, and not a shrug about committees being committees. What's interesting is the squeeze: you can be responsive to what the members think, you can take the questions one at a time, and you can demand the group's views hang together — but not all three together. Let the reader feel that without the poem announcing it as a theorem.
- Mixed audience. Half of them know this literature cold (doctrinal paradox, discursive dilemma, whatever label they prefer) and half have never heard of it. It should work as a poem for the second group and as a small accurate model for the first. No philosopher names, no citations, nothing that reads like a bibliography.

Tone: wry, not jokey. Roughly 24 lines including the couplet. And if the centos come out sounding too much like the stanzas they're cut from, that's fine — that's the argument.
