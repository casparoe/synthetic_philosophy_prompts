I am a novelist, mid-draft on a book whose hinge is a single inexplicable event: one character witnesses it, alone, at night, and never tells anyone — neither in the story nor, as things currently stand, in the narration. My editor's advice is to leave it permanently ambiguous whether it happened at all. I am not persuaded, and I have been rereading A Christmas Carol in order to understand how Dickens secures assent to something far more extravagant than anything I am attempting, without ever appearing to argue for it.

What I want is a reading of the novella through Hume's section on miracles in the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding — one that takes both the fiction and the philosophy seriously, and that is equally alert to the places where yoking them together is a category mistake. I am not asking you to quote either text; describe the passages you mean, and keep the claims about Hume to what his argument actually does.

The specific questions I would like addressed:

1. Hume's case is framed almost entirely around testimony — what others report, and what we should credit on the strength of their reporting. Scrooge is not a recipient of testimony but a first-person witness. Which of Hume's moves survive that transposition, which do not, and what would have to be added to his argument before it could reach a man who has seen the thing himself and intends to tell no one? My own project depends on the answer.

2. Hume defines a miracle as a transgression of a law of nature brought about by a particular volition of the Deity, or by the interposition of some invisible agent. Does Marley's return fit that definition, or is the novella trafficking in something Hume has no clean slot for — a portent, a haunting, a visitation that violates nothing anyone inside the story treats as a law? Does the definition illuminate the text or merely fail to grip it?

3. Consider the narrator's own evidential posture. He insists, at the outset, that Marley's death be accepted as certain, on the explicit ground that otherwise nothing wonderful can come of the story he has to tell; and in the same pages he digresses on the deadness of a door-nail, conceding that he knows nothing of the matter from his own knowledge before repeating the assertion with renewed emphasis. Is this narrator performing Hume's requirement — establish the baseline of uniform experience firmly before asking anyone to credit a departure from it — or is he doing something for which Hume's vocabulary has no name?

4. Scrooge's first hypothesis about the apparition is a physiological one: his visitor is owed more to his dinner than to the grave. The novella poses the balancing question Hume poses, and then, it seems to me, declines to settle it by argument. Does it settle it by fiat, and is that a failure of the book or the whole of its method?

5. Take Hume's further considerations in turn — the number, honesty and education of the witnesses; the agreeable passions of surprise and wonder that dispose us to credit the marvellous; his claim that such reports abound chiefly among ignorant and barbarous peoples and in the early history of every nation; and the cancellation effected when rival religions each possess miracles of their own. Apply them to Scrooge: solitary, grieving, miserly, drinking alone on Christmas Eve, and passed through the whole business in a single compressed night, with no rival apparition to contradict him. Does the novella hand Hume an ideal disqualifying witness, and does that concession cost Dickens anything?

6. The fruit offered as evidence is Scrooge's reformed life. Hume's target, though, is testimony marshalled to establish a religious system, and these spirits require Scrooge to believe very little beyond the fact that he should live otherwise. Does the pragmatic upshot answer Hume, evade him, or quietly grant him the field?

Finally, I want the resistance reported honestly: where the novella is not engaged in Hume's enterprise at all, where the reader's assent to a fiction is not belief and the comparison therefore collapses, and where my framing has been doing violence to Dickens rather than learning from him. End, if you can, with what the book contributes to the debate over miracles that argument alone does not — and with whatever you think that implies for a writer trying to make a single unreported event credible without explaining it away.

Please write in British English.
