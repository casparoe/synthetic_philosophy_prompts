I am preparing a written statement of my position on the epistemology of disagreement, to be circulated in advance of a departmental reading group and then attacked in detail. Before I circulate it I want a consistency audit of the package — not a survey of the literature, not a defence of any camp, and no reading list. Here are the commitments, as I intend to state them.

1. Uniqueness. For any body of total evidence and any proposition, there is exactly one credence the evidence permits. Rationality leaves no slack: two agents with the same total evidence cannot both be rational while disagreeing.

2. The equal-weight rule. If I learn that an epistemic peer on the question at hand holds 0.2 in a proposition to which I assign 0.8, I ought to move to 0.5. Peerhood, for me, is settled by four criteria: comparable general ability, comparable honesty and diligence, access to the same evidence, and a comparable track record on questions of the same general kind.

3. No re-examination. The move in (2) must be made without going back over my own first-order reasoning on the disputed question. If I were allowed to re-run my argument and satisfy myself that it was sound, the rule would never bite; the whole point of conciliation is that I cannot settle the dispute by adjudicating the very reasoning that is in dispute.

4. Downward revision of peerhood on private evidence. I may discount an apparent peer where I hold evidence about their reliability that they lack: that they barely slept the night before, that they stand to gain from a particular answer, or that across a hundred comparable questions in the past they have turned out wrong rather more often than I have.

5. Order-invariance. What I ought to believe is a function of my total evidence alone. Two agents who end up with the same total evidence ought to end up with the same credences, however the components of that evidence reached them. I regard this as a minimal requirement of coherence rather than a substantive thesis, and I expect nobody to quarrel with it.

6. Steadfastness at the meta-level. Where the disagreement concerns the epistemology of disagreement itself — my peer endorses steadfastness, I endorse conciliationism — I need not conciliate. I may hold fast about the rule while obeying it on first-order questions, on the ground that a principle governing how to respond to evidence cannot itself be fed into that principle as one further hypothesis about which it advises me.

7. No spinelessness. Conciliation cannot drive me to scepticism. Since I conciliate only with genuine peers, and a genuine peer is by construction no less likely than I am to have got the question right, moving halfway can never carry me from a confident and well-supported judgement to a sceptical one.

One illustration I plan to include in the handout, and whose upshot I take to be obvious: I assign 0.8 to a claim; three colleagues, each of whom satisfies all four of my criteria in (2), assign it 0.05. I learn of their views one at a time. I am confident that having conciliated with all three I shall still believe the claim, with a credence comfortably above 0.5, and that nothing in the case requires me to have misjudged their peerhood.

Three things I should like from you.

(a) Go through the list and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to. For each genuine tension, state how severe it is, say precisely what would have to be given up to remove it, and offer the cheapest revision available together with what that revision costs me elsewhere on the list. Where a tension could be removed by adding a principle rather than dropping one, say what the principle would have to be and whether the rest of the list permits it. Please do not report every pair as problematic: I want to be told plainly which combinations are stable, and why, as much as which are not. I expect that at least one pair that looks like the most obvious target will turn out to be innocent.

(b) Wherever the diagnosis turns on numbers — and I suspect that in more than one place it does — show the arithmetic explicitly rather than asserting the result, including for the illustration above.

(c) Close by identifying the single commitment whose abandonment would leave the largest coherent remainder, and say what distinctive feature of my position I should lose along with it.

I have no attachment to any particular item and would rather find out now, in private, which of them cannot be kept.
