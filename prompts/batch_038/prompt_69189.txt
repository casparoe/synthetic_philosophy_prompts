Every year I get the same thing in my first-year history methods seminar, and I'm tired of it. We do a week on Hobsbawm on invented traditions and Anderson on imagined communities, and the students come out having collapsed both into a single claim — nations and their rituals are made up, therefore bogus — and then they think they're done. They never get that invented isn't a synonym for false, and they never once feel the pull of any of it. 18 of them, one 100-minute block, I can print things and there's a whiteboard, no budget and no props.

So before we touch the readings I want them to build one. Design an activity where, early in the session and without being told that's what they're doing, the group invents a ritual for some fictional organisation, and then roughly an hour later has to decide whether to keep it when somebody proposes scrapping it. The thing I want them to run into is the gap between knowing perfectly well that they made it up forty minutes ago and no longer being able to treat it as arbitrary. Ideally the rigidity shows up by itself: they discover that this invented practice is the one thing in the room that everyone shares, and they start defending it by how long its been going on rather than by what it does.

What I need from you:

- the phase structure with rough timings across the 100 minutes
- roles and rules, including who gets to be the reformer and exactly how that person is briefed before they walk in
- a small but real stake in the final vote, so that keeping the ritual costs the keepers something they'd actually be annoyed to lose. Not money, and not extra homework.
- they must not hear the phrases invented tradition or imagined community until the debrief, and the debrief shouldn't be a lecture either
- a debrief that maps particular moments from what just happened onto the features Hobsbawm picks out — the invariance, the rule-governed formality, the appeal to a past which in this case they were present for the entire length of — and onto his distinction between an invented tradition and ordinary custom, where the custom keeps adapting precisely because it's doing a job. Then the Anderson half: why the fact that they built the thing together doesn't make their attachment to it counterfeit, and what it means that they felt obliged to a set of fictional members who were never in the room at all.
- what predictably goes wrong the first time you run it and how you'd handle it. My guess is the invention phase turns into clowning and nobody takes the ritual seriously, which would sink everything downstream, but tell me if the real risk is somewhere else.
- and be straight with me about which feature of actual tradition-invention a hundred minutes in a seminar room structurally cannot produce, so I know what I have to supply afterwards by talking.

One wrinkle you may or may not be able to use: two of the students sit on the committee that runs our student union's founding dinner, which began maybe twelve years ago and is now referred to in total seriousness as an institution. If you can rig the activity so that lands on them personally, great, but don't build the whole thing around it.
