I work in the research unit of a mid-sized city government, and last autumn our department ran a two-day workshop for regional policymakers on whether municipal budgets ought to be guided by wellbeing indicators. Two of the invited speakers — a health economist and a philosopher who works on social measurement; I would rather not name them, because the exchange got pointed — argued in front of the rest of us on the second evening about national happiness rankings. I kept notes, partly out of habit and partly because I have since been asked to write a two-page memo for the budget committee on exactly this question, due at the end of the month. Neither of them conceded much and I genuinely cannot tell who came out ahead. Here is the argument as I recorded it, in six moves.

(1) The philosopher opened by saying that the World Happiness Report does not measure happiness at all. Its ranking rests on a single ladder question that asks respondents to place their lives on a scale running from the worst possible life to the best possible life, which is a request for a reflective verdict on one's circumstances, not a report of how one feels. She said the confusion is built into the title, and that plenty of people living in the countries at the top of the list would insist they are not especially happy in the ordinary sense of the word.

(2) The economist replied that this is fair as a point about vocabulary and trivial as a point about substance. The people who produce the report distinguish life evaluation from affect and say plainly that they rank on evaluation, so the objection hits the marketing rather than the instrument. And evaluation is worth having: if you want to know whether a country's arrangements are working for the people living under them, a considered verdict on one's life as a whole is at least as good a target as a tally of yesterday's smiles.

(3) The philosopher said the deeper problem is that the ladder is self-anchoring. The top of it is defined as the best possible life for the respondent, so the yardstick travels with the person holding it: someone whose idea of the best possible life is a secure job and a roof may plant herself at an eight, while someone with more ambitious standards and better circumstances says five. Add adaptation — the well-documented tendency of people in deprived situations to lower their expectations and then report satisfaction — and you get averages that are not comparable across borders. Her phrase was that the ranking is 'a comparison of incommensurables.'

(4) The economist answered that the self-anchoring worry predicts something we should be able to see: a compression of national scores toward the middle, with poor countries flattered and rich countries penalized. Instead the spread of national averages is wide, they line up closely with income, life expectancy, whether people have someone to count on, and whether they trust their institutions, and they move when circumstances move — sharply down in a country undergoing economic collapse, back up again afterwards. She said a measure that behaved the way the philosopher described would not do that, and that if the ladder merely recorded local conversational standards it would be far noisier and far less responsive than it is.

(5) The philosopher then turned to the question the workshop had actually been convened for: even granting the instrument, should a government try to top the list? She gave three reasons against. The ranking is of national means, so a country could climb it while its worst-off fifth grew more miserable. The gaps near the top are small relative to the report's own published confidence intervals, so first place is frequently a sampling artifact wearing the costume of an achievement. And once a rank becomes a target it ceases to be a measurement and becomes a managed quantity, because what determines the score is what people say to a pollster — all the more so, she added, since the report builds its score out of income, life expectancy, social support, freedom, generosity, and perceived corruption, which makes the ranking partly a measure of GDP in a subjective disguise.

(6) The economist said the first two of those are true but harmless: nobody sensible has ever proposed a rank as a policy target, and the report's authors themselves warn against treating small differences as real. The third is the interesting one, but overstated. Her counter was that the levers that move this measure are not exotic — better mental-health provision, housing security, shorter commutes, less petty corruption — and a government that pursued those for their own sake would move the ladder along with them; the possibility of gaming a survey is not a reason to refuse to improve what the survey imperfectly tracks. She closed by saying, rather uncharitably, that the philosopher's position amounts to declining to use a crooked ruler when the alternative is no ruler at all.

I would like your adjudication of this exchange, not an essay of your own on national wellbeing statistics. Please take the six moves in the order I have given them and say for each whether it holds up, is half right, or fails, and what would have to be true for it to hold. Three things especially interest me. First, does the self-anchoring objection really establish non-comparability, or is it answered by the responsiveness and covariation cited in move four — and note that covariation with objective indicators might have a common cause with the very cultural response styles the philosopher is worried about, which is not the same thing as validating the scale. Second, is move five accurate about how the score is actually constructed, or has she misdescribed the instrument in a way that matters to her argument? Third, is the economist right that nobody targets the rank, or does the existence of the list plus the annual press coverage make that denial too convenient; and is the crooked-ruler line a legitimate point or a false dilemma? Finally, tell me what both of them left out — including anything about the difference between a national mean and a distribution, or about what a city rather than a nation could sensibly do with a number like this — and say whether any of it would have changed the direction of the argument had either raised it.

You are the reader I would want for this: someone who works on the philosophy of measurement and has actually looked at how these surveys are built and analysed, not someone who has only read the newspaper coverage each spring. Think it through carefully before writing, and open with a few sentences orienting me on what the instrument does and does not claim, since the adjudication depends on that. If the strongest version of any move differs from how I recorded it, say so and judge the stronger version instead. I am not asking for sources, references, or a reading list — I have no way to check anything and would rather not be handed invented ones — only your judgment. Please write the whole reply as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, and no numbered lists, even though my reconstruction is numbered. Close by telling me how confident you are in your overall verdict, and by singling out the one point in your analysis where you are least sure that you are right.
