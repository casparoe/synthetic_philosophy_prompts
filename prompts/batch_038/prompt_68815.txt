So six weeks ago I got talked into standing for the membership committee of our regional worker co-op federation — its a volunteer job, eleven of us on a video call most months plus two part-time staff — and on Tuesday we have to rule on an application that has the committee split right down the middle. What the committee actually controls isn't prestige, it's plumbing: accredited co-ops get access to a low-interest loan fund, a group procurement deal on insurance and cloud hosting, and the right to use the federation mark in their marketing. Our constitution says an applicant has to be "substantially controlled by the people who work in it," which is about as useful as it sounds and has never been defined, because until now the only two contested cases were obvious. I should flag my own bias up front: I co-founded my co-op eleven years ago and we ran on temps and non-member contractors for the best part of two years, so I'm not the neutral referee this needs.

The applicant is a software and design firm of about 125. Eighty of them are member-owners, one share one vote, and they cap the spread between highest and lowest member pay at 3 to 1. The other 45 are salaried employees with no vote on anything, and no path to one right now: the bylaws require a capital buy-in that new hires can't front, and membership is frozen until an internal loan is paid off. Their pitch is that they need the non-member tier to scale fast enough to bid against venture-backed competitors, that the tier is transitional rather than structural, and they've offered a written four-year conversion schedule with annual milestones. Half the committee wants to admit them and take the schedule as good faith; the other half says a firm with a standing class of workers who have no voice is just the wage relation wearing a nicer logo, and that admitting them quietly rewrites our standard for every future applicant who watched this meeting. Sitting in the same pile is a much smaller application, a 12-person bakery where one long-serving worker has been offered membership twice and refused both times because she doesn't want the capital risk — and I keep feeling these two cases ought to come out differently without being able to say why, which is annoying.

It's gotten weirder because one of our members is a Mill obsessive and has been waving around Mill's late, posthumously published socialism writing — Chapters on Socialism, written near the end of his life — insisting Mill is on the applicant's side: that Mill wanted competition between worker-owned firms rather than a state takeover, that his real fear was centralized control crushing individuality and dissent, not co-ops that still have to fight for contracts, and that Mill's whole point was that the existing arrangement never got a fair trial so we shouldn't hold applicants to a purity test the incumbents were never held to. Another member says Mill is on the opposite side, because for Mill the problem is dependence — people who rely on others for their subsistence lose the standing to say no, which is exactly what a non-member tier manufactures. Draft me the decision memo I'll circulate before Tuesday. I want the bottom line in the first two or three sentences (admit, reject, or admit with conditions), then reasoning the committee can genuinely argue with, then concrete conditions if it's conditional — what changes, by when, what we'd monitor, what happens if the four-year schedule slips. I also want a general test we could apply to the next applicant rather than just a verdict on this one, and I want the bakery case settled even if that takes one paragraph. Treat the losing side's strongest argument at full strength, no strawmen. And if you bring Mill in, actually put him to work on this case rather than dropping his name as decoration — but no bibliography or reading list, I can't chase sources down. Short paragraphs are fine, bullet lists aren't, and please keep the whole thing under 700 words.
