For the past three years I have kept the books, unpaid, for a small mutual-credit scheme in the town where I live: a few hundred households and shopkeepers who extend one another short-term credit denominated in a token of our own, which we spend and re-earn rather than buy. I have no training in economics or philosophy and I am not at a university; what I know comes from doing the accounts and from reading on my own, so please correct me where I have got things wrong instead of assuming competence.

In the spring, a consultant advising the council on local economic development dismissed the scheme in a single sentence, and that sentence has been repeated back to me ever since: money is a store of value in the way a battery is a store of charge, and a token that cannot hold its charge is not money but a coupon. Because our token loses a small fraction of its face value each month if it is not spent — a deliberate design, meant to stop people sitting on it — he said we had built a leaking battery, and that anyone who accepted our tokens was quietly forfeiting what they had earned.

Reading against this, I turned to Berkeley's Querist, the long sequence of numbered questions he put to the Irish public in the 1730s about how a country could be poor in the midst of plenty. As far as I can tell he thinks the battery picture has it backwards. Money on his account is not wealth at all; wealth is the capacity to set industry in motion, and money is worth what it is worth only as a tally or counter that records that capacity and passes it from hand to hand. He seems untroubbled that the counters might be paper, provided they keep moving.

The argument over our scheme now turns on these two pictures, so I want them audited rather than described. Please give me the correspondences explicitly — what plays the battery, the charge, the terminals, the leakage, the appliance; what plays the counter, the score, the game, the players, the moment the game ends — and then say which features of each source domain the analogy quietly imports that money lacks. I am thinking of things like charge being a physical quantity that sits inside the object, decays on a schedule nobody can renegotiate, never increases of its own accord, and works whether or not anyone else agrees to it; and, on the other side, a score existing only inside a game with rules and other players, being useless once you leave the table, and not being anything you can consume. Tell me which of the disanalogies actually bear on the council's decision and which are merely picturesque.

Then tell me what each analogy licenses: whether saving is prudence or stagnation, whether a place with very little money can nonetheless be rich, whether a falling token destroys wealth or merely rearranges who works, and whether tokens require something intrinsically valuable standing behind them. Does the consultant's objection survive all this intact, or only in some restricted form — for instance as the right picture of some subset of money (reserve assets, a currency held abroad, a household's retirement fund) and the wrong picture of the subset our scheme occupies?

I would also like Berkeley's own analogy put under the same pressure, since I am leaning on it: where does the tally or counter break down — money being spendable on things that do not yet exist and with people one will never meet again, the counter's worthlessness outside its game, the fact that some counters historically win out over others, and episodes in which tokens plainly failed to stir up any industry at all. Finally, if neither picture will carry the weight, propose a better one — money as a running tab kept between people, perhaps — and say what it can and cannot support, especially about deliberately fading tokens. And if you conclude that the consultant was simply right, I would rather hear that plainly than be reassured.
