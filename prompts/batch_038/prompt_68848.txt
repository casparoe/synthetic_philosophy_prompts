I work in patient experience and quality at a mid-size hospital and I've been staring at our pain protocol for a while now and something about it bugs me, though I dont have the vocabulary for what the bug is. Here's the practice, more or less as it actually runs:

Every admitted patient gets asked to rate their pain on a 0-to-10 scale at every shift and after every intervention, and it gets charted right alongside temperature, pulse, and blood pressure — everybody calls it the fifth vital sign. There's a national patient-experience survey with an item asking people whether their pain was well controlled during the stay, and our score on that feeds into how much we get paid, so there's a dashboard with a target percentage. But then: for a patient who can't report — an ICU patient on a vent, somebody with advanced dementia, a preverbal kid — we throw the self-report out and score them on a behavior checklist instead, grimacing and guarding and restlessness and consolability. And separately, our palliative care people talk about total pain, meaning the existential and social and spiritual kind, and nobody puts that on a 0-to-10 and nobody gets dinged on the dashboard for leaving it untreated. And our chronic pain clinic explicitly does not aim at zero, it aims at function, and it will discharge someone who is still hurting. Meanwhile we're perfectly willing to buy a kidney stone patient a score of 0 with a drug we know can addict him, and we would never pharmacologically abolish the grief of a mother whose son just died, even though grief is aversive and we have the chemistry.

So: what theory of suffering does the 0-to-10 protocol presuppose? Is it committed to suffering being intrinsically bad, and to being bad in proportion to how bad it feels from the inside? Committed to the sufferer being the sole authority on how bad it is — and if so, how is that consistent with the behavior checklists, which assume pain is a neural state an outsider can read off the body? Does the fact that the target is well controlled rather than absent quietly concede that some suffering is permissible, and if so, on what principle is the line drawn, since it doesn't look like the same principle in the ICU and in palliative care and in the chronic pain clinic? I'd like you to separate out what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit comfortably, because I suspect a lot of it is licensable by several rival views of suffering and I don't want to overclaim when I present this to a committee.

Then press it against the strong version. I half-remember reading about something called the hedonistic imperative — the idea that we should be using biotechnology to phase out aversive experience altogether, not just in hospitals — and I want to know whether our protocol is a cowardly half-commitment to that view or actually something else entirely. If you did take the strong view seriously, which parts of what we currently do would it condemn, which parts would it leave untouched, and what would have to change institutionally — the scale itself, the survey, the target, the triage? And is the strong view even coherent with things we'd never give up, like letting a competent patient refuse analgesia, or the possibility that some of what we're abolishing is doing work for the person?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question back to me. American English please.
