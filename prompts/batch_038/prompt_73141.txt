I'm doing the recorded interview for our ethics center's speaker series next month. The guest is a philosopher who's spent the last several years arguing for one particular way of drawing the manipulation line, and I want questions that actually test the view instead of letting her summarize it for forty minutes.

Her account, roughly: manipulation is influence whose causal mechanism only works because the target doesn't register that its working on them. On her view what matters isn't whether the influencer bypassed the target's reasoning capacities, and it isn't the influencer's intentions or the wrong of treating someone as a tool — it's whether the mechanism is self-concealing. She explicitly denies that manipulation has to be secret in the ordinary sense (she'll point to cases where everyone knows the strategy), and she equally denies that anything short of outright deception is just persuasion. She's also written op-eds about dark patterns, ad tech, and AI companions, and she uses the account to sort which of those are genuinely manipulative.

Give me nine or ten questions in the order I'd ask them, each with a follow-up underneath keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — and where you can, one line saying what that expected answer is, so I know what I'm setting up. I want at least one question on each of these, and feel free to fold others in if they're sharper:

- the open manipulator. Someone announces the mechanism honestly — a fundraising letter that says it's leaning on guilt because guilt moves people, a companion app that discloses it's built to keep you talking — and it works anyway. Does she have to say that's not manipulation? If she says the disclosure is insincere, or that announcing it changes what the mechanism is, then press the version where the target reads the disclosure, understands it perfectly, and is moved regardless. What's left of the opacity condition at that point?

- disclosure that's public but unreadable: the ninety-page terms, the ad library nobody visits, the cookie banner. Is manipulation then a fact about the influencer, or about each individual target's awareness? If its target-relative, the same act is manipulative toward one person and not another, which would make the concept unusable for exactly the regulation she wants to inform. Get her to say whether she accepts that cost.

- the benevolent cases: a therapist's technique, a parent steering a five-year-old, a caregiver managing someone with dementia. On her account these come out manipulative. Is that a bullet she bites or a reductio she thinks I've mis-stated? If she says they're manipulative but permissible, push on whether the moral work is being done by something other than opacity — and if so, why define the term that way at all.

- manipulation by appointment. I pay a coach to shame me, I ask my partner to guilt me into the gym, I build myself a commitment device. I know exactly how it works and I authorized it. What resources does the account have here, and what's left of opacity once standing consent is on the table?

- true but strategically deployed reasons: ordering, timing, emphasis, one vivid anecdote sitting next to accurate statistics. Where does she put that? Does her line swallow ordinary teaching and advocacy, or does it let the classic misleading-but-technically-true campaign ad off the hook?
