I sit on the ethics advisory board of a regional health authority covering a catchment of roughly nine million people, and next month we vote on whether to retain a new adjuvant in the routine childhood immunisation schedule. The technical staff have given us two figures, and the whole dispute turns on them. Over the forty years the schedule is expected to run, about 61 million doses would be given; on the manufacturer's data, essentially every recipient would experience a single wave of nausea lasting about four seconds, usually forgotten within the hour. Under the old formulation, by contrast, the same period would be expected to produce two cases of a permanent, treatment-resistant pain syndrome that begins in adolescence and does not remit for the rest of the patient's life. Nobody on the board disputes that the syndrome is incomparably worse than four seconds of nausea for the person who suffers it. What we cannot agree about is whether 61 million four-second waves amount to more than two such lives, and last Thursday's meeting collapsed into precisely that question, with both sides accusing the other of not really hearing the argument.

My wife is eleven weeks pregnant with our first child, who would fall in the earliest cohort under either option, so this is not an abstraction for me; and I have been asked to prepare a short reading for the four members who joined the board this year and were absent on Thursday. I would like that reading to be a dialogue, because the minutes make both positions look far weaker than they are. Please write it: two of our members — one who holds that the nausea aggregates and the switch should be made, one who holds that it does not and the old formulation should be kept — alone in the committee room after the chair has adjourned the meeting, each trying in good faith to move the other before the vote. Both should be at the top of their form. The aggregative member should not be a cold arithmetician who has never noticed that persons are separate, and the anti-aggregation member should not be a squeamish rule-worshipper who cannot explain why numbers never count for anything. They have read each other's position papers, so neither should be surprised by the obvious lines, and I would like each to meet the other's best move rather than the version that is easiest to dismiss.

To keep the exchange from turning into a well-rehearsed recital of the standard dilemma, have them work through a variant our staff floated on Thursday, which neither of them had expected. Because the four-second nausea is so widely distributed, an alternative design confines the burden to the roughly two million adults who bring children to the clinics and who derive no benefit from the vaccine themselves: a prophylactic given at the door, producing about two minutes of nausea per adult rather than four seconds per child. The totals are very nearly the same — the concentrated scheme comes out at around four million minutes, the dispersed one at a little over four and a half — so the two members should find themselves disagreeing about a choice that their arithmetic says is close to a tie, and each should press the other on what, if not the total, is doing the work. That variant also brings in consent and the fact that these adults are being burdened for someone else's sake, which cuts in a direction neither of them wants. I would like the dialogue to reach at least one genuine concession and at least one moment where an argument that sounds decisive gets defused, and I would like it to end somewhere honest: either a crux that is now clearly visible to both of them, or a narrowing of the disagreement that leaves the vote still genuinely open. Around fifteen hundred words. Have them argue from the substance rather than from authority — no trading of names, book titles, or case citations, since the new members would not recognise any of them, and I would rather the reasoning stand on its own.
