I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a feature of about four thousand words on the argument that keeps flaring up inside evolutionary biology over whether the modern synthesis needs to be replaced. Two threads are in my notebook. The first I picked up from a guppy researcher I interviewed in Trinidad: the extended-synthesis crowd — niche construction, developmental bias, plasticity-led evolution — whose pitch, roughly, is that organisms are not merely objects of selection but partial authors of their own selection pressures, bequeathing modified environments alongside modified genomes. The second is a set of philosophers who argue that natural selection does not name a causal force at all but is a statistical summary of what happens to a heterogeneous population, which would imply that a good deal of causal talk in biology is aimed at the wrong target. My problem is that Karl Popper gets invoked by both camps, occasionally within the same article, and almost always via the line he wrote in the mid-seventies in *Unended Quest*: "I have come to the conclusion that Darwinism is not a testable scientific theory, but a metaphysical research programme—a possible framework for testable scientific theories." Far less quoted is how much of the rest of his life he spent retreating from it; by 1978 he was writing, "I have changed my mind about the testability and logical status of the theory of natural selection; and I am glad to have an opportunity to make a recantation." And alongside both sits his remark in *Conjectures and Refutations* that "There exists no law of evolution, only the historical fact that plants and animals change, or more precisely, that they have changed." Popper died in 1994, so neither of my two threads was mature enough for him to see. My editor wants the piece to have a spine rather than a literature review, and I've decided the spine is Popper — the man who both delivered and then withdrew the most quotably devastating objection to Darwinism ever written by a philosopher of science.

So what I want from you is a prediction, argued rather than asserted: what would Popper have made of these developments had he lived another twenty-five years? To be clear, I don't want a survey of his philosophy of biology, and I don't want the reflexive move of concluding that he'd have embraced everything that flatters falsificationism. Work it as a situational analysis of a dead philosopher — reconstruct his problem situation, not his personality. Concretely: (1) Which of the two threads would he have taken more seriously, and would he have read the statisticalist position as vindicating his old tautology charge, as dissolving it, or as something he'd have attacked from the other direction for conceding too much and explaining too little? (2) Would he have judged the extended synthesis a progressive or a degenerating metaphysical research programme, and by what criterion — keeping in mind that he graded such programmes on the fertility of the problems they generate rather than on their truth, which cuts both ways here? (3) Does the synthesis's ambition to state general principles about evolution collide with his flat denial that any law of evolution exists, or does that denial only rule out laws of historical direction while leaving laws about mechanisms intact? (4) His own evolutionary epistemology cast every organism as a problem-solver running trials and eliminating errors, with human beings singled out by their capacity to let conjectures die instead of themselves: would niche construction have looked to him like the generalization he always wanted, or like a collapse of the very distinction between an organism's active problem-solving and the blind sieve of selection? And given how firmly he rejected Lamarckism, would plasticity-first evolution — a novel phenotype appearing before the genetic accommodation that consolidates it — have struck him as Lamarckism in respectable clothes? (5) Of all this, which single judgment would he most likely have gotten wrong, in the sense that subsequent work has settled the matter against him?

Two ground rules. First, I have no way to check anything you tell me, so please don't send a bibliography and don't attribute a position to a named living author unless you're genuinely confident of the attribution — describe the views instead, and if you need to gesture at a paper, characterize what it argues rather than inventing a title. Be explicit throughout about which parts of your reconstruction are anchored in something Popper actually wrote and which are extrapolations a Popper scholar would contest; I'd rather have a marked-up conjecture than a confident pastiche. Flag as well where you suspect my framing has already loaded the dice — if you think the two threads I chose are not the ones Popper would have cared most about, say so and tell me what he would have latched onto instead. Aim for something between a thousand and thirteen hundred words, and close with a short paragraph on your own confidence: which of your claims about his likely views you would defend in print without hedging, which you'd hedge, and the one claim you are least sure of — the one you'd retract first if someone who knows the corpus better than you pushed back.
