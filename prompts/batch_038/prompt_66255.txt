I am the archivist responsible for description at a county historical society, and our board has asked me to supply a written statement of descriptive principles to accompany the policy manual we are rewriting this year. I have been drafting it by writing down what I actually do and then generalizing, and the result worries me: I have defended each item separately at various points over fifteen years, but I have never laid them out side by side, and I suspect I have a list rather than a position. The collection that forced the issue is the records of the county infirmary, 1868 to 1929 — admission registers in which most inmates appear only as a number, one column that sorts them by race in the administrative vocabulary of the period, no names at all recorded for the people entered in that column, no separate register for them among the surviving papers, the superintendent's annual reports to the county commissioners, and a photograph album whose captions are in his hand. Here is what I currently hold. (1) The catalog entry should say what a record is and what it says, not what it means; our description should be the one part of the repository's output that a researcher of any politics can rely on, with neutral tone, the least loaded vocabulary available, and interpretation left to the scholarship that cites us. (2) The language we inherit is not innocent: titles, series labels, and subject headings handed down from creators and from earlier staff were chosen by people who had standing to name others and no standing to be named, and when we transmit them without comment we reproduce the register's point of view as though it were the world's. (3) Where an item bears an original title or label, we transcribe it as it stands and flag it as transcribed, and we do not modernize or bowdlerize it, including where the wording is a slur or a demeaning designation. (4) Absence is part of the record: where a group appears only obliquely or not at all, the description should register the gap and give the best available account of why it is there, rather than letting a researcher conclude that these people did not exist. (5) Where the records concern an identifiable community that still exists, that community has authority over the terms we use for it in our description; we ask its members what they call themselves and we adopt it. (6) Arrangement follows provenance and original order; we do not re-sort a creator's files to match how we now carve up the subject, and we do not build synthetic collections out of material pulled from several fonds because a present-day constituency finds that arrangement more legible. (7) Description is infrastructure: entries carry persistent identifiers and get quoted in print, so someone who cited our description three decades ago should be able to find what they cited, which means we keep superseded wording in a version history but change the current entry's substantive claims rarely and reluctantly.

What I want is an audit of this set, not a lecture on the literature and not a bibliography. Tell me which of these can be held together and which cannot, and please discriminate rather than flag everything: some of these only look like they conflict, and I would rather be told that plainly, with the distinction that dissolves them, than have the whole set treated as equally unstable. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, identify precisely what has to give, and say what giving it costs me — including whether the cost falls on the infirmary registers specifically, since that is the collection I have to describe next quarter. I would especially like to know whether the principles can deliver a description of the people who appear in the race column as numbers and in no other form, because that is the case I started from and I no longer trust that my list reaches it. Then tell me which single revision would buy the most coherence across the whole set, and what I should expect to lose by making it. I deliberately am not telling you which commitments I would be most reluctant to abandon; I suspect that reluctance is itself part of the problem, and I do not want the audit shaped around it.

One further thing. For each tension you identify, say how confident you are that it is real rather than an artifact of the way I have phrased the commitment, and note where a different phrasing of mine would make the conflict disappear. At the end, single out the one claim in your audit that you are least sure of, and say what consideration would settle it either way.
