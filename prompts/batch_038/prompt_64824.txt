I convene a small reading group in the philosophy of cosmology, and for the last three terms its de facto arbiter has been a colleague of mine — I shall call her H., a philosopher of probability who came to cosmology late and has no formal physics training, which the group finds liberating rather than limiting. Each week one of us brings a toy cosmology, described in words rather than equations, and H. pronounces on it in a fixed two-term vocabulary: a model is either *acceptable*, meaning it could be believed as it stands, or *ruled out*, meaning it must be modified or abandoned rather than merely distrusted. She has never issued any third verdict, never withheld, and never, so far as I can tell, wavered. The subject matter for the whole term has been fluctuated observers — self-aware systems assembled by chance out of a thermal or vacuum background rather than by stellar evolution — and what a cosmology's commitment to them does to its standing. Next week the case is mine to propose, and I should like to predict her ruling before I hear it, both for the pleasure of it and because I want to know whether I have correctly identified the principle she is running, or whether I have merely memorised a list.

Her record, as near as I can render it: (i) ΛCDM extrapolated to its asymptotic future — a cosmological constant, an empty de Sitter phase with a horizon at nonzero temperature, running without end — is **ruled out**, flatly, since over unbounded time the horizon assembles fluctuated observers in numbers that swamp every civilisation that ever evolved. (ii) The same universe, except that dark energy is a slowly rolling field that eventually decays and the cosmos recollapses some twenty billion years hence rather than accelerating forever, is **acceptable**; she said she would happily live in it. (iii) A time-symmetric cosmology in which entropy increases away from a low-entropy moment in both temporal directions, so that no global equilibrium state ever obtains and no fluctuation regime is ever reached, is **acceptable**, and she is impatient with those who dismiss it as extravagant. (iv) A small universe containing one galaxy, one civilisation of a billion souls, and, exactly once in its entire history, a single momentary fluctuated subject furnished with apparent memories of a life much like yours, is **acceptable**, though she allowed that the subject's predicament is genuinely unfortunate. (v) A universe in which evolved observers number ten to the hundredth power and fluctuated ones only ten to the tenth, but in which the fluctuated ones are the only subjects whose apparent records form a coherent, lawlike, scientific civilisation — the evolved ones being scattered, confused, and mutually inconsistent, and the fluctuated ones being long-lived and seamless, so that nothing available from inside could tell the two kinds apart — is **ruled out**, and would be, she said, even if the disparity of numbers were a million times greater in the other direction. (vi) A metastable vacuum that decays by bubble nucleation long before fluctuations could assemble anything in our own pocket, embedded in a wider multiverse containing other, permanently stable vacua in which fluctuated observers arise without limit, is **ruled out**, on her insistence that one must reckon with every observer the whole theory posits and not merely with those in the neighbourhood one happens to occupy. (vii) A universe that spends essentially all of its history in equilibrium, populated only by fluctuated subjects, with no stars and no evolution anywhere, is **ruled out**, which she considers trivial and I consider a useful control.

The case I intend to bring was built by a doctoral student of mine, and it has four features. First, it is an eternally inflating multiverse: pockets nucleate without end, and in infinitely many of them conditions permit stars, planets, and evolved civilisations, so that genuine observers are infinite in number. Second, every pocket eventually empties into a de Sitter phase with a horizon at nonzero temperature, and so every pocket also generates fluctuated observers without limit — not isolated brains this time, but complete flicker cosmoses, whole spacetime regions assembled at once whose inhabitants experience what seems to them to be billions of years of stellar history, fossils, and a microwave background supporting precisely this multiverse theory. Third, the flicker cosmoses are momentary: each decoheres in the instant of its appearing, so that its inhabitants never in fact have a next moment of experience, whereas genuine pockets persist indefinitely and their inhabitants' experience continues to run in an orderly way. Fourth, the theory fixes no preferred regularization: under a global time cutoff the flicker cosmoses outrun the genuine ones past any ratio you care to name, while under a cutoff tied to causal diamonds, or one that counts pocket by pocket, the genuine observers win. Predict H.'s ruling. In doing so, lay out the candidate rules her seven verdicts leave standing — I can think of a dominance rule keyed to raw observer counts across the whole theory, a rule keyed to the conditional likelihood of records like ours, a rule about whether a theory undercuts the very evidence that recommends it, a rule that refuses to rule anything out absent an observational difference, a rule that counts only one's own pocket, and a rule on which a theory is condemned only if the fluctuation problem arises on every admissible measure — together with any I have missed; say which verdict in the list kills which candidate, and note where a candidate survives only by collapsing into another. Then give me your best guess at her ruling with the reasons for preferring it to the live alternatives, and tell me whether the honest prediction is instead that she withholds, in which case explain what in her record entitles you to a verdict type she has never once issued. Name one further toy cosmology, and its verdict, that would most cleanly separate the surviving candidates. And if you conclude that her seven rulings genuinely underdetermine the eighth, say so plainly, and identify which of the seven would have to have gone the other way for the case to be decided, together with the principle she would thereby be shown to hold.
