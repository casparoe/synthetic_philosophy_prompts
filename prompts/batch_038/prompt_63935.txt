so here's a thing I need a referee for. I've got no education in any of this — left school at 16, work in a hardware shop — but I've been reading old philosophy books on my own for a few years, mostly whatever turns up at library sales. Last month I picked up a battered translation of Rousseau's long letter arguing against letting Geneva open a theatre, the one he wrote back at d'Alembert, who'd said in his encyclopedia piece that a theatre would be good for the town. I lent it to my mate Ken, who's in the same amateur drama group as me, and he passed it to Elsie, who directs us. They went at it after rehearsal for the best part of an hour and I sat there taking notes on my phone becuase I honestly couldn't tell who was ahead. Roughly how it went:

1. Ken said Rousseau's main point still stands and nobody's ever answered it: you sit in the dark and cry over some invented family losing a child, and the crying feels like you've done your duty, so you walk home past the real family two streets over without a second thought. Theatre, he said, is a machine for turning pity into a ticket stub.

2. Elsie said that proves far too much — by that logic a sad song, a novel, or a friend telling you her mum's in hospital are all immoral, since they all stir up sympathy that could get used up on the spot. And Rousseau himself wrote an opera that got performed in front of the king and a novel that had the whole of Europe sobbing. So either he's condemning every story ever told or he's a hypocrite, and neither one saves the argument.

3. Ken said he isn't against stories, he's against the setup: you pay, you sit still, and someone whose job it is to fake feelings fakes them at you. One-way traffic. His own alternative was the open-air dance and the village fete where everyone's in it and nobody's handing money to a professional to be somebody else. That's the line, and a story between friends falls on the right side of it becuase it ends in you actually doing something.

4. Elsie said then the line's dead, since our little group is exactly what he'd have to approve of and he'd have no grounds left: nobody gets paid, the takings go to the food bank, half the audience knows someone on stage. And his fetes are spectacles too — a crowd in a square being arranged and worked up by whoever chose the programme is still a crowd being engineered into feeling something, just with a different director. So either he objects to being moved as part of an audience at all, which nobody can actually live by, or he's really just saying some plays are bad plays, which is a review and not an argument against theatre.

5. Ken said no, the deepest bit is about the actor: a person taking money to be somebody else in public, selling his own self, which is why Rousseau thought the trade degrades the person doing it no matter what's being performed. Nothing to do with content.

6. Elsie said fine, but that lets us off completely, and it also indicts a priest, a teacher, a barrister and anyone who performs a role for a wage, so it's stopped being a theory of theatre.

Then Ken said she'd quietly shrunk the whole thing down to whether Rousseau would approve of them, which wasn't the question, and she said he'd blown up a grumpy eighteenth-century rant into a theory of everything. Neither budged.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each of the six, rather than just who won overall? Two extra things: I only read one translation and skimmed chunks of it, and they were both arguing from memory, so please flag anywhere one of them has put something into Rousseau's mouth that he didn't say, or walked past a piece of his actual case that would have settled one of those points. And lastly — is there any version of his argument against theatre that survives everything Elsie threw at it, or is it just dead?
