Spent basically the whole of yesterday arguing with my friend Sam about Malthus and we got nowhere, and now the line he kept using is stuck in my head. His slogan, which he must have said twenty times: "Malthus was right, he was just early."

What made it impossible to pin down is that he used it two different ways and I couldn't seperate them at the time. When I brought up the demographic transition — fertility fell on its own once kids stopped being free farm labour and once child mortality dropped, and per-capita food output has risen faster than population for two centuries now — he said that's not a refutation at all, the constraint just hasn't bound yet, it will. Then ten minutes later, when I said fine, so it's a claim that can never be caught out, he pointed at England: real wages basically flat from about 1790 to the 1840s while output per head took off and population ballooned, so the mechanism was visibly running exactly when he said it would. The first use makes the theory unfalsifiable-in-practice, the second says it already got confirmed. Those can't be doing the same work, but I couldn't say why.

So: take that one sentence apart. What are the distinct precise claims "he was right, just early" could be making in this case, from the near-trivial ones through the substantive ones to the ones that are false or just uncatchable? For each reading I want (i) what exactly is being claimed to have been right — the doubling-every-25-years number he lifted from American colonial growth? the geometric-versus-arithmetic asymmetry? the misery-and-vice conclusion? the underlying feedback mechanism itself? — (ii) whether that's actually true, and (iii) what evidence could ever show it false. Then say which of the readings licenses the two moves Sam made.

Three things I want a real answer on rather than a shrug:

1. For the "the mechanism is genuine but its trigger conditions haven't obtained yet" reading to be a legitimate scientific move instead of an epicycle bolted on afterwards, what would have to be inside Malthus's own theory? Does he give you any independent handle on when the constraint binds versus when it's slack — anything that says what would have to be exhausted, or what would have to stop improving — or is "early" available forever no matter how much counter-evidence piles up?

2. He rewrote the book substantially between the 1798 edition and the later ones, and the later versions lean much harder on delayed marriage and moral restraint as a check rather than on famine and disease. Does that cut for Sam (he refined it, that's just how theory-building goes) or against him (he quietly relocated the prediction so nothing could hit it)?

3. Is the English wage data even Malthusian evidence? Flat real wages while output per head rises looks to me more like a distributional story about who captured the gains than like population pressing hard on the food supply. If that's right, does his best piece of confirming evidence actually confirm anything?

I don't want a balanced survey of the Malthus debate, I've read that a hundred times. Give me the readings as a numbered menu with a verdict attached to each, then commit: say plainly which reading, if any, Sam is entitled to given what he cited, and whether his two uses can stand together or one of them collapses under the other. Last line must start with "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of: true-on-a-reading-he-isnt-using / defensible-but-hollow / unfalsifiable-rescue. Pick one, don't split the difference.
