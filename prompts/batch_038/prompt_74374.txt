I am preparing a short statement of my position on belief revision under uncertain evidence for a formal epistemology reading group, and before I circulate it I would like a consistency audit. These commitments were arrived at separately, from different directions — some from the philosophy of perception, some from the technical literature — and I have not systematically checked them against one another. Here they are.

(1) Fallibilism, in a strong form: no experience can render me certain of any contingent proposition. Part of what moves me is the familiar argument that if experience were permitted to produce certainty in such a case, every prior probability I assign to a contingent hypothesis would already have to be 0 or 1, and I am not willing to pay that price.

(2) Jeffrey conditionalization is the correct general rule of revision; strict conditionalization is merely the degenerate case in which one cell of the partition is driven to 1.

(3) There is no such thing as my total evidence conceived as a single proposition that I could condition on. That denial is a large part of why I favour (2) over the textbook rule.

(4) The rigidity condition is what makes an update determinate: an experience shifts my probabilities across a partition, and my conditional probabilities given each cell of that partition remain exactly as they were.

(5) Evidentialism about revision: the only thing that may move my credences is evidence. A change in my probability function that is not a response to something I have learned is not rational, however coherent it may be.

(6) Order-independence: two pieces of uncertain evidence should leave me in the same state whichever order they arrive in. A rule whose output depends on the sequence in which the world happens to hand me information is tracking my biography rather than my evidence.

(7) A case verdict I want preserved. A bolt of cloth in a shop, examined under a skylight. Beforehand my credence that it is teal is 0.4. Looking at it, I move to 0.7 — a genuine perceptual update of the kind (2) and (4) are meant to model, and I stand by it as rational at the time. Minutes later the clerk mentions that the skylight glass is heavily tinted yellow. I take it as a datum, not a hunch, that I should end up back at 0.4: the whole excursion was an artefact of the light, and my original figure was already my estimate for the colour under ordinary viewing conditions. A rule that parks me at some other number has misdescribed what happened.

(8) Old evidence is not a problem for me. Since, by (1), my credence in the perihelion data was always below 1, nothing in my system blocks those data from confirming general relativity once the theory arrives; the confirmation is just ordinary updating, and I need no special machinery for it.

(9) I am a subjectivist about priors and an objectivist about the rule: coherence is the only constraint on where I begin, but there is exactly one correct way to move from there, and I have stated it in (2) and (4).

What I want is an audit rather than a tutorial. Tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do; I expect that some pairs will appear to conflict and dissolve on inspection, and I would rather be told so explicitly than have everything flagged. For each genuine conflict, show the mechanics — run the update, or sketch why it cannot be run — instead of asserting that a problem exists, and then say what would have to give and what the concession costs me. Rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and close by telling me which single revision would buy the most consistency per unit of damage. If a conflict can be dissolved by reinterpreting one of the commitments rather than abandoning it, say so and give me the reinterpretation.
