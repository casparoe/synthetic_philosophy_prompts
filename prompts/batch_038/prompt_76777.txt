I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy, working in social epistemology, and next month I am giving a forty-minute research talk in a law school's institutions-and-public-law seminar series. I would like a Q&A preparation sheet aimed at that room specifically, not at a philosophy audience that already knows the technical literature.

My thesis has two halves, and I will state it as directly as I can so that the objections have something to bite on. First, when a collegial fact-finding body — an appellate panel, a regulatory commission, an administrative tribunal — arrives at a judgment, that judgment is a genuine judgment about the world rather than a convenient way of talking about the members' votes; but it is not a judgment of any entity with psychology independent of its members, since it is wholly grounded in the members' attitudes together with the inferential structure the institution preserves among them. I intend this as a middle course between strong group-agent realism and the view that collective ascriptions are nothing but shorthand. Second, for bodies of this kind, premise-first aggregation is mandatory rather than merely permissible: voting only on the conclusion discards the inferential structure that gives the output its content, and it can produce outcomes that contradict what every member would have reasoned to, or that no member reasons to at all. Hence my institutional prescription — decompose the findings, take the vote issue by issue on the premises, let the conclusion fall out, and publish the reasoning as the record. Two corollaries I plan to defend openly, and which I expect to attract fire: a collegial body can be wrong in ways in which no individual member is wrong; and a judge may be under a duty to accept the collective conclusion even when it contradicts what her own reasoning supports, where accepting is not the same thing as believing.

The seminar is not a philosophy audience. As I understand the room: two doctrinal scholars working on constitutional or administrative law, who think in terms of institutional settlement, deference, and reasoned decision-making as a legal requirement; two empirically minded political scientists who study judicial behavior and are broadly skeptical that written opinions report anything other than post-hoc rationalization of prior commitments; one law-and-economics professor who does know formal social choice and will be the only person present who has actually read the judgment-aggregation literature; a couple of philosophy graduate students whose interests run to legal legitimacy; and a practising regulator who sits on multi-member panels and will judge everything by whether it could survive a real meeting.
