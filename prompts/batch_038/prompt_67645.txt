I'm six weeks into a jurisprudence seminar that did the Hart–Devlin exchange on the legal enforcement of morals, and I have to write my final paper on it, and I've hit a wall. The original occasion was a proposal to decriminalise homosexual conduct between consenting adults in Britain, and the positions look simple enough: Devlin says a society is more than a collection of institutions and contracts, that it's held together by a shared moral fabric, that if you let enough of that fabric rot the society itself comes apart, and so the state has at least a prima facie right to punish conduct that is grossly immoral even where nobody is harmed by it. Hart says the disintegration thesis is either an unfounded empirical generalisation with no evidence behind it, or a conceptual sleight of hand where 'society' gets defined so that any change in its morals counts as its death — and either way it can't carry the weight Devlin puts on it. Hart also charges him with sliding between the morality people happen to hold and the morality that is actually right, and with using a test for identifying the shared morality — the gut feelings of revulsion, indignation and disgust of the ordinary reasonable person, the sort you'd find in a jury box — that cannot tell a genuine moral consensus apart from a panic or a prejudice. On paper thats a clean kill.

Except it isn't, and my reading group is living proof. Three sessions on this now: everybody agrees about the empirical facts of every example we throw at it — cannabis, paying for sex, commercial surrogacy — everybody agrees that state coercion needs a warrant and that mere dislike isn't automatically one, and yet we split down the middle every time and nobody budges an inch. Half the group says the Hartian side is just refusing to see that the criminal law does something other than prevent harm, that it declares and teaches, that a ban on buying sex isn't only an instrument for reducing trafficing but a statement about what kind of community we're claiming to be. The other half says that's Devlin with better vocabulary, and that 'declaration' quietly reimports the contested premise. My own hunch, which I cant defend yet, is that this isn't a substantive disagreement at all: partly verbal, since the two camps mean different things by 'harm' and 'society' — one camp's harm includes the degradation of what a community stands for, the other's excludes it by stipulation — and partly a fight about who carries the burden of proof once coercion is on the table. But I might be wrong about both.

So diagnose it for me. Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one, a hidden empirical question (does the disintegration thesis have any testable content at all, and would we recognise the evidence if we saw it?), or two camps answering different questions under one heading — 'may the state punish harmless immorality?' versus 'is harm prevention the only thing a criminal law can express?'. Work from what each side actually argues rather than surveying who weighed in afterwards, and skip the bibliography, I have more than I can read. Tell me which of my two candidate diagnoses survives, or give me a better one, and then say what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the impasse, an empirical finding that would settle it, a change of question — or a reason to think no progress is available and this is just what happens when reasonable people disagree about the point of the criminal law.
