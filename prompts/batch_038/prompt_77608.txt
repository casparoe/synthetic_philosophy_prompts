I teach high school bio and I also run a little pond-water/microscopy meetup on weekends, and I've been keeping notes for a couple years on how every "is X actually alive?" argument goes. They all go the same way and it drives me nuts, and I can't find a word for the specific thing that bugs me.

Somebody asks about a virus, or a brine shrimp egg that's been dormant in a drawer for a decade, or a prion, or one of those endosymbiont bacteria that's thrown away most of its genome and can't exist outside its host cell. Somebody else produces the list: metabolism, growth, response to stimuli, reproduction, homeostasis, adaptation over generations. Then they run the thing down the list and hand back a verdict. Great.

The part I keep noticing is what happens after. You can always find something everyone agrees is plainly alive that fails the list — obligate parasites that never reproduce on their own, seeds and spores with no measurable metabolism, mules, sterile worker ants. So the list gets patched, and every patch sounds reasonable in isolation. Reproduction becomes "reproduction, or reproduction by its lineage." Metabolism becomes "at some stage of its life cycle." Fine, I get it, no complaint there.

But then the patched list never gets pointed at anything that wasn't already in the pile of examples it was patched to accommodate. Nobody ever says "right, so with this version of the list, here's what we'd expect to find about some case we haven't examined." It just shadows the sample. And years later when something genuinely new turns up — a minimal-genome synthetic cell, a self-replicating RNA in a droplet, one of those little frog-cell robots — the reflex is to check the list, and if the verdict is uncomfortable, to say the case wasn't really what the list was for.

So the list can't come out wrong about anything. But it isn't the normal kind of unfalsifiable, because nobody is dodging an attack. There is no attack. The list was assembled by summarizing living things, and it then gets quoted as though it told you something about life that you didn't already have from summarizing living things. And the people doing it are smart and completely honest and would be insulted to be told they were cheating, and I don't think they are cheating, I just think the move has a shape and I don't know what to call it.

Is there a name? Closest I've got, none of which quite fit: overfitting, but that's about a model predicting new data badly and here there aren't predictions to be bad; ad hoc rescue, but that requires a theory under fire and nothing is under fire; circular definition, but the list isn't literally circular, every item on it is an independent fact about the organism. It's more like the criterion is guaranteed to match its own sample and then that match is treated as though it were a finding.

I don't just want the label, I want the carving. Is this recognized anywhere — in philosophy of science generally, or specifically in the long-running argument about whether "life" can even be defined? What's the nearest established concept, and at exactly what point does my description stop matching it? Is it actually a defect, or is this just what a descriptive definition of a messy natural kind looks like when it's done honestly? And if nothing fits, coin something and tell me where its edges are — like, does a definition of water escape this, or a chemical element, and if they do, what do they have that "life" doesn't?
