The question I set:

Goodman floats the predicate "emerose": something is emerose just in case it is either an emerald examined before some fixed time t or a rose not examined before t. Every emerald anyone has examined so far has been an emerose. (a) Explain why this is a problem for an account of confirmation on which a general hypothesis is confirmed by its positive instances. (b) Explain how entrenchment is meant to fix the problem, and why Goodman thinks it doesn't just collapse into saying that green carves nature at the joints. (c) A classmate argues that since entrenchment is only a fact about which predicates a community happens to have been using, Goodman is committed to holding that a community could entrench "emerose" overnight by decree, and so that he is a relativist about induction. Evaluate this.

The student wrote:

(a) The trouble is that one and the same body of evidence supports hypotheses that disagree about the future. All the emeralds examined so far have been green, and that confirms "all emeralds are green" if confirmation is just a matter of instances. But those very same emeralds were also grue, so the same evidence confirms "all emeralds are grue," which says the next emerald will be blue. Two hypotheses, equally backed by every instance we have, opposite predictions. An instance account has no resources at all for choosing between them, so it can't be the whole story. The emerose version makes the point more sharply because the disagreement doesn't even need a future change of colour: "all emeroses are green" and "all emeroses are gred" are equally supported by everything examined so far. One might object that grue is disjunctive while green is simple, but that begs the question, since in grue-bleen terms green is definable as grue-and-examined-before-t-or-bleen-and-not, so green is just as disjunctive as grue is.

(b) Goodman's answer is that the difference between green and grue is a difference in naturalness: green corresponds to a real similarity in the world and grue is a gerrymandered predicate that doesn't. Entrenchment is his name for the fact that green tracks this natural similarity, so the more entrenched a predicate is the closer it is to the joints of nature. That's why the emerose hypothesis isn't confirmed even though all its instances are.

(c) The classmate is right that entrenchment is a matter of a community's usage, but wrong to think a decree could create it, since entrenchment builds up only through a history of past projections. Still, the deeper point stands: what Goodman has done is justify induction by its past success, which is circular in exactly the way Hume showed, so Goodman's real conclusion is that induction has no non-circular justification and the choice of green over grue is only a local convention.
