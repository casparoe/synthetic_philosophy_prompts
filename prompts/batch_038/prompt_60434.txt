Friday's meeting of my university museum's acquisitions committee — I sit on it as the graduate member and am a philosophy PhD student — has to decide a gift our collections policy has no way to record. An alumna is offering the image-generating system she trained, together with a one-page instruction text for running it; each run yields a unique large-format print.

Three features are causing trouble. She refuses to cap the edition, on the grounds that the work is the running of the system rather than any output, so a limited edition would misdescribe it. She will transfer the model but not the training corpus, which she assembled by scraping and cannot clear: we could run the system indefinitely yet never inspect its sources or retrain it. And each run emits its own certificate signed with a key she intends to destroy at her death, after which nothing further could be authenticated.

Our policy commits us to acquiring work made after 1960 and requires each accession record to state a medium, dimensions, and an edition size. The paintings conservator on the committee argues we would be acquiring something that cannot be conserved — weights degrade, runtimes obsolete — so the gift is a liability in the costume of a benefaction. Another member argues that declining merely applies a conception of art built for objects to something it was never designed to cover.

Please draft the memo I will circulate Thursday evening: a recommendation to accept, decline, or accept under conditions, with reasoning the other members could actually argue against. If you recommend accepting, say precisely what is accessioned and how the required fields can be completed without misdescribing the work. Keep distinct two questions the committee keeps collapsing — whether this is art, and what thing the museum would come to own — and be explicit about whether our vote recognizes that status or confers it, since the answer constrains what we may write in the minutes. Give the conservator's objection full weight rather than disposing of it in a clause, and close with what the decision commits us to when the next gift of this kind arrives. No bibliography; I need a document we can act on.
