Last spring I was appointed mid-term to the governing council of a national federation of agricultural co-operatives: roughly nine hundred member co-ops organised into eleven regional branches, with a shared reserve fund, a jointly owned processing network, and a single common brand under which everything is marketed. I should say at the outset that I have no training in political philosophy — I spent twenty years in grain logistics and have ended up, improbably, on a council that is now running what is in all but name a secession dispute, and Thursday's meeting is the first at which we are expected to take a position rather than commission further papers.

One branch — call it the Peninsula, since it is geographically and linguistically distinct from the interior and its co-ops are export-oriented — has petitioned to withdraw from the federation and affiliate directly with the international body. The petition runs to some forty pages, and its substance is threefold. First, a grievance: for two decades, the federation's pricing formula and capital-allocation rules have, they argue, systematically transferred value from export-oriented branches to the interior, and every reform proposal has been voted down by the interior's bloc. Second, a mandate: in an informal ballot last autumn, seventy-eight percent of those voting supported withdrawal — but the franchise was restricted to members in good standing with ten years' continuous residence in the region, which is why the figure is contested, and why a substantial number of Peninsula growers, disproportionately younger and recent arrivals, have written to us saying they wish to remain and have no vehicle through which to do so. Third, a claim of principle: that membership in a voluntary federation cannot amount to a life sentence, that the constitution's silence on withdrawal is not a prohibition, and that a body whose stated purpose is the self-determination of producer co-operatives cannot coherently refuse it to a branch that tries to exercise it. On the legal question our own counsel is unhelpful — she reads the silence as permitting withdrawal on ninety days' notice, while a retired judge on the council holds that membership is perpetual absent expulsion, and neither reading strikes me as compelling. Two further facts will not stay out of the discussion. The Peninsula operates the federation's only deep-water grain terminal, through which roughly a third of the remaining branches' contracted exports must move; and their proposal offers continued terminal access for five years at cost, with terms to be renegotiated thereafter. They have also proposed what they describe as a reversible separation: a five-year trial withdrawal, the Peninsula's share of the reserve fund held in escrow, a negotiated re-entry clause, and automatic restoration of full membership if either side invokes it. A colleague on the council thinks all of the principle is decoration and that this is simply about the terminal; a sixth branch, meanwhile, has written to ask what any precedent set here would mean for its own long-standing complaint about the branding rules.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday. It should recommend a concrete course of action — grant, refuse, or grant on stated conditions — with conditions specific enough to be put in a motion and a timetable running to the annual general meeting in five months. It should engage the Peninsula's strongest argument on its merits rather than disposing of it as a procedural question, and it should tell us what we can honestly offer those Peninsula members who want to stay, saying so plainly if the answer is that we can offer them nothing. It should assess whether the escrowed, reversible arrangement actually answers the objections to exit or merely defers them, including as regards the terminal. It should distinguish what turns on facts we do not yet have — the value-transfer claim, for instance — from what turns on questions of value the council is not entitled to settle unilaterally, and say which of those belong to the full membership. And it should set out the strongest case against whatever you recommend, whether that is the fiduciary duty owed to the remaining co-ops together with the precedent for the sixth branch, or the charge that a federation which refuses exit was never voluntary to begin with, and explain either why the recommendation nonetheless holds or where it should be modified. Write for councillors who have never read the literature: the ideas you need about when a group may leave a political association, whether a grievance is required or a vote suffices, how jointly built assets are to be divided on a divorce, and the difficulty of settling who is entitled to vote on the boundary in the first place, should be translated into the terms of this case rather than named, cited, or footnoted.
