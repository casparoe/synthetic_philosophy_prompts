I'm putting together a problem set for my grad seminar on inherited national guilt and I'm tired of the examples everyone reaches for — they're all the same case with the names swapped, and the students have already seen the moves. So I want you to design one for me to spec.

The spec. Jaspers's old four-way split from the German-guilt book: criminal guilt (courts), political guilt (what your state does in your name, judged by whoever ends up winning), moral guilt (your own deeds, judged by your conscience and by the people who know you), metaphysical guilt (the bare fact of being a human among humans when not all of them survived). I want a case that drives a wedge between the 2nd and 3rd of those.

Concretely: invent a fictional present-day state that is carrying out some ongoing institutional harm — a resource concession, a reservoir, a debt regime, a border-enforcement contract, whatever you like, but make it up — and then give me two people:

- a citizen of that state who campaigned and voted against the policy, who is not merely unbenefited by it but demonstrably worse off, who protested it in public and paid something for doing so, and who bears no criminal or moral guilt at all; and
- a foreigner, citizen of some other state, who did the same things for the same reasons with the same results — campaigned, lost, protested, paid.

The case has to make it true, and not merely stipulate, that the first bears political guilt in Jaspers's sense and the second does not.

What it's for, since that fixes what counts as cheating: I'm testing whether political guilt can be a freestanding category, or whether it's just a placeholder for grounds an individualist will supply anyway — benefit received, causal contribution, tacit consent, culpable ignorance, failure to exit. So if your dissenter turns out to have any of those, the case has failed. The wedge has to be load-bearing with everything else held fixed.

Constraints: no real countries, no real historical atrocities, no genocide or slavery stand-ins (the students go straight there and stop thinking); one paragraph for the scenario itself; nothing science-fictional; and no reading list, no citations, no apparatus — I want the reasoning.

Then, after the scenario:

1. Walk through what each of the two categories says about each of the two people and why, including why the verdicts seperate instead of tracking one another.
2. State the difference between the citizen and the foreigner in a single sentence, and tell me whether it's a relation to the state's power, to its name, to its future, or to something else. If you can't state it without pointing at benefit or agency, say so plainly.
3. Say which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the political-guilt verdict if I strip it out.
4. Pressure-test it: does the verdict survive if the dissenter renounces her citizenship but stays and keeps paying taxes? Does it survive if she left as a child and never voted?
5. Give me the strongest attack a strict moral individualist would make — someone who thinks guilt is always a person's own deed and nothing else can make it so — and tell me honestly whether it lands.

If it turns out you can't satisfy all of that at once, say which constraint you'd drop and why rather than quietly breaking one.
