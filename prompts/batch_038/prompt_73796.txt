im helping organize a one-day workshop at our institute on whether Keynes's 1930 essay on our grandchildren got the good life right — half the room is macro people who want to argue about compound growth and hours worked, the other half is ethics people who think the whole question is malformed, and im stuck opening the thing with something that makes both sides feel the problem is theirs. the organizer asked me for a short poem to read aloud before the first paper. ~3 minutes, so keep it around 28 lines.

here's the conceit i want, and please treat the ideas as a hard constraint rather than decoration. you're both a working poet and someone who has actually taught Keynes's moral writing, so think carefully before drafting: first spend a few lines brainstorming out loud for me — list the specific claims from each side that you think can survive being put into a voice, and the ones that would get garbled or overclaim if versified, then say which stanza each survivor goes in. only then write the poem.

the poem is two voices of the same man, in alternating four-line stanzas:

- the first voice is the 1930 essay: the arithmetic of it — capital compounding, technique improving, the economic problem declared solved inside a century, the working week collapsing to a handful of shifts. but this voice has to carry the two things in the same essay that undo the prediction, and carry them as its own, not as a later critic's: the split between needs that are absolute and needs that are only felt by comparison with your neighbours and may never be filled because the higher the general level goes the higher those ones climb; and the worry that we've been trained for generations in a purposive habit — never enjoying anything now, always pushing the good into the future — so that when the plenty actually arrives nobody will know how to use the freedom, and the ordinary person will come apart precisely because there's no longer anything to struggle against.
- the second voice is the late memoir about his own Bloomsbury years, the one he read to the Memoir Club in 1938 and which came out after the war: the Moore-derived ethics he'd grown up on, states of mind valued for their own sake, general rules about conduct refused, love and beauty and the apprehension of what's good as the whole of it — and the admission that this left the group with nothing to meet what came after, that it was thin about the future and about obligation. this voice is the one that thinks it knows what the good life would consist in once the arithmetic finishes, and it's also the one that suspects its own answer.

formal requirements, and these should do the argumentative work, not just decorate:

1. the 1930 voice is in strict iambic tetrameter — regular, compounding, the meter itself acting like the accumulation it's describing. the 1938 voice is in loose free lines of visibly uneven length.
2. three stanzas each, alternating, so six stanzas of four lines, then a seventh four-line stanza at the end where the two voices have collapsed into one and it's genuinely impossible to attribute the lines to either — and in that last stanza the meter starts in the loose mode and snaps into tetrameter partway through a line, then fails in the middle of the next one.
3. no verbatim quotations from Keynes anywhere. if you put his words in quotation marks you'll get them wrong, so paraphrase everything, including the well-known bits. don't name the texts in the poem itself; the voices should be recognizable from what they care about, and someone who knows the material should be able to say which stanza is which and why they end up in the same mouth.
4. don't resolve it and don't moralize. i specifically don't want either "he was right, we're just bad at leisure" or "he was naive about capitalism" — the point of the whole thing is that the tension is inside one person's thinking, not between a young optimist and a chastened old man.

after the poem, give me a short prose note, not verse: where you took liberties with the ideas or leaned on a contested reading — in particular whether the late memoir really undercuts the essay or only adds to it, since people disagree about that; then tell me how confident you are, roughly, in each attribution you made (which claim you placed in 1930 versus in the memoir), and single out the one point you're least sure of and what would settle it. no bibliography, no citations, no page numbers, no reading list, and please just say plainly if you're reconstructing from memory rather than actually knowing the text — i'd much rather have a flagged guess than a confident-sounding invention.
