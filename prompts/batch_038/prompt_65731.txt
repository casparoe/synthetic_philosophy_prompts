I co-direct a two-week residential intensive on decision theory and its applications to AI. The cohort is 28 graduate students, roughly half philosophy and half computer science; all of them have done the standard reading on causal and evidential decision theory and can compute an expected value unaided. I have one 75-minute slot and about $150 of my own money that I am willing to spend in the room.

I want an exercise that makes them feel evidential cooperation in a large world: the thought that your own choice can be decision-relevant evidence about the choices of a great many agents you cannot influence, and that this can rationally require you to contribute to a collective good that is, from where you sit, causally hopeless. Design it as something they actually do, with real money moving, not as a case they discuss.

Structural requirements:

1. The 28 are joined by a substantially larger cohort of decision-makers facing the identical problem on identical information, whose choices are sealed and outside the room's causal reach. The aggregate over the whole population decides a real donation to a charity the class selects, under a threshold rule that no single participant — and not the room even acting as a bloc — can control.
2. For each participant, declining to contribute must be strictly dominant in the ordinary causal sense, yet the private gain from declining must be small relative to what the threshold is worth, so that the evidential calculation genuinely conflicts with the causal one. Show me the arithmetic: the figures you would use, and the strength of correlation between a participant's choice and a cohort member's choice at which the two theories' recommendations flip.
3. Before anyone chooses, have them record anonymously and in writing both that nothing they do now can affect the cohort's choices and that their own choice is nevertheless evidence about those choices. The tension should come out of their own hands.
4. Partway through, introduce a single piece of information that erodes the similarity between the room and the cohort while leaving the causal structure untouched, and run a second round that is otherwise identical. The contrast between the two rounds is the lesson.

Then supply: a materials list and a minute-by-minute timeline; a credible way to recruit and seal the cohort such that these students will believe in it, plus a fallback if I cannot assemble one of that size; a version that costs nothing if my budget disappears; and the debrief, which should map each stage onto the corresponding step of the argument, leave Lewis's charge that evidential decision theory merely buys good news standing against the view rather than answering it for them, and withhold the name of this family of cases until the last five minutes — telling me which name you would give it and why that one.

Finally: three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and what to do in the moment when each does; and a frank account of what the exercise cannot reproduce, in particular what is lost when the correlation is manufactured out of shared instructions and a shared room rather than out of nothing but two agents being alike.
