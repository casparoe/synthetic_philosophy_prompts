So I'm rebuilding a seminar on the ethics of tradition — the conservatism side of it, Burke and Chesterton mostly — and I want to hang one session on Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery," which half the students will have read in high school and therefore think they already know. Here's what I want from you: a genuine reading of that story through Chesterton's fence and Burke's account of inherited prejudice, not a summary of either.

The setup I care about. Chesterton's principle, roughly: if you come across a fence across a road and can't see what it's for, you don't get to tear it down; you go away and think until you can say what it's for, and only then may you remove it. Burke adds something stronger — that the accumulated prejudices and habits of a community are a kind of stored practical reason that any one individual's reasoning is too thin to replace. A colleague of mine has a deflationary take that I find annoying but can't quite dismiss: that the fence isn't a substantive claim about the world at all, just a burden-shifting rule, a defeasible presumption in favor of the status quo that assigns the onus to the reformer and says nothing whatever about whether the fence actually has a function. I want you to test that deflation against the story, because on my reading Jackson gives us a village where the burden was never discharged and nobody even noticed it was outstanding.

The thing I keep tripping over, and the thing I most want you to work through rather than around: the fence is asymmetric. It's aimed at the reformer who doesn't know why the thing exists. It says nothing about the conserver who doesn't know why. Jackson's villagers are not Chesterton's gaily modern reformer — they've kept the practice while losing everything that gave it meaning, the box is battered and its original paraphernalia gone, the old formalities have been dropped or half-forgotten, and the only person who can articulate a reason for it is the old man with his garbled bit of doggerel about the harvest, which nobody in the story seems to take seriously. So does the story refute the fence, or does it expose a hole in it that Chesterton and Burke both left open — the fossilized fence, the one whose function has died but whose form keeps killing? Take a position.

Please also deal with these, because they're where the students go wrong:

- Other villages have given up their lotteries, and one character reports this almost apologetically while the old man reacts with contempt and predicts nothing but trouble. On the deflationary reading that's a live counterexample doing work in the text: fences have been removed elsewhere and the sky hasn't fallen. Does Jackson let us draw that inference, or does she withhold it deliberately? Say which, and say what turns on it.
- Tessie Hutchinson is perfectly cheerful about the lottery until her family's name comes up, and then she's protesting that it wasn't fair. Is her objection a conservative objection or a fairness/impartiality objection? The students will want to cast her as the truth-teller, and I think that's too easy. Tell me whether the story lets you tell the two kinds of protest apart, and whether Burke has resources for the idea that a tradition can be criticized from inside itself — by its own standards, for being botched — rather than from outside.
- Be honest about where the reading breaks. Jackson wrote a story, not a treatise; the horror of the ending does a lot of persuasive work that an argument can't, and I don't want a reading that just uses the story as an illustration with the philosophy supplying the content. Where does the story resist Chesterton and Burke, and where does it resist the progressivist reading too?

End with what the story adds to the debate over the fence that argument alone doesn't get you — something about what it feels like to participate in a practice you can't justify, or about the difference between not knowing why a thing exists and having stopped wondering. That last distinction might not be one Jackson intended, and I don't need her to have intended it; I need to know whether the text supports it or whether I'm smuggling my own view in. And if you think my whole framing is wrong — if you think the fence just isn't the right lens and something else in the conservatism tradition fits the story better — say so and tell me what that is instead. I'd rather be corrected now than have twenty students politely nodding at a bad reading for ninety minutes.
