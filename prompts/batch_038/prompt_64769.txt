**The question (25 points):**

> A county uses a single pretrial risk score, with one cutoff applied to everyone, to decide who is detained before trial. The vendor states that its score is calibrated within racial groups. A civil-rights coalition commissions a study of the resulting binary decisions and reports two findings: (i) among defendants flagged high risk, the share subsequently arrested for a new crime is essentially the same for Black and white defendants; (ii) among defendants *not* subsequently arrested, Black defendants were flagged high risk at roughly twice the rate of white defendants.
>
> (a) [6 pts] Using the sufficiency / separation / independence vocabulary, say which criteria the binary decision satisfies and which it violates, and what the two findings together tell you about base rates.
> (b) [8 pts] State a theorem that explains why the county cannot in general have everything it might want here. Be precise about the conditions under which the impossibility bites, and about any exceptional cases in which it doesn't.
> (c) [8 pts] The county proposes to keep the same score but set different cutoffs by race. What does that buy, what does it cost, and does it resolve the impossibility or relocate it?
> (d) [3 pts] A commenter on the coalition's report writes: "Because this is a mathematical impossibility, no amount of better data or better modeling will help. The county just has to pick a definition of fairness and live with the consequences." Assess.

**The student's answer:**

> (a) Finding (i) is predictive parity: the probability that a flagged defendant reoffends is the same across groups, and that is the substantive content of sufficiency in this setting. Finding (ii) is an unequal false positive rate, which violates equalized odds, and hence separation, since equalized odds asks for equal FPR and equal TPR across groups. But note that the unequal FPR also shows the score cannot be calibrated within groups, whatever the vendor says: if it were calibrated, the same score value would carry the same risk in both groups, and a single cutoff applied to equally calibrated scores would have to produce the same false positive rate in each group. So the vendor's calibration claim and the coalition's finding can't both be true. As for base rates, finding (i) tells us the groups have similar base rates among the flagged, and the findings say nothing about base rates in the population at large.
>
> (b) The relevant result is the impossibility theorem for risk scores: except in degenerate cases, a score cannot simultaneously be calibrated within groups, have equal false discovery rates across groups, and have equal false negative rates across groups. Equivalently, calibration together with balance for the positive class and balance for the negative class forces the two groups to have equal base rates. So the county faces a genuine either/or: keep calibration and accept the FPR gap, or equalize error rates and give up calibration. There is no third option that is mathematically coherent.
>
> (c) Group-specific cutoffs are what the literature recommends. The standard postprocessing result (Hardt et al.) shows that you can take any trained score and, using different threshholds per group plus a bit of randomization, get a classifier satisfying equalized odds — and the same machinery preserves calibration, since it only changes where you cut, not what the score means. So the county can get equal FPR and equal FNR while keeping a calibrated instrument. What it gives up is nothing statistical; what it gives up is legal and political comfort, because it now has to classify by race explicitly, which invites a disparate-treatment challenge. In other words group-specific thresholds dissolve the impossibility, which suggests the theorem was never really about the world but about the arbitrary decision to use one cutoff for everybody.
>
> (d) The commenter is basically right. The theorem is a constraint on definitions rather than on data, so collecting more features or fitting a better model doesn't touch it. Accuracy is orthogonal: a more accurate score is still subject to exactly the same trade-off. What's left for the county is normative — decide whose errors you care more about — and the technical fixes are a distraction from that.
