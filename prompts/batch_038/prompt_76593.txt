I am a staff software engineer, and I have no formal training in philosophy — never taken a course, never read the primary literature. A friend of mine teaches an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of language course, and she has asked me to contribute one applied essay question to her final exam. Her reason is that her students handle the assigned readings well but tend to freeze when a question is posed in terms of a concrete situation with numbers in it, and she wants one item that forces them to reason rather than to recognize. She will vet whatever I send, so I need it to be philosophically respectable; but the case itself comes from my own work, and I am supplying it below as raw material for you to shape into a proper exam item.

The case. We operate a service that routes each incoming request either to a legacy storage shard or to a modern one. Over one month we recorded 10,000 requests:

- 1,000 went to the legacy shard; of those, 850 met our latency target and 150 missed it.
- 9,000 went to the modern shard; of those, 8,650 met the target and 350 missed it.
- Overall: 9,500 met the target, 500 missed it.

Two sentences an on-call engineer might write in a postmortem:

- (I1) If the request did not go to the legacy shard, it met the latency target.
- (I2) If the request missed the latency target, it went to the legacy shard.

These are contrapositives of one another, and as far as I can tell any purely truth-functional treatment gives them the same content — the probability that a randomly chosen request either went to the legacy shard or met the target is 0.965 in both cases. Yet (I1) reads like something I would happily assert on this data (0.96), while (I2) reads like something I would refuse to assert (0.30). Separately, consider one particular request, r, which went to the modern shard, carried a 4.2 MB payload (larger than 99.5 percent of our traffic), and completed in 180 ms against a 200 ms budget. Our internal benchmarks show that the legacy shard takes roughly 900 ms on payloads of that size, because it lacks the columnar cache. So the subjunctive sentence

- (C) If r had gone to the legacy shard, it would have missed the latency target

looks plainly true to me, even though the corresponding conditional probability computed over the whole fleet — the chance of missing the target given legacy routing — is only 0.15.

Finally, a wrinkle I would like built into the item. A colleague of mine is constructing a replay harness: it takes the recorded traffic, flips the routing decision for a chosen request, and re-executes everything else from the log. He maintains that this settles the philosophical question, because it makes "would have" mean exactly "does, in the replay." My instinct is that he is wrong — the replay has to decide which recorded facts to hold fixed and which to let shift along with the routing change, and different choices produce opposite verdicts on the same (C) — but I cannot articulate the objection. Please sharpen it and make it part of what students have to engage with.

What I want from you, as a single package:

1. The exam question exactly as students will see it, with all the data and background it needs written into the question itself. It is a closed-book final; students may not consult anything. Two or three lettered parts are fine, provided it reads as one problem rather than as unrelated sub-questions. It must be answerable in 45 minutes by a strong student writing roughly 800 words, and it must not be answerable by defining the two constructions and giving stock examples.

2. A model answer of 700 to 900 words.

3. A marking scheme totaling 20 marks, itemized: each move a top answer must make, the marks it is worth, and a one-line note on how students typically botch it.

4. A short list of the confidently stated positions that should cost marks — the ones that sound right and are not — each with one sentence saying why it fails.

5. An explicit statement of what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one on this item.

Constraints. I do not know the technical names of the competing doctrines my data bears on, so please supply them where they belong in the model answer and the rubric; students will have met them in the course. Students may name positions, but I want the marks to come from what they do with my numbers, not from name-checking, so please make the rubric say so. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, page references, or any instruction to cite sources — the item must stand alone. Do not put any wording inside quotation marks and attribute it to a published author; if you need to describe a position, paraphrase it. Write everything in American English, in spelling, punctuation, and idiom.
