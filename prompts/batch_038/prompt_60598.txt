I sit on the practice review board of a mid-sized county child welfare agency, and this month the board has to rule on one case that has split it down the middle. I have been asked to circulate a written opinion before we meet.

The facts. A caseworker — call her D. — carried a heavy rural caseload. Over four months she drove a mother roughly forty minutes each way to two court hearings in her personal car, because the county's transport voucher system was backlogged; she paid for groceries out of pocket twice after the emergency assistance fund ran dry; and she attended the older child's seventh birthday party and brought a small gift. She disclosed the first two to her supervisor, who approved them verbally. The birthday she did not disclose. The mother has since missed two scheduled appointments, and the children remain in the home under a safety plan. Two board members want a boundary violation entered in D.'s file, on the grounds that favors of this kind cannot be distributed evenly, are invisible to audit, and leave the client owing something she can only repay with compliance. Two others want the record to show that D. did exactly what responsive practice calls for, and that sanctioning her will teach the whole unit to stop disclosing what actually works.

There is a complication the board keeps circling. Eighteen months ago we piloted a "relational continuity" indicator that scores workers partly on client-reported trust and on how long a case stays with the same worker. D. is at the top of her unit on it. One member who had no hand in designing the indicator argues that it measures something corrosive: that a worker scored on whether a family feels cared for is, in effect, being paid to be liked, and that the indicator therefore tilts practice away from the recommendations that are hardest to deliver and easiest to defend afterwards — removal above all.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate, on the order of two pages. It must land on a definite recommendation — sanction, no sanction, or sanction under specified conditions — and it must also say what the board should do with the indicator: keep it, revise it, or drop it. Treat the two camps as making arguments rather than displaying temperaments, and give the fairness objection to D.'s conduct its strongest form, including the version about the families she had no capacity to drive and the version about an agency that converts its own funding shortfalls into individual workers' private charity. If your recommendation leans on a reading of care ethics that its critics dispute — the charge that attentiveness to particular others supplies no way to adjudicate between competing claims, and so tends to ratify the asymmetries it finds — say so explicitly rather than passing that reading off as established. Throughout, separate what people who work in this area genuinely agree on from what remains live and unsettled, and mark which of your substantive claims fall into the second category.
