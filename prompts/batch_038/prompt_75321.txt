I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm trying to settle an argument I overheard rather than report it, because I could not work out which speaker was right. It broke out at a conference reception between a psychometrician (A) and an economist (B), both of whom work on well-being measurement.

A began by asserting that the World Happiness Report does not measure happiness. Its ranking rests on a single survey item asking respondents to place their own life on a ladder running from zero, the worst possible life, to ten, the best possible life. That is a considered judgment about how one's life is going, not a report of how one feels; and the same poll's questions about yesterday's enjoyment, worry and sadness line up only loosely with it across countries, which is what you would expect if the two were tracking different things entirely.

B replied that this assumes the narrower of two defensible meanings. What people have reason to care about, B said, is whether their life is going well; a measure that is stable, that varies a great deal between countries, and that is explained by the actual conditions of people's lives does more work than a measure of yesterday's mood — and the report's authors chose an item that never uses the word happiness precisely so that it translates and travels.

A's second move: then the ranking is mislabeled, and every front page announcing the happiest country on earth is reporting something nobody measured. B said that is a complaint about a headline, not about the numbers.

A's third move was that because respondents anchor against their own best possible life, the top of the ladder means something different in Oslo than in Lilongwe; part of what the ranking orders is ambition and cultural reference points, so the top of the table is not commensurable with the bottom. B answered that distortion of that magnitude would be extraordinary, and would have to be, because the ladder ranks countries in the same order as income, social support, healthy life expectancy, generosity and perceived corruption — variables collected independently; a badly warped instrument would not fall into line so tidily with its own putative causes.

A's last move: then the ranking measures the Nordic welfare state, and once a country is handed a score it will manage the score, improving services or coaxing people into more favorable self-assessments without anyone's experience getting better. B said that a government which improves the conditions people actually use when judging their lives is not gaming the measure but hitting the target.

Please adjudicate move by move. For each contribution by A and by B, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or mistaken, and why; then say who is more right overall about whether the report measures happiness; and say what neither addressed — I noticed that neither mentioned how much of a country's population a national average conceals, or whether their arguments would change if the ranking used the median. End with a line in exactly this form: Verdict: [A / B / Neither] — [fifteen words or fewer naming the decisive point].
