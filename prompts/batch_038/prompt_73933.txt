A former colleague of mine, an engineering manager, was fond of telling his teams to control the controllables, and after a two-year initiative of his collapsed he turned the maxim against us: the failure had been foreseeable, he said, but the team had wasted months petitioning leadership over a budget it never had authority to set. What unsettles me is that the maxim was doing reproach work there rather than its usual consolatory work, and I can no longer say what it actually asserts. Lay out the distinct precise claims that could be hiding inside it: what counts as being within one's control (causal sufficiency? counterfactual influence? mere immunity from external prevention?), whether the partition is exhaustive or requires a third class of things only partly within it — health, say, or reputation, or the decisions of other people — and whether the maxim describes how the world is in fact divided or prescribes where effort and concern ought to go. Grade each reading as a platitude, as substantive and defensible, or as false, and say for each whether it could license reproaching people for having tried rather than merely consoling them for having failed. Finish by identifying the reading my colleague needed, and by saying whether the ancient version he was paraphrasing — Epictetus's division of things into what is up to us and what is not — would have supplied it.
