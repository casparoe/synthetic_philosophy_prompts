I run a tiny disaster-prep nonprofit in a river town, and I also, reluctantly, do my own household finances, which is how I ended up arguing with my accountant about the shape of probability weighting functions. A friend in a philosophy PhD program lent me Buchak's *Risk and Rationality* (2013) and said it would explain why the choices I make look insane on a spreadsheet but don't feel insane at all. The pitch I took away: don't shove risk attitude into the utility function as diminishing marginal utility and then call it a day, because that conflates "I care less about each extra dollar" with "I hate the shape of this gamble"; instead keep utility over outcomes roughly honest and put the attitude toward risk into a separate function that re-weights probabilities according to where outcomes sit in the ranking. One global weighting function, same one for everything I'll ever decide. That last part is where I get stuck, because I'm pretty sure I don't have one, and I want to know whether that's a fact about me or a bug in the theory.

Here are my actual verdicts, and I've tried to write them with real numbers so you can check the arithmetic instead of taking my word for it.

1. Phone coverage: $1.20 a month against a 1-in-1,000 yearly chance of a $700 replacement. I'm paying about $14 to guard 70 cents of expected loss. I buy it and I don't feel like a mark.
2. A catastrophic-medical rider at $8,000 a year covering a 2% chance of a $300,000 bill. Expected loss $6,000, so it's a bad deal, and I'd sign today.
3. Certain $500 versus a 50/50 shot at $1,200 or nothing: I take the $500, and I know the expected value is $600.
4. The film investment. A friend's project: I put in $9,000 that came out of a small inheritance I had already mentally written off, 3% chance it comes back as $400,000, 97% chance it's gone. Expected value is maybe +$3,000, but here's the thing — I'd take it at 1.5% too, where the arithmetic goes negative. I'd take it knowing it's negative.
5. The exact same film deal, same odds, same $9,000, but the money now comes out of my emergency fund — the pot that covers a broken furnace or a quarter with no grants. I refuse, flatly, and I think I'd refuse at 10% for $400,000 as well. Nothing changed except which pot the money is sitting in. My total wealth in the two versions is identical, so I know case 4 and case 5 are the same act described two ways, and I still feel the difference in my chest.
6. One 50/50 flip for +$11,000 or −$10,000: I turn it down, even though it's $500 in my favor. Offer me a hundred independent copies of that same flip and I want all hundred, no hesitation.
7. Public side: the levee. 1-in-200 annual chance of $2 billion in damage, and $6 million a year cuts it to 1-in-2,000. Build it, obviously. But I will not insure the county's vehicles above the deductible even when the math is the same shape scaled down, and I'd tell my neighbor not to buy the rider in case 2 — he's got no buffer at all, which by my own logic should make him more careful than me, not less. That one is embarassing and I don't have a story for it.

My own tentative principle, which I want you to attack rather than compliment: call it the floor rule. There's a level F, the worst situation I've actually lived through and could live through again, and it's pinned to what my life costs, not to what's in my accounts, so a windfall doesn't move it. Probability doesn't get weighted by itself; it gets weighted by where the outcome lands relative to F. Outcomes below F get their weight multiplied up hard. Outcomes between F and a bit above F get roughly their bare probability. Outcomes way above F, the ones that change the story rather than pad it, get a modest bonus. That's supposed to deliver 1, 2, 4 and 5 together — in 4 the $9,000 is above F and never was load-bearing, in 5 losing it drops me under F — and it's obviously not the same as a single global weighting function over probabilities, since mine takes the outcomes as arguments too.

So: give me the principle you actually think is right, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you've never seen — a rule or formula with explicit clauses, and for each clause tell me which of my numbered cases is doing the motivating. Then tell me what it says about the two I'm torn on (whether case 6's bundle is genuinely a different act or just me contradicting myself, and whether case 7's levee/vehicles/neighbor split is survivable), and invent one further case of your own that discriminates between your principle and my floor rule, and rule on it. Be committed: pick one, say which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and argue for that rather than laying out a menu and letting me choose. If you think the floor rule is just prospect theory with a folksy name, or just a fine-grained redescription of outcomes that collapses back into a single weighting function once you admit that "losing the furnace money" and "losing windfall money" were never the same outcome to begin with, say that flatly and say which of the two escapes it is.
