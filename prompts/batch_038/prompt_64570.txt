I've been circling Szasz for years, and I've never managed to work out how much of the quarrel with his critics is about the world and how much is about the word "illness." So I'd like to pin it to a number with the arithmetic left visible.

Fix the target first, since most arguments about Szasz turn out to be arguments about which of his claims is being attacked. Call it M, and read it as two conjuncts. First, a claim about the concept: a condition counts as an illness only if a deviation from the normal structure or function of some organ or tissue can be exhibited in the patient's body, by methods that examine the body rather than the patient's conduct, reports, and complaints. Second, a claim about the extension: no condition that psychiatry treats as a mental illness in its own right — as distinct from a disease of the brain whose manifestations happen to be psychological, which Szasz was glad to hand back to neurology and internal medicine — satisfies that standard. M is not the claim that psychiatric patients don't suffer, that their suffering isn't real, that nothing helps, or that psychiatric coercion is impermissible. Those are separate theses; I want them kept out of the arithmetic entirely.

Assign a credence to M, then take it apart. I'd expect at least the following terms, but add, split, or replace any you think are doing more work:

1. The probability that there is a fact of the matter about the concept of illness at all — that one candidate criterion is correct, rather than the choice being a stipulation with nothing to recommend it beyond usefulness.
2. Conditional on that, the probability that the correct criterion is the lesion-based one in M, as against the hybrid account that has absorbed much of the philosophical discussion — disorder as the failure of an internal mechanism to perform the function it exists to perform, together with harm by the standards of the sufferer's culture — and as against treating illness as a family-resemblance concept with no necessary conditions.
3. The probability that the extension-claim holds of the core syndromes as the nosology currently stands, given how the criterion would have to be applied to a manual that also contains delirium due to another medical condition, the major neurocognitive disorders, and the substance-induced disorders.
4. The absorption term: the probability that if biomarker or etiological validation of the sort the NIMH's domain-criteria program has been chasing ever succeeds for, say, psychosis, the result would be read as confirming M — the validated entity reclassified out of psychiatry, as general paresis became neurosyphilis — rather than as refuting it. This is the term that decides whether M is empirically exposed at all, and I suspect it is where the real action is.

A number and one or two sentences of justification for each, then how you combine them, then: which term your total is most sensitive to, which you're least confident in, and what piece of evidence — empirical rather than philosophical — would move your number furthest.

Finally, a diagnostic use for all of this. My credence in M is about 0.35; a friend three years into a psychiatry residency puts it at 0.05 and regards the question as closed. Which term is the gap between us most likely hiding in, and is there a single sub-question on which, if we converged, most of the remaining difference would collapse? No bibliography, please — I want your own reasoning, and I'd rather you say plainly where you're guessing than dress it up.
