I am a doctoral researcher in history, and next term I am to give a position statement at an interdisciplinary methods seminar. Rather than survey the literature, I have been asked to set out my own commitments about what historical explanation amounts to, so that the room can attack them directly. What follows is the draft. I should like an audit of it before I present it, not a lecture on the debate.

My commitments:

1. The aim of explanation. To explain a historical outcome is to show that it was to be expected, given the antecedent conditions one cites. I take this from the covering-law tradition as Hempel framed it, but I do not think the expectation has to be articulated as an explicit law; what matters is that the account renders the outcome expectable rather than merely recorded.

2. Singular causation suffices. Many good historical explanations consist in identifying the cause of an event — the mislaid despatch, the cabinet decision of a particular week, the harvest failure in this province and not that one — and I hold that such an identification can be a complete explanation even where no covering law is invoked or known. Whatever law there may be is background, not part of the explanation.

3. There are no laws of history. No strict laws govern historical processes. The macro-generalisations that historians and historical sociologists deploy — about state capacity, about fiscal crisis and political mobilisation, about frontier closure — are at best ceteris paribus rules whose provisos cannot be specified in advance, and I do not think they are reducible to, or derivable from, lower-level regularities in psychology or economics.

4. Reasons are causes. Explaining an agent's action by citing their reasons is a species of causal explanation. I see no need for a separate, non-causal relation of rationalisation, and I am not persuaded that deliberation stands outside the ordinary causal order.

5. Intelligibility. Nevertheless, what makes a rationalising account explanatory in the strong sense, as opposed to merely true, is that it lets one see that what the agent did made sense to them. A purely causal story of the same events, however complete, would not supply that, and I regard what is missing as a loss of explanation rather than a loss of something else.

6. Narrative is explanatorily load-bearing. A well-constructed narrative cannot be paraphrased into a list of singular causal claims plus generalisations without loss of explanatory content. Order, selection, the decision where the story begins and where it stops, what is foregrounded and what relegated to scene — these are not presentational ornament draped over an argument that could be stated baldly.

7. Difference-making. A factor explains an outcome only if the outcome depended on it. Conditions present throughout whose absence would have changed nothing are background, not explanans. I want this as a disciplinary check on explanation-by-atmosphere.

8. Counterfactual history is inadmissible as a method. Historians are right to refuse the counterfactual genre; the subject is what happened, and speculation about what might have is unfalsifiable entertainment. I have been assuming this sits comfortably with 7, on the grounds that one concerns what makes a causal claim true and the other what evidence we may offer for it.

9. Convergence and particularity. Historical inquiry should aspire to the evidential and inferential standards of the social sciences, and I regard comparative and quantitative work as the progressive core of the discipline. I also hold that what history explains is the non-repeatable configuration, and that generalising methods are structurally unable to capture it. I have been telling people these two are complementary rather than competing.

10. A case from my own work. My dissertation concerns the withdrawal of a colonial administration from a policy of indirect rule. I maintain both that the governor's decision in a given month explains the withdrawal, and that the outcome was such that many distinct antecedent configurations — a different governor, a differently composed cabinet, a budget crisis arriving two years later — would each have produced the same withdrawal, in much the same shape, within a few years. I have never thought the second claim undermines the first.

Please go through this set and tell me which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, ranked by severity rather than listed as equally bad, and which of the tensions I have half-anticipated dissolve on inspection and why. For each genuine tension, state what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would cost me elsewhere in the list. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the most. Two specific questions: is my complementarity defence of 9 adequate, or is it doing covert work to keep something else alive? And in 10, is principle 7 to be abandoned, refined, or is my verdict on the governor's decision to be surrendered?
