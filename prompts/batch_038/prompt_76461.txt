I have been going around in circles on Molyneux's problem, and I think the way out is not to argue about the original case but to work through my verdicts on a pile of variants and see what they add up to. I have eleven cases, a tentative principle that I can already see is broken, and two pairs of verdicts I cannot reconcile. I would like a principle, not a survey.

Throughout, "Yes" means: at the moment of the test, the subject identifies the object correctly without touching it, in a way that is not a lucky guess. "No" means: he cannot.

**The cases and my verdicts.**

- **C1 — The original.** A man blind from birth, now forty, who has distinguished cubes from spheres by touch all his life and knows both words. His sight is restored. An hour later a cube and a sphere are placed on a table before him and he is asked which is which, without touching. **No.**
- **C2 — The ten days.** The same man, same objects, same question, but asked after ten days of ordinary active sight: walking about a furnished house, reaching for things, watching people move. Nobody has pointed out a cube or a sphere to him in that time. **Yes** — and I do not think the ten days are a dodge; they are simply what learning to see amounts to.
- **C3 — The reverse.** A normally sighted adult who has, let us stipulate, never handled a cube or a sphere, is instantly and permanently blinded and then has a solid cube and a solid sphere put into his hands. He is asked which is which. **Yes**, immediately, without hesitation. I freely admit I cannot explain why this direction is so much easier than C1.
- **C4 — The substitution device.** A man blind from birth has worn, for three years, a device that renders the image from a head-mounted camera as a grid of vibrating points across his upper back. He navigates crowded rooms, catches a ball rolled to him, and finds a cup on a cluttered table. When a cube is placed a metre in front of the camera he says "cube" at once, though he has never touched one. Asked what that is like, he says the shape is out there in front of him and that he sees it. **Yes.**
- **C5 — The echolocator.** A man blind from birth who over thirty years has become an expert clicker-echolocator. From the echoes off a cube and a sphere placed a metre away he says which is which on the first try, though he has never touched either. He describes them as out there in front of him, at a distance, with size and solidity. Asked whether he sees them, he says no: he hears where they are and what they are like. **No.**
- **C6 — The instant prosthesis, forward.** The man of C1 is fitted with a device that drives his visual cortex so as to produce, at the moment of the test, exactly the pattern of activity a normally sighted person looking at that cube would have. He has had no visual experience of any kind before that moment. He is asked. **No.**
- **C7 — The instant prosthesis, reverse.** The man of C3 is blinded and fitted with a device that drives his somatosensory cortex so as to produce exactly the pattern a normally sighted person handling that cube would have. He has had no tactile experience of solid objects before that moment. He is asked. **Yes.**
- **C8 — Raised lines.** The man of C1 has been told in words everything true about cubes and spheres, and has also been taught to read raised-line drawings, including a perspective drawing of a cube and a plain circle. His sight is restored and he is shown a line drawing of a cube in perspective and a circle, and asked. **Yes.**
- **C9 — Words only.** The same man with the same verbal instruction but no training in raised-line drawings. Sight restored; shown the solid cube and the solid sphere. **No.** So it is the pictures and not the propositions that do the work in C8, but I cannot say what the pictures gave him that the words did not, since both were explored with the same fingers.
- **C10 — Pointing instead of naming.** The man of C1, one hour after restoration, is asked nothing. He is shown pairs — cube and sphere, then cube and cone, then sphere and cylinder — and made to point. He points correctly nine times out of ten. **Yes.** The naming in C1 was never the real question.
- **C11 — Motion without form.** A subject whose restored vision supports movement perception and gross brightness discrimination: he tracks a rolling ball and flinches at an object approaching his face. But on every test he fails to tell a cube from a sphere from a cylinder. **No.**

One further datum, not a case: I take it that in actual reports of late sight restoration the answer is No at first and becomes Yes after weeks of ordinary visual life, and becomes Yes sooner for objects the patient had previously handled. Please treat that as consistent with the above and fold it in, rather than as something to argue with.

**My tentative principle.** A subject answers correctly if and only if, at the moment of the test, he already possesses a concept of the shape that both senses can address — that is, if and only if no new cross-modal translation has to be learned on the spot.

I know it misfires, in both directions, and I cannot repair it myself. The man in C1 plausibly has an amodal concept of a cube: he reasons about it, predicts how it would feel from an angle he has not touched, describes it accurately in words. So the principle says Yes where I say No. Meanwhile in C3 the reverse subject plainly does have to learn something on the spot — what a cube feels like for the first time — so the principle says No where I say Yes.

**What I want back.**

1. One, or at most two, candidate principles stated precisely enough that a careful reader could apply them to a case they had never seen without coming back to me with questions. For each clause, say which of C1–C11 forced it and what it rules out.
2. Your recommended principle applied to all eleven cases.
3. Three variants of your own that I have not listed. For each, say what your principle predicts and which clause does the work. At least one of the three should be a case you think I would get wrong, given the pattern of my verdicts.
4. An honest list of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. If it can save every one of them only by adding machinery, say what the machinery costs and where it starts to look ad hoc; then say which single verdict you would overturn in order to get a materially simpler principle, and argue for overturning it.
5. An explicit ruling on the two pairs I flagged: C4/C5 and C6/C7. For each, either identify the feature that genuinely justifies my opposing verdicts, or concede that one of them has to go and say which.
6. What your principle implies for the question as Molyneux posed it. Is there a determinate answer, and if so what is it? If you think the question is malformed or ambiguous, name the exact ambiguity my case list has been trading on.

Two constraints. Reason from the cases themselves: no citations, no named researchers, patients, or works, no reading list. And do not answer every case with a shrug about individual variation — I want commitments, and where you think the verdict is genuinely indeterminate, say what would settle it.
