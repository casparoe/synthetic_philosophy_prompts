Since the start of the year I have been drafting the statement of principles for a coalition that lobbies our state legislature on sentencing reform, and the philosophical preamble has now been stuck for six weeks in a way I did not foresee. Our working group contains two camps, and they are not the camps I expected to be arguing with. One takes its cues from Dennett: responsibility is not a metaphysical substance to be discovered but a competence constituted and sustained by the practice of holding one another to account; a person is an agent in part because she is treated as one; and a system that gave up desert would not become more humane but would lose the only vocabulary in which an offender can be addressed as a citizen rather than managed as a risk. The other takes its cues from Caruso and the free-will skeptics generally: nobody is ever responsible in the basic-desert sense, retributivism is therefore indefensible however humane its practitioners feel, and sanction must be rebuilt on something like a public-health-quarantine model — forward-looking, minimally restrictive, proportionate to the danger rather than to the guilt. What frustrates me is that both camps accuse the other of changing the subject. The Dennettians say the skeptics are attacking a straw notion of contra-causal desert that no court has ever actually required, and that the skeptic's "revisionism" is a bait-and-switch that keeps the warm residue of accountability while removing its normative bite. The skeptics reply that the compatibilists have stretched the word until it is compatible with anything, and that what remains deserves the name responsibility only by courtesy. Both principals have already argued this out at length in print, which makes the impasse more striking rather than less.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than accept, is that this is not a disagreement about metaphysics at all but about the direction of justification: one side treats the practice of holding responsible as explanatorily and normatively authoritative, so that metaphysics must be made to accommodate it, while the other holds that metaphysics constrains which practices may be justified in the first place. If that is right, the dispute is methodological, and methodological disputes of this kind may be structurally immune to argument from cases — which would explain why the stock intuitions have been run through the machine so many times with each side claiming the verdict. The familiar set keeps recurring in our meetings: the sniper whose autopsy revealed a tumour pressing on his amygdala, the covertly manipulated agent, the addict, the negligent driver who never thought to check the load; each side treats these as confirmations and the other as question-begging restatements. I would also like you to weigh an alternative diagnosis I cannot shake, namely that the two sides are answering different questions about whom a justification must be addressed to — system-level legitimacy for the compatibilist, agent-facing legitimacy for the skeptic, who insists that any restriction must be justifiable to the person it falls on. A third possibility, which cuts against both of my hypotheses, is that there is a live empirical matter buried underneath: some of the experimental work I have read suggests that ordinary blame attributions do not track people's own abstract commitments about determinism, so that what looks like an argument about the concept of desert may be an argument about a psychological regularity nobody has pinned down.

So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this impasse substantive, verbal, methodological, a dispute over which intuitions count as data, or simply two questions travelling under one name? Support the diagnosis by reference to what each side actually argues — the structure of their moves, not a survey of positions or a list of who has said what — and tell me honestly whether my direction-of-justification hypothesis survives contact with the material or whether I have merely relabelled the deadlock. Then say what would count as progress given the diagnosis you reach: a distinction, a case that the two methodologies would genuinely answer differently, a result about how blame attributions actually behave, a change of question, or a concession that no progress should be expected and why. Finally, and concretely, since I have to put something in front of a legislative committee: is there a formulation of the responsibility principle that both camps could sign without either feeling it has surrendered the metaphysics, or would any such text be dishonest in a way I should refuse? Please write to me in continuous prose, as an email or a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables, and give me your diagnosis in the opening paragraph before you begin defending it.
