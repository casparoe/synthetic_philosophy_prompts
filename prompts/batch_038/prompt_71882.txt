I write literary fiction, and the book I am at work on is assembled in part from documents — minutes, correspondence, panel statements, the paper trail of institutions. One of the documents I want to include is the joint statement issued at the close of an adversarial collaboration between two philosophers of mind who have argued about the same question for a decade. I want it drafted as they would have drafted it, honestly enough that a philosopher who picked up my novel would not wince.

The two camps.

One is an illusionist about phenomenal consciousness: someone who holds that the appearance of ineffable, intrinsically felt qualitative character is an artefact of introspection itself. On this view the brain builds a simplified model of its own processing and presents it to itself the way an operating system presents a desktop icon to a user; introspection then credits the underlying machinery with properties nothing in it instantiates. Consciousness, in the sense at issue, is a user illusion — not a mystery but a misdescription.

The other is a realist about phenomenal consciousness: someone who holds that there is a genuine, non-functional property — what it is like to see red, or to be in pain — whose existence is the datum any theory must accommodate rather than explain away, and that illusionism either contradicts itself or quietly changes the subject.

What I need is the document those two could both sign. Not a dialogue and not a dramatized exchange; I will do the dramatization myself afterwards. Not a verdict on who is right. A joint statement, containing:

1. A preamble, a paragraph or two, stating the question the collaboration took up in terms a lay reader could follow, and what the two parties undertook to do.

2. A numbered section of agreed claims: propositions both can endorse, worded so neither has to swallow anything it rejects. Some will be empirical — both can accept, I take it, that introspective report is constructed after the fact and can be confabulated, that the apparent richness of the perceptual field outruns what is actually represented, that attention and report come apart. Some will be conceptual — both can accept that the dispute is not about whether brains cause behaviour, and that any adequate theory must explain why it seems to us that there is something it is like to be us. Be severe here: a claim that is agreed only because it is vague is worthless to me, and a claim that is true only on one party's favoured reading belongs in the next section.

3. A disagreements table, one row per genuine disagreement, with columns for the issue, the illusionist's formulation of their own side in wording they would accept, the realist's formulation of theirs, and a judgement on whether the disagreement is substantive or in whole or part a dispute about how words are being used. Where you call a row verbal, say what has to be held fixed to see that it is.

4. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one party. For each, give the case, argument, or evidence both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, in their own terms. Three I want you to consider — refine, reject, or reframe any of them if my instinct is wrong:

- The sense in which things seem. When the illusionist says it seems as though we have phenomenal properties, is that seeming itself phenomenal? If it is, the view looks self-undermining; if it is not, the illusionist owes an account of what kind of state the seeming is and why the self-model it belongs to presents itself as something more. What test would both parties accept as bearing on this? I have in mind something like: what happens to a subject's reports when they are trained, over months, to look for exactly the construction the illusionist posits. Does scrutiny dissolve the appearance or sharpen it?

- Interface or hallucination. A desktop icon compresses a file without lying about it. Is introspection like that — omitting and simplifying, but pointing at real states — or does it attribute positive properties nothing instantiates? I suspect the two can agree on a great deal of the empirical material about apparent excess of richness while disagreeing about what to call it, and I want that boundary drawn precisely.

- The evidential standing of articulate self-report. I spend my working life producing careful, revised, artful first-person accounts of what it is like to be a particular person, and philosophers on both sides quote novelists when it suits them. Is a well-made literary rendering of an experience data for this debate at all? The realist may say such reports are defeasible evidence about the structure of experience, and that their convergence across centuries and languages counts for something; the illusionist may say they are evidence about the conventions of an interface and of a genre, and that convergence is explained by shared modelling machinery and shared literary form rather than by shared acquaintance with properties. Say whether this is a crux in its own right or downstream of the first two, and give each side's prediction about whether introspective report improves or degrades under the kind of attention literary training imposes.

5. At least one further crux that neither I nor, so far as I can tell, the published exchanges have framed — something load-bearing that tends to get stepped over.

6. A falsification section: what each party would now count as evidence or argument against their own view, written down in advance; and whether there is anything both agree would genuinely settle the matter as opposed to redescribing it.

7. A short closing note recording what the collaboration failed to resolve and why, without pretending to a truce that is not there.

Two requests I mean seriously. First, I have no technical training, so gloss any term of art at its first appearance — but do not thin the content on my account; I would sooner look things up than be handed something shallow. Second, be scrupulous in separating what is settled in the philosophy of mind from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on it. Illusionism is a minority view, and I do not want a draft that smuggles either it or its rival in as the professional consensus. Wherever you place a claim in the agreed section, add a marginal note telling me whether that agreement is real common ground in the literature or something only these two particular parties would sign; and if at some point you find yourself advancing a contested position as though it were received, flag it in the note rather than smoothing it into the prose.
