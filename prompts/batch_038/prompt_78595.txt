I write a monthly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past two years I have also run a small public evening salon attached to whatever piece I happen to be reporting — twenty-odd readers, a couple of graduate students, one or two working engineers, most of them scientifically literate but none of them physicists. This month's session runs ninety minutes in the back room of a bookshop, and it is attached to a long feature I am writing on the physics of information. The topic is Maxwell's demon, which creates a problem the usual classroom version of this exercise doesn't have: nearly everyone in the room has already heard the name, and roughly half of them have absorbed the pop-science moral, which is that information has a thermodynamic price and the demon is billed for what it learns. That half-understanding is more obstructive than ignorance, and I would like the evening to dislodge it rather than confirm it.

So please design a game, not a demonstration. The constraints I can give you are these. Materials must cost under fifty dollars and involve nothing with a screen: index cards, tokens or poker chips, cups, dice, paper, string. No equation may appear on a board at any point, though I will allow you one written line at the very end if you think the room has earned it. The core apparatus should be a stylized one-molecule engine — some physical stand-in for a box with a partition that can be inserted and then used to push a piston — played over repeated cycles, with a token currency standing in for extractable work and a central pot standing in for the heat bath. Two features are essential to me. First, the participants must be able to test the hypothesis that the demon pays for looking, and falsify it with their own hands, before anyone says anything: I want a round in which measurement is charged for and a round in which it is free, and I want the accounting to make the difference visible rather than argued. Second, the demon's memory must be a strictly finite resource — a limited stack of blank slips, say — and wiping a used slip must be the expensive move, so that a team which refuses to wipe does not lose immediately but instead grinds to a halt when the blanks run out, and is thereby forced to confront the difference between an engine that runs in a cycle and a one-off miracle that works only as long as the notebook is deep. The token arithmetic should come out exactly break-even at best under honest play, so that no amount of cleverness inside the closed system beats it. Then plant the temptation: make it possible, and make it discoverable, that the only way to finish ahead is to import blank memory from somewhere outside the system — borrowing another team's slips, quietly reusing a written one, hoarding them between rounds without ever paying to clear them. I want at least one team to try that and to be caught, by a rule I want you to give them rather than by my intervention. Build in roles — an operator, a demon, a bookkeeper, an auditor whose job is to challenge entries — and tell me what each of them should be watching for.

What I need back, concretely: the full ruleset and setup; a materials list with quantities; a timeline for the ninety minutes that leaves real space for the debrief; the payoff accounting in a form I can print on a single card; the debrief itself, which should map specific moments of the evening onto the ideas they instantiate — the one-molecule engine, the distinction between a record that can be reconstructed from its output and one that cannot, the claim that resetting a bit has an unavoidable dissipation cost, the reversible-computation reply to the demon, and the point that a blank memory is itself a low-entropy resource the demon did not create and will eventually have to return; a candid list of what will go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure; an honest account of which feature of the physics the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself — I expect that includes the fact that the cost is not a toll booth but a consequence of squeezing phase space and dumping heat somewhere, that the game simply stipulates the principle it ought to be deriving, that my tokens fudge together work, heat, and information, and that the fluctuation-theorem-era refinements allow individual runs of a real feedback-driven engine to beat the bound transiently even though the average holds; and a script for the skeptic, because there is always one, who says the whole thing is rigged since I wrote the rules and the rules encode the conclusion. Tell me whether he is right and what I should concede. Finally, and this is the part that is actually for me rather than for the room: note where you expect participants to get stuck, and what their stuckness will look like in practice — the wrong inference they draw, the question they ask instead of the one you wanted. Where people jam is usually the spine of the article, and I would rather find out on Tuesday night than in draft. Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; I need something I can run, not something I can look up.
