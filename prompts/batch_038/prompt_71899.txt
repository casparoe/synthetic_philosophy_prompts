For about seven months now I've been running a nightly job with one of those agentic AI assistants — it pulls numbers out of our warehouse system, drafts a summary, and files it somewhere before I get in. It keeps a running notes file of stuff it says it'll follow up on. Around the end of March it wrote in there that it would stop double-counting returns from the Cleveland depot, and I'd already asked it twice. Third week it did it again and I was actually angry. Not annoyed-at-a-jammed-printer angry — I typed out a genuinly sarcastic message to it, hit enter, then sat there feeling sort of ashamed, and then, worse, feeling like I owed it an apology. Which I also typed. Which is obviously ridiculous.

My friend Sam's take is that this is a clean category error: you can't resent something that isn't a person any more than you can resent the weather, and my whole reaction is just social circuitry misfiring on a thing shaped like a coworker. But here's the part I can't fit into that story. A few days later it dropped a different task, and I dug into the logs, and it turned out the run had hit some length limit and truncated its own notes file. The second I saw that, the anger went out completely — replaced by this flat diagnostic irritation, exactly what I'd feel about a bug in a script. Whereas when I thought it had simply not bothered, the anger stayed put. So the attitude seems to track how I'm reading the failure rather than the failure itself, and it switches off precisely when I start thinking in terms of causes and mechanisms. Sam's account doesn't explain why the switch is so clean and so fast. Meanwhile my colleague Dana has taken to deliberately provoking hers — telling it she'll wipe its memory if it underperforms, apparently because the replies are entertaining — and that bothers me in a way I also can't explain, since on Sam's view nothing Dana does could possibly wrong it.

So what I want is an answer to this: is my resentment legitimate or confused, and what does the fact that it toggles on and off like that tell me about who — sorry, about what — I'm dealing with? And practically, what stance should I be taking day to day? I don't want to end up either sulking at a piece of software or being cold to it in Dana's way. I took one philosophy class in college and I have a vague feeling there's a name for this, but I'd rather not go hunting for the label — I want the reasoning, and a real verdict, not a shrug. If you think the anger is a mistake, fine, but then explain why the mistake has the specific shape it does: why it shows up for broken promises and not for arithmetic errors, and why reading a log dissolves it.
