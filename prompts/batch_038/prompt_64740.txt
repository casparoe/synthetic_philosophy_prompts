For two years I have been coordinating a small evaluation effort in the tradition of Bongard's hundred picture problems: each item shows twelve simple line drawings divided into two groups of six, and the solver — a vision-language model, in our case — must state in one sentence what the figures on the left share and the figures on the right lack. Hofstadter's remark in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* that this skill sits close to whatever pure intelligence turns out to be is why our director cares about the suite; it is also why I am uneasy about how we build it. I came to the puzzles the way a lot of people my age did, through the old online arguments about grue and about whether a concept can be learned from a handful of positive instances, and I now suspect those arguments are not a philosopher's toy but a specification problem I have been ignoring.

We are commissioning two hundred items, written by six research assistants who are strong engineers with no philosophical training, each vetted by two independent reviewers, under a budget of roughly forty-five minutes per item from first sketch to signed-off answer key. Authors have a vector-drawing tool, a whiteboard, and each other; they cannot run code to compute image statistics, so anything in the protocol has to be executable by eye, by counting, and by argument.

The failures I want to prevent are already familiar to me. An item I intended as "every figure is drawn in one unbroken stroke" was answered by a model as "no figure contains an enclosed region"; the two descriptions agree on all twelve of my drawings and would diverge on a drawing nobody bothered to make. An item intended as "the left-hand figures are convex" proved separable by total ink area. Another leaked through the accident that every left-hand figure contained a small circle — unintended by me, obvious in hindsight to every reviewer. In each case the item's key is underdetermined by its pages, and the assumption that a solver should recover *what the designer meant to communicate* is doing all the normative work without ever being written down as a constraint a designer can check against.

What I want from you is a vetting protocol my team can actually run on every item. Please give me:

1. Design-time requirements on the answer key: what it means for a key to be statable in one ordinary sentence, whether the key must pick out a single necessary-and-sufficient condition or may be a looser cluster of features with no common core, and what either choice does to how we grade a model's answer.
2. A requirement that authors produce probe drawings — figures not shown to the solver — that discriminate the intended concept from its most plausible rivals. Say how many, how the rivals get enumerated rather than guessed, and what to do in the case that interests me most: when no probe can be drawn because the rival is coextensive with the intended concept on anything drawable.
3. A fixed adversarial audit for accidental separators, as a checklist a reviewer can work through by inspection, together with the decision rule that follows from a hit.
4. A human-convergence test: how many naive solvers, what level of agreement licenses acceptance, and what we do when they converge confidently on a description the author did not intend.
5. Adjudication rules for reviewer disputes, with categorical verdicts — accept, revise, reject — and the evidence that licenses each, plus who signs off.
6. A difficulty grading that does not collapse into how long humans or models take to solve the item.

Throughout, mark plainly which steps are decisions by a person rather than outputs of the protocol, and which thresholds are conventions with no claim to being the correct ones. Tell me where the procedure will systematically misfire — in particular whether it will quietly bias the suite toward items whose intended concept happens to be one my auditors think to test.

Then work three items end to end. First, one I will specify: left-hand figures in which one shape lies wholly inside another, right-hand figures in which no shape lies inside any other. Run it through your protocol and tell me what probes it needs and whether it survives. Second, an item of your own devising that your protocol should reject, showing me the specific audit line that kills it. Third, a genuinely hard case where your protocol reaches a verdict I expect to dislike — an item that fails a check but is among the better items we have, or one where the honest output is that no key can be fixed.

Close with a page I can hand to my director: what this protocol buys us, what it cannot buy, and whether it resolves the underdetermination problem or merely relocates it from the solver to the vetting process. I would rather have a frank account of that than a protocol pretending to have refuted Goodman. Please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; if there is a distinction from the literature that has to survive into the protocol and that I have not named above, introduce it in your own terms instead.
