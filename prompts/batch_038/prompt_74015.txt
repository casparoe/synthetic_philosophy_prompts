I cover research policy for a general-interest science magazine, and I am writing about a proposal that would in effect equalise childhood environments across a whole population: income transfers, universal high-quality early education, nutrition, lead abatement, school funding. One commentator has argued such a programme cannot narrow the spread of adult cognitive test scores by much, because twin studies put the heritability of those scores around 0.6 and heritable variation is variation the environment cannot touch.

Let me state the claim precisely. Call it H: suppose a programme of that kind genuinely succeeded in levelling family and school environments across a population, and suppose h² = 0.6 beforehand; then the variance of adult test scores in the levelled population would be at least 20 per cent lower than before.

Give me your credence in H as a single number, then decompose it into separate terms — whether a twin-derived 0.6 is roughly unbiased here (equal-environments assumption, assortative mating, passive gene–environment correlation), whether the stipulated levelling is achievable in the residual variance at all, whether h² licenses any counterfactual about a changed environment, and any further term you think belongs. One line of justification for each, then show how they combine. Finish with the term your number is most sensitive to, and the single piece of evidence that would move it furthest. Please answer in British English.
