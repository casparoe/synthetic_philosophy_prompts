so me and my friend Dan got three hours deep in the pub last night about whether England should disestablish the Church of England, and I've come away genuinely unsure who won. His whole case rested on an analogy he obviously thought was decisive, and I want it properly taken apart before I concede anything.

Dan's line: an established church is like the village post office, or the last pub in the village. Almost nobody uses it — a handful of regulars, and everyone else turns up only for a wedding, a funeral, or a national emergency. But it holds the place together, and if it shut, the people who never once set foot inside would be loudest about the loss. So counting how many people actually go is the wrong test, exactly as counting pints sold is the wrong test for whether a village needs a pub: you judge it by what its absence would cost. On that basis he says establishment isn't a justice problem at all, just a taste problem, no different from moaning that the post office is a waste of money.

Then he brought in a second analogy for the funding side. Look at Germany, he said, where the state collects the church tax straight out of your income tax and you leave by filing a form. That's just a subscription, like a gym membership, and nobody thinks a gym membership constitutes an establishment of fitness.

My counter was that it's more like a nationalised utility you're auto-enrolled in and can't switch supplier on — except this one also gets two dozen-odd seats in the upper house and a hand in who becomes head of state. At which point Dan said I'd taken his own analogy and put it in a bad mood, because it's structurally identical with the valuation flipped, and I hadn't actually answered him.

what I want from you:

1. correspondence tables for all three — post office/pub, gym membership, nationalised utility. what plays the part of the non-user, the service obligation, the subsidy, the exit door, the regulator, and the thing being provided.
2. where each mapping breaks, and — this is the bit I actually care about — sort the breaks into ones that matter for what Dan used the analogy to do versus ones that are just decorative. my suspicion is that the pub analogy is answering a different objection than the one I was pressing: that it defeats "the church is useless" and does nothing whatever against "the church is unfair to the rest of us", and that he slid between those two all evening.
3. what each analogy quietly imports that the target lacks. especially: can the fact that non-users value a pub without using it carry the extra weight he needed — that everyone pays, that it holds constitutional privilege, and that it's the same body standing for the whole country? a pub doesn't claim to be true about anything and doesn't get a say in the coronation.
4. he made an extension move I couldn't answer: if the logic works for one provider it works for any number, so the state should recognise and fund several churches on the same basis, and disestablishmentarians can't refuse without giving up the pub logic entirely. does the analogy survive being multiplied, or does multiplying break the exact feature — one shared thing, one village — that made it appealing in the first place?
5. on the German bit: is a subscription still a subscription when the state's tax machinery collects the dues and leaving means filing with a court or registry office rather than cancelling with a company? and does it matter that the church he was defending isn't the one being funded that way — that in England the support isn't cash but status, which is much harder to opt out of?
6. and finally, is there a better analogy for establishment that does the work he wanted without importing the stuff that makes it collapse? tell me whether some weaker version of the pub one survives — like, maybe it licenses keeping the buildings open and the pastoral provision going, but not the seats in the legislature.

don't send me a reading list, I just want the reasoning.
