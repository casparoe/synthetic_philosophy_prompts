I write about the science of belief and the philosophy of religion for a general-interest science desk, and for the past year I have been reporting on the hiddenness argument: the inference that runs from the claim that a perfectly loving God would want a personal relationship with every person capable of one, through the claim that such a relationship requires the person to believe that God exists, to the observation that some people are capable of that relationship and have never resisted it yet do not believe, and thence to the conclusion that no perfectly loving God exists. Since J. L. Schellenberg set it out in the early 1990s, the argument has been restated by its author and met by a fairly stable set of replies: that relationship does not in fact require propositional belief, or requires something weaker such as trust or a readiness to be addressed; that God might have reasons to withhold evidence that would otherwise be overpowering, including reasons bound up with the moral formation of the person; that resistance can be unconscious, so nobody is positioned to say from the outside who the nonresistant nonbelievers are; and that if the argument succeeded it would also undercut the grounds on which many believers take themselves to be in relationship with God at all. As far as I can tell, nobody has moved, and the author has answered each objection in turn without abandoning the argument.

I had assumed the impasse would sit where the problem of evil's does, in disputes over how much we can know of God's reasons. Having read the exchanges twice and interviewed people on both sides of the pews, I now doubt that this is the main fault line.

Here is material that I do not think has entered the literature in this form. Over eighteen months I spoke to two groups whose first-person testimony is nearly interchangeable and whose self-descriptions are opposite. The first: people who left committed Christian practice after years of what they describe as sincere seeking. Almost all report that the final phase of their belief was marked by prayer that felt unanswered, by a sense of speaking into an empty room, and by the absence of any particular piece of counter-evidence; several cannot identify a moment of decision, only a fade. When I asked whether they had believed God existed during the last year before that fade, about half hesitated, and two said they thought they had gone on assenting to the proposition for months after it had stopped organising anything in their lives. The second: retreat directors in a Carmelite contemplative tradition, several with thirty or forty years of practice, who describe long stretches in which God is wholly absent — no consolation, no sense of presence, no answer — but who reject, sometimes emphatically, being described as unbelievers during those stretches, insisting that what was missing was experience rather than belief, and that belief for them names a standing orientation of the will towards God rather than a felt state or a disposition to assent.

What strikes me is that I could have swapped transcripts between the two groups without a reader noticing, yet one group counts itself as having come to lack belief without ever having resisted, and the other counts itself as never having lacked belief at all. That suggests the parties are not chiefly disagreeing about God's reasons; they are quantifying over different populations when they say that there are people who do not believe God exists, because they operate with different criteria for who falls under that description — avowal, felt presence, disposition to assent, orientation of the will — and the argument's crucial datum is supplied by whichever criterion is adopted.

My hypothesis, offered as something to be tested rather than defended: the disagreement is not primarily probabilistic but concerns what kind of state belief that God exists is supposed to be. If so, the argument's central claim and the central objection to it may not be contradictories at all, and thirty years of stalemate would signal not that the question is hard but that two questions are being asked inside one sentence. I may well be wrong. It may be a substantive dispute about how much we can infer concerning divine reasons; or a classification problem that is genuinely intractable, since no third-person method sorts the sincere seeker from the self-deceived one; or the two sides may be answering different questions — one about whether theism is probable, one about what the experience of religious absence means — that have simply been filed under a shared heading.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on theism, and not a survey of the argument and its replies. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side says and does: where the fault line runs; whether it is verbal or substantive; whether some hidden empirical question about belief, avowal, or the phenomenology of absence is doing the real work; whether the impasse marks an ill-posed question or merely a hard one. Say explicitly whether my interview material tells for or against each candidate diagnosis, including the possibility that it is journalistically evocative and philosophically inert. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction, a reformulation, a change of question, an empirical protocol, a formal result about the probabilistic structure of the inference — or argue that nothing would move it and explain why the debate nonetheless persists. I am after your own reasoning, not references or a reading list. Please write in British English.
