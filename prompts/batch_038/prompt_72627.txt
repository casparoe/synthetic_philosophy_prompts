I write novels — literary-ish, small presses, twelve years at it — and I've got a short story I think is the best thing I've ever written, and it has a problem. It's going to a magazine I'll just call the Quarterly: three editors, and every issue they run a "notes from the slush pile" column where they actually explain a handful of their decisions about machine-assisted work, which is unusually decent of them. My agent says submit it and don't overthink, my husband says pull it, and I want a third opinion before I decide. Their rulings over the past year, as near as I can reconstruct them from the column:

1. Accepted, no note needed: a story whose writer said in her cover letter that she'd run the draft through a tool configured to highlight weak verbs and any sentence over forty words, that she turned the rewrite suggestions off, and that she fixed every flagged spot herself.
2. Rejected: a poem where the conceit, the images and the title were all the poet's, but she'd had a model write the third of five stanzas — the one carrying the turn the ending depends on — and she changed two words in it. The board's note said the hinge of that poem was not the poet's.
3. Accepted with a one-line note: a memoir essay where the writer had a model read and compress forty years of city council minutes and local papers, then wrote every sentence of the essay herself and went back to the originals for anything she quoted.
4. Rejected: a story the writer described as a duet — he and a model alternated paragraphs, he wrote the first and the last, and he kept the best lines out of the model's four middle ones because he liked them too much to change them.
5. Accepted with an editors' note: a new translation of a public-domain story, model first pass, then a complete human retranslation worked against the original; they said the published text shared no sentence with the machine draft and that the writer had used it the way she'd use a crib.
6. And in the same column they wrote a line I keep turning over: that they aren't in the business of asking whether a piece could have been made without a machine, they're asking whether a person made it.

Here's my story. I wrote a 6,000-word draft entirely by hand, no tools, about my grandmother's dry-cleaning shop — competent, lifeless, I knew it. Then I fed it to a model with one instruction: tell me what this is actually about, and propose three restructurings, one of which I would never choose. Its diagnosis — that the story isn't about my grandmother at all, it's about my own refusal to inherit the shop, and she's barely in it — was the truest thing anyone has said to me about my work in years, and it changed what I believed the piece was. So I cut 4,000 words, reordered what was left, wrote about 2,500 new ones, and used none of its language: not a phrase, not a title, not a name, not one of its metaphors. The shape I landed on is the restructuring it offered as the one I'd never choose. My cover letter discloses all of this in detail, including the instruction I gave it.

Pretend you're an editor who's spent twenty years on submission panels and also does philosophy of art on the side — you know the creativity debates, the authorship and originality stuff, without needing me to name names. Think it through privately first: brainstorm every rule that could generate all six precedents before you look at my story, because I suspect at least two of them survive the whole set and then split on mine. Then give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the candidate rules that are consistent with all six precedents, stated so I can tell them apart; (2) what each one predicts the board will do with my story; (3) your best guess at the actual verdict, which precedent is the closest analog to mine, and why you favor that rule over the others; (4) what principle the board would be committing itself to by ruling each way — what a rejection entrenches, what an acceptance gives up; and (5) the one further submission that would decisively separate the rules, plus a yes/no on whether my hunch that it involves flipping which layer is machine-made actually discriminates. Don't hedge and don't tell me it depends — pick a verdict and own it.
