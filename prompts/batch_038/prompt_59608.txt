For two years I've been the person coding societies as tight or loose for a cross-cultural psych lab — you know the framework, the one that came out of that big multinational study in Science around 2011 where tight means the norms are strong and widely shared and deviants get punished or shunned fast, loose means people basically do as they like. Our coding manual is a page and a half: rate norm strength, punishment severity, and tolerance of deviance across a bunch of domains, average them, weight the domains equally, done. The problem is my PI doesn't actually code that way, and she's on sabbatical for the semester so I can't just ask her. I've been keeping a private log of every case where she overruled or "adjusted" our aggregate scores, because for the renewal I have to argue the project is internally consistent and eventually write a manual that describes what she really does.

Her track record so far:

1. Affluent island city-state, extremely dense formal regulation of everyday life — dozens of domains, instant standardized fines, compliance measured at basically 100%. But almost no informal sanctioning: locals barely react when someone breaks a norm, and our survey item on tolerance of deviance scored very high. Manual said tight. She scored it loose, and wrote that compliance nobody has to enforce isn't constraint.

2. Pastoral region with chronic drought and a long history of raiding. Norms script nearly every hour of the day and are near-universally endorsed, but the only sanctioning body is an elders' council that meets seasonally, so enforcement is erratic and actual deviance is rampant. Manual said tight. She said loose-ish: they're tight in aspiration, loose in practice.

3. Mid-sized industrial country where the aggregate landed dead center. She called it clearly tight, and the reason she gave was that there's almost no variation in how people behave across situations — the same person acts the same way at a funeral, a football match, and a tax office. Narrow behavioral bandwidth, she called it.

4. Typhoon-battered fishing archipelago. Only about five norms are strong at all — crew assignment, storm readiness, sharing the catch — but those five are absolute and breaking one gets you expelled from the harbor. Everything else is unregulated. Manual said loose. She said tight, and wrote that the five norms are the ones keeping people alive.

5. Wealthy capital where political speech is heavily policed and punished severely, but dress, religion, sexuality, business hours, noise, public conduct are all wide open with huge visible variety and near-zero sanctioning for lifestyle deviance. Coders wanted loose on the aggregate. She said tight — in the only domain that matters to whether the place stays intact.

6. Remote highlands, no state presence, sanctions limited to gossip and ostracism, ritual norms incredibly elaborate but wildly different from one valley to the next. Manual said loose, she agreed, noted there's simply no mechanism.

7. Society with a devastating famine and civil war three generations back, so threat history scores through the roof, but today norm strength is low, punishment is mild, tolerance of deviance is high. Manual said loose. She agreed: the threat was then, what matters is what's constraining behavior now.

8. A wealthy university town that scores loose on everything except a handful of very strict, very strongly enforced norms about queueing and noise after ten. She scored it loose overall, and specifically declined to treat it like case 4. I have no idea why 4 and 8 come out different and I've stopped trying to guess on my own.

The one I have to submit by Friday: a privately governed special economic zone on leased land, about 400,000 residents from thirty-odd countries, no state at all, just a corporate charter. The operating company has written rules covering hundreds of micro-behaviors — bin lids, balcony plants, decibel ceilings, guest registration windows, scooter speeds, recycling streams, even a mandatory format for reporting your neighbors' violations. Enforcement is fully automated, cameras and sensors and fines deducted straight from your account, instant and exceptionless. Measured compliance is essentially perfect. But nobody knows or cares what the norms are; tolerance of deviance scores very high because residents say they wouldn't dream of judging a neighbor over anything; there is zero informal sanctioning, no gossip, no ostracism, no shame — and yet on the self-report items residents say they feel watched constantly and describe their own behavior as extremely constrained. Also, they rate their zone as more precarious than anywhere else in our whole sample: the lease could fail to renew, nobody has citizenship rights, everybody depends on one employer, and there's a genuine coordination problem across thirty language groups with no shared norms to fall back on. The manual's aggregate comes out mid-range and half the coding team wants tight, half wants loose.
