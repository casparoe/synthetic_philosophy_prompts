Analyze the collapse of non-unanimous criminal juries in Louisiana and Oregon as a test case for the Condorcet jury theorem. The facts you need: Louisiana entrenched the practice in its 1898 constitution — nine of twelve jurors could convict at the time, ten of twelve by the time Evangelisto Ramos was convicted in 2016; Oregon's rule dates to a 1934 constitutional amendment requiring ten of twelve for every felony except first-degree murder; courts in both states acknowledged that race was a motivating factor in adopting the rules; Louisiana voters abolished the rule prospectively in 2018; and when the Supreme Court overruled its own 1972 decisions in Ramos v. Louisiana in 2020, it reasoned from history and stare decisis rather than from any claim about how accurate split verdicts are. A year later it held that Ramos does not reach convictions already final on federal collateral review.

Do the mapping first. What is the binary question in this setting, who are the voters, and what does the independent correct answer the theorem posits amount to in a criminal trial? Then audit each assumption — independence, common competence above chance, sincere voting, fixed group size — against how those juries actually ran, and do the arithmetic: under the theorem's own assumptions, what does moving from unanimity to ten of twelve do to the probability of convicting a guilty defendant and to the probability of convicting an innocent one, and why does the ratio between those two numbers, rather than either of them, matter to a system that demands proof beyond reasonable doubt?

Say plainly where the theorem contributes nothing: hung juries, which its binary framing excludes; deliberation, which destroys independence; and rules engineered to correlate votes along a racial line, which is not a competence problem the theorem can absorb. Then run the analysis in the other direction — does this episode confirm the theorem as a guide to institutional design, expose an assumption that was doing invisible work, or mark out a class of aggregation problems, rules chosen to entrench a majority's power rather than to track truth, that it was never built to describe?

Throughout, keep what is settled separate from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this: the arithmetic that follows from stated assumptions is not in dispute, but whether those assumptions describe any real jury very much is. Do not present a contested view as the consensus. Close with a table auditing the assumptions, one row each, with these columns: assumption as the theorem needs it; verdict for these juries (satisfied, violated, or indeterminate); the specific feature of Louisiana and Oregon practice that decides the verdict; and what that verdict does to the theorem's applicability here.
