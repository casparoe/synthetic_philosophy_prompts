In three weeks I am giving a departmental colloquium in a history department — not a complex-systems venue, and not a social-science methods series — and rather than the usual slides-and-prayers approach I would like to build a proper Q&A prep sheet around it. The format is forty minutes of talk and twenty of discussion. The room will be mostly early modern social and political historians: a specialist in seventeenth-century French fiscal administration, someone who works on the Ming–Qing transition, and an Ottomanist whose dissertation was on provincial tax farming. To these I should add two economic historians who do genuinely quantitative work but of the cliometric, identification-strategy variety and who regard time-series modelling of anything before 1800 as an exercise in false precision; one philosopher of science who works on model-based inference and measurement in the historical disciplines and who will care about confirmation rather than narrative; and a scattering of graduate students, several of whom are writing dissertations built on correspondence, court records, and chronicles and are primed to defend them. Nobody in the audience works on cliodynamics. Most have encountered it only through press coverage, which means they have absorbed the confident headlines about cycles and forecasting without any of the mechanism, and their default commitments are source criticism, contingency, and a strong allergy to what they would call physics envy.

My thesis is that structural-demographic theory does real explanatory work and is not merely a redescription of what historians already knew in narrative form. The argument I will lay out has four steps. First, agrarian bureaucratic polities exhibit recurrent long-duration sequences — demographic expansion, popular immiseration, elite numerical expansion and fragmentation, fiscal strain, and eventually state breakdown and depopulation — the pattern Goldstone assembled from early modern cases and Turchin generalized into the secular-cycle framework. Second, the timing of breakdowns tracks elite-side variables (credentialled and office-seeking aspirants relative to available positions, wealth concentration at the top, state fiscal capacity) considerably better than it tracks popular misery alone. Third, these relationships have been coded across enough polities, with enough explicit attention to source uncertainty, to support the published cross-cultural result — the 2018 PNAS analysis of the Seshat databank — that a single dimension of sociopolitical complexity structures most of the global variation in social organization. Fourth, the framework's forward-looking test, namely the short 2010 piece in Nature forecasting a decade of growing instability in the United States and western Europe together with the retrospective assessment of that forecast published in 2020, counts as at least a partial instance of out-of-sample prediction rather than curve-fitting. I will also mention, briefly and with some embarrassment, that several of the underlying intuitions go back to Ibn Khaldun's account of dynastic rise, group solidarity, and decay.

What I want from you is the objection set I should actually expect in this room, ordered by how likely each objection is and by how much damage it would do to the talk if I fumbled it — not the objections a modelling audience would raise, and not the ones generated by the popular-press controversy about predicting the 2020s. Phrase each objection in the voice and vocabulary of the person most likely to raise it: the fiscal historian will not say what the philosopher of science says, and the cliometrician will say something quite different again, so the wording matters as much as the content. For each one, give me a two-minute reply I could realistically deliver standing up; a note on which parts of the objection I should concede inside the talk itself, early, in order to buy credibility; and an honest assessment of where my reply is softest, distinguishing softness that is merely rhetorical from softness that is structural and cannot be argued away in a colloquium. Then tell me the single concession I have to make in the first ten minutes and the single claim I must not surrender, since losing either would collapse the rest of the argument. Finally, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one for which there is no good answer in twenty minutes — together with what I should say to survive it without appearing evasive, and a note on what I would have to change in the research program for that question to stop being fatal.
