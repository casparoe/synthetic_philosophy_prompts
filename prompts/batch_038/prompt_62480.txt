I cover animal minds and food systems for a general-interest magazine — a beat that sits uncomfortably between behavioural science and ethics — and I am currently reporting a long feature on the expansion of aquaculture. After a recent column on crustacean slaughter drew letters accusing me simultaneously of sentimentalising invertebrates and of applying a double standard to fish, my editor has suggested I publish a short sidebar setting out my working assumptions explicitly, so that readers can see where the reasoning starts rather than inferring it from my choice of adjectives. Before I draft that sidebar, I would like the underlying commitments audited rather than summarised. Here is the package as I presently hold it.

(1) Evidentially, I write about animal experience the way I would write about a contested result in physics: I report what published, replicable behavioural and physiological studies support, and I treat anthropomorphic interpretation as the default error rather than the default assumption.
(2) Accordingly, when I cover the long-running dispute over whether fish feel pain — including the critics who argue that the observed responses are nociceptive reflexes unsupported by the integrative neural machinery that, in mammals, goes with suffering — I present it as genuinely unresolved, and I do not assert in print that fish are in pain.
(3) At the same time, I have written without hedging that boiling a crab or a lobster alive is indefensible, and I have argued for a legal requirement that decapods sold for food be stunned first, partly on the strength of the independent evidence review commissioned by the UK government ahead of its animal sentience legislation, which concluded that decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs should be treated as sentient.
(4) My stated rule for cases like (3) is precautionary: when the evidence is genuinely balanced and a decision has to be made now, the uncertainty should be resolved in the animal's favour.
(5) I also reject a particular kind of negative argument. The claim that fish cannot suffer because they lack the cortical structure that subserves suffering in mammals strikes me as a non sequitur — evolution produces convergent solutions, and the absence of one lineage's implementation of a capacity is not evidence that the capacity is absent.
(6) Nevertheless I eat mussels and oysters, and I have told readers that bivalve farming is the nearest thing to a free lunch in animal agriculture, on the grounds that bivalves have no centralised nervous system to speak of.
(7) On the shape of welfare itself, I hold that what matters is whether an animal's life is on balance worth living — space, water quality, disease, handling, transport — and that the manner of death is a comparatively small part of the total.
(8) Even so, most of my reporting effort in this area has gone on slaughter: stunning equipment, the absence of stunning requirements for farmed crustaceans, chilling in ice slurry. I have twice described the lack of any general stunning mandate as the most shameful gap in aquaculture regulation.
(9) Finally, I have written that the sheer growth of aquaculture is a moral catastrophe in aggregate, because of the number of individual animals it brings into existence and kills.

What I want back is an audit, not a defence. Go through these and tell me which pairs actually pull against one another and which only appear to. I suspect that some of the apparent conflicts are not real — (2) and (3), for instance, feel to me like one standard applied to two different kinds of output, a claim about the world in the one case and a recommendation about what to do in the other — but I would rather be told whether that defence holds or whether it is a rationalisation, and I would like to know whether my characterisation of the fish evidence in (2) as merely unresolved rather than as balanced is doing quiet work for (4). For each genuine tension, say what has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list — I am particularly interested in whether any single commitment, once amended, would force amendments to three or four others. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging, and end by naming the one change that would clean up the most. I am not asking which of these views is true, and I have no idea yet which of them I would be most willing to abandon, so do not assume I have a favourite.
