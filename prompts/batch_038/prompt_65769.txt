I would like help determining whether a structural feature of certain decision problems has an established name, and, if it does not, what it ought to be called. I will describe it twice: once in a case I have worked out numerically, and once in a case where someone produces the situation on purpose.

**Case 1.** A courier drives a night route that passes under the same road twice, at two underpasses that are indistinguishable in the dark. He must leave the road at the second one. Taking the first puts him on a service road from which he has to double back (worth 1); taking the second completes the delivery (worth 6); taking neither means the delivery fails (worth 0). Because he cannot tell the two apart, whatever he does at one he does at both, so his only genuine choice is a single probability q of turning, applied at whichever underpass he happens to be standing at. The best such policy is q = 7/12, worth 7q − 6q² = 49/24, about 2.04.

The part I want named is this. Standing at an underpass, the courier has no evidence favouring one over the other, so the natural way to think is: half the time this is the first, half the time the second. Turning is then worth 0.5·1 + 0.5·6 = 3.5, while continuing is worth 0.5·(6·7/12) = 1.75, so turn. But turning is itself a policy, and the policy of turning at every underpass is worth 1 — worse than the 2.04 he would get by following q = 7/12. The reasoning that seems mandatory at the moment of action is worse than the plan it undermines, if that reasoning is adopted at every moment at which it seems mandatory. Oddly, the discrepancy vanishes if he derives his odds about which underpass he is at from the policy itself instead of assuming they are even (12/17 and 5/17, at which point turning and continuing both come out to 42/17) — but I cannot see what entitles him to do that, since the policy is precisely what he is in the middle of choosing.

**Case 2.** An acquaintance of mine pays a fixed sum into an account on a schedule he deliberately does not track: he has the statements destroyed and never checks the running total. His reason is not fear of temptation, since he cannot touch the money either way. It is that he has noticed a thought pattern — when he knows he has already made several payments in a row, the reflection that he has done enough and that one omission will not matter becomes hard to resist, and any rule permitting an omission whenever that reflection is available is not a rule he wants to hold. So what he removes from himself is not information about the value of his options. It is information about where in the sequence he stands. He renders his own occasions indistinguishable in order to keep one rule applying to all of them.

What I keep needing a term for is the shared feature: situations in which an agent's evidence about which of several indistinguishable occasions he occupies is itself a product of the policy he is evaluating, so that the ordinary question — given where I am, what should I do? — cannot be asked in that order, and the choice has to be made once, as a rule covering all the occasions together. Two features strike me as distinctive. Nothing about the payoffs is hidden; the agent knows the whole structure, and what he lacks is his own place in it. And the ignorance is sometimes an achievement rather than a defect, produced not by removing options but by removing the discrimination between occasions.

I know roughly where the neighbourhood lies. There is a well-known puzzle about a driver and two identical exits, of which I take case 1 to be a variant; there are terms for plans an agent would later want to abandon; terms for restricting your own options in advance; and terms for preferring not to know things. None of these seems to be what I am after, but I may be wrong.

Please do the following:

(a) Identify the established concepts that come closest — I would expect at least three or four — and state for each precisely where the fit breaks down.

(b) Say whether any of them, singly or combined, actually covers what I have described, and in particular whether one notion can cover both the involuntary and the deliberate forms or whether two are needed.

(c) Address directly whether this is nothing over and above the familiar injunction to choose policies rather than acts, or whether the policy-dependence of the agent's evidence about his own location adds something that injunction does not already capture.

(d) If nothing established covers it, coin a term: supply a definition, a test for deciding whether a given case falls under it, and two cases that resemble it closely but do not qualify.

(e) Tell me separately whether the move in case 1 — forming one's odds about which occasion one is in from the policy one is choosing — has a name of its own, since the whole difficulty seems to turn on it.

I am not after a bare label. The point is the carving: what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges fall, and I would rather have that spelled out than be handed a piece of vocabulary.

Your final line must be exactly of the form

Verdict: <term>

giving the single best label for the phenomenon — the established term if one genuinely applies, otherwise the term you have coined.
