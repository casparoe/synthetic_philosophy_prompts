I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and for a piece on machine-written news copy I spent several weeks with a philosopher who works inside the Nyāya tradition — the school that recognizes four routes to knowledge: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony, and that denies independent status to postulation and to non-apprehension, folding them into the others. To keep my reporting honest I catalogued this scholar's verdicts on a series of cases, in their own glosses:

1. Smoke rising above a hill: knowledge of fire, by inference.
2. A traveler told by a forester that a wild ox resembles a cow, who later meets the animal and connects the name to the kind: knowledge, by comparison, and a route not reducible to inference or to testimony.
3. The heavy man who eats nothing by day: the conclusion that he eats at night is knowledge, but it arrives by inference; postulation is not a fifth route.
4. The bare floor where a jar was expected: knowledge of the absence, but by perception of the floor so qualified; non-apprehension is not a separate route.
5. A headman's written order read aloud by a clerk who does not know what it concerns: the villagers know what it says, because the author, not the voice, is the trustworthy source.
6. A merchant's ledger, copied faithfully through four hands from an entry made by the man who weighed the goods: knowledge, even though the present copyist has no contact with the facts.
7. A known liar's true statement: the hearer understands the sentence but does not thereby know it.
8. A trained bird repeating a sentence it has been conditioned to utter: nothing known, however accurate the content.

Now the case I actually have to write about. A transit authority publishes a status line on its website that is composed automatically from live sensor feeds and from maintenance crews' logged reports; no human reviews any individual line. The authority issues it under its own name, keeps a duty officer nominally responsible for the system, and has a five-year record of accuracy that I checked myself. Today the line says the riverside bridge is closed. It is closed. I could not verify it independently — the bridge was out of sight and there was no second source — and I formed the belief, rerouted, and filed the item.

Would this scholar say I knew the bridge was closed, and if so by which route? I want the candidate rules that fit the whole verdict set, with a note on which verdicts each explains and which it strains; what each rule predicts about my case; your best guess with reasons, identifying the closest analog on the list and saying what would have to be added to or removed from my case to flip the verdict; a further case, not on my list, that would separate the surviving rules; and, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine this, an account of which principle the scholar would be committing to under each option. Finally: if machine-composed sentences can transmit knowledge without a knower behind each one, what becomes of the school's insistence that an authorless text transmits nothing — the line it uses against the rival tradition that treats scripture as beginningless and still authoritative?
