Ok this is a weird one, but I've been reading Hume's essay on the standard of taste (the 1750s one, in the Essays collection) over the weekend and I'm stuck on a passage with nobody around to ask. I'm a software engineer, zero philosophy background, so please don't assume I know the vocabulary or the secondary literature.

Why I'm even reading it: my team builds a system that scores short-form video on quality — production value, whether it's engagement bait, whether it holds up on a second watch. Two camps. One says just average all the user ratings, taste is taste, aggregate it and be done. The other says no, you have to filter the raters — train a panel, screen for calibration, treat the panel's verdict as the label, because raw averages get gamed. Someone dropped a Hume line into the Slack thread to win the argument and I went and read the actual essay, and now I'm less sure than both of them.

The bit I'm stuck on is the paragraph where he says what entitles someone to be a judge of taste. My copy is a garbage OCR scan so I can't paste it cleanly, here's my paraphrase: the qualities that entitle critics to this character are strong sense combined with delicate feeling, sharpened by practice, completed by comparing lots of works against each other, and cleared of prejudice; and the joint verdict of such people, wherever they're to be found, is the true standard of taste and beauty.

What I'd like help with:

1. Is my read fair that this is basically calibrated annotators? Like, you can't state the target directly, so you define it as whatever a filtered set of raters converges on, and you select the raters on features you can actually measure. If that's right, does his standard end up being a procedure rather than a fact about beauty — i.e. is he closer to my first camp or my second, or is that the wrong question entirely?

2. The circularity bothers me. He seems to identify the true judges partly by which works have kept being admired across centuries (the Homer example, still admired in modern capitals despite changes in religion and government and language), and he identifies the great works by the judges' verdict. In metrology we'd say he's missing a primary standard — there's no traceability chain back to something independent. Does he ever break that circle, or is he knowingly offering a limit case you approach but never reach, like a frictionless plane? And if it is a limit case, is that enough to do the sorting work he wants it to do? Related: he also says you should make allowances for the manners of the age a work was written in, which sounds like the judge has to be cleared of his own prejudices while simultaneously discounting the author's — I can't tell if that's a coherent instruction or a fudge.

3. The wine story — Sancho's two kinsmen in Don Quixote, one says the wine is good but tastes of iron, the other says good but tastes of leather, everyone scoffs because the cask is clean, and then when it runs out they find an old key with a leather thong at the bottom. That's a physical check: you can go look and find the key. There's no key in a poem. Does he notice? Or is the story maybe only about sensitivity — detecting faint signal that others miss — and not about correctness, in which case it's doing much less work in the argument than the Slack quote implied?

4. What does delicacy actually mean for him — resolution, like an instrument that picks up small differences, or selectivity, like someone hard to please? I have a feeling he uses the same word elsewhere about the passions, where it means something like easily wounded by life, which feels like the opposite of what you'd want in a judge.

5. Earlier in the essay he makes what I think is the move that every sentiment is right, since a feeling doesn't reach beyond itself, while judgments can be wrong because they're answerable to something outside. How is that consistent with some tastes being flat-out better than others? Is it that the felt response is incorrigible but the judgement built on top of it isn't? And if that's the shape of it, which of my two camps does it favor?
