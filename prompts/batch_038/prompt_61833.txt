I am a second-year graduate student in philosophy, and my metaphysics reading group has spent the term working through cases of qualitative indiscernibility, beginning with Black's two spheres. One member of the group — I will call her V. — has an unusually stable and unusually explicit set of verdicts, which I have been writing down after each session, because I intend to write something about the structure of this debate rather than about its outcome. Her record so far, in my own words:

1. The familiar case: a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, with nothing whatever to distinguish them except the distance between them. V. says this is a genuine possibility, and that there are two spheres. She adds that nobody's being able to tell them apart, or to point to one rather than the other, is beside the point; that objection, she says, confuses epistemology with metaphysics.

2. The same universe, except that space has no distinguished directions — no left or right, no up or down, no external frame of reference — so that there is no fact of the matter about which sphere is the one on the left. V. says: still two.

3. The same universe, except that the spheres are replaced by two exactly similar simples: no parts, no surfaces, no internal structure, nothing but their relative distance. V. says: still two.

4. A member proposed that we settle the matter by naming: stipulate that one of the spheres is called Castor, whereupon the other becomes describable as the sphere that is not Castor, so that the two are strongly discernible after all. V. rejected this as a cheat. Naming, she said, is something we do to things that are already many; it cannot be what makes them many.

5. A member proposed two exactly similar universes, each containing a single sphere, with no causal or spatiotemporal contact of any kind between them. V. said there would be two universes. Pressed on how, she said only that she saw no reason why a criterion of individuation should have to operate within a single spacetime.

6. A member proposed that any two things are distinguishable by the relation *is not identical with*, so that weak discernibility is available for free wherever there is plurality at all. V. rejected this as well: if the only thing distinguishing them is that they are not one thing, she said, then nothing has been said, and what she wants is an answer to the question of what makes them two. Asked whether that answer must always be a qualitative one, she declined to commit.

7. A sphere exists alone for a while, ceases to exist, and an exactly similar sphere comes into existence afterwards, with nothing else in the universe at any time. V. says: two spheres; the length of the gap makes no difference.

Next week the case is mine, and it is this. A universe containing no space and no time at all — nothing extended, nothing successive, no distances, no before or after — but exactly two simples, each of which is aware of the other and of nothing else. Neither is aware of itself, so awareness runs strictly between them, and no relation of any other kind holds between the two. Call them souls if you like; the mutual awareness is intended as the only handle on either of them.

I am not asking for your own opinion about the case. I want a forecast of hers. Specifically:

(a) Set out the candidate principles that are jointly consistent with verdicts 1 through 7. I have in mind something like individuation by spatiotemporal position, something like a requirement of weak discernibility by a substantive irreflexive relation, and something like the view that numerical diversity needs no qualitative ground at all — but tell me whether that is the right list, whether any of the seven verdicts already rules one of them out, and whether there is a fourth candidate I have overlooked.

(b) State what each surviving principle predicts V. will say about my case, and why.

(c) Give me your single best guess about her answer, with the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives given her record.

(d) Name the modification of my case — or a different case altogether — that would most cleanly settle which principle she is in fact running, and say what each of the surviving principles predicts about that one as well.

Finally, if your forecast is that she will say there are two, I would like the reasoning made explicit on one point: the mutual awareness in my case is, on the face of it, a relation of exactly the kind she brushed aside in verdict 6, since nothing qualitative distinguishes the two simples and the relation runs between them only. Explain what is supposed to make it non-empty by her own standard, and tell me whether verdicts 5 and 6 can both be honored by any single principle at all.
