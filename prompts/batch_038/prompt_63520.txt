I sit on the board of a fund whose three trustees hold openly incompatible probability judgments about the same prospects, with no one's treated as authoritative. I have been recording my verdicts on candidate projects and want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

First, I accept without hesitation anything that, in every state of the world any trustee regards as possible, leaves every beneficiary group at least as well off as doing nothing, and strictly better off in some state. Nobody can complain in any world they admit is live.

Second, I accept a reservoir that gives every household in the valley a reliable supply and carries a one-in-five-hundred chance of a spill that ruins one farm. The farmer sits on the board and votes for it. Her expected gain is modest but real; in the state she fears, she is far worse off than doing nothing.

Third, I reject this. Trustee A is 99 percent confident the world is one in which a joint venture pays her beneficiaries well and B's poorly; Trustee B is 99 percent confident it runs the other way. The venture raises both trustees' expected returns. Each faces a large loss in a state she concedes is possible. Structurally this is the reservoir with the single farmer replaced by two farmers trading risk, yet my verdict flips.

Fourth, I reject the proposal of a trustee who assigns one chance in a million to a hypothesis on which an experiment yields an astronomical payoff, and who wants the fund to impose a sure, modest cost on the other trustees' beneficiaries to run it. Her expected value is enormous. I cannot say how this differs from the reservoir, which also trades a small-probability extreme outcome against a certain moderate one.

Fifth, a trustee who assigns probability 0.2 to a claim that a routine vaccination program will harm his own community wants to veto it. Under every other hypothesis anyone holds, the program is safe for all. I think his veto should not be honored — which looks flatly inconsistent with the weight I gave the farmer's one-in-five-hundred.

My own first attempt at a principle is this: no one may be left worse off in a world she admits is live for the sake of gains that obtain only in worlds she is merely confident about. It condemns the reservoir, which I want to keep, and it does not explain why the 0.2 veto fails where the one-in-five-hundred succeeds.

Is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or most of them? State it precisely, identify which clause is doing the work in each case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon rather than save. Then give me its verdict on two cases I have not raised: the same modestly risky project proposed again and again in succession, each instance acceptable on its own but not jointly; and a version of the reservoir in which the farmer is offered state-contingent compensation that makes her whole exactly in the spill state at negligible expected cost. Finally, should the rule care at all about states that no trustee considers possible but which in fact obtain?

Please write in continuous prose, the way you would in a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
