I co-lead an informal reading group — eight of us: three economists, a doctor, two lawyers, a retired schoolteacher, and me — and we are closing a year on value theory with a session on hard choices. I have tried twice to write something to hand out at the end of that session and both times it came out as a shrug, so I am commissioning it instead. First, the piece of the literature I want the poem to hold. The claim is that some choices are hard not because we are ignorant, or weak-willed, or torn between options that sit very close together on a single scale, but because the ordinary three-way vocabulary — better than, worse than, exactly as good as — simply runs out. Ruth Chang's name for the leftover is parity, and the important thing about it is that she treats it as a fourth positive relation, not as the mere absence of a relation or as a mysterious unmeasurability. The route to it is the small improvement argument: if two options genuinely were equally good, then improving one of them by a hair — a slightly larger salary, a slightly shorter commute, a room with a window — ought to tip the balance; but the balance does not tip, the improved option still fails to win, and that failure is supposed to show the two were never equal in the first place. The argument iterates, which is why the situation does not feel like a tie that is merely too close to call. And of course the deflationary readings are always waiting: the values are vague, or the options are incomparable in the stronger sense that no comparison holds at all, or "hard" describes our epistemic predicament rather than anything about the values.

For the handout I want a sestina in the standard form — six sestets and a three-line envoi, thirty-nine lines, the end-words rotating in the usual pattern, in loose unrhymed lines of roughly ten to twelve syllables — and I want the form to carry the argument rather than decorate it. The six end-words should be two concrete nouns naming the two options and four words from the vocabulary of comparison, and I would like those four to be better, worse, equal, and par. Par is the load-bearing choice: it should be used so that a reader who has never met the literature takes it in its everyday senses — on level terms, the going rate, the standard score — while a reader who has met it sees the fourth relation standing there inside the same list as the other three, and the poem must never say which is meant. Since the rotation sends every end-word to every position over six turns, the comparatives keep changing places while nothing gets settled, and I want that felt rather than explained: the words trade positions and the choice does not move. One stanza, probably the third or fourth, should stage the small improvement argument concretely — one option acquires something small and unambiguously good, and the speaker notices that the addition changes nothing about which to take, which is itself the evidence that the two were never equally good. Then the envoi should be where the compression happens, all six words in three lines, three at the line-ends and three tucked inside, the one place in the poem where the whole comparative vocabulary is small enough to hold at once. It is also worth exploiting the fact that the standard pattern needs a seventh stanza to return to its opening order: the poem therefore stops one turn short of closing its own circle, and I would like that truncation to be the reason the speaker stops, not a stand-in for having decided.

For the situation, something like this: a woman in her early forties has a month to say whether she will stay in the port town she grew up in — the ferry, the house that smells of woodsmoke and salt, the mother who is beginning to need someone in the next room — or take up a chair inland, in a city she likes, doing the work she is actually good at. Both lives have to remain genuinely attractive throughout; if one side comes off as obviously worse, the poem collapses into an ordinary difficult decision, which is not the topic. Keep the technical labels out of the body: no incommensurable, no parity, no vagueness, no naming of philosophers, and no footnotes — par is the only piece of the jargon admitted, and it stays ambiguous. Do not end with the heart, or love, or courage, or both-are-gifts uplift, and do not let the six returning words become a tic that the final stanza explains away. If she does finally commit, the commitment has to read as a making rather than a finding: she does not discover that one side was better all along, she does something else, and the poem should not pretend that what she has done is the same thing as having been right. It also has to work as a poem for the three economists in the room who have never heard any of this — they should come away convinced that the tie is not a tie, without being told why.
