Five of us sit on a state environmental compliance board. Cases come one at a time, and each of us votes yes/no on two findings and then on the bottom line, which is whether there was a violation. The violation finding is supposed to fall out automatically: it holds just in case the measured emissions went over the limit AND the overage can't be pinned on a certified instrument fault. Last month we hit a wall. Real vote pattern, relabeled A through E:

- A: over-limit yes / not explained by instrument fault yes → violation yes
- B: over-limit yes / not explained by instrument fault no → violation no
- C: over-limit no / not explained by instrument fault yes → violation no
- D: over-limit yes / not explained by instrument fault yes → violation yes
- E: over-limit no / not explained by instrument fault no → violation no

Tally the findings and both come out yes 3–2, so the violation is made out. Tally the bottom line and it's no, 3–2. Same five people, same meeting, two defensible answers that contradict each other, and a company waiting on a letter. We tabled it, and now I want to know what I should have been demanding of a procedure in the first place.

So write me the spec. What does any rule for agregating individual judgments into one corporate verdict have to deliver — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not blur, the background results about voting it can't flatly contradict — with the case or consideration behind each entry, not just a label on it. And tell me which of the things people insist on here are genuinely load-bearing and which are just the taste of one camp. I specifically want a verdict on these: whether giving every member equal weight is a real requirement or something we can drop given that our chair holds a casting vote; whether the published reasoning has to be traceable to findings members actually endorsed, or whether that's a nice-to-have; whether the rule has to be hard to steer by someone voting a finding they don't believe in order to get the bottom line they want; whether the output has to be a single determinate verdict rather than a set of admissible ones; and whether anything changes because we sit again next year, with a rotating member, on a case that's nearly identical to this one.

Keep the purpose in view when you judge adequacy, because I think a lot of the disagreement in this area is people specifying for different jobs. Ours is narrow: the board has to be able to own the verdict as one agent — sign it, defend it if the company appeals, and not have to explain away a contradiction when a similar matter comes back around. Not an ideal of collective rationality in general, and not a theory of what democracy owes people.

Then the part I most want: if some cluster of these individually reasonable requirements can't all be met at once, say so, and name the smallest such cluster plus which member of it is doing the damage — that's a result about our board, not just a complaint. Also which single requirement knocks out the most candidates. After that, briefly, how the obvious contenders do against the list: decide the findings and derive the bottom line, decide the bottom line straight, or decide the findings first and only consult the bottom-line votes when they come apart. Two or three lines each is plenty, the list is the deliverable and the scorecard is secondary. No citations or further reading, please, I have no way to chase them down.
