So my reading group is doing the empire debate — Ferguson's case that the British Empire was on the whole a force for modernity, Tharoor's Inglorious Empire as the other side — and one of the guys in the group, who's a stats person and not a philosopher, came back with this argument that he says shows the whole debate is rigged and that Ferguson is right about the method even if he's wrong about the ledger. Here it is:

(1) Whether a regime's rule over a people is on balance justified depends only on how it affected them: legitimate if they ended up better off than they would otherwise have been.
(2) You can never observe the otherwise. There is no control group for a colonised country, no counterfactual India that ran its own experiment.
(3) So the only evidence anyone can ever have is the before-and-after record, and since a claim nobody could ever check is not a claim at all, the before-and-after record is what justifies or condemns a regime.
(4) Grant, just for the sake of the argument, that the record for British India is positive on the standard measures: average incomes, life expectancy and literacy all higher in 1947 than in the mid-1700s, and add the railways, the canal colonies, the census, the courts and the civil service.
(5) So British rule in India was a net credit, and the famines and the deindustrialisation are debits that don't outweigh it.
(6) Nothing in (1)–(3) is specific to India, so the same reasoning applies to any rule under which the measured averages of the subject population improved: England after the Black Death, where the survivors' real wages shot up; Mexico and Peru a century after contact, where the remaining population had far more land and livestock per head than their grandparents did.
(7) Nobody thinks that shows the plague or the conquest of the Americas was on balance a good thing for the people it happened to.
(8) Therefore (1) is false: you cannot settle the legitimacy of a regime by its effects.

The reason I'm asking rather than just telling him he's wrong is that my two instincts pull in opposite directions and I cant get them to line up. Part of me wants to grab (4) and start listing famines and the drain and textile collapse, but that feels like ducking, because I've already stipulated his numbers and the argument should still stand or fall on its own terms once I have. And part of me wants to hold onto (1) with both hands, because I'm a consequentialist and because (8) looks like it costs way more than he admits — if effects can't justify a regime, they cant justify anything, and the same move would take down public health campaigns and every welfare state on earth, which he says he doesn't want but I don't see how he escapes it. So: which step do you actually reject, and if more than one is in trouble, say which is load-bearing? For each candidate I'd like to know why the premise looked so reasonable in the first place and what giving it up costs you — especially for (1), since dropping it seems to hand the debate to the people who say morality isn't a ledger at all, and I'd need to know what a consequentialist who keeps (1) owes by way of an account of the baseline, the measure, and whose wellbeing gets counted, given that (2) says the counterfactual is unobservable. Also tell me straight whether (7) even gets him to (8), or whether the last step is a separate jump he hasn't earned.
