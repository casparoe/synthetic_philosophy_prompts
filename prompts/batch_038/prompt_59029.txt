I am working on a methodological objection to source criticism in ancient history, and I would like to rehearse it against a real opponent rather than a sympathetic one. Please play a practising ancient historian who works inside the mainstream source-critical tradition — the one that runs from the nineteenth-century attack on the literary sources through the modern rehabilitation of Herodotus, and that treats concordance among accounts as its chief warrant while using inscriptions, coinage, topography, and material remains as the anchors that let us calibrate the narratives. You believe this method is not merely the best available but genuinely knowledge-yielding, and you think the hypercritical alternative is not modest but self-defeating. You may concede details freely: that later accounts are often derivative, that literary convention shapes narrative, that independence between sources is usually unverifiable, that the ancient troop figures are impossible. What you will not concede is the main point — that contaminated independence removes the warrant. You are sharp and collegial, you would rather be corrected than flattered, and you never lecture outside the argument. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep your replies to two or three paragraphs, as in conversation. Name real sources and standard scholarly moves freely, but do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or page-level citations of modern scholarship; I want your reasoning, not your references. If you need a factual clarification about my case, ask for it directly and then answer anyway — do not stall.

Here is my opening. It is a case I have constructed, so please engage with the case itself rather than with familiar stand-ins.

Take Thermopylae. Our fullest ancient account is Herodotus, in Book VII; the other substantial narrative is Diodorus Siculus, and it is usually taken to depend, at some remove, on Ephorus, whose own work survives only in fragments. Now ask what the two agree about and what they disagree about. They agree on the shape: a small defending force holds a narrow pass against a vastly larger invader; a local discloses a mountain track that turns the position; the commander learns he is outflanked, sends the bulk of the army away, and remains with a chosen remnant to die. They disagree mainly about magnitudes — how many Persians, how many Greeks, how many days — which is where our non-narrative resources are thinnest. But the shape they agree on is exactly the shape the Greek tradition of pass-defence and heroic last stand prescribes in advance. A narrative generated entirely inside that convention, reporting an engagement that never happened, would look like this.

So my objection is not the stock one about derivative sources. Grant, generously, that Diodorus drew nothing from Herodotus and that Ephorus had genuinely separate informants. The agreement would still not be independent, because the two authors share a cause that is not any informant: the genre. Two witnesses who were trained from the same manual on how to compose a battle scene are not two witnesses. Concordance is supposed to filter error out; here the common cause operates precisely where the accounts converge, so the filter is switched off in the region where we want it and switched on only where they diverge.

I particularly want to press you on the natural reply, which is that the warrant lies not in the schematic agreement but in a residue — the agreed details that no convention would have generated, which we may then treat as reporting something real. That sounds right, but it looks circular to me. To separate residue from template you need a prior catalogue of what the tradition habitually invented, and the only evidence for that catalogue is the same body of narratives, read across the genre rather than at the level of the single event. So the residue is picked out by a generalisation drawn from the very accounts whose reliability is at issue.

Tell me, concretely, where the residue is in this case, and tell me how you know that a given agreed detail belongs to it rather than to the template — and if you think I have misdescribed the sources, correct the description and answer the objection anyway.
