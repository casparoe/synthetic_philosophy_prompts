I work in analytics at a hospital system, and a health-data newsletter I subscribe to recently ran a column on fairness impossibility results. I quoted it in a meeting about a readmission-risk model we are considering, and afterwards our machine-learning lead told me the piece was sloppy but would not say where. Here is the passage I relied on, reproduced as faithfully as I can:

"Before you deploy that readmission model, there is an impossibility result you need to understand. Suppose your model assigns each patient a probability of being readmitted within thirty days, and suppose the two populations it serves have genuinely different readmission rates — say 22 percent in one group and 11 percent in the other. There are two things you might reasonably demand of it. First, calibration: when the model says 20 percent, about 20 percent of the patients it scored that way should actually come back. Second, equal error rates: the model should wrongly flag low-risk patients as high-risk at the same rate in both groups, and should miss genuinely high-risk patients at the same rate in both groups. The results proved around 2016 by Kleinberg and his coauthors and, separately, by Chouldechova show that when base rates differ, you cannot have both. This is a theorem about numbers, not an engineering shortfall you can close with more data or a cleverer architecture. And the theorem tells you the direction of the failure: a calibrated model must generate more false positives in the higher-base-rate group — with base rates like the ones above, roughly twice as many. Some people suggest you can escape all this by applying different thresholds to different groups, but the impossibility results rule that out as well, since a model that gives two otherwise identical patients different treatment on the basis of group membership is precisely what the whole enterprise was supposed to prevent. The honest conclusion is that risk scores of this kind cannot be made fair in any coherent mathematical sense, and we should stop pretending otherwise."

I am inclined to think the bottom line is defensible even if some details are loose — that a calibrated score cannot simply be called fair and left at that — but I cannot tell which parts of the argument actually carry that conclusion and which are decoration. Could you audit the passage for me?

In doing so, please address at least the following, since these are the points I would be most embarrassed to have gotten wrong:

- Whether the impossibility results really take the two-criterion form the column gives them, and whether calibration and equal error rates are even the same kind of object — one sounds like a property of a probability score and the other like a property of a yes/no decision.
