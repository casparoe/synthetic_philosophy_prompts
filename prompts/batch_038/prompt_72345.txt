I'm preparing a session for a philosophy-of-language reading group and want to use Dickens's *A Christmas Carol* as the test case for the indicative/counterfactual divide. The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come shows Scrooge a plundered deathbed and a neglected grave, and Scrooge responds by asking the spirit whether what it shows is what will be or only what may be — a question the silent spirit never answers, although the Ghost of Christmas Present had already hedged its forecast about Tiny Tim with a clause to the effect that the child dies only if the shadows remain unaltered by the future. Read the novella against Adams's thesis that the assertibility of an indicative conditional tracks conditional probability, on one side, and similarity-based accounts of counterfactuals of the Lewis–Stalnaker kind on the other, and tell me which sort of conditional the spirits' showings actually are. The difficulty I want pressed is this: if the showings are indicative predictions, Scrooge's conversion falsifies them and the spirits have misled him; if they are counterfactuals whose antecedent is something like his going on as he has, then that antecedent is precisely what he is being invited to change, which seems to make the reassurance he takes from them vacuous. Say too what the "unless the future alters them" hedge amounts to — a ceteris paribus clause, a restrictor on a modal, or evidence that Dickens's prophecy is a self-undermining prediction rather than a conditional at all — and whether the final stave amounts to claiming that one might-have-been has been replaced by another. Don't summarize the plot or catalogue the standard accounts; argue for a reading, and say where the novella resists it. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two: one on the modal question Scrooge puts to the spirit, one on the cost of the indicative reading, one on the cost of the counterfactual reading, one on the hedge, and one verdict on whether the novella knows which kind of conditional it is trading in and what it contributes to the semantic debate that argument alone does not. No introduction or conclusion.
