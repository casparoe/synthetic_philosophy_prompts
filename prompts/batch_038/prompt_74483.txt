I am a software engineer with no training in history or moral philosophy, and the reason I am writing is that an argument I have been having for about fourteen months with a former colleague has stopped producing anything, and I would like to understand why rather than win it.

The subject is the balance sheet of the British Raj. He reads it through Niall Ferguson's Empire; I have been reading Shashi Tharoor's Inglorious Empire, and we both watched the widely circulated Oxford Union debate on reparations. His position, as I understand it: whatever the intentions, British rule left India railways, a common legal framework, secure property rights, capital markets, a professional civil service, an administrative lingua franca, and eventually representative institutions; and the only fair comparison is not India in 1750 against Britain in 1947 but India under the Raj against plausible alternatives, such as a longer-lived Maratha confederacy, a Sikh state, or a competitive scramble by other European powers. My position: the railways were financed out of Indian revenues with returns guaranteed to British shareholders and routed and priced for freight and troop movement rather than for famine relief; the law and the administration were applied unequally by race; a large share of revenue was remitted to London; Indian textile manufacture was destroyed; and famine mortality stayed high or rose while per-capita income was close to flat for most of the period.

The part that has me stuck is this. To try to settle it empirically, we built a shared spreadsheet of the underlying series — India's share of world output, per-capita income estimates, railway mileage, grain prices, famine death tolls — and the same rows are being read by each of us as support for the opposite conclusion. We have added data three times. Neither of us has moved.

My hypothesis, offered strictly as a hypothesis: we are not having a single disagreement about facts. I suspect at least three different disputes are being run under one heading — one about which counterfactual baseline is legitimate, one about whether colonial administrative statistics function as evidence or as instruments of the thing being measured, and one about whether the moral question is aggregative at all, that is, whether anything on the asset side can in principle offset a famine. I may have that wrong.
