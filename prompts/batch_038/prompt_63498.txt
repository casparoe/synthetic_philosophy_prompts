I'm preparing a framing talk for a public series our city's heritage commission is running alongside its review of a cluster of early-twentieth-century monuments, and I've decided to build the whole thing around Sophocles' Antigone. The hook is that Creon's edict is not a private decision about a corpse but a piece of commemorative policy: two brothers, who between them brought the same war down on the same city, are assigned opposite posthumous standings — one buried with full civic honors as a defender, the other denied burial and handed over to dogs and birds, with a public death penalty attached to anyone who reverses the designation. That is the structure of almost every argument we are having, where the question is not what happened but what public standing a polity should confer on the person it happened to. A classicist friend objects that the analogy is a category mistake: the Greeks were worried about passage to the underworld and about the pollution of a city that leaves its dead unburied, whereas a statue or a street name is honorific political speech, and nobody thinks an unmemorialized person is suffering in Hades. I want the talk to take that objection seriously rather than wave it away, and I'd like your help producing the reading.

Here is what I'm after. Read the play through the ethics of commemoration as it is currently argued — the distinction between honoring someone and merely recording them, the question of whether a monument endorses a whole life or only the aspect it depicts, the claim that removal is censorship set against the claim that it is simply the polity revising its own speech, and the more radical argument that democratic communities have no business with honorific monuments at all because honor as such implies hierarchy. Then map the play's voices onto that terrain: Creon's account, on which commemorative standing tracks contribution to and betrayal of the civic order; Antigone's, on which certain obligations to the dead are non-negotiable and prior to any political assessment; Haemon's, which reads oddly like a public-opinion survey and raises the question of whether a polity's commemorative judgments must answer to what its citizens actually believe; Teiresias's, which is consequentialist and concerns the corruption of the city's entire sacrificial life; and the chorus, which ratifies the edict and then abandons it. I especially want you to press on Antigone's own reasoning instead of treating her as a stand-in for a principle she never states. Her argument for why this brother uniquely cannot be substituted for — the one she shares with a certain wife in Herodotus — is famously particular, and at no point does she ask whether Polyneices deserved anything. Does the play therefore supply a universalist ethics of commemoration, or something closer to an ethics of partiality that our monument debates have no room for?

Finally, the reading has to be honest in both directions. Tell me plainly where the analogy to statues and street names holds and where it snaps, including whether the play ever distinguishes burial as such from burial with honors, and what turns on that distinction. And tell me what the ending does for the argument: Creon eventually performs precisely the act that the moderates on our commission would call the remedy — he reverses the designation and, in effect, recontextualizes — but he does it too late, and the reversal costs him his son and his wife rather than restoring anything. Is the play saying that belated correction is worthless, or only that it is ruinously expensive? Where Hegel's reading of the tragedy as a collision between the law of the family and the law of the state is worth invoking, invoke it, but say what it adds or costs for the monument question specifically, since that framing tends to make Creon's position look more respectable than the text does. Don't give me a survey of the statue debate and don't pad this with a cast list; I have the text open beside me, so no quotation is needed, and I would rather have your judgment about which scenes and exchanges actually carry the weight. I'd like the piece to close by saying what Sophocles contributes to this debate that the philosophical literature on commemoration has not managed to say on its own — and it needs to work for an audience that is part lay public, part city council.
