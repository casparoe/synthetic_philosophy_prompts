I'm helping to design the closing session of an interdisciplinary workshop on clinical AI governance. The room will be roughly half hospital administrators and half philosophers, and I want to end not with a summary but with a staged dialogue that makes each half of the room hear the strongest version of the position it finds least comfortable. The dialogue should run on a single case, which you may fill in with concrete detail but should not restructure: a large teaching hospital deploys an autonomous sepsis-detection and antibiotic-stewardship system that is authorized to withhold or override a clinician's order when it judges the evidence of infection insufficient. The system was properly validated at purchase; a year into use the vendor's engineers adjust its decision thresholds in response to a rise in resistant organisms, and the adjustment is reviewed and approved by an internal committee that follows every applicable procedure but lacks the clinical expertise to evaluate it. Over the following months, ward staff learn to read the system's silence as authoritative and stop double-checking, partly because the sheer volume of alerts has made sustained vigilance exhausting. A patient then dies of untreated sepsis. No one in the chain — vendor, committee, procurement, attending, resident, nurse — acted negligently by the standards of their own role; each can point to a procedure they followed correctly, and each is telling the truth when they do.

The two speakers should be genuinely matched, and neither should be permitted a strawman. The first maintains that the case exhibits a real responsibility gap: on any account that ties blameworthiness to a control condition and an epistemic condition, no individual in the chain qualifies, the system itself is not the sort of thing that can be blamed, and so the reactive attitudes the family understandably feels — resentment, the demand that someone answer for this — have no legitimate target, which is a genuine loss and not merely a bookkeeping inconvenience. The second maintains that the gap is an artifact of an unexamined picture of what responsibility must be: it assumes responsibility has to be backward-looking, individual, and fault-tracking, and once you allow forward-looking and collectively held obligations — the standing duty of those who benefited from and maintained the system to explain themselves, to compensate, and to change the arrangement that produced the death — there is no vacancy at all, only a distribution problem that the first speaker's narrow frame makes invisible. To keep them from talking past each other, please have the first speaker press the point that "answerability without fault" is a different concept wearing the same word, and that renaming a compensation scheme does not fill a gap in desert; and have the second press the point that the fault-first picture is not neutral, that it reliably manufactures scapegoats (the nurse who clicked past the alert), and that the epistemic condition is satisfiable at the organizational level even when it fails for every person inside it. I'd also like the dialogue to keep four things distinct that this debate tends to fuse: a gap in blameworthiness, a gap in legal liability, a gap in institutional accountability, and a gap in the appropriateness of the family's resentment. Have the speakers explicitly disagree about which of the four is actually at stake in the case, and let at least one of them concede ground on one of the four without conceding the argument.

Please write roughly 1,600 to 2,200 words of dialogue, with the speakers named and lightly characterized by their professional standing rather than by personality tics, and with enough stage business — the printout of the event log, the pause before an answer — to keep it from reading as two alternating essays. Each speaker should land at least one blow the other cannot fully parry, and a reader should not be able to tell which side you favor. Have them converge, near the end, on a concrete proposal the hospital could actually adopt: designating a single human officer who is formally accountable for the system's outputs whether or not anyone is at fault — a deliberate responsibility sink. Then let them split sharply over whether that proposal vindicates the second speaker or quietly concedes the first one's point, since a sink is only needed if there is somewhere for the responsibility to fall through. Close with one of them saying the sentence they would actually say to the patient's family, and the other objecting — not to its wording or its kindness, but to whether that speaker is entitled to say it. No citations, no references to the literature, and no bibliography; everything needed should be argued from within the dialogue itself.
