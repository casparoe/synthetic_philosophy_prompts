Our astronomy society went to the pub after a talk on the Fermi paradox and two of the members got into a proper row about the dark forest hypothesis. I was sat next to them, couldn't work out who was winning, so here's roughly how it went.

(1) Nadia said the argument is basically airtight: if survival is your primary aim and resources are finite, then becuase you can't rule out that another civilisation will one day wipe you out, and you can't rule out that they're getting stronger faster than you, striking first dominates — and a great silence is exactly what a forest full of hidden hunters looks like.

(2) Owen said she'd slipped. A silent sky is also what an empty forest looks like, so the silence can't count as evidence for her hypothesis over "there's simply nobody there". It explains the data no better than the null does.

(3) Nadia said that's too quick: the two come apart over time. Given a billion years, some civilisation somewhere broadcasts, or leaks, or gets caught by somebody else's careless transmission. So dark forest predicts a silence that stays total, which is a much stronger claim than "quiet so far".

(4) Owen said fine, but she's now bought something absurd — that across countless worlds and countless factions, not one of them ever defects, ever. And worse, her chain-of-suspicion premise cuts against her: if you model the others as rational and frightened, what you should predict from them is concealment rather than aggression, so the more carefully you reason about them, the less reason you have to shoot.

(5) Nadia said that only holds if the shot is expensive. If destroying a rival is cheap — a rock at relativistic speed, or just publishing their coordinates so someone else does the job — then even a modestly capable and thoroughly terrified civilisation fires, and cheapness is precisely what her technological-explosion premise implies: today's weak player is tomorrow's threat, so you shoot while shooting is still cheap.

(6) Owen said now it's biting its own tail. The civilisations for whom a shot is cheapest are the ones already so far ahead that they have the least to fear, and the ones with the most to fear are the ones too weak to shoot. So on her own premises the shooters turn out to be exactly the people with no reason to shoot.

Neither of them gave an inch. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each of the six — rating them, not just picking an overall winner — and then tell me what neither of them seemed to notice?
