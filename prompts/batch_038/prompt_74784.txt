I am writing a chapter for an edited volume on the ethics of coming into existence, and the object is Kazuo Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go*. Please work from the novel rather than the film; if any scene I mention below belongs to the 2010 adaptation and not to the book, tell me.

The lens is the procreation asymmetry in the four-clause form David Benatar made familiar: the presence of suffering is bad; the presence of pleasure is good; the absence of suffering is good even when there is no one around to enjoy its absence; and the absence of pleasure is bad only if there is somebody whom it deprives. Applied to Hailsham, the asymmetry implies something the novel never states outright — that the guardians' whole project, the art and the Exchange and the cultivated childhood and the gallery assembled to demonstrate that the students had inner lives, cannot repair the original wrong, because there was no one waiting to be benefited by being created.

Two of the terms I want you to use are my own and you will not find them in the literature, so treat my definitions as authoritative rather than reaching for anything similar you may know. Call an amelioration a *masking improvement* when improving a life's conditions works, in practice, to make the wrongness of having started that life harder to see and harder to name. Call a good *unowned* when it accrues to no subject at all — which is precisely what the third clause of the asymmetry posits, since the absent suffering of someone never created is good without being good for anyone.

With that in hand:

1. Does the novel endorse, dramatize, or refuse the asymmetry? Kathy's narration is elegiac but never accusatory; she does not ask whether she should have been born, and late in the book she suggests that the donors' lives are not so very different from anybody else's. Is that reticence evidence against the asymmetry — a demonstration that a life's value is not hostage to its origin — or is it the purest case in the book of a masking improvement having done its work, so that adaptive preference rather than insight explains the silence? Give me the reading you can actually defend, not the balanced one.

2. Miss Lucy and Miss Emily disagree about disclosure to the children. Put that disagreement in the asymmetry's terms. Is Miss Lucy the position the novel vindicates, given that she is dismissed and that her honesty changes nothing about the students' fate? Or does the book ultimately side with Miss Emily, whose Hailsham is on my account a masking improvement of the purest kind? The scene in which Tommy and Kathy are received at Madame's and Miss Emily's house ought to settle this; tell me whether it does, and what it costs each side.

3. Which scenes carry the philosophical weight? I have in mind the cassette tape and the dance Madame watches from the doorway, the gallery and its purpose, the search for Ruth's possible in Norfolk, the grounded fishing boat in the marshes, the rumour of deferrals and the couple's appeal against it, and the flat flooded field where the rubbish collects at the end. One or two sentences each on what it contributes to the asymmetry reading — and then tell me which of them I am over-pressing, which are simply novelistic texture that my lens will flatten into illustration.

4. Where does the reading break? My worry is that Ishiguro wrote a parable about ordinary mortality, and that the donation programme is a magnifying glass rather than a case about procreation, in which event the asymmetry is the wrong instrument. Say how much of the novel survives the lens and how much is distorted by it. And if you think the non-identity problem is the better fit — the clones exist only because of the programme that will kill them, so there is no one they could have been instead — explain how the two frameworks interact here and whether the novel stages a standoff between them rather than choosing a side.

5. What does the novel give the debate that argument alone cannot? Here is my speculative answer, and I want it tested rather than flattered: the asymmetry's decisive clause requires us to count a good that belongs to nobody, but a first-person narrator can only mourn people who existed. Kathy can grieve Tommy and Ruth and cannot register the unowned good of the children never conceived once Hailsham closes and the programme simply continues elsewhere. If that is right, the novel's form makes the asymmetry's central claim unthinkable from inside a life, and I want to know whether you take that as a limit of fiction or as a genuine objection to the asymmetry — something about a principle no subject can ever experience as vindicated.

No plot summary, no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations of any kind; I want the argument, not the apparatus. Do not quote the novel — describe scenes and paraphrase what is said, since I will be checking against my own copy. Write in continuous critical prose at the length of a substantial essay rather than an outline, and treat both the novel and my coinages as things that may turn out to be wrong: if a masking improvement is not a real category, or if my unowned-good argument simply confuses impersonal with person-affecting value, say so plainly and explain why.
