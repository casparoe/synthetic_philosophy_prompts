I write a weekly-ish column on science and ideas for a mid-size magazine, and I'm currently working on a piece about how democracies argue about inherited wrongdoing — reparations, apologies, museum labels, the whole terrain. Last month I was at a small conference on memory politics, and during a long rain delay between panels I ended up in the hotel bar while two of the speakers kept at each other. One was a legal philosopher who works on transitional justice (I'll call him D), the other a historian of postwar Germany (M). It was by far the best thing I heard all weekend, and I want to borrow the structure of the exchange for the column — but I'm not a philosopher and I can't tell which of them actually won which round. Here's what I got down in my notebook, roughly in order:

(1) D opened by saying a nation can't be guilty in any literal sense, because guilt is a predicate that needs an agent with intentions. He said Jaspers' real achievement in *The Question of German Guilt* — the 1946 lectures — was refusing that slide by splitting the concept four ways: criminal guilt, which is individual and gets settled by a court; political guilt, which citizens bear for the deeds of their state and which gets discharged by paying for the consequences; moral guilt, which you can only pronounce on yourself; and metaphysical guilt, the solidarity of humans as humans, which nobody is in a position to adjudicate. So the honest sentence about a country today, on his view, isn't "Germany is guilty," it's "Germany is liable."

(2) M said that's a lawyer's move dressed up as a moral insight. If the polity collectively owes restitution and its members collectively have to discharge it, then "liable" and "guilty" are doing identical work in the sentence, and choosing the colder of the two words is branding rather than argument.

(3) D answered that the difference is exactly about who can be punished: criminal guilt licenses punishment of a perpetrator, political guilt licenses only claims against the polity's resources. Collapse the two and you're back at Kollektivschuld — a people as such being tainted — which is the concept that has historically been used to justify doing awful things to descendants.

(4) M said fine, then push on the timeline. Jaspers grounded political guilt in being a citizen of a state whose acts you could have opposed or at least not enabled. What happens when the last person who could have done that is dead? A German born in 1995 failed to prevent nothing. So either political guilt expires with the perpetrators — in which case D's "liable" claim expires with it — or it doesn't expire, in which case it is being transmitted across birth, which is the mechanism he just condemned as toxic.

(5) D said no, because what's transmitted isn't guilt but obligations attached to a continuing entity: the postwar state inherited the assets, the institutions, the treaties, the industrial base, some of it built with forced labor. You can't accept the inheritance and refuse the encumberance. That's succession, not taint.

(6) M said that's the strongest version of his position and it makes her point about language for her. A balance-sheet frame gives you a debt, and it gives you the psychology of a debt: you pay it, it's discharged, everybody goes home. The reason the word "guilt" matters isn't metaphysical, it's motivtional — remorse doesn't amortize. Then she added a second claim: even if descendants aren't guilty, they lose the standing to moralize, because people whose families were enriched by a crime don't get to sit in the neutral judge's chair when the victims' grandchildren are the ones bringing the case.

(7) D said she's now running three different questions together — what is owed, who owes it, and who is permitted to say so. Standing is about hypocrisy and about the position of the speaker; it isn't evidence that anyone bears guilt. And her "remorse doesn't amortize" line, he said, is just Jaspers' metaphysical guilt wearing a costume: it's the one category that does bind everyone across generations precisely because it isn't a political claim at all and can never be cashed out in a payment.

Please act as a philosopher who has actually worked on collective responsibility and transitional justice, not as a general-purpose explainer, and referee this properly. Before you grade anything, spend a paragraph on the conceptual setup: what has to be true for each side's central claim to hold, and whether the two of them are even answering the same question — my hunch is that at least one round here is pure talking-past-each-other and I want to know which. Then go through moves (1) to (7) and rate each one on a four-point scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a couple of sentences of justification per move; feel free to split a move if one half of it lands and the other doesn't. Then tell me who's more right overall, and separately what both of them missed, since I suspect at least one live issue never came up at that table. Finish with the part where you tell me what would change your mind: which of your ratings is shakiest, what argument or evidence would flip it, and where your reasoning is most likely to break down — if you're leaning on a contested reading of Jaspers, or on an intuition about inherited obligation that's carrying more weight than you're admitting, say so explicitly rather than smoothing it over.
