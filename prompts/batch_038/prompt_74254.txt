I'd like you to take the role of a professional philosopher of science who also works in historiography — someone comfortable with the demarcation problem and with how archaeology and history actually police their own boundaries. Please think carefully before answering, and treat the case material as data to be explained rather than as occasions for summary. Before you commit to a criterion, sketch two or three candidates you might adopt (falsifiability, the claimant's standing and accountability to a community, how a claim absorbs contrary evidence, the social role the claim plays) and say briefly why the ones you discard fail. Only then build the account you want to defend.

Here is my situation. I have been collecting my own verdicts about when a claim concerning the past counts as pseudohistory, case by case, and I want to know whether they add up to a single principle. Where they do not, I want to be told which verdict to abandon rather than handed a criterion with six hedging clauses.

My verdicts are these.

1. The Atlantean dialogues themselves are not pseudohistory. Plato is telling a political fable and is not, so far as I can judge, trying to get anyone to believe it. But the modern enterprise of locating Atlantis as a real sunken civilization is pseudohistory — and it cites the very same text. So on my view the pseudohistorical thing can be a practice of reading rather than a claim, which I find strange.

2. The identification of Atlantis with the Bronze Age eruption of Thera and the buried settlement at Akrotiri is, I think, pseudohistorical in spirit — and yet the eruption is real, the archaeology is real, the reasoning is an ordinary inference to the best explanation, and the conclusion may be roughly what Plato had in mind. I cannot defend this verdict, and I keep wanting to retract it.

3. Von Däniken's ancient-astronaut reading of the Nazca figures and of the megalithic stonework at Puma Punku is clearly pseudohistory.

4. By contrast, the skeptical investigator who, with volunteers, stakes and string and only technology available to the Nazca people, reproduced one of the large ground figures closely enough to remove any need for aerial assistance: I call that good historical work. Note that it involved no excavation, no dating program, no peer review, no academic appointment, and was reported in popular venues. Note also that a trained mathematician who spent decades surveying those same lines with real instruments and argued for calendar-astronomical alignments was largely ignored by the discipline; I do not think her work was pseudohistorical either, though her interpretation now looks mistaken.

5. Fomenko's New Chronology is pseudohistory, unambiguously. But it is the work of a highly regarded professional mathematician, it deploys formal statistical methods and astronomical computation, it is quantitative in a way that most narrative history is not, and its central theses — that the conventional timeline duplicates itself, that much of classical antiquity is medieval fabrication, that the crucifixion belongs in the twelfth century rather than the first — are argued rather than merely asserted. I have no story for why this is worse than a respectable monograph resting on a single contested manuscript reading.

6. Monte Verde and pre-Clovis settlement of the Americas. When the southern Chilean site was reported with ages far outside the accepted framework, senior figures in the discipline rejected it in terms that strike me as dismissive rather than evidential. It eventually became accepted, and its dating has been re-litigated at intervals since. My verdict is that the excavator was never doing pseudohistory and that some of his opponents behaved badly — but at the time, nobody could have known which side was right.

7. The popular account of a lost technologically advanced civilization destroyed at the end of the last Ice Age is pseudohistory. The difficulty is that 6 and 7 look structurally identical from the inside: a handful of anomalies, a proponent treated as an outsider, an establishment appeal to the absence of evidence, a thesis that would rewrite the standard story. I want a criterion that separates them in advance of the outcome, because a criterion that amounts to whichever side turned out to be true is not a criterion at all.

8. The proposed cosmic impact of roughly 12,800 years ago, held responsible for the abrupt cooling, for megafaunal extinctions, and for melted glass and impact spherules at various sites, is neither. Its advocates include working geologists, its claimed evidence is physical and checkable, and the objections concern contamination, misidentification and the absence of a crater. I will not call it pseudohistory and I will not call it accepted science. I lack a name for the zone it occupies, and I suspect my criterion needs such a zone.

9. The Solutrean hypothesis — Ice Age Europeans crossing the Atlantic ice edge and becoming the ancestors of Clovis — was published by a university press and argued from lithic typology, and has been rejected largely on genetic grounds. I think it is wrong and poorly supported. But part of my confidence in that verdict comes from knowing how the claim has been taken up by white supremacist groups, and that disturbs me: I appear to be treating social consequences as evidence about method. My verdict is bad science but not pseudohistory, though I doubt I would hold it if the promoters were people I did not already distrust.

10. Schliemann at Hisarlik: guided by Homer, he found genuine Bronze Age remains, announced a treasure he associated with Priam, and excavated in a manner that destroyed the strata he most needed, taking as Homeric Troy a level considerably older than the plausible candidate. True conclusions, dreadful method, colossal self-promotion. Not pseudohistory, I think — yet 3 seems to be built from the same three ingredients.

11. Piltdown: a fabricated skull accepted for four decades by the professional establishment and exposed in the 1950s. The fabrication was pseudoscientific; the discipline's long acceptance of it was not. My criterion must permit a field to be entirely non-pseudoscientific while being systematically fooled.

12. Geoffrey of Monmouth's twelfth-century history of the British kings, which professes to translate an ancient book, invents a Trojan founder for Britain and a continental empire for Arthur, and was read as straightforward history for centuries. Not pseudohistory, because nothing like the category existed — which suggests pseudohistoricity is a property of a claim relative to the standards available to a community rather than a property of the claim in itself. If that is right, Fomenko is flouting standards he was trained inside, whereas Geoffrey was working within every standard his culture had, and I do not see how one account treats those two symmetrically.

13. Two further cases that cut in opposite directions. A map of the Norse Vinland, acquired by a major research library, published with fanfare, pronounced a forgery in the 1970s and finally settled by materials analysis: everyone agrees about what happened and nobody thinks the episode reflects badly on the discipline. The Bosnian pyramid claim at Visoko, advanced from the mid-2000s over hills that geologists take to be natural, backed by an organized institute, a tunnel network and considerable local enthusiasm: pseudohistory, I say, even though much of its supporting material is physically inspectable and it has attracted academic collaborators.

What I want from you is the following. State the principle, or the small set of rival candidates, precisely enough that someone else could apply it, clause by clause, and say which case or which pair of cases motivated each clause. Then tell me which of my verdicts the best candidate cannot deliver and which one I should give up; I would rather lose a verdict than multiply qualifications. Then apply the resulting account to cases I have not listed, and in particular to two I care about: an interpretive framework used by entirely mainstream historians that is difficult or impossible to falsify (longue-durée reasoning, or world-systems explanations of antiquity, or the frontier thesis), and a hypothetical claim defended only by a documentary's producers, supported by genuine artifacts, resting on contested dates, and without a single academic proponent. Address the crux directly — how 6 and 7 come apart without hindsight — and if your account cannot separate them, say that plainly rather than papering over it. Finally, tell me whether social uptake of the kind in 9 can ever enter the criterion without turning demarcation into a matter of politics, and if not, explain what I should do with the fact that it visibly influences my own verdicts.

A note on form: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text and no tables. Continuous prose only, in the manner of a long letter or email. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I want your reasoning, not your sources. If you think some of my verdicts are simply confused, or that I have mischaracterized one of these episodes, say so directly — I would much rather be corrected than accommodated.
