ok so I'm on the science panel of a regional land trust and this quarter's ugly decision has landed in my lap. Let me give you the setup and then what I actually need, because I think the hard part is the scoring sheet, not the animals.

The reserve: 3,800 hectares, fenced on the landward side since 1979, last of the native large predators shot out in the 1950s. So nothing about it is untouched — its a box we built. Four decades with no predation plus a run of mild winters took the introduced deer population from about 600 to somewhere around 5,400. They've stripped the shrub layer; three ground-nesting birds are functionally gone from it and our own habitat models say the understory does not come back under current browsing pressure. Range staff put carrying capacity at roughly 2,100. Base case is a crash inside two winters — on the order of 3,000 animals going down over one or two bad seasons, mostly starvation, dying in the open because there's nothing left to eat and nothing to kill them quickly.

Four options, same money, same land, we fund one:

A. Reintroduce a predator. Twelve founder animals of the peninsula's historically native large felid, expected to bring deer to ~2,000 over nine years and hold them there. Best biodiversity outcome by a distance — the only option that puts a missing interaction back. But deer deaths become predation deaths: 400–500 a year, heavily skewed to fawns, plus some unknown number of wounded animals that take days.

B. Immunocontraception. Darting plus bait-delivered vaccine, re-dosed every 18 months for twenty years. Population settles at ~2,400 and stays. No predation, no starvation. Cost: an acute stress event for a big fraction of the herd twice a year, forever, and the understory only partially recovers, so the birds probably don't come back.

C. Cull. Contractors, two seasons, down to 1,800, then ~150/year maintenance. Fastest habitat recovery, about a quarter the cost of A. Per-animal the welfare case is arguably the best of the four — one clean shot versus a season of starving or a throat being opened. But the board has a standing no-lethal-control commitment dating from a donor blowup in the nineties and the panel cant override it without two thirds, which we don't have.

D. Monitored non-intervention. Camera traps, population counts, let the crash run. Cheapest. Favored by two of the ecologists on the panel, on the grounds that natural processes have a value we're in no position to trade against and that our welfare models are far too crude to license killing thousands of animals on the strength of them.

Here's what's making me crazy. We just took a large multi-year grant from a foundation whose agreement requires every project to be scored on net sentience impact alongside biodiversity, on their sheet. The sheet counts welfare in 'animal-years of acceptable life.' Deaths the project causes are entered at full weight. Deaths the project averts are entered at 0.4, on the stated rationale that averted suffering is less certain than inflicted suffering. There is no line anywhere for animals who are never born — foregone lives simply don't get scored. And where it wants a per-species sentience weight it tells you to use adult body mass as the proxy.

Run the four options through it honestly and D wins. The option that produces the most misery is the one the rubric likes best, because all that misery sits in the discounted 'averted' column and because a starvation crash leaves no body you can point at. C — which I think is right on the welfare arithmetic, and I don't enjoy thinking that — scores worst, since every one of its deaths is a caused death at full weight and the 3,000 it prevents barely register.

Related, and I want the memo to answer it: at the last meeting the donor's rep made a remark nobody laughed at and nobody answered, to the effect that if what we actually care about is fewer animals suffering, then the cheapest and most reliable welfare intervention available to us is paving the place, because no habitat means no deer. I think he meant it as a reductio of his own foundation's sheet, and he's not wrong that the sheet sort of agrees with him. Give me two or three sentences the panel could put in writing about why that doesn't follow — or a candid line about how much of it does.

So: the memo I'll circulate before Thursday. Recommend one option, and say what we should do about the sheet — score it as written, score it as written with a formal objection attached, or tell the donor we can't use it — because those are three different memos and the panel has to pick one. Reasons the losing side could actually answer, not strawmen; the D people on my panel aren't squeamish, they have a real argument about what we're entitled to claim to know. Be straight about the soft spots: whether a predation death is worse per animal than a starvation death, whether a deer's life is net positive on balance, whether body mass is a defensible proxy for anything, and whether it matters that we built the fence and shot the predators, i.e. whether 'letting nature take its course' even names a thing here. And one paragraph I can read out to the board about the two wardens who know individual deer by sight and have said they'll resign over C — I don't think that's just sentiment, but I can't yet say what it is or whether it should move us.

Around 900–1,000 words. Header, recommendation inside the first three lines, then reasoning, then a short 'what would change this recommendation' section at the end. No citations and please don't invent studies or reports to lean on — I don't need authority here, I need the argument. And don't let it land on 'it depends'; we vote Thursday.
