You are a philosopher of science who works on demarcation in history and archaeology — someone equally at home with the pseudo-claims and with the refutations of them. Please think carefully rather than answering from the shape of the question.

I am a novelist. One of the two protagonists of the book I am drafting is a historian, and I have built her public record out of verdicts I assembled from essays and lectures of the sort she would plausibly have written, so that her reactions on the page are ones a real expert would have rather than ones a screenwriter would invent. The record is as follows.

- Atlantis: pseudohistory. A story that exists, so far as anyone can tell, only in Plato, re-inflated for two thousand years.
- Ancient aliens at Nazca and Giza: pseudohistory. Our ignorance treated as evidence, plus an insult to the people who did the work.
- Fomenko's New Chronology: pseudohistory. Real data, rigged method, and every contradiction reabsorbed as further confirmation.
- Velikovsky's planetary catastrophism: pseudohistory.
- The Bosnian "pyramids": pseudohistory.
- The Antikythera mechanism: history, without qualification.
- The Baghdad battery jars: not pseudohistory. The objects are there, the electrochemical reading can be tried in a laboratory and has been, and it simply remains unproven.
- The Voynich manuscript: not pseudohistory — an unsolved problem. The crank readings of it are the pseudohistory, not the manuscript.
- A claim by a palaeographer that a particular authenticated medieval charter is a Victorian forgery, which she submitted to ink analysis and then withdrew when the analysis came back negative: history, and a credit to the field.

Her next case, in my plot, is this. A team led by a credentialed sedimentologist proposes, on the strength of optically stimulated luminescence dates and a layer of impact proxies — melted glass, magnetic spherules, a charcoal-rich horizon — that a well-documented coastal site was occupied several millennia earlier than the accepted regional chronology allows, and that this chronology survives only because the field systematically explains away its own stratigraphic outliers. They publish in peer-reviewed journals, release their raw data, and invite replication. Two colleagues who re-ran the samples independently obtained the same dates. A third did not, and reports that the deposit was disturbed. No one alleges bad faith. The lead author has, in the manuscript, staked her tenure on the result.

Before you commit to an answer, brainstorm: set out the candidate rules that would account for all nine verdicts at once — object-realism, falsifiability, whether the proponent has ever actually abandoned a claim on adverse evidence, whether accepting the claim requires an entire established field to be systematically wrong, whether gaps in the record are being spent as positive evidence, whether the claim trades on disparaging its makers — and note which of my nine verdicts each rule is genuinely doing work on and which it merely happens to fit. Then tell me what she would say about the new case, what you take her operative rule to be, and how confident you are; flag any verdict in my list that turns out to be load-bearing for one rule and idle for the others. Finally, name the single further case that would best separate the rules that survive, and say what it would have to look like.

If you think the exercise is a trap — if these nine verdicts are not jointly explicable by any single rule, because a working expert's judgments in this area are patchwork rather than principled, or because I have mis-described one of the cases in a way that changes everything — say so plainly instead of manufacturing a tidy theory to please me.
