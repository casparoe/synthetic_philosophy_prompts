I'm prepping the first session of my graduate philosophy-of-science seminar and I want a handout that sorts students by what they actually believe about the pessimistic induction — not by which label they say they like. Most of them walk in with one pop version of "science has been wrong so many times that realism is dead" and one pop version of "but come on, electrons are real," and I want the instrument to force the real distinctions into the open.

Treat yourself as a philosopher of science who has taught this debate for years and who cares more about getting the logical space right than about the names attached to the cells. Before you write the tree, do a short brainstorm out loud: which distinctions here genuinely do sorting work, and which are just vocabulary differences people fight over? Then build the thing from the space, and only locate the actual positions in it afterwards.

What I need:

1. A decision tree, roughly 10 to 12 branch nodes and 8 to 10 leaves. Every node phrased as a question a student can answer without knowing which leaf it leads to — questions about cases and claims, never anything like "are you a realist?"

2. It has to include nodes on at least the following: what counts as the success of a past theory (novel prediction vs. accomodation vs. merely working as an instrument); whether a discarded theory's central term failed to refer at all, or referred to something not as described; whether "approximately true" can mean anything for a theory whose ontology is gone; whether the historical sample these arguments draw on is skewed toward failures because we only remember the ones that died; whether the pessimistic induction's own evidence depends on our current theories being roughly right (the reflexive problem); whether the worry is that past theories were false or that we systematically miss unconceived alternatives, the way Stanford's new-induction argument has it; and whether the Ramsey-sentence maneuver can be run by a structural realist without it collapsing into something trivially satisfiable, the Newman-style worry.

3. Leaves: constructive empiricism, entity realism, epistemic structural realism, ontic structural realism, semirealism, selective realism, instrumentalism, and at least one cell you think nobody actually occupies. For the empty one, say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it yet. Every leaf gets three things: the position's name, one sentence on what it says survives of a past successful theory, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

4. Flag at least two pairs of leaves your tree can't cleanly seperate, and say what extra question would seperate them — or say plainly that no such question exists and they differ only in tone.

5. Then run three cases through the finished tree and report where each one sends you and which leaves disagree about it: phlogiston; the luminiferous ether; and Newtonian mechanics as it's still used today. For the third one, state explicitly which leaves are supposed to be split by it and whether they actually are, in your own tree.

A few constraints. No bibliography, no reading list, no citation apparatus — I know the literature and the students get it separately; plain prose and position names only. Keep the register of the node questions neutral: I teach a mixed room and I don't want the anti-realist branches sounding silly or the realist ones sounding smug, because I want the students who land somewhere surprising to land there honestly. And be candid inside the handout itself: if a node is really a matter of temperament dressed up as a question, mark it with a short aside so I can warn them in class. Think carefully about the ordering — a question that only makes sense after three earlier ones has been answered shouldn't be the second node, and if you have to put it there, tell me why.
