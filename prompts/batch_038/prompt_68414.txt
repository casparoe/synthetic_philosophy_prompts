I spent thirty-odd years building numerical models — mostly sediment transport in estuaries, with a long stint on parameterizing processes we couldn't resolve — and I've only gotten into philosophy properly since retiring. So I come at this from a slightly odd angle: I keep wanting to know whether a given model is load-bearing, or just a nice picture of the modelers own assumptions with the answer built in.

The thing I'm chewing on is Copycat — the Hofstadter/Mitchell program, with the slipnet, the coderack full of little codelets, the workspace, the parallel terraced scan, that temperature knob. It does letter-string analogies: abc goes to abd, so what should ijk go to. And the pitch, as I understand it, is that what this shows is that concepts are fluid — they slip into neighboring concepts under pressure — and that slippage is the engine of analogy-making rather than a failure of categories to be crisp. Roughly half of what I read treats that as a genuine discovery about concepts. The other half says it's a hand-built toy: the designer already knew what answers were plausible, wired that knowledge into the slipnet's link weights and the codelets' firing conditions, and then the program "predicts" what it was told to predict. Both camps sound intelligent to me and I cant tell who's right, so I don't want an essay adjudicating it.

Instead: draft the joint statement a committed fluid-concepts person and a committed skeptic could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. I want a numbered list of claims both would actually put their names to — about what the program is and does, what the letter-string microdomain amounts to, what's been demonstrated and what hasn't — and where a claim is only agreed at a fuzzy level, say that in the wording instead of making it look crisper than it is. Then a table of what's left: each crux stated in wording each side would accept for its own view, the specific test or case both would agree bears on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance.

Seed it with the cruxes I'd guess at, but fix me where I've got them wrong:

(a) whether the hand-coding of slipnet and codelets smuggles in the answers — and whether there's a held-out set of microdomain problems that would settle it, i.e. problems the codelets weren't tuned against;
(b) whether the temperature parameter does real explanatory work or is a fitted noise dial, and what human data could discriminate;
(c) whether conceptual slippage names something a static category theory can't also predict — say, priming of the concept that gets slipped to;
(d) whether the microdomain is a legitimate idealization the way a frictionless plane is, or a rigged testbed, and what would count as a controlled relaxation of it;
(e) whether the later models in other domains (SeqSee for number sequences, Phaeaco for Bongard problems) are independent replications or just more bespoke hand-building.

If one of these turns out to be a merely verbal dispute — my hunch is that the two sides aren't using "concept" in the same sense at all — say which, say why, and pull it out of the table rather than letting it masquerade as substantive.

Two asks about how you handle it. First, be explicit about what's settled versus what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this. I keep reading summaries that state one side's framing as though it were the field's consensus, and I want to see clearly marked where you're recording a live disagreement rather than a fact. Same for my guesses above: if one rests on a misunderstanding of how the program works, tell me flatly rather than quietly working around it.

Second, hard cap of 800 words, table included. That means maybe six to eight agreed claims, short and sharp, and every table cell earning its place — I'd rather get four real cruxes than eight thin ones.
