The situation:

I'm on the board of a county historical society. We run a small public film program funded in part by a bequest from the 1960s whose terms say the money goes to films treating local history "accurately and with respect for the people in it." That's the whole of the language. Nobody living from that family has ever weighed in on anything.

This spring we programmed a 90-minute docudrama about Josiah Vance, who ran the county's only Black-owned funeral home from 1919 to 1963 and who kept the private ledger that later let dozens of families prove ownership of land that had been quietly struck off the tax rolls. The filmmaker is a competent local documentarian; we gave him part of the budget.

The problem is one scene. In 1947 Vance declined to testify at a coroner's inquest. The film shows him deciding not to because he'd been having an affair with a white woman whose husband sat on that jury. That's invented — the filmmaker says so plainly, and his grant application to us listed the scene under a heading called "dramatization." What's actually documented, including in our own collection, is a letter to his brother saying he wouldn't testify because it would expose the names of the families using the ledger.

The granddaughter, who's a historian, objects. Her objection isn't mainly "that didn't happen." Her claim — she calls it "explanatory displacement," her own phrase — is that the invented cause doesn't just add a falsehood, it evicts a truth: after the film, everyone who sees it will carry around *he stayed quiet because he was afraid about an affair*, and the real reason, which is documented and which is the genuinely interesting thing about him, becomes harder to get at. She's said she'd rather we screen it with something attached than not screen it at all, because her grandfather is almost totally absent from the county's public memory and she thinks that's the worst outcome. She'll also talk to the local paper either way.

The filmmaker will add a single end card saying some scenes have been dramatized. His case, in his words: the film brings an audience to a subject nobody would otherwise watch; the affair thread is what makes the 1947 sequence dramatically legible to a general viewer; cutting it collapses the middle third; and he never claimed the scene was documented, he told us it wasn't.

Two board things on top. Our county attorney says we have zero legal exposure, since the dead can't be defamed and there's no surviving privacy interest — I want that kept out of the reasoning as much as possible, it feels like it's answering a question nobody asked. And staff drafted a policy last year that was never adopted, which would require on-screen tier labels next to dramatized material: "attested," "inferred," "invented." One board member wants us to adopt it now and apply it to this film as a condition of screening. Another says the scheme is unusable in practice, because when you actually sort claims nearly everything lands in "inferred" — so you either get a label every twenty seconds, or you get a system where audiences read anything unlabeled as proven, which is worse than no labels at all. I think she's onto something but I can't quite articulate it.

Timing: screening is in 11 days, tickets are already on sale, there's a paper feature scheduled, and the filmmaker has a finished cut.

The deliverable is the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. Around 700–900 words. It needs to recommend one concrete course of action, with conditions and dates, not a menu. And it has to do these things:

1. Say what we owe Vance himself — a person who is dead, who on most accounts can't be harmed anymore, and who arguably has no interest in how he's remembered. Don't dodge that; if you think the honest answer is that we owe *him* nothing and everything here is owed to other people, say so and show what follows.
2. Keep that separate from what we owe the granddaughter and from what we owe the audience — those come apart in this case and I want to see where.
3. Take the tier-label proposal seriously: adopt it, modify it, or reject it, with reasons that engage the "everything is inferred" objection rather than around it. If there's a better mechanism than labels, propose one we could actually implement in 11 days.
4. Give the filmmaker's strongest version its best shot — specifically the claim that the film's net effect is to make Vance more present in public memory than any alternative available to us, and that our squeamishness is an aesthetic preference wearing an ethical costume.
5. Address what "accurately" in the bequest is even asking of a dramatized scene. That's an interpretive question about a 60-year-old standard, and I think the board is treating it as settled in both directions when it isn't.

Bottom line up front, memo headers, prose rather than bullet confetti. And if you actually think we should screen it unchanged, say that — don't manufacture a compromise just because compromises look thoughtful. Don't end by asking me clarifying questions.
