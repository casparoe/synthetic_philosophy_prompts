In a philosophy of science seminar I attend, the visiting lecturer defended scientific realism with an analogy he returned to at several points. Science, he said, is like a surveying party working its way up a mountain toward a summit it cannot yet see: each theory is a camp on the slope, later camps are genuinely higher than earlier ones even though none of them is the top, and the relation between Newtonian mechanics and the special theory of relativity is the paradigm case, since the former is recovered as a limiting case of the latter at low velocities. From this he concluded that the pessimistic meta-induction loses its sting: the long catalogue of discarded theories is no evidence against realism, because discarding is simply what climbing looks like, and the convergence in the celebrated example is a mathematical fact rather than an article of faith.

Two of us were unpersuaded, and I want to test our objections before raising them. One pointed out that the limit relation between the Newtonian and the relativistic treatment of motion is proved inside a shared formalism — the same equations, the same variables, one parameter sent to a limit — and that this is exactly what goes missing when the target terms change meaning or drop out altogether, as with phlogiston or the luminiferous ether. The other noted that Newtonian mechanics conserves mass and relativity does not, so it is not obvious that the same quantity is being approximated in the two theories. The lecturer's reply was that a sequence can be known to converge without the limit ever being observed: a Cauchy sequence in a complete space has a limit whether or not anyone is in a position to name it.

Please audit the analogy in full. Specifically, I would like:

(i) an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the summit, the altitude function, the route, the camps, the index of the sequence, and the metric in which distances are measured;

(ii) the disanalogies, separated into those that bear on the intended use (defeating the pessimistic meta-induction, and establishing that a successor theory is truthliker than its predecessor) and those that are decorative;

(iii) an account of what the analogy quietly imports — a single determinate target, a complete space of theories, a linear ordering of them, and the stability of the measuring rod across the whole ascent — together with an assessment of whether each import is available to a realist who may not already assume what she is trying to prove;

(iv) a verdict on the Cauchy-criterion reply: whether it survives the collapse of Popper's original quantitative definition of verisimilitude, on which no two false theories can be ordered by closeness to the truth at all, and whether the convergence picture does any work that a merely comparative account of truthlikeness could not do without it;

(v) if the mountain cannot carry the conclusion, a better analogy or model for successive approximation — one that delivers a graded notion of closeness without presupposing a summit we are licensed to reach — along with a candid statement of what the replacement costs.

I am not after a bibliography or a list of further reading. I want the reasoning laid out, and I would rather you say plainly where the analogy is carrying weight that the argument cannot.
