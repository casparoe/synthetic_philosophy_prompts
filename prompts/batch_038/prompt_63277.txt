Two people in my reading group have spent the better part of a year talking past each other about the boundary problem — who belongs to the demos, and who gets to decide that. One is a committed all-affected theorist: if a collective decision touches your interests, you're owed a say, and demoi don't come with natural edges drawn around them. The other defends the all-subjected view: the claim to a say tracks being subject to an authority that coercively claims jurisdiction over you, not being causally downstream of everything that authority does. Each keeps accusing the other of changing the subject mid-argument, and I think at least one of their fights really is about words — but I can't get them to concede it in the same room.

So write the document they should have produced a year ago: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two. Anchor everything to one running case, which I invented, so don't go hunting for it:

Kalder is an island territory of about 90,000 people, formally part of the state of Fenmark. Around 20,000 seasonal fishery workers from a neighboring country spend eight months a year there and pay local taxes, but can't vote in anything. Fenmark's national government has approved an offshore wind array in waters Kalder's fleet works; the export cable comes ashore on Kalder, and the revenue goes to the national treasury, not the island. The turbines also shift the migration route of a stock that a third country's fleet harvests further out. Kalder's assembly now wants a referendum on independence. On the table as the franchise for it: registered residents of three years' standing; all residents including the seasonal workers; all Fenmark citizens nationwide, on the grounds that secession changes their country too; or nobody, with the national parliament deciding instead. Separately, the seabed lease is paid into a trust whose beneficiaries will mostly be born after the referendum, under a contract that runs sixty years. And the assembly wants to put the franchise question itself to a vote — which rather assumes you already know who's voting.

What I want in the document:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. Some should be about Kalder specifically, not just abstract machinery.
2. A disagreements table with four columns: the issue; how the all-affected side states it in wording its own advocate would accept; how the all-subjected side states it in wording its own advocate would accept; and a verdict on whether that disagreement is genuine or verbal, with one line of reasoning. At least one row has to come out verbal and at least one has to survive as real.
3. Three cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of the two. I'd bet one is whether "affected" and "subject to" pick out the same set in ordinary cases and only come apart at the edges; I'd bet another is the trust beneficiaries who can't be consulted and won't exist to be included. Pick your own third — the franchise question putting itself to a vote looks promising to me. For each crux, give the case, argument or test both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction, recorded in advance of seeing the other's.

Two rules. No strawmen: if you notice you've written one side's position so that it obviously loses, rewrite it until a partisan of that side would nod. And no citations, no bibliography, no quotations from anyone living or dead — I want your formulations, not your recall, and if you can't resist naming a book, cut the sentence.

Finish with a short paragraph saying how confident you are in the position-attributions you've made here, and naming the one claim you're least sure both parties would actually sign.
