I'm drafting the second chapter of my dissertation on bounded rationality and I want to know whether my own verdicts about when settling for good-enough is rational actually add up to a principle. Going case by case:

1. Picking the fourth restaurant that clears my bar instead of walking the whole street: rational.
2. Accepting the first job offer above my reservation wage, in a market where plenty more offers are coming: rational.
3. Accepting the first person who clears my bar as a partner, on the same logic, bc there'll be more people later: not rational — and I can't say why in cost/benefit terms, because the search structure looks identical to 2.
4. A doctor who stops at the first treatable diagnosis when a ten minute test would probably find a better one: not rational.
5. Hunting for a better apartment after I've already signed a lease, where looking costs me nothing but attention: also not rational, but in the opposite direction from 4.
6. Refusing on principle to switch to a slightly better index fund: rational.

The pairs that fight each other are 2/3 and 4/5. For 2/3, same expected-gain-vs-search-cost math, opposite verdicts. For 4/5, one says keep gathering info when it's cheap and the other says stop when it's free, so a pure value-of-information account seems to rank them the wrong way round.

Give me a principle — or two or three competing ones — that delivers as many of these as it can. For each, say what every clause is doing there and which case motivates it, what it predicts for a case I haven't listed (say, whether to keep interviewing candidates for a job I could fill tomorrow), and be straight with me about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. No lit review or reading list, I just want the account stated precisely enough that I could argue with it.
