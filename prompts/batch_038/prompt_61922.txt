Fifteen years as a developmental editor at a trade press, mostly history and biography, and two years ago I started auditing a philosophy of history seminar, partly just to get language for what I'd been doing at my desk all that time. We've been doing the old argument about whether historians explain by appealing to laws or by telling stories, and I keep feeling like both camps are talking past something I ran into constantly and nobody's named. My seminar partner thinks I'm describing the difference between a good lecture and a good outline, which is not it.

Here's the situation. A historian hands in a chapter explaining some collapse, or some decision, and it works — you finish it and you genuinely understand why it happened. Then you need the argument in one paragraph for jacket copy, and the author supplies one, and it's completely accurate: same factors, roughly the same order, same causal connections, nothing added, nothing hedged. And it explains nothing. Not because it's compressed, either — I've read plenty of compressed arguments that land hard. It's that the paragraph only makes sense to someone who has already read the chapter. To anyone else it's a list of labels with arrows between them. Meanwhile the chapter, which contains no claim the paragraph doesn't contain, does all the work.

I've tried to locate where the difference lives and its not in the facts, because you can't point to a fact in the chapter that's absent from the paragraph. And it isn't just the sequence: I once had an author restructure a perfectly good chapter so it opened with the assassination instead of closing on it, and it stayed a good chapter, a different one. What dies under compression is the sense, at each step, that the alternatives to that step had just been closed off. The chapter walks you through a narrowing, and you're at each point ignorant of what comes next; the paragraph reports that a narrowing occurred. So the explanatory force seems to depend on the reader being moved through the elimination of options in a particular order under conditions of not-yet-knowing, and that's exactly the thing a summary of the same content can't carry.

I don't want to be told this is narrative explanation and sent away — I'm sure it is, and that's not narrow enough to be useful. I want the narrower thing: an account whose content is entirely propositional and fully stateable, but whose explanatory force isn't transferable to any statement of that content. I know people have written about how a good historical account is something you can follow along in, and about tying events together under some larger heading, and from the other side I've seen the claim that narrative is packaging doing no cognitive work whatsoever, and none of those is this. So — what are the two or three nearest established concepts, how is what I've described different from each, and which fits best? If nothing fits, coin something and give me a definition with the boundary cases worked out, because that's the part I actually need: does a proof in mathematics have the property? a well-made diagram? a mechanism description in biology? a detective novel? If the answer turns out to be yes to all four, then I've described something trivial and I'd rather find that out now than in week nine.
