I spent three hours last night arguing with a friend of mine — she's a fourth-year graduate student in the physics department; I'm in philosophy — at a diner two blocks from campus, over coffee that went cold around eleven and a paper placemat she eventually covered in state vectors. The argument began, as these things always begin, with a reading-group discussion of the Everett interpretation, and it ended where it always ends: whether the many-worlds picture can make any sense at all of probability. She thinks it can, and thinks the decision-theoretic program shows precisely how; I think that program either proves considerably less than its partisans claim or assumes the very thing it was supposed to derive. Neither of us budged, and she left to catch the last train. I've been turning it over since, and I'd like you to reconstruct the argument the way it ought to have gone, between two characters who are both markedly better at it than we were at one in the morning.

Write a dialogue of roughly 1,100–1,400 words between an Everettian and a sceptic about Everettian probability. Set it concretely: the two of them are in the instrument bay of a teaching lab just after a long run has finished — a thousand measurements of the same observable on identically prepared spin-½ systems, each prepared in a state whose two amplitudes are 1/√3 and √(2/3), so that the squared weights are one third and two thirds. The readout shows a frequency close to two-to-one, and that is what they are arguing about: what those numbers were evidence of before the run, what either of them would have bet on beforehand, and whether "two thirds" means anything at all in a theory where both outcomes happen every time. Speaker labels, light stage direction, and prose that sounds like two clever people talking rather than one paper sawed in half.

Both characters must be fully competent and neither may be a strawman; they should answer each other's actual points, and at least twice one of them should be pushed into conceding or refining something rather than simply repeating a line. The Everettian has to reconstruct the machinery of the derivation instead of waving at it — the symmetry principle that branches of equal amplitude must be treated alike, and the indifference principle according to which an agent should not care about a branching that leaves the pairing of rewards to outcomes untouched — and has to deploy the fine-graining reply: if branch *number* were what mattered, one could double the count of up-branches simply by describing the environment in more detail, which cannot be right when decoherence makes the branch structure vague and divisible at will. The sceptic has to press two objections and keep them distinct. The first is that both principles already encode a claim about how much an agent ought to care about the various successors she will become, and that caring in proportion to squared amplitude just *is* the Born rule wearing a decision-theoretic hat. The second is that in a deterministic theory where every outcome occurs, probability has no subject matter: nothing happens less often than anything else, and the Everettian's fallback — that most branches, by measure, will see Born-rule frequencies — uses the measure in question to define "most," which is exactly what needed justifying. The Everettian may bring in the indexical line, that between the measurement and the looking there is a genuine interval of self-locating uncertainty about which branch she will find herself on; the sceptic should press whether indexical uncertainty can bear that weight if all her successors exist equally. No named authorities, no titles, no bibliography, no "as X argued" — the arguments must stand on their own, and any numbers, amplitudes, and weights must be used consistently and correctly. Do not resolve the dialogue: nobody converts, nobody concedes the main point. But the final exchanges should leave the disagreement sharpened to a fine edge, so that a reader can say exactly which principle is in dispute and what it would take to settle it.
