I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a feature on the durability of the explanatory gap: why the claim that consciousness has finally been naturalised keeps arriving, and keeps having to be walked back. One section concerns the Victorian precursors, and the pivotal figure there is John Tyndall, specifically the argument he advanced in the address he gave at the Royal Institution in 1870 on the scientific use of the imagination, which was subsequently gathered into his collected essays. My desk copy is in the office and I am working from notes, so treat the provenance as provisional and everything below as a close paraphrase in my own words rather than as Tyndall's phrasing.

The paraphrase runs as follows. Tyndall invites us to suppose that the structure and dynamics of the brain have been completely mastered, so that every molecular motion, grouping and discharge is known, together with the states of consciousness that answer to them. He then asks whether we could reason from one set of facts to the other, and answers that we could not: the inference lies beyond our reach, and the passage from the physics of the brain to the corresponding facts of consciousness is unthinkable. There is a further sentence, one I have not managed to pin down, to the effect that even were we to possess the intellectual power, or if need be the intellectual organ, required to make that inference, it would remain beyond us; and he observes that between the two classes of fact there is no intelligible nexus. He then makes a move I think is usually skipped. If molecules became conscious in the sense of knowing their own condition and operations, or those of other molecules, what would such a consciousness learn about molecules as distinct from billiard balls? Nothing whatever. Finally, he insists that none of this overthrows the materialist position, since matter and mind are alike symbolic constructions, with something inscrutable behind and above both.

Three questions, in order.

First. That conditional concerning the intellectual power, or if required the intellectual organ, is doing something I cannot determine, and my section may turn on it. I can read it two ways. On one reading Tyndall is saying that bridging the two vocabularies would require a cognitive endowment we lack, which makes the gap a fact about our equipment and therefore closable in principle by a differently constituted intellect — a nineteenth-century anticipation of the claim that we are cognitively closed to the mind-body connection. On the other, he is granting the endowment hypothetically and holding that the inference fails even then, which would make the gap structural rather than merely ours. Which reading does the remainder of the argument support, given the billiard-balls remark and his refusal to abandon materialism? If the text genuinely admits both, tell me what would have to be true of Tyndall's wider commitments for one of them to be correct.

Second. Is this the same gap as the contemporary one? The modern formulation is that a complete structural and dynamic account leaves open the further question why any of it is accompanied by experience at all, and that no further structure closes it. Tyndall's complaint sounds different in kind: it concerns the impossibility of an inference between two vocabularies, which a physicalist may shrug off by saying the relation in question is identity rather than inference, and that deducibility is the wrong thing to demand where there is only sameness. Does Tyndall have the resources to resist that response? My hunch is that the resistance lives in the billiard-balls remark, because it denies that the mechanical vocabulary reaches its own objects either, so the identity reply is left with nothing determinate to identify with. Test that hunch, and tell me bluntly whether it holds or whether I am loading a rhetorical flourish with argumentative work it cannot bear.

Third. Is it defensible to present Tyndall as an ancestor of the hard problem, or does that misrepresent him in a way an informed reader would catch? Does anything here commit him to dualism, to epiphenomenalism, or to placing consciousness outside nature? And if he is not an ancestor of the modern debate, how should I characterise the positive position he is actually defending?

A note on the form of your answer. Between seven hundred and a thousand words of continuous prose pitched at an intelligent general reader, since I may adapt some of it, answering the three questions in order. Do not supply quotations, citations, editions or a reading list; every attribution will be checked against the book before publication, and a plausible-looking quotation is worse to me than none at all. Where my paraphrase looks as though it has drifted from what Tyndall argues, say so and say how. Where the material underdetermines an interpretive question, say that rather than choosing the tidier answer. Close with a single short paragraph telling me which of my two hunches — the cognitive-closure reading in the first question, or the resistance to the identity reply in the second — I ought to strike from the draft.
