For thirty-seven years I worked as an experimental scientist, and a great deal of that time was spent looking at pictures: diffraction patterns, thin sections, autoradiographs, the kind of data where the question of whether there is a signal present or merely a stain on the emulsion is settled, in the end, by looking, and then by having someone else look. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy of science with a small reading group, and last week we arrived at the familiar claim that all observation is theory-laden. One of my fellow readers put it to work in a way I have not been able to answer. We had been discussing two laboratories examining the same raw data and reporting different things — one sees an effect, the other sees an artefact — and he said that because all observation is theory-laden, there is no such thing as retreating to the naked evidence; the dispute cannot be adjudicated by further looking, however careful, and can only end when one side abandons its framework. I found this unsatisfying but could not say why, because in the laboratory that sentence meant something altogether more modest and, so far as I could tell, uncontroversial: that a new graduate student does not see in a micrograph what an old hand sees in the same micrograph, and that this is precisely why we blind the scoring, calibrate against standards, and insist that plates be read twice by different people. Those practices seem to me to presuppose that looking can be made neutral, or at any rate more neutral, by suitable precautions — not that neutrality is a comforting fiction.

So I should like the sentence disassembled. What are the distinct claims that "all observation is theory-laden" could be making? Please lay them out as a menu, running from the near-trivial — perhaps something about the vocabulary in which an observation is reported, along the lines of Hanson's example of Tycho and Kepler watching the same sunrise and not, on his telling, seeing the same thing — through to the radical ones about the perceptual event itself. For each reading I want three things: a statement precise enough that one could tell whether it is true or false; a verdict, marking it as a platitude, as substantive but defensible, as genuinely contested, or as false; and an account of what would establish or refute it, whether by argument or by experiment. In particular, I want to know which readings require genuine cognitive penetration of perception in the technical sense, and which remain perfectly compatible with perception being encapsulated, since I suspect that several of the readings concern things that happen before the seeing — what one points the instrument at, what one attends to, what one is trained to look for — or after it, in what one says, remembers, and concludes, and that these are not remotely the same thesis wearing different clothes.

Finally, tell me which reading my colleague actually needed. His conclusion was not the mild one that the two laboratories disagree sincerely on the same evidence; it was that no quantity of fresh, careful, blinded looking could settle the matter, and that only a conversion could. Which entries on your list would license that, and are they the same entries that make Hanson's example philosophically interesting, or has something been smuggled across? If the strong claim turns out to be unsupported by any reading that survives scrutiny, please say so plainly rather than offering a diplomatic compromise; and if some weaker claim does survive, tell me what it implies about my old profession and what it conspicuously fails to imply. I should like something I can read out at next week's meeting, so short labels I can keep straight in my head would be a kindness.
