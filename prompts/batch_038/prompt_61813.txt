I'm a software engineer — distributed systems for most of my career, and increasingly ML infrastructure — with no formal philosophy training, so I'd ask you to correct my framing where it's naive rather than work inside it.

The situation: during a post-incident review last month, one sentence ended an argument, and I've since noticed the same sentence doing the same work in three unrelated contexts. We had a fleet-wide failure in which every individual service behaved exactly to specification and yet the global behaviour was one nobody had designed, predicted, or could point to in any component's code. I asked for the mechanism — which interleaving, which queue, which retry policy, what the actual causal chain was — and the response was that global behaviour of that kind is emergent: the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, and looking for a component-level mechanism is a category error. The phrase also turns up constantly in the ML material I read, where capabilities that appear only above some scale are called emergent, and in at least one widely discussed argument the apparent sharpness of those transitions is attributed to the researcher's choice of evaluation metric rather than to anything in the system. And I've heard it used a third way, in a discussion of whether an ant colony or a market has beliefs and goals, where it seemed to mean something closer to "there is a real agent up there."

My suspicion is that one sentence is covering at least four or five different claims, that some of them are trivial, that some of them are incompatible with each other, and that the reason it shuts arguments down is that it slides unnoticed between them. I want a disambiguation, not a general introduction to emergence — please don't survey the territory or tell me which camp is which; I want the claims separated and priced.

Specifically:

1. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making. For each, be explicit about what is said to be irreducible to what, and in which sense of irreducible: not derivable in principle from base-level laws plus boundary conditions; derivable but only at infeasible computational cost; not deducible without bridge principles or identifications that are themselves no part of the base theory; not capturable by any compressed macro-level model; not predictable by an observer situated inside the system; possessing causal powers the base level lacks; or not supervening on the base level at all. Give each reading a short label of your own devising so I can refer back to it, and order them from least to most radical.

2. For each reading, give me a verdict rather than a neutral description: is it trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive and probably false, or incoherent? Where a reading is defensible, say what would have to be the case for it to be true; where it fails, say how it fails.

3. Say which readings collapse into one another or into something with an unrelated name — for instance, whether any of them turns out to be a claim about our models or our compute budget rather than about the world, and whether any of them turns out to be nothing more than the base-level description being long.

4. Mark clearly which readings are epistemic and which are ontological, and identify the points at which an argument can move from one to the other without anyone noticing. I want to know whether the rhetorical force of the slogan depends on that slide, or whether it would still work if everyone were careful.

5. For each reading, answer the question I actually brought: does it license the dismissal? Does it make my demand for a causal chain a category error, or does it merely make the demand expensive, or does it leave the demand untouched? If no reading licenses it, say so plainly.

6. Here is my own candidate precisification, which is engineer-shaped and may be worthless. Call a global property emergent relative to a base-level description if and only if the shortest program that predicts the global property from that description is not appreciably shorter than a full simulation of the base level — that is, if no compressed macro-model exists. Please evaluate this seriously: does it capture anything my colleague wanted, is it stable under changes in how the base level is described, does it conflict with determinism or with the causal closure of the base level, and does it collapse into the idea of computational irreducibility or into one of the readings above?

7. Two knots I can't untangle myself. First, if every component behaves exactly as the base-level laws dictate and those laws are causally closed, is there any room left for a distinct global cause — or must the global cause always be either identical to some enormously complicated base-level event or not a cause at all? I understand there is a general argument in this vicinity to the effect that higher-level causation is excluded by the completeness of the lower level; tell me which of the readings on your menu survive that argument and which do not, and whether any reading is specifically designed to survive it. Second, is the ML usage — a capability appearing at scale — even in the same family as the usage in my incident review, or do the two share nothing but a word? If the metric-artifact argument succeeds, which readings does it defeat and which does it leave standing?

Length is not a concern; precision is. I'd rather have a shorter menu with honest verdicts on each item than an exhaustive one that declines to say which claims are false.
