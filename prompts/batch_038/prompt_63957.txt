I'm TA-ing a second-year decision theory seminar and week 4 is the two-envelop problem, which goes badly every single year. Half the room read some blog post over the weekend and turn up already sorted into camps, and the other half think the whole thing is a cheap trick with an obvious answer and get bored. Last year I handed out a sheet that just listed the standard diagnoses with the names attached, which meant everyone looked the names up in advance and then spent the hour arguing about who said what instead of about the actual arithmetic. My supervisor has asked that this year's version not mention a single philosopher, paper or book — no names, no citations, nothing to go and google.

So what I want instead is a decision tree. It should start from a student who accepts both of the following: that the naive swap calculation seems to hand them a quarter more than they're holding, and that the counting argument — the two amounts are X and 2X, you're equally likely to be holding either, so you gain X or lose X — seems to show swapping can't possibly help. And it should end at a position, except that the labels at the leaves have to be descriptive ones you make up, not anyone's name. Ten to twelve branch nodes, seven to nine leaves. The important bit: every node has to be a question a student can answer without knowing which way the answer pushes them, so no node may be answerable by declaring a camp. I'm thinking of questions about what the letter for the amount in your envelope is actually picking out in the two different places it turns up in the calculation; about whether any probability assignment over what the organiser chose could make "the other one is double" and "the other one is half" equally likely for every amount you might be holding; about whether it matters that the pair of amounts was fixed before you picked rather than generated from your envelope afterwards; about whether they've been allowed to look first; about whether they're prepared to live with an expectation that comes out infinite or just doesn't settle down; and about whether the right frame is what policy to commit to in advance or what to do now with a number in front of them.

For the leaves, I want one sentence each on the most awkward commitment the position carries, plus which of the variants below it handles worst and what it's forced to say there. The variants I'll print on the same sheet, all spelled out from scratch so nobody needs outside knowledge: (a) the plain one — organiser picks an amount, fair coin decides which envelope gets the double, you choose blind and can't look; (b) same, but you may open your envelope and see the number before deciding; (c) same as (b), except you're told in advance that no envelope contains more than £10,000; (d) the lopsided organiser — she writes a number in the first envelope, then flips a coin and puts either double that or half that in the second one, and you're holding the first; (e) the one where the organiser's amount comes from something with no finite average, so the expected gains never settle; (f) the threshold policy — after opening, you manufacture a random number with your own device and swap if and only if what you see is below it, which apparently lands you the bigger amount strictly more often than half the time whatever the organiser did. And please build the tree from the logical space first: give me the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the actual positions in that space, marking any combination that nobody occupies and saying whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's thought of it yet. Finally, flag honestly any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate. My suspicion is that the "you're using the same letter for two different quantities" diagnosis and the "no such probability assignment exists" diagnosis give identical advice in (a) and (b), so if they end up on the same branch, tell me which further question would pry them apart — or admit that it can't be done at this size and that I should just merge them.
