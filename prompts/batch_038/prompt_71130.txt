I'm setting the final paper for our third-year philosophy of mind module and I want to write the consciousness question. It's a weird cohort — about half are psychology students who've done some actual consciousness science and think philosophers just talk in circles, half are philosophy students who've mostly read summaries of the summaries and think the psychologists are missing the point entirely. Three hours, they answer two out of six, 25 marks each, so call it 50 minutes per question.

The crux I'm going for is this. Global workspace theory looks like it flat-out identifies phenomenal consciousness with global availability — ignition, broadcasting to the rest of the cortex, whatever label you prefer — and if that's right then Block's access/phenomenal distinction doesn't just lose an empirical battle, it stops being a distinction with any content, because P collapses into A by definition. But Block's overflow cases are supposed to be empirical counterexamples rather than conceptual ones, and the obvious comeback is that the only handle we ever get on access is report or task use, so the debate just relocates into how you operationalise the word "access." I want the question to make students say what kind of result could actually wedge the two positions apart, and then decide whether that report/accessibility worry is a knockdown objection to the distinction or just a restatement of it in experimental clothing. What I absolutely do not want is anyone banking marks for correctly defining P-consciousness and A-consciousness and then stopping, or for narrating the ignition story back at me.

So I've made up a study and I want the question built around it, partly so it can't be answered from memory and partly because I like the shape of it. Six letters flashed for 50ms in a circle around fixation, three conditions. Condition 1, no mask: cued-position report is about 95% correct, and a letter at that position produces a standard priming effect on a lexical decision 200ms later. Condition 2, pattern mask 100ms after the array: cued-position report drops to chance, but the priming from the masked letters is exactly the same size as in condition 1. Condition 3, mask again, cue delayed by 2 seconds rather than given immediately, plus a separate no-report block where nothing is ever asked: cued-position report at chance, priming intact, and the no-report forced-choice about which half of the circle looked brighter sits at 68% against a 50% baseline while participants insist on every single trial that they saw nothing at all. I want students to have to argue, condition by condition, whether any of this is evidence for experience outrunning access; to say what a workspace theorist who takes access to come in degrees rather than all-or-nothing does with the priming and the brightness numbers; to name the control or manipulation you'd need before the data counted against the theory at all; and to give a verdict on whether a theory is empty if every candidate measure of P turns out to also be a measure of A. If you think that's too much for 50 minutes, cut it down and tell me what you cut and why.

What I need back: the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, roughly 300–350 words, self-contained enough that a student who has never seen the study can work with it (describe the three conditions in the question itself, don't assume they know partial-report paradigms); a model answer of about 850 words that would genuinely get full marks and not just a competent B; and a marking scheme out of 25 broken into five must-hit moves, with the marks for each and, for each one, a line on the specific way students reliably mess it up — I'm expecting things like treating the priming result as automatically a case of consciousness without access instead of asking whether priming is itself a form of access, and declaring the theory unfalsifiable without ever saying why ignition isn't just an empirical postulate that could be wrong. Also tack on one short optional part worth 3 bonus marks aimed at the very top end, and tell me where in the scheme you'd expect my psychology students and my philosophy students to each lose marks, because I want to see whether the question is actually fair to both halves of the room or whether I've written something that only rewards one of them.
