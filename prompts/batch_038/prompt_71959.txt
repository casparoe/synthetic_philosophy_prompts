Two months ago I took a seat on the governing council of a professional association — roughly four hundred members, a written constitution, and, like most such bodies, an amendment article near the end that says the constitution may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the council followed by ratification by a simple majority of the membership. Last month the council used that article to amend the article itself, dropping the membership-ratification step for changes characterized as "procedural." A retired member objected in writing that the change is void, on the ground that a power cannot be used to enlarge itself: the amendment article authorized amendments to the constitution, he argued, but an amendment that rewrites the conditions of its own authorization has no source of authority left once it takes effect. Nobody on the council, myself included, could say whether that was a real objection or a rhetorical one, and we have no court to appeal to. Trying to think it through, I kept landing on the British Parliament Acts, which seem to be the same problem at scale and with an authoritative answer attached.

Here are the facts as I understand them, so you do not have to reconstruct them. The Parliament Act 1911, extracted from the Lords after their rejection of the 1909 budget, let a public bill become law without the Lords' consent if the Commons passed it in three successive sessions with at least two years elapsing between the first second reading and final passage — subject to two carve-outs, for money bills and for "a Bill containing any provision to extend the maximum duration of Parliament beyond five years." In 1949, over the Lords' continued objection, Parliament used that very procedure to amend the 1911 Act, cutting three sessions and two years down to two sessions and one year. Legislation under the amended procedure has been enacted several times since, including the Hunting Act 2004, which is what was challenged. The challenge reached the United Kingdom's highest court in 2005 on the argument that the 1911 Act created a delegated or subordinate lawmaking power — Commons and Crown acting under authority conferred by the full Parliament — and that a delegate cannot use that power to widen the power delegated to it. The court rejected the argument and upheld the 1949 Act, and with it the Hunting Act. I also understand, though I am less sure of the detail, that members of the bench raised without deciding whether a two-step would stand differently: first repeal the express carve-out under the amended procedure, then extend the life of Parliament.

Please treat that episode as a test case for the theory my colleague was groping toward, which I will state as bluntly as I can. On this account, the validity of any legal norm must derive from a higher norm that is in force at the moment of enactment; a rule that authorizes its own revision therefore faces a self-referential difficulty, since the norm supplying its authority is the very one it displaces, and the sequence either regresses or lapses into something like the liar — a constitutional version of "this clause is repealed." Rival accounts deny the premise: one holds that sovereignty may be either continuing (no Parliament can bind its successors) or self-embracing (it can, at least as to manner and form), and that the choice between them is not forced by logic; another holds that the ultimate rule identifying what counts as law in a system is not itself a legal rule but a social fact about official practice, so "self-amendment" is a category mistake rather than a paradox.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a survey. Identify the players and the steps precisely: at each moment in 1911, 1949, and 2004, which body is acting, under what claimed authority, and which rule supplies that authority — and whether the authority in question is exhausted, narrowed, or preserved by the act performed. Then say which features of the episode the self-amendment paradox actually explains and which it cannot touch. My suspicion is that it gets real purchase on the express carve-out and the two-step worry, where the text itself seems to place something beyond the reach of the procedure it creates, but that it says nothing about why fifty-odd years of untroubled official acceptance of the 1949 Act carried the day, and nothing about how much work the characterization of the 1911 Act power as "delegated" rather than as an alternative manner of legislating was doing in the appellants' argument. Tell me whether that suspicion is right, and where the continuing-versus-self-embracing and rule-of-recognition accounts do better or worse than the paradox — in particular whether either can explain why the second order (removing the carve-out) feels different from the first (shortening the delay), or whether that difference is only a matter of political salience. Finally: does any of this transfer to a private association's bylaws, where there is no sovereign, no rule of recognition in Hart's sense, and no court of last resort — and if the logic does not settle whether our amendment is valid, what does?

Two constraints. Please flag uncertainty about any detail of the litigation rather than filling in around it, and do not build the answer on materials I have not described; I cannot check them.
