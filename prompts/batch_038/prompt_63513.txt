So, context: I'm a third-year political theory PhD student and I co-host our department's little podcast, which in practice means I book the guests and my co-host does most of the actual talking. Next month we've got a senior scholar coming on who defends a fairly heterodox line on dirty hands. Roughly: the dirt generated by the familiar official wrongdoing — the torture-the-bomber's-brother case, the negotiated-with-terrorists case, the assassinate-the-insurgent-leader-and-let-the-collateral-damage-happen case — doesn't stay on the official's hands at all. It travels *up* the chain of authorization and settles on the demos that installed her and keeps re-electing her. On her picture the authorized official is literally acting as the people's hands, so the people are the dirty ones and the ones who owe the remorse and maybe the punishment, while the official's own standing is comparatively clean — at most she bears a kind of taint-by-participation rather than guilt. I've only made it through two chapters of the book, so most of what I know comes from reviews and a couple of her journal articles, and I'm worried I'll end up asking her softball questions she's answered four hundred times.

What I want is eight to ten interview questions, ordered from most concessive at the top to most adversative at the bottom, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — not a vague "and why do you think that," but a real rejoinder that presupposes her actual reply and pushes one step further. A few pressure points I definitely want hit, in whatever order works: (1) whether the transmission move just relocates the dirt or quietly deletes it, i.e. if an act is authorized and therefore not the official's wrong, in what sense is the episode still morally dirty rather than merely grim or costly; (2) secrecy — what happens under her view to covert action the demos never learns about, which seems to be exactly the category where officials most need the concept; (3) non-democracies, where the transmission mechanism is broken or fake, and whether she's then back inside the standard Walzerian picture or committed to saying the autocrat is clean; (4) whether she's just redescribing the principal-agent structure of representation with moral vocabulary, and what work the moral talk is doing that the ordinary accountability talk isn't; (5) the complicity side of things — if the dirt goes up, does every voter get a share, does abstention or protesting-then-losing wash your hands, and how does she avoid making voting itself a dirty act; (6) who now owes what — apology, punishment, reparations — and what democratic guilt is supposed to feel like institutionally as opposed to just being a metaphor; (7) whether her view can make sense of the intuition that we want officials who are *reluctant*, and of the difference between an official who does the dirty thing and loses sleep over it and one who does it cheerfully, if neither is actually guilty.

Ground rules: no generic questions of the "what drew you to this topic" variety, and nothing that just asks her to summarize the book for listeners who haven't read it — our audience is grad students in political theory and philosophy, so they'll already have read the Walzer paper and at least skimmed Weber on the ethic of responsibility versus the ethic of conviction, and I'd rather not spend twenty minutes of the recording on that history. One of the questions should be a genuine opener that lets her state the thesis in her own words without being a summary request, and I'd also like one "interrupt" question held in reserve for the case where she starts reciting the Weber/Walzer lineage instead of defending the upward-transmission claim. Keep each question to two or three sentences so it's actually speakable out loud, and flag the two or three places where you think she has the strongest answer, because I'd rather know in advance which of these I'm going to lose.
