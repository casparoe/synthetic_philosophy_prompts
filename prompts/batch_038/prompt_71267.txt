Six weeks ago I accepted the post of unpaid secretary to our parish flood action group, largely because I was no longer prepared to be the person who merely complains. My own house in Calderwick took nine inches of water last November, and since then I have found that the neighbours expect the group to answer questions nobody in it is qualified to answer. We are eleven volunteers; we advise the parish council, which holds roughly £40,000 a year of its own and can bid into a county grant scheme; and we meet on the second Tuesday of each month.

The difficulty is not the hydrology, it is the arithmetic of belief. Asked how likely a repeat is within twenty years, we are handed four things that will not combine: twenty-two years of gauge readings on the brook, a figure from a modelling consultancy that shifts appreciably between runs, the insistence of a retired hydrologist on the group that the record is far too short for anyone to state a number narrower than a factor of three either way, and a single value quoted from the national agency's published risk map. Averaging these feels like a lie; choosing one feels like a worse lie. Meanwhile the county grant form asks for "the annual probability of the hazard" as a decimal, in one box, with no room for commentary.

I should like you to write a decision procedure this group can actually run, grounded in whatever the formal literature on imprecise probabilities offers that is usable by people with A-level mathematics at best: representing belief as a range or a set of distributions rather than a point, the competing rules that have been proposed for choosing between options when one's probabilities come as intervals, and the result that acquiring further evidence can leave one's range wider than before rather than narrower. Tell me which of these ideas bear weight in a setting like ours and which are curiosities I can safely ignore.

The procedure should give the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which it stops with "we cannot recommend this"; the point at which it hands the matter to the parish council because what remains is a question of values rather than of likelihood; and an explicit rule for converting whatever range we end up holding into the single decimal the grant form demands, together with the sentence of cover we should write in the covering letter. It must also separate two things we currently run together — vagueness caused by having too little data, and vagueness caused by genuine disagreement about which model of the catchment is right — since I suspect they warrant different responses, and it should say what those responses are. Constraint: it has to survive being run in forty-five minutes by volunteers, and its output has to be recordable in minutes that a county officer may later read.

Please then run it on three matters before us. First, whether to spend £12,000 on resistance measures for the six worst-affected homes, two of which are on my own street; I want this funded and I am braced for the procedure saying otherwise. Second, whether to object to forty houses on the field upstream, where the developer's consultant reports a negligible addition to our peak flows and our hydrologist says the model behind that report omits a culvert that has already been surcharging. Third, a resident's written request for the chance that her particular house floods within twenty years — a request I expect the procedure to refuse, in which case tell me what to put in the letter instead.

Mark plainly which steps are judgement calls wearing a procedural costume, and close by stating what this procedure cannot do and the uses to which it must never be put. Begin your response with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single most important thing this group should change about how it handles probabilities — and then give the reasoning and the procedure, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
