I write a fortnightly column on the science and philosophy of decision-making for a general-interest magazine, and I have been commissioned to deliver roughly two thousand words on why groups that look unanimous are not necessarily groups that know anything. The occasion is a widely covered episode in which a single unreplicated preprint appeared to reorganize a field's research agenda inside a month, and my editor wants the piece to be about the mechanism rather than the scandal.

What follows is the analytical core of the draft, about seven hundred words, in which I make most of the substantive claims I intend to defend. Everything before and after it is scene-setting and reporting, which is not what I need help with. I would be grateful for critical comments on this section before it goes to my editor.

DRAFT — THE ANALYTICAL CORE

There are two familiar stories about collective judgment, and most popular accounts treat them as rivals. The first says that averaging many independent guesses beats consulting the best expert: pool the estimates and the errors cancel. The second says that groups stampede — one person buys, the next sees the purchase and buys, and by the tenth the whole street holds an asset nobody has independently valued. The second story is usually told as a story about weakness of character: fear, fashion, the wish to belong.

The interesting version of it involves no weakness at all. In the standard models of informational cascades that appeared in the early 1990s, the herd is populated entirely by competent Bayesians. What makes them imitate is not a defect in their reasoning but a defect in their data. Each observes the actions of those ahead, and actions are coarse. You can see that your predecessor bought; you cannot see the private information that led her to buy, or how strong it was. Once the public history is long enough, that coarse record outweighs anything an individual can learn on her own.

The arithmetic is worth spelling out, because it is the whole argument. Suppose there are two possible states, your prior is even, and each of you receives a private signal that is correct two-thirds of the time. The first mover follows her signal. The second sees one purchase while holding a signal pointing the other way: the history counts two-to-one for buying, her signal counts two-to-one against, and she is exactly indifferent — so the outcome turns on a tie-breaking convention rather than on evidence. If she buys anyway, the third mover faces a history worth two signals, and a contradictory private signal no longer overturns it. From that point the public record conveys nothing new. Everyone after the third is imitating, the imitation is rational, and the group has stopped aggregating information at precisely the moment when it looks most unanimous. Note also that the number of people who genuinely act on their own information stays bounded however long the sequence runs.

Three consequences deserve more attention than they get. First, unanimity is cheap in a cascade: the agreement of a hundred people carries less information than their number suggests. This is the condition under which the classical jury-theorem result fails. That result requires errors that do not move together, and once errors are correlated beyond some threshold, majority rule can be worse than no rule at all. Second, cascades are fragile rather than stubborn. Because the accumulated history is a thin veneer, one public event, or one observed dissent, can overturn a herd that had looked settled; the unanimity that makes the crowd persuasive is what makes it unstable. Third — the point I most want to press — the complete-herding conclusion was later qualified by work showing that if private signals are sufficiently informative, full herding need not occur at all. Beliefs can keep moving even when actions do not, and there are configurations in which the population settles on the wrong answer while every individual continues to update perfectly sensibly. Whether a crowd stampedes depends on how much a single person's evidence can carry, not merely on how many people are watching.

I want to keep all of this distinct from Keynes's newspaper-competition picture, in which the difficulty is not to identify the prettiest face but to predict which face the average competitor will pick. That problem concerns guessing at other people's guesses; a cascade concerns reading other people's evidence. The two are routinely run together in financial writing.

So the stories are not rivals. Aggregation is about collecting private signals simultaneously; stampeding is about observing public actions sequentially. They describe different informational architectures, and a group can be moved from one to the other without anyone in it changing character. That is why the familiar exhortation to think for yourself does not touch the problem — independence is not a virtue individuals possess but a property of the arrangement they are placed in. It also complicates the advice to defer to experts. Expert communities are sequential too: they read one another's papers, cite one another's conclusions, and observe one another's hiring and funding decisions. Nothing about expertise automatically converts a cascade into an aggregation.

END OF DRAFT

I would like comments organized as numbered points, and I would rather receive four serious objections than a courteous summary. Please do not open with praise, and do not pad the response with restatement of what I have already written. Nine hundred to twelve hundred words is the right length. Specifically:

1. Check the technical claims item by item: the worked two-thirds example, including the indifference at the second position and the impossibility of overturning at the third; the claim that the number of people acting on their own information stays bounded; the claim that unanimity in a cascade is informationally cheap; the claim about correlated errors defeating the jury theorem; and the qualification about sufficiently informative private signals. For each, tell me whether it is correct, incorrect, or correct but standing in need of a hedge, and say what the hedge is. Where your confidence is low, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over — I have no way of checking anything you assert, and a false assurance costs me more than an admission of doubt.

2. Do not supply references, bibliographies, or further reading, and do not attribute claims to named papers or authors. My fact-checker handles attribution, and your guesses would only slow her down. If you believe something is true but cannot verify it, mark it as unverified.

3. Tell me whether the simultaneity-versus-sequence distinction actually carries the weight I put on it, or whether it is a familiar observation in new clothes. If you can construct a case that cuts against it — a group that observes actions sequentially and yet aggregates well, or one that collects signals simultaneously and yet stampedes — I want it, because my editor will ask for one.

4. Say whether my separation of the cascade from the beauty-contest problem survives contact with real financial markets, where prices function simultaneously as evidence about value and as part of what determines value. I suspect a reader will call it a distinction without a difference, and I would like to know whether I should argue for it harder or drop the paragraph.

5. Give me the single strongest objection a specialist would raise against the closing claim about expert communities, and tell me how I might absorb that objection without gutting the paragraph.

6. Identify the two sentences that are doing the most rhetorical work for the least argumentative support. I have my own candidates, and I would like to see whether they match yours.

7. Tell me whether the final paragraph is earned by what precedes it or is a leap I should explicitly mark as speculation. Note that I am aware the piece slides from models of individual decision-makers into a claim about institutions, and I want to know whether the slide is visible to a reader or merely to me.

8. Close with a verdict: does the central thesis survive scrutiny as stated, and what is the single change that would most improve the section?
