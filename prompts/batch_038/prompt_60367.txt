I work part-time for a parliamentary committee support unit, and our committee has been asked to advise on whether the government should adopt a single national wellbeing indicator to sit alongside GDP. In preparing the briefing I have spent several weeks with the annual global happiness ranking that is published each spring, together with the polling methodology behind it, and a worry has been growing in me that I cannot quite articulate. I suspect it is a philosophical worry rather than a statistical one, but I lack the vocabulary to state it, so I would like you to reconstruct it for me as precisely as possible.

The practice runs roughly as follows. The ranking rests on one survey item, answered by a sample of adults in each of well over a hundred countries, which Gallup describes as the Cantril Self-Anchoring Striving Scale: "Please imagine a ladder with steps numbered from zero at the bottom to 10 at the top. The top of the ladder represents the best possible life for you and the bottom of the ladder represents the worst possible life for you. On which step of the ladder would you say you personally feel you stand at this time?" Each country's answers are averaged, the averages are ordered, and the resulting table is reported in the press as a list of the happiest countries in the world. Alongside the ladder, the same polling operation also collects separate items about the previous day's experiences — whether the respondent smiled or laughed, felt rested and was treated with respect, versus whether they worried, felt sad or angry, or experienced pain — and it compiles those into distinct indices of daily positive and negative affect. Countries do not come out in the same order on those indices as they do on the ladder, and the gap is sometimes large. Meanwhile the ranking has become a scoreboard: one government created a cabinet-level portfolio explicitly dedicated to happiness and set national targets tied to it, while another long-running national index dispenses with the single question altogether and composes a score out of several domains, including psychological wellbeing, health, education, use of time, cultural vitality, governance, community life, ecological resilience, and material living standards.

Here is what I want from you. First, reconstruct the theory of wellbeing that the ladder-based ranking presupposes — as distinct from the theories it merely sits comfortably with — and say which rival accounts are ruled out by the very design of the question rather than by anything its authors argue. I am struck in particular by the fact that the top of the ladder is indexed to the respondent's own best possible life, not to any externally specified standard, and by the fact that the answer is taken from the person whose life is being assessed and accepted without correction. What does that commitment to a self-anchored, self-reported, single-dimensional number say about whose judgment counts, about whether a person can be mistaken about their life going well, and about whether one person's nine and another person's nine are the same quantity that can meaningfully be averaged? Second, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which parts are licensed equally well by a hedonist account, by a desire-satisfaction account, and by an objective-list account, such that the choice of instrument is not itself a commitment — and which parts genuinely require one of them. Third, address an apparent inconsistency that I cannot resolve: the ranking orders countries by the mean, while the accompanying commentary regularly urges attention to how happiness is distributed and to the plight of those at the bottom. Does ranking by an average presuppose that only the total matters, or is that presupposition contingent and detachable? Relatedly, the ladder measures where people say they stand relative to their own aspirations; if aspirations adjust to circumstances, what does the instrument then track, and what follows from the fact that a government could in principle raise its score by lowering its citizens' expectations rather than by improving their lives? Finally, say what the practice of a government adopting the ranking as a target presupposes about the aims of the state — including whether it is compatible with a view on which government is bound by rights and duties rather than charged with maximizing aggregate welfare — and what would concretely have to change, in the item, the aggregation, or the target itself, if we replaced the presupposed theory with an objective-list account or with one built on experienced affect. Please write in American English, and do not feel obliged to supply citations or references; I am after the argument and the reconstruction, not a bibliography.
