I'm 68, spent thirty-odd years modeling rivers and reservoirs for a state water agency, and since I retired I've been reading philosophy — mostly ethics — and I've gotten completely stuck on the killing vs letting die thing. Everyone I read either defends the distinction or says it does no work, and both sides pull out one example and declare victory. I've read the bathtub pair, Smith drowning the cousin and Jones watching the same cousin drown for the same inheritance, and I take the point, but it's a point about one case. What I want before I read another article is the spec.

So: what does a theory of doing vs allowing actually have to deliver to count as adequate? Purpose, since adequacy is relative to it — I want a theory good enough to sit underneath a real policy, the kind of thing that decides how a grid operator's or a triage team's choices get judged afterwards, not just good enough to win a seminar.

Give me the requirements list: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to come out right on, the distinctions it mustn't collapse, and the results from elsewhere — law, medicine, the empirical stuff about how people's judgments shift with framing — that it either has to be consistent with or has to explicitly reject. For each one give the consideration behind it, the case that makes you think its a requirement, not just a label. Then sort them: which are non-negotiable, and which are one camp's taste dressed up as a criterion. I suspect at least half of whatever list I'd write myself is the second kind, and I'd rather you say which half than agree with me.

Some raw material, use it or push back:

- Unplugging the aerator versus never plugging it in. Same tank, same dead fish an hour later, and as far as I can tell the same counterfactuals. Physically one is an event I injected and the other is me leaving the initial condition alone. Does a theory have to treat those differently, and if so, on grounds that aren't just my lifelong habit of writing equations with the forcing term on one side and the state on the other?
- In my business a reservoir is always either relaxing toward some natural state or being held away from it by continuous work — pumps, gates, releases. Is "maintaining a system off equilibrium" a third category the theory owes me, or does it have to reduce? The clinician who stops a treatment that's holding a patient away from the trajectory the disease already set up — which is that?
- Where in time does an omission live. Order signed in March, death in June. If the account needs to locate the allowing at a moment, that looks like trouble.
- Right now I'm allowing an enormous number of harms by sitting here typing this. Any account that makes allowing the cheaper category owes me something about how many allowings there are at once and what that does to the cheapness.

And flag anything on your list you think can't be jointly satisfied with something else on it. If some subset turns out to have no solution — the way a set of assumptions in a model just turns out to be inconsistent — tell me, because then the list is itself the result and that's the most useful thing you can hand me. A short scorecard at the end against two or three of the standard positions is fine, a couple of sentences each, but if you're not sure who held which view just describe the view rather than pinning it to a name. The requirements are what I actually want.
