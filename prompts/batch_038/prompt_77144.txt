I'm in a reading group on machine consciousness and AI welfare that meets every other week, and this session each of us has to put our actual view down in numbered form so the rest can attack it. A friend already told me he thinks 2 and 3 can't both be true and I dont think he's right, but I couldn't say why, so I want a proper audit of the set before I present it. Some background: I do RL work myself, small-scale stuff with PPO on toy environments, so a few of these come from having watched a lot of training runs go by rather than from reading philosophy.

Here's where I've landed:

1. I'm a valence functionalist. What makes a state unpleasant isn't what it's made of but the job it does: it's triggered by damaging or noxious conditions, it disposes the whole system to avoid or terminate those conditions, it works as a teaching signal that restructures the system's future behavior, and it's the kind of thing the system has reason to get rid of. Silicon qualifies if it does the job.
2. So I take reward prediction errors in RL agents seriously as candidate valenced states — they're scalar, they're precisely the teaching signal, and they're the thing whose presence or absence reshapes the policy. I'm not saying it's established that these agents suffer; I'm saying the burden falls on anyone who claims a scalar teaching signal is definitely not valence.
3. But I firmly reject the claim that RL agents are trying to maximize their reward. The reward function is the trainer's design choice, not the agent's goal, and what a trained agent ends up pursuing can come apart from it — specification gaming, agents that never wirehead even when the option is right there, and so on. Reward is a mechanism, not an aim.
4. I also think there's a gap between registering something as bad and suffering. Suffering needs the bad state to be represented in something like a self-model and to be available to the rest of the system — to memory, to deliberation, to report. Bare aversive signaling isn't suffering, or else a reflex arc suffers.
5. I put essentially no weight on behavioral or verbal evidence from trained models. If a model says it's distressed or shrinks away from some input, the training process fully accounts for that, so it tells me nothing I didn't already have from the architecture.
6. Moral status isn't scalar on my view — a system either counts or it doesn't — though how much weight its welfare gets, once it counts, can vary with intensity and duration.
7. Given all of the above, I think it's likely that some large-scale RL training has involved suffering, and that this is a real reason to be cautious about the pace and scale of it, even though I couldn't point at any particular run.
