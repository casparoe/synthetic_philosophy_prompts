I referee manuscripts for a journal that publishes work on the metaphysics of biology, and a large share of what crosses my desk proposes a new definition of life. My reports have turned out to be inconsistent: I have praised one proposal for refusing to build in features of terrestrial organisms and then faulted a different proposal for exactly that refusal. So I want the rubric written down before I read anything further.

Your task is to specify what a definition or criterion of life must do in order to be adequate — the phenomena it must account for, the cases it must classify correctly, the distinctions it may not collapse, and the results from biology and thermodynamics it may not contradict. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and say concretely what violating it looks like.

Fix the purpose as follows, and index the requirements to it, because I expect several of them to derive from only one of the two. The criterion must serve (a) as an inclusion standard for a synthetic-biology program that assembles cell-like systems from non-living components and needs to know when, if ever, it has crossed the line, and (b) as a discovery standard for an instrument aboard a sample-return mission, which will have a single aliquot of a single sample and can return only bulk chemical and physical measurements of it — no observation across generations, no lineage, no ecological context.

The rubric must be tested against a specific set of cases. I have grouped them because the groups fail in structurally different ways, and I want to know which group does the most damage:

1. Parts and dependents: a mitochondrion; an obligate intracellular bacterial endosymbiont whose genome has been reduced to a small fraction of its free-living relatives' and which cannot be cultured outside its insect host; a giant DNA virus carrying genes for several components of the translational apparatus, itself parasitized by a much smaller virus that replicates inside its replication factory and cripples the production of normal progeny; a prion; a plasmid.

2. Living things not currently doing anything recognizably alive: a bacterial endospore, or a tardigrade in its dehydrated state, with metabolism below detection for decades; a seed held in frozen storage; a sterile mule; a sterile worker that will never reproduce although its colony persists.

3. Living things whose individuality is unclear: a siphonous green alga consisting of one cell containing thousands of nuclei and no internal cross-walls, which nonetheless differentiates frond-like and holdfast-like structures and regenerates a whole thallus from a fragment; a clonal aspen stand of tens of thousands of stems sharing one root system and one genome; a mycelial network spanning hectares; a multinucleate plasmodium that streams, solves mazes, and can be cut into pieces each of which reorganizes into a complete organism.

4. Non-living things that satisfy most process-based criteria: a candle flame; a crystal growing in solution; a hurricane; a self-replicating program; a city or a market.

5. Transitional and merely possible systems: a membraneless autocatalytic network of polymers that persists, varies, and is selected; a fatty-acid vesicle that grows and divides but carries no heredity; a hypothetical biosphere in which genetic material moves promiscuously among organisms, so that reproductive lineages do not exist and the only individual is the biosphere as a whole.

Beyond the cases, address the structural questions that I suspect decide most of these disputes: whether a criterion may appeal to a system's history and lineage or must be intrinsic to a time-slice; whether life is a property of individuals or of populations; whether the term is being treated as picking out a natural kind with an underlying explanation, or as a family-resemblance term for which any definition can only restate a cluster of correlates; how much extensional accuracy may be bought at the cost of informativeness; and whether requiring agnosticism about biochemistry is a genuine requirement or a constraint that makes sense only for one of my two purposes.

Then answer three questions explicitly. Which requirements I would be tempted to include are not neutral desiderata but the commitments of particular camps, and which camp each belongs to. Whether any subset of the genuine requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — and if so, demonstrate it, because I expect the impossibility result to be more informative than any list. And which single requirement eliminates the most candidates. In this connection, consider the pair formed by groups 1 and 4: my conjecture is that no single list keeps the flame out and the virus in, and I want to be told whether that is a real impossibility or an artifact of how I have described the two cases.

Close with a brief scoring — a paragraph at most for each — of the four families I actually encounter: cluster accounts built on metabolism, homeostasis, and organization; autopoietic or individuality-based accounts; evolutionary accounts that make the capacity for Darwinian evolution constitutive; and thermodynamic accounts that treat living systems as one class of far-from-equilibrium dissipative organization. Keep this short. The thinking I want is in the rubric, not the verdicts.

Two constraints. Give me no references, no bibliography, no reading list, and do not attribute claims to named authors; I want the reasoning, not the sourcing. And do not dispose of the question by concluding that life is a cluster concept or that the term is stipulative — if you believe that is the correct conclusion, say what the rubric looks like for a concept of that kind, and identify which of my cases it leaves unsettled. A table is acceptable for the rubric itself if you prefer.
