I am responsible for a single fifty-minute session in a doctoral research-methods sequence for students in clinical epidemiology. The nominal topic is the interpretations of probability: what a probability is on a long-run frequency view, on a personalist or subjectivist view, and on a propensity view. The students are quantitatively fluent — they fit models, report confidence intervals and hazard ratios, and some of them use both Bayesian and frequentist machinery in their own work — but none of them has been asked what the numbers they produce actually claim about the world. I do not want you to give me the explanation. I want a plan for how I should deliver it, and I want it to be honest about trade-offs under the time limit.

Please address the following.

**Sequence.** Set out the order in which the components must be introduced, and justify the ordering by what depends on what. In particular, tell me whether single-case probability — a probability attached to one unrepeatable event involving one particular individual — should be my entry point or my destination. I suspect it has to come early, because otherwise the frequency view is never under any pressure, but I also suspect that raising it early will make the propensity view look like an obvious rescue rather than a substantive thesis with its own problems. Resolve that tension explicitly.

**The carrying case.** I intend to build the whole session around a risk-score output of a kind the students will recognise: the ten-year cardiovascular risk assigned to one specific patient, described by age, sex, blood pressure, lipids, smoking status. Tell me whether that single case can carry all three interpretations without straining, or whether it quietly favours one of them and distorts the other two. Say what the case must have and what it must lack to do the job — must the event be genuinely non-repeatable, must it concern a particular rather than a type, must a decision be attached to it — and if my case fails on any of those counts, propose the replacement.

**The reference-class problem.** I want to end on the observation that the ten-year figure is not settled until one says which class this patient belongs to, and that the choice of class is where the frequency view's claim to objectivity is most exposed. Judge whether that is a legitimate terminus for fifty minutes or whether it will swamp everything before it, and tell me where the seeds of it have to be planted earlier if it is to land at all.

**Misconceptions.** Map the ones they will bring and the ones the session itself will generate. I anticipate: that frequentism is the objective, data-driven option and subjectivism is an admission that the numbers are made up; that the propensity view is either frequency talk with decorative vocabulary or else covert mysticism; that a ninety-five per cent confidence interval states a ninety-five per cent probability about the parameter, which the convenor has specifically asked me to defuse; and that because Bayesian and frequentist procedures diverge, one of the two camps must be mistaken about the world rather than about what to report. For each, say the precise point in the sequence at which I should head it off, and where that point falls relative to the introduction of the view it threatens.

**Constraint.** I have banned coins and dice from the session, on the grounds that they import exactly the wrong picture — endlessly repeatable, symmetric, physically identical trials. Tell me what that ban costs me and whether any of it is irreplaceable.

**Check for understanding.** Give me one closing question, of a kind that can be answered in two sentences, that would distinguish a student who has grasped that the interpretations disagree about what probability *is* from one who merely thinks there are two technical toolkits that sometimes disagree. State what a good answer sounds like and what the characteristic bad answer sounds like, since I will be reading thirty of them.

**Omissions.** Tell me what to cut. I am tempted by Dutch-book arguments, by the representation theorems, by Bertrand's paradox, and by the question of how single-case chances constrain credence; I assume not all of these can survive. Say which go, and give me the sentence I should say aloud about what I am leaving out and why, so that the omissions read as deliberate rather than as gaps.
