I spent the first two weeks of this month at a summer school in legal and social philosophy, and on the last night three of us walked back from dinner together. At an empty intersection at close to one in the morning, Ines — who is from that city and had been our host for the evening — stopped at a red pedestrian signal and waited for it to change; Marcus, who is there for a semester from a country where crossing against the light is both universal and entirely unenforced, walked straight across and waited for her on the far corner. Nothing was said at the time. The two of them had already had a shorter version of this argument that morning in a queue for coffee, about whether it is permissible to hold a place for a colleague who has not yet arrived, and at lunch the next day the whole thing reopened and ran for three tables' worth of time, getting considerably further from the intersection as it went. I have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can, because I want to use it as the worked example that opens a chapter I am writing on the normative force of conventional rules, and at present I cannot tell which of them is winning. I would like you to referee.

The exchange, as near as I can render it:

(1) Ines opened by saying that Marcus's crossing was a small wrong — not because anything came of it, but because a signal of that kind is maintained only by everyone's expectation that everyone else will comply; each visible instance of non-compliance loosens that expectation, and his had been visible to her and to whoever else was on the street.

(2) Marcus replied that this is a drop-in-the-ocean argument, and that it either proves nothing or proves far too much: no individual crossing makes any detectable difference to anyone's expectations, so if wrongness is a matter of aggregate effects there is no wrongness here at all. He added that on that night there was no driver within sight and nobody delayed by him, so his crossing neither endangered anyone nor took advantage of anyone.

(3) Ines said the free-riding charge does not depend on that particular crossing. Marcus benefits every day from drivers' standing assumption that pedestrians will stop: they accelerate through green lights, they brake late, they do not scan a dark crosswalk, because they can rely on compliance. He takes that benefit and declines the correlative cost in precisely those instances where the cost to him is nothing, which she said is the free rider's characteristic move rather than an escape from it.

(4) Marcus then said that if a fairness principle of that shape condemns a crossing that injures nobody, it condemns defection from any convention that generally works, whatever its content — including ones he thinks he is required to undermine. He does not tip in this country, ever, deliberately and as standing policy, on the ground that tipping converts an employer's payroll obligation into a levy on customers and makes a server's income contingent on customers' moods and prejudices. If Ines's principle forbids the crossing at one in the morning, it forbids that too; and he regards the second defection not as permissible but as obligatory.

(5) Ines said he is trading on an asymmetry he has not stated. A pedestrian signal, she argued, is a coordination convention: it distributes nothing, it only resolves a collision problem, and its content — which colour means stop — is morally arbitrary. Tipping is a distributive convention, and its content is exactly what he objects to. There can be reason to work against a distributive convention because of how it distributes; there is no analogous reason to work against a coordination convention, since there is nothing in it to correct. She added that his refusal to tip functions as protest only if it is public and joined by others, and that a lone customer who simply keeps the money is indistinguishable, from the server's side, from a cheapskate.

(6) Marcus denied that the distinction does the work she needs. Pedestrian infrastructure is distributive too: signal timing encodes a standing priority of cars over people on foot, and enforcement of jaywalking rules falls hardest on those who can least afford either the wait or the fine. Once one looks at any convention closely enough, he said, the premise of arbitrary content disappears, and Ines's principle turns out to bind only those conventions one has not inspected. He said further that as a visitor he is in any case outside the scheme whose benefits she is totting up: he did not join it, he cannot opt out of walking its streets while he is there, and four months of incidental benefit is not membership.

(7) Ines conceded the point about timing and enforcement, but said it cuts against him. The people who pay for the erosion he causes are not the planners who set the signal; they are the pedestrians whom drivers now expect slightly less of — the most exposed road users, who have no way of receiving his crossing as a protest addressed to anyone. His refusal to tip, by contrast, is at least addressed to parties capable of registering it — the restaurant, the other customers, the server — even if it is ineffective. Her question, which he did not answer, was what his criterion is for a defection's being addressed to anybody at all, and whether the crossing at one in the morning meets it.

What I want is a rating for each of the seven moves on a five-point scale — clearly mistaken, weaker than the reply it drew, even, stronger than the reply it drew, clearly right — with a sentence or two of justification for each rating, followed by a verdict on which of them is more right overall and on whether this exchange has a determinate winner at all. Then tell me what both of them are missing; my own suspicion is that neither ever asks what the convention is doing for someone who is not a member of it and is leaving in four months, but I would rather you found the gaps than confirmed mine. There is also a distinction I have been carrying around that Ines seems to be reaching for in (7): that a defection is permissible only where it can be received as a claim by the party it is addressed to, and impermissible where it is audienceless in that sense. Say whether that principle survives contact with the argument as it actually went, or whether it is only a redescription of Ines's side. Please work from the moves as I have rendered them rather than from a survey of positions — I do not want a map of the literature, and I do not want to be told which philosophers hold which view of conventions. If a move is ambiguous in a way that would change its rating, say which reading you adopted and what the other reading would have yielded. Finally, since I intend to build a chapter on your verdict: state how confident you are in each rating, and single out the one point in the whole adjudication that you are least sure of — the place where a better philosopher than you could defensibly land on the other side.
