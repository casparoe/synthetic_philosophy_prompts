I serve as chair of a seven-person hospital research ethics board, and I need advice on a governance problem that has become personal. Our charter says a study may be approved only if two conditions are both met: the science is sound enough to justify the risks to participants, and the consent process is adequate. At last month's meeting we voted on each condition in turn; both passed 5–2. We then voted on approval, and it failed 4–3 — three members who had just voted that the science was sound and the consent adequate voted against approving. The investigators appealed, arguing that the board contradicted itself and that approval should have followed automatically. The appeal panel remanded the matter to me and left the procedure for the new vote entirely to my discretion.

So I can either take separate votes on the two conditions and treat approval as following automatically, or take a single vote on approval and let each member weigh the conditions privately. I know this membership well enough to predict that the first route approves the study and the second rejects it. That unsettles me in two ways. It looks as though I am simply choosing whichever outcome I prefer, and the losing side will say exactly that. Yet it also seems wrong to call the choice arbitrary: we are required to publish reasons for our decisions, and I doubt both routes yield a set of reasons anyone can sign onto coherently.

One wrinkle: the three yes-yes-no members insist they were not inconsistent. They say the charter's two conditions do not exhaust what they think matters, and that they weighed a third consideration privately.

Please think this through carefully before writing, in the role of a philosopher who works on social choice and collective decision-making and has actually advised deliberative bodies. Then answer: am I merely picking an outcome, or is one procedure genuinely more defensible, and on what grounds? What should I make of the dissenters' claim — does it dissolve the appearance of contradiction or create a worse problem? And would it help to put the choice of procedure itself to a vote, or does that only relocate the trouble? End with a recommendation I can act on at next week's meeting.

Open with your bottom line in two or three sentences, and keep the entire response under 500 words.
