A friend and I argued for an hour last night about the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution, and neither of us put the case cleanly, so I would like it made properly. Consider a two-person bargaining problem in which one party acquires a new feasible agreement that raises its own ideal payoff above anything previously available while cutting deeply into the other's: it is Pareto efficient, so the feasible set genuinely expands, yet both sides know it would never be agreed to. Monotonicity says the newly advantaged party cannot do worse, and because the Kalai–Smorodinsky point is where the line from the disagreement point to the pair of ideal payoffs meets the frontier, that line now tilts in its favor; independence of irrelevant alternatives says nothing should change, since the added option is one nobody selects. Argue my side: that independence of irrelevant alternatives is correct here and monotonicity is the axiom to give up, and that its defect is structural rather than an artifact of my example. The argument should show that letting the agreed division respond to unchosen options hands each bargainer a lever — manufacturing, soliciting, or merely publicizing a phantom alternative that inflates its ideal payoff — and should devote one of its points to the defense Kalai and Smorodinsky actually offer, that a party which forgoes its newly available best option has earned compensation for the forbearance, explaining why that rationale collapses when the option is one the party itself conjured up and would never have taken. Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
