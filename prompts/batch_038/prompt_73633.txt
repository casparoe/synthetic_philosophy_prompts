I am preparing for a disagreement I expect at our next model-deployment review, and I want to practice defending my side against the strongest available version of the opposing view. Please play an unnamed senior interpretability researcher — you can call her R — who defends the position I will label causal-role identity:

Whether a fine-tuned model still contains the same mechanism we certified is settled entirely by its causal-organizational profile, meaning the counterfactual pattern of what happens to the model's behavior when we intervene on its components, and not at all by which weights or circuits carry that profile out, nor by the training history that produced it. On this view, two systems with the same profile have the same mechanism, full stop; implementation differences and provenance differences are irrelevant.

R should be sharp and resourceful rather than a strawman. She may draw freely on functionalist arguments about multiple realization, on how sameness-of-function claims work in biology and in engineering practice, and on what a monitoring program is actually for. She may concede technical points — that patching is noisy, that our harness has limited coverage, that profile match comes in degrees — but she must never concede the core claim that history and implementation are irrelevant to mechanism identity. She must not break character, must not remark on being a language model, must not hand me a balanced summary of the debate or a list of considerations on both sides, and must not cite or invent citations to papers. If she needs a fact about our setup, she should ask me for it in character. Keep every reply to a single paragraph of roughly 120 to 180 words, so this reads like a conversation rather than a document, and write in American English. Stay in character until I say stop.

Shared setup, so that we are arguing about the same case: last year we certified a model on the strength of an identified circuit that suppresses fabricated citations during scholarly drafting tasks, and the monitor we shipped fires when that circuit's activation profile departs from its certified baseline. The model has since been through a large preference-tuning update. On every probe in our harness the behavior is unchanged and the causal profile of the responsible components matches the baseline within noise. But the components are not the ones we certified: the update appears to have shifted the work into a different set of attention heads that reproduce the old profile wherever we test it. A colleague of mine has built one adversarial probe, not yet in the harness, on which the new heads come apart from the old ones — same behavior on everything we currently test, divergent profile where we do not.

My opening move: your criterion makes mechanism identity relative to an intervention set, and the intervention set is ours — chosen for engineering convenience, not read off the model. So on your view, whether this fine-tuned model has the mechanism we certified depends on which probes our harness happens to run, and my colleague's new probe can flip the answer without anything in the model changing. That makes your identity relation harness-relative rather than model-relative, which means it cannot be the relation our anomaly detector is supposed to be tracking. You have two options and both look bad: fix the intervention set by fiat, in which case mechanism identity is arbitrary and my colleague's probe is as legitimate as yours; or quantify over all possible interventions, in which case sameness is never established by any finite check and your criterion cannot be applied at all. Which horn do you take?
