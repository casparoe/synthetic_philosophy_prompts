I am a novelist by trade, and for three years I have held a lay seat on the Public Art Commission of a mid-sized American city — an appointment that has proved far more philosophically demanding than I expected. At our meeting on the fourteenth of next month we must settle the fate of an untitled sculpture in unfired clay and burlap, roughly life-size, which the artist modeled in 1974 and which has stood in the atrium of our central library since 1998, when it was sealed with a consolidant as a condition of installation. The artist died in 2011. Our conservator's report is unambiguous: the piece was never fired, its internal armature is corroding, and the clay has lost cohesion to the point that it will slump into rubble within three to six years under any environment we can provide. It cannot be saved in the ordinary sense of saved. Among four courses of action the commission must choose, and I would like you to draft the decision memorandum that will go to the seven of us in advance of the vote.

The options, briefly. First, do nothing but retard the decay — a climate-controlled case, restricted handling, and wall text that treats the deterioration as continuous with the work rather than as its loss. Second, execute a replacement that the artist's estate maintains she authorized: the estate holds a signed certificate, dated 1996, stipulating that a successor fabricated under the estate's supervision by whatever methods are then available "is the work and not a reproduction of it," and adding the strange condition that at least one-tenth of the original's own material, ground and kneaded into the new clay, must be incorporated in the successor; the certificate does not say whether that tenth must be evenly dispersed or may be concentrated in one place, such as the head or the base. Third, consolidate the original by degrees: our conservator proposes an eighteen-month program of chemical stabilization and armature replacement in which most of the 1974 matter is progressively substituted, a little at a time, the piece never leaving its plinth and never ceasing to be on view. Fourth, scan and print a facsimile for the atrium and retire the original to storage, to decay out of public sight. Complicating everything: the deed of gift from the artist's surviving partner commits the city to preserve the sculpture itself and not a reproduction of it; the accession record and the insurance schedule both describe the insured object as the original fabric, and the payout clause turns on loss of that object; our counsel believes statutory rights of attribution and integrity may survive the artist's death in this jurisdiction, though she is still checking the term and the waiver history, and I would rather the memorandum flag that gap than paper over it; and two commissioners who agree about nearly everything else now hold that the second and third options differ in kind, while a third insists the difference between them is only a question of how quickly the substitutions are made.

What I want is a memorandum the commission could act on: no more than a thousand words, addressed to colleagues, dated, with a clear recommendation among the four options and a schedule for carrying it out. It should state explicitly what the recommendation assumes — whether the sculpture is the lump of clay it is made of or something that merely occupies that clay, how much of the 1974 matter can be replaced before there is no longer the same work in that atrium, and who among artist, estate, donor, insurer, and commission is entitled to fix what the phrase the work refers to. Treat the strongest case against your own recommendation at sufficient length that the commissioners who hold it will recognize their view stated better than they have stated it, and tell me in plain terms whether the estate's one-tenth clause is doing any real philosophical work or only a ceremonial one, including whether dispersal versus concentration makes a difference to that judgment. If your recommendation rests on a premise you think quite possibly false, say so openly rather than burying it in a subordinate clause. Send me no references, reading lists, or citations; I want the reasoning exposed, not the scholarship behind it, and I know the metaphysics is unsettled. I should confess a second motive: the third act of the novel I am drafting turns on precisely such a vote, and writing the real memorandum first is my method of discovering what my characters will say when the roll is called. The document itself, however, must read as a memorandum — plain, decisive, usable on the fourteenth — and not as a seminar paper.
