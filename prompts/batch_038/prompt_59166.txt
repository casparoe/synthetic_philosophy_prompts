Schelling built his account of strategy out of ideas that seem to presuppose actual causal contact between adversaries: credibility, commitment, the difference between a threat and a promise, the advantage of appearing irrational. Now consider a notion that only circulated after his major work was done, in rationalist and effective-altruism writing: acausal trade, in which two agents who can never communicate with, observe, or influence one another nonetheless cooperate, because each models the other's decision procedure and does what the other wants on the expectation that the other is doing the same.

How would Schelling have received this idea? Sort his conceptual toolkit into those pieces that depend on one party being able to affect the other and those that depend only on one party being able to predict the other, and say for each group whether it survives the removal of causal contact or collapses with it. His analysis of coordination without communication — strangers converging on the same salient option in the absence of any signaling — looks like the closest precedent in his own work, so explain whether that precedent licenses the acausal-trade move or is being asked to do something it cannot do. End with a definite verdict rather than a hedged one: would he have treated acausal trade as the limiting case of his own theory, or as a category mistake that borrows the vocabulary of bargaining while abandoning every mechanism that makes bargaining work?
