So I've been working the gate desk at a mid-size airport for about four years and I keep getting dragged into this argument with my roommate, who's doing a philosophy degree, about when it's ok to just say something flat out instead of hedging. He keeps telling me there's some clean rule, and every time I try to write down what I actually think I end up with a mess. Here are my verdicts, going by gut:

Passenger asks if flight 214 is on time. Board says on time, but I know the inbound plane is still sitting in Denver. I say "it's on time." Not fine, obviously. Same passenger, same question, but I've just refreshed the screen ten seconds ago and there's no reason to doubt it — I say "it's on time." Fine. And I say it's fine even though I know the boards are wrong maybe one time in seven and I've been wrong before on exactly this. But then my friend texts me the same question an hour after my shift, and I tell her "it's on time" off memory of that same board — not fine. My roommate says those last two can't both be right, because the epistemic situation is basically identical, and I don't have an answer, I just know they feel different.

My uncle does the weekend weather on a community radio station. He says "rain tomorrow" out loud when he's privately at maybe 60 percent. I think that's fine, it's the job, people listening know the genre. But the same man, at my cousin's wedding, asked whether it'll rain for the outdoor thing Saturday, says "it'll rain Saturday" at the same 60 percent — and I think that's not fine. Same guy, same confidence, different room. At the pharmacy counter next door there's a tech who tells patients the drug does what the label says while privately thinking the studies were garbage: I say fine. That same tech telling a patient "you'll feel noticeably better by Friday," which she does believe but has no way of knowing: not fine. Telling my sister "you'll get the callback" when I'm pretty sure she won't, because she needs it: not fine, even though it would help her. Reading allergens off a package to a worried parent on the phone, with no ability to double-check anything: fine. Saying "the airline says it's on time" when I actually doubt it: fine, and I think that's an easy one.

My own attempt at a rule is the betting one: you may say it flat out only if you'd put a month's pay on it in front of the person you're telling. But that seems to wreck the radio case and the allergen case and maybe even the board case, and I don't want to give those up. The distinction I keep reaching for is between *posting* — putting out the line that belongs to the desk, the label, the station — and *vouching*, where it's me on the hook. Posting seems permissive, vouching seems strict, and I think that's what makes the board-at-the-desk vs board-over-text pair feel different, but I can't say exactly where posting stops and vouching starts, and I worry I'm just relabeling the thing.

So: is there a principle, or a small number of candidate principles, that actually gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely — clause by clause, and tell me which of my cases is doing the work for each clause, because I want to see whether some clause is only there to rescue one weird verdict. Then tell me what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on: the incident review, where I'm like 90 percent sure a specific crew's late arrival caused the delay because I heard it on the radio, but I can't corroborate it and my name goes on the report. And what it says about a case I haven't thought through at all — the automated announcement system saying "flight 214 will depart on time" when nobody has checked, and the airline's chatbot saying the same thing to a passenger. Who's asserting there, if anybody, and does posting/vouching even apply? Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle can't keep. If the cheapest coherent option turns out to be just "say it only if you know it" and eating the loss on the radio forecast and the board case, tell me that straight and tell me what that costs me at work, rather than building me a baroque thing with six clauses that saves everything.
