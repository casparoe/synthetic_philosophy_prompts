Between 1991 and 2004, the courts of reunified Germany tried East German border guards — and, above them, the defense officials and Politburo members who had commanded the border regime — for the killings at the Berlin Wall. Please treat the following as given rather than reconstructing it: the first of these trials concerned four soldiers involved in the killing of Chris Gueffroy in February 1989, the last person shot dead while trying to escape over the Wall; the defendants argued that East German law authorized the use of firearms against border-breakers and that they had acted under orders; the Federal Court of Justice held that the killings were punishable under East German law itself, treating the Border Act's justification for the use of firearms as valid in principle but only on an interpretation friendly to human rights, which imported a proportionality requirement from the GDR's own constitution and its international human rights commitments; in 1994 the same court added that deliberately killing unarmed fugitives was unlawful even by East German standards, because it flagrantly and intolerably violated elementary principles of justice, and that if the Border Act could not be read in that friendly way it could all the less justify the shootings; the Federal Constitutional Court and later the European Court of Human Rights found no breach of the prohibition on retroactive punishment; about half of the defendants were acquitted, convicted shooters typically received suspended sentences of a year or two, and the political and military leadership received several years' imprisonment as the principals behind the shooters; and much of the German legal academy criticized the whole enterprise precisely on retroactivity grounds.

I want a careful reading of this episode as a test of the Hart–Fuller debate — Hart's separation thesis and rule of recognition against Fuller's internal morality of law, with Radbruch's formula (extreme injustice is not law) as the doctrinal vehicle the courts actually reached for. Specifically:

1. Separate the two strands in the courts' reasoning and say which is positivist. The interpretive strand looks source-based: identify East German law by its pedigree — statute, constitution, ratified treaties — and then construe it. But the selection of a human-rights-friendly reading from among available readings may be doing moral work that pedigree cannot supply. Is that strand genuinely available to a positivist, or is it Radbruch's fallback wearing an interpretive costume? And what does the fallback, invoked only in case the interpretation fails, concede?

2. Bring in Fuller's eighth desideratum, congruence between official action and declared rule. The GDR kept a proportionality requirement on the books while instructing, rewarding, and commemorating the opposite. Does that failure of congruence dispose of the case without any appeal to the morality of the content — that is, does the internal morality of law, as a set of procedural criteria, do the work here? Then press the point against Hart's objection that Fuller's criteria are really conditions of efficacy, perfectly compatible with great iniquity: does the border case confirm that objection or defeat it?

3. Consider the rule of recognition across two rival systems. West German courts declared what the law of a defunct competitor had been. If law exists in virtue of official practice and acceptance, then the practice of East German officials — the orders, the medals, the disciplinary record — is the best evidence of what East German law was, and Hart's own theory seems to point away from the courts' conclusion. Does he have the resources to resist this, or is his theory simply silent here? Does Fuller's framing do better?

4. Address the conscript's epistemic position. What do Fuller's requirements of promulgation, clarity, and possibility of compliance, and Hart's insistence that the question whether something is law is separate from the question whether it ought to be obeyed, each imply about convicting a teenage guard for failing to apply a reading of his own system's law that its officials had taken systematic steps to keep him from reaching? Note that no court in these trials took Hart's advice and announced that the rule was law but too iniquitous to apply. What does that choice cost each side?

5. Say plainly where the debate has nothing to offer: the sentencing gradient between shooters and commanders, the selectivity of the prosecutions relative to the total number of deaths at the Wall, and the fact that the accusing state was the successor and rival of the accused one, which critics characterized as victor's justice. If these belong to a different jurisprudential question, name that question rather than stretching Hart or Fuller to cover it.

The analysis should run in both directions — not only what the two theories say about the judgments, but what the judgments do to the theories: which view, if either, emerges better supported, and whether the episode functions as a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case neither position anticipated. Where a theory is silent, say so instead of making it speak. Please write in American English, in a formal register, organized under clear headings. You need not cite or recommend sources; if any part of your argument depends on a feature of the case beyond the facts supplied above, flag that dependence explicitly rather than filling it in.
