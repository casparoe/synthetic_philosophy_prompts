My housemate is a condensed-matter PhD who's been reading philosophy of mind on the side, and last night he cornered me with an argument meant to show that the whole Russellian / panpsychist line refutes itself -- not by being false, but by making continued existence morally impossible. I find the first four steps basically irresistable and the last step absurd, and I can't locate the break. Here's his numbering, cleaned up a bit:

(1) Fundamental physics tells us what particles and fields *do*, never what they *are*: mass, charge and spin are exhausted by their place in the equations, so the description is structural all the way down.
(2) A description consisting of nothing but dispositions can't be complete, because dispositions have to be grounded in something non-dispositional, or every term just points at another term and nothing ever lands.
(3) The only non-dispositional properties anyone is acquainted with are experiential or proto-experiential ones -- we know the intrinsic nature of some matter from the inside, in our own case.
(4) So the intrinsic nature of physical stuff is experiential or proto-experiential. Call it Russellian monism.
(5) Therefore wherever there's physical stuff there's some experiential character: the granite countertop, the coffee in it, the silicon in a hard drive.
(6) And that character is valenced -- for that stuff there's a way it's going, better or worse, not just a neutral hum.
(7) Anything for which things can go better or worse has interests, and anything with interests is an object of moral concern.
(8) Now bring in information. Every logically irreversible operation deletes a distinction in the physical state of matter, and Landauer's principle says erasing a bit has to dump at least a minimum amount of heat into the surroundings. Deleting a distinction in the state of matter is deleting part of the intrinsic character of that matter -- i.e. destroying part of what it's like to be it.
(9) So computing, digesting, boiling a kettle, walking across a lawn, and a rock weathering on a hillside are all subject-destruction at a scale nobody can count.
(10) A view that turns ordinary existing into an uncountable catastrophe, with no available way of opting out, isn't an ethic at all but a reductio. Hence (1)-(4) are false and physics is complete without intrinsic natures.

My instinct is that something slides around (5)-(6) -- proto-phenomenal isn't obviously phenomenal, and having *some* character isn't obviously having a welfare -- and that (8) does something shady with the phrase "distinction in the state of matter," since a bit is a distinction relative to a description, not obviously a chunk of subjecthood. But I want each candidate priced, not just flagged. What breaks if I deny (5): does the combination problem eat the view anyway, and does denying it collapse panpsychism into something indistinguishable from physics-plus-hedging? What breaks if I deny (6): do I end up with a panprotopsychism that has zero ethical consequences, and is that a real cost or is it a quiet surrender of the entire motivation for accepting (3) in the first place? What breaks if I deny (8), given that (8) is the only step that actually uses physics rather than intuition? And is (10) even the right conclusion -- he's using a moral upshot to refute a metaphysics, which feels like inference running backwards, plus (9) is only catastrophic if the subjects destroyed are the kind of thing whose destruction is a harm to them, which is exactly what's in question. Tell me which step you'd pull, why it looked innocent going in, whether a version survives with the slide removed, and if it does how bad its conclusion really is. I'd rather keep (1)-(4) and swallow a weird metaphysics than lose them, but I want to know what I'm signing up for.
