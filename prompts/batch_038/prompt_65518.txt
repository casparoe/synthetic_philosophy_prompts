ok so context: i'm the volunteer coordinator for a large online forum for parents of kids with a rare-ish developmental condition — a few thousand active families, about 40 volunteer mods spread across timezones, none of us with any medical or philosophy training. Over the last year a chunk of the community has drifted into a hardline anti-clinic / pro-suppliment-protocol thing, and the mod team keeps arguing about whether to answer with more information, just delete stuff, or do nothing. Nobody can agree, mostly because we can't even agree on what were looking at.

I've been reading around and the distinction that seems most useful to us is the one C. Thi Nguyen draws between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers — bubbles are places where other voices simply aren't heard, chambers are places where members have been actively taught to distrust every outside source. Different structure, different fix, and exposure is supposedly the cure for the first and roughly useless (maybe even fuel) for the second.

Your role: you're a social epistemologist who has also spent real years on a trust and safety team, so you know what a volunteer moderator can actually do on a Tuesday night with a phone in one hand. Think carefully about this. Before you write the procedure, spend a few lines brainstorming the ways a diagnostic like this typically goes wrong in a community like ours — false positives, mods reaching for it as a weapon to label people they just don't like, the whole community getting tarred because one subforum is bad, etc.

Then give me a procedure the mod team can actually run: 20 minutes max per flagged thread or subforum, done by one person, no outside expertise, no special tools. I want:

- the questions to ask, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each
- hard rule: no step may ask a mod to judge whether a medical claim is true. we're not qualified and it's not our job. the diagnosis has to run on the structure of trust and information flow, not on the content of beliefs. if that constraint makes some cases undiagnosable, say so explicitly and route them somewhere
- where the procedure stops with "no action," and where it hands the decision to a human (me, or our clinical advisory panel) — and what the handoff note should contain so the human isn't starting from zero
- which steps are honestly judgment calls dressed up as procedure, flagged as such rather than hidden

One hunch of mine I want you to pressure-test: I think you can tell distrust that's earned apart from discredit that's been manufactured. Earned distrust is specific (this clinic dismissed my kid twice), revisable when the clinic changes, and doesn't spread to everything else. Manufactured discredit is global, attaches to any source nobody inside has pre-approved, and gets treated as a loyalty test. Turn that into three or four concrete things a mod can look for in a thread — or tell me straight that the hunch is wrong or unusable in practice, i'd rather know than have it flattered.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. our "new here" subforum, where questions get answered almost entirely out of an internal wiki and outside links mostly just get ignored — no hostility, no pile-ons, they just sink
2. a thread where a well-known protocol promoter's supporters are working from a running list of which doctors and journalists are shills, and newcomers get warned off those sources by name before anyone has read them
3. our own pinned resources megathread and how the mod team handles supplement talk — i.e. run the procedure on us. I fully expect this one to come back with an answer I won't like; if it does, give it to me straight instead of softening it.

Finally, a short section on what the procedure can't do, and what would let me notice it's failing — how I'd know a month from now that we're using it wrong rather than that the community is getting worse.

No bibliography or reading list please — I'm not going to go look things up and you'd be guessing anyway. Just make the reasoning visible inside the procedure itself.
