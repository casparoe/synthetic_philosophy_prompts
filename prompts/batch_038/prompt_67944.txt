So I run a very small podcast — me, a borrowed mic, maybe three hundred listeners — and next month I've somehow landed a guest: a philosopher who's just put out a book defending tranquilism. In case the term isn't familiar, she uses it for the view that only the absence of craving and suffering has positive value, so a life can be made worse by what it contains but never better by what it contains. Her particular version has three moving parts, and I want the questions to actually engage them instead of orbiting them:

1. She's a relief theorist about pleasure. Joy, flow, sexual pleasure, the delight of finally getting a proof — all of it, on her account, is really the felt subsiding of some prior aversive state, an itch being scratched. There is no pleasure that isn't a relief, and so no positive hedonic quantity to add up.
2. She denies this is an argument for extinction, on the grounds that value needs a valuer: a world without sentient beings isn't a world where craving is absent, it's a world where the question never comes up. Absence of craving only matters when it's somebody's.
3. She isn't an aggregative utilitarian about the bad side either — she runs a threshold or lexical view where intense suffering swamps everything and mild unfulfilled wanting barely registers, which she thinks keeps her from having to say that a billion contented people is worse than a million.

The trouble is that she's extremely good at absorbing objections into (1). Any case you bring her of a pleasure that doesn't look like relief — a song you weren't missing, a taste you weren't hungry for, a view that catches you mid-stroll — she'll redescribe as the relief of some craving you hadnt noticed you had, and honestly that move usually wins the room. So what I want is a sequence of interview questions that makes that absorption progressively harder to pull off: start somewhere she'll feel comfortable, then tighten the screw question by question, and end on what observation, or what kind of first-person report, would count as evidence against tranquilism rather than as more fuel for it. Along the way I definitely want one question aimed at the seam between (1) and (2) — if absent craving only matters when someone experiences the absence, isn't the valuable thing an experience, i.e. something positive? — one at the testimony of people who say their lives are worth living not because they've stopped wanting things but becuase of what they want; and one on whether the view is self-undermining, since pressing it in an argument seems to require caring about being right, which is craving.

For each question, write out the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give: predict her reply in a sentence or two, then give the second question that meets that specific reply. Nine to eleven main questions is about right. No softballs, nothing of the "what drew you to this?" variety, and American English throughout, please. One last thing: I won't be able to look anything up while recording, so if a question leans on a detail of her book that I haven't described above, flag it and hand me a fallback version that works off my description alone.
