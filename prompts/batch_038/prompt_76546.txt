ok so I'm in a seminar on extended cognition and this week we're doing a "precursors" session, and I got assigned Peirce's "Some Consequences of Four Incapacities" (Journal of Speculative Philosophy, 1868) — the one where he follows up on the earlier essay "Questions concerning certain Faculties claimed for Man." I can't tell whether the bit everybody keeps pointing at actually has anything to do with the extended mind thesis, or whether people are just reading Clark and Chalmers backwards into him. Here's the passage I'm stuck on. Heads up: the transcription I'm reading online has some obvious typos, I'm copying it as-is:

> That criticism of certain faculties resulted in four denials. which for convenience may here be repeated:
> 1. We have no power of Introspection. but all knowledge of the internal world is derived by hypothetical reasoning from our knowledge of external facts.
> 2. We have no power of Intuition, but every cognition is determined logically by previous cognitions.
> 3. We have no power of thinking without signs.
> 4. We have no conception of the absolutely incognizable.

Denial 3 is the one that gets quoted at me, but I think 1 and 2 are doing the actual work and I can't reconstruct how. Can you walk me through this? Specifically:

1. What exactly does Peirce mean by "thinking without signs" here — is "sign" doing the job that a contemporary philosopher of mind means by an external representational vehicle (a notebook, a map, Otto's diary), or is it something weirder, like the claim that there's no non-representational inner medium at all, so even sitting quietly thinking is already a sign-process? If it's the second thing, does that mean the inner/outer boundary the whole extended-mind debate runs on gets dissolved instead of crossed, and if so what's left of the thesis?

2. How do denials 1 and 2 get him to 3? I keep thinking the move is: if nothing is immediately given to consciousness (no intuition) and I can't inspect my own mind directly (no introspection, only hypotheses posited to explain outward facts), then there's no privileged private stuff for thought to be made of, so thought has to be in signs. Is that the argument, and is it valid or is there a gap?

3. Here's the part that confuses me most. Denial 1 says knowledge of the internal world is inferred from external facts. That sounds like it goes the *opposite* direction from extended mind — not the mind spilling out into the world, but the mind getting absorbed into publicly checkable behavior and signs. So which is it: is Peirce extending cognition, or is he eliminating the inner theater that would need extending? And does denial 1 leave him exposed to the coupling-constitution complaint — the one where critics say Clark and Chalmers confuse a process being *causally coupled* to cognition with its *being part of* cognition? Because if Peirce is happy to say the inner is just a hypothesis about the outer, I don't see how that objection could even get a grip on him.

4. Later in the paper he pushes this much further, to something like the conclusion that the man himself is a sign and that a person's language is roughly the sum total of the person. Take that at face value for a sec: is that a claim about where cognition is located, or is it a claim about what persons are, i.e. metaphysics of self rather than philosophy of mind? Can you tell me what would have to be true for those two readings to come apart, and which one makes Peirce an actual ancestor of the extended mind thesis rather than just a guy who said suggestive-sounding things about signs?

No need to give me references or a reading list, I just want the interpretation and the reasoning behind it. If you think the standard "Peirce anticipated extended mind" line is wrong, say so and tell me what it actually is.
