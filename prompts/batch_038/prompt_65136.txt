I have run an amateur film society for six years and, quite separately, been reading in aesthetics; over the past year our attempts to discuss the paradox of tragedy have gone around in circles, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another tour of the positions. Let me first set out the material I keep bringing to these conversations, which none of the accounts I have read seems able to accommodate cleanly.

Our members consistently rank highest, and most want to see again, the films that reduce them to tears on the third or fourth viewing as much as on the first; several say the response deepens once surprise has been exhausted, which sits badly with any account that makes curiosity, uncertainty, or the pleasure of finding out what happens do the explanatory work. A clear majority also report seeking out devastating films more often when they are already low rather than less often, which is the wrong way round for mood-repair and compensation stories. And when pressed about where the value actually lies, many of them point not at the film but at themselves, saying things to the effect that it is good to know they can still be moved, or that the viewing reminded them what they care about. On one reading that makes the pleasure a species of self-regard, and I find it striking that the literature treats this either as the solution or as beside the point, rather than as a live aesthetic problem in its own right.

Meanwhile the theories on offer have looked stable to me for decades: the ones that locate the explanation in the audience's awareness that they are safe, Walton's suggestion that what is felt are quasi-emotions rather than emotions proper, the much older Humean idea that pain is converted into pleasure by the force of the representation's eloquence, the accounts that put the weight on a second-order response to one's own first-order feeling, and the physiological conjecture, which I think originates with Huron in connection with sad music, that a grief-like response triggers a compensatory release of prolactin and so leaves a consoling feeling without the loss that would normally occasion it. Everyone seems to know everyone else's objections, and nobody is converting anybody.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a dispute about what we mean by enjoying or taking pleasure in something, such that several of these views do not genuinely contradict one another? Is it a quarrel about what counts as an emotion, and therefore really a philosophy-of-mind debate wearing an aesthetic costume? Is it a disagreement about whether there is a single explanandum at all, that is, about whether the paradox names one phenomenon or a bundle of at least three that admit of different answers? Or is it a substantive dispute with a shared target that is simply hard, in which case the stability is not a symptom of confusion? My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than endorse, is that the parties are answering different questions — one causal, one evaluative, one about the individuation of mental states — under a single heading, and that the appearance of deadlock is an artifact of that.

Tell me whether that is right, and if it is not, what is. Argue from what each camp actually claims rather than from my summary, since I may have mischaracterized the views. Then tell me what would count as progress on the diagnosis you settle on: a distinction that needs drawing, a case that would discriminate between rivals, an empirical result, a change of question, or a reason to expect no progress at all. One further request about form: please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or a long email, with no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italicized labels, and no tables. I would rather follow a single argument through to its end than navigate a structured document.
