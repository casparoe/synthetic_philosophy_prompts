I'm one of five people on our state's long-term disability appeals panel — we hear denials and write short opinions that get circulated internally and quietly turn into precedent. The plan pays only while a claimant has a "significant reduction in her capacity to perform her own occupation." Our vocational analyst scores that as a percentage of job tasks the person can still do. This month's claimant, a warehouse inventory clerk with a nerve condition, comes in at 65%. Four years ago we granted somebody at 62% in a three-paragraph opinion nobody can reconstruct; last year we denied somebody at 85%; everyone on the panel agrees 40% is a clear grant and 85% is a clear denial.

There are two fights and I need the written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. One is the case itself: grant or deny, with reasons the claimant's rep could actually argue against rather than just receive. The other is procedural. Our vice chair wants us to publish a bright line — anything above 60% retained capacity is not a significant reduction, effective next quarter — so we stop making it up case by case. Another member thinks publishing a number is straight-up dishonest, on the grounds that the phrase has no boundry for a number to track, so all we'd be doing is picking one of several readings we could live with and dressing the choice up as a discovery. I lean the other way from her: I think there's a fact of the matter about whether this woman qualifies, we just could never know which side of it we landed on, because a finding that 65% doesn't qualify had better also hold at 64.9% and 64.8% or it isn't knowledge at all. Two more things the opinion has to handle. First, our decision form has a third box, "record does not clearly establish," and I need to know whether checking it is ever the honest verdict or just a cop-out, given that under her picture its perfectly true that she either qualifies or she doesn't without that telling us which. Second, our counsel points out that a published 60% line runs into the same trouble one level up — 60% is itself a borderline place to put the line.

So: a real disposition on both fights, and an account of what the panel is entitled to assert in the finding depending on which picture of vagueness is right — including what we owe that old 62% grant, which I think cuts against us under one picture and maybe doesn't under the other. Close with the strongest thing that can be said for whichever side you recommend against, phrased so that side would sign off on the wording.
