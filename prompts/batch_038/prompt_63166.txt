I'm not an academic — I do demand forecasting for a mid-size retailer — and I fell into game theory through the centipede game, because a friend of mine runs a little reading group in his living room and we spent a month on it. We did the standard thing: backward induction says the first player should grab the pot on move one, passing is a mistake at every node, and then we looked at what actually happens in the labs, where almost nobody plays that way — most pairs push the pot along several times and only a small minority stop it at the very first decision. Fine, humans aren't the payoff-maximizers of the textbook. But what stuck with me afterwards wasn't the mismatch, its the repair job. Nobody in the room said "backward induction is just wrong about people." The move was: suppose a small share of players are the altruistic or confused type and everyone else is rational, and then the observed paths come out about right. Except the share is never measured independently. It gets inferred from the same data it's brought in to explain. Nudge it up, you get more passing; nudge it down, you get more grabbing. Whatever the subjects actually did, there's some value of the knob that reproduces it, and the core theory walks away from the whole episode looking untouched.

Now I see the same shape everywhere. The other one that hooked me is how the backward induction paradox itself gets dissolved: the argument that the chain only unravels if each player is certain the other is rational, so even a sliver of doubt — a small chance you're sitting across from someone who cooperates no matter what — makes it rational to cooperate for most of the game. Sounds decisive until you ask how big the sliver has to be, and the answer seems to always come back as: big enough. Any nonzero amount buys you a while, so the same framework is equally at home with the pot being taken on move one and with it riding almost to the end. Same repair job. The trouble doesn't get relocated into the theory, it gets relocated into something unobservable about the players — their types, their error rates, the parameters of their preferences — and since nobody can see those, nothing much can contradict the setup afterwards.

So does this move have a name? I don't mean "ad hoc," which people slap on anything they dislike, and I don't mean the general point that one failed prediction doesn't bury a whole theory by itself — I've got that, it's not what I'm after. I mean the narrower thing, where the rescue consists in positing an unmeasured mixture of agent types, the mixing weight is free, and it's set from the very data the rescue is meant to explain, with the result that the theory is compatible with any behaviour and still gets presented as an explanation of that behaviour. I want the carving more than the label: what exactly is this thing, what is it not, and where do the edges sit? Because I honestly can't tell whether the same charge should be pressed against stuff like quantal response equilibrium or level-k thinking, which also bolt on a free parameter — a noise temperature, a distribution over how many steps of reasoning people run — but seem to pay for themselves by getting things right in games they weren't fitted to. And I can't tell whether what I'm describing is any different from the old complaint that revealed preference can't fail, since you can always cook up preferences matching whatever someone chose. My friend says I'm just describing how all empirical science works, which may be true, but then I'd like to know why some patches feel like cheating and others don't. If there's no settled term for it, make me one: a definition I could actually apply to a paper, plus two or three near misses that fall just outside it, so I can tell when I'm watching the real thing and when I'm only watching ordinary scientific adjustment.
