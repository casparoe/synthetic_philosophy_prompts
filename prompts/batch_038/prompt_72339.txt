I am a fourth-year PhD student in political science, and this term I am leading a 90-minute discussion section of fourteen upper-level undergraduates attached to a course on international security. My assigned week concerns historical analogy in foreign-policy judgment: the worry that Munich, the domino theory, Suez, and the Thucydides trap each license incompatible readings of the same situation, and that the analogy a decision-maker reaches for is often selected by considerations that have nothing to do with how well it fits. I know the literature and can lecture on it competently, but I would rather not lecture.

Design a structured deliberation exercise in which the section runs into that problem from the inside, before anyone names it. I would like the following:

- An invented crisis on a single page, set in a fictionalized region so that no student has prior knowledge of how it "really" turned out, but constructed so that four or five genuine historical analogies each have a non-trivial surface fit — the exercise must not be dismissible as rigged against them.
- A private assignment mechanism giving each student one analogy and one bureaucratic or factional role, arranged so that no one knows which analogies are in the room.
- A first round in which small groups must commit to a recommendation and, separately and in sealed form, to predictions about what the central issue at stake is, how events will unfold, and what the principal danger of inaction and of action respectively would be.
- A second round that swaps the analogies while holding the brief and the roles fixed, so that students can observe whether their recommendation tracks the facts or the frame.
- A debrief that maps particular moments of the exercise onto the distinction between an analogy that is doing merely rhetorical work and one that is genuinely diagnostic, plus some way of scoring the sealed predictions that requires no money.

Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I already expect that someone will recognize their assigned analogy and begin performing it rather than reasoning from it — and be candid about which feature of analogical judgment in real decisions the exercise cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
