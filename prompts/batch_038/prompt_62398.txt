So my decision-theory reading group is doing a session that pairs the Death in Damascus case — the one Gibbard and Harper introduced back in the late seventies, via Maugham's Samarra story — with Sophocles' Oedipus the King, which about half the group has actually read and the rest only know from the film versions or from Freud. I want a real reading of the play that uses the machinery instead of just announcing that it's about fate and therefore fatalism. The thing I keep coming back to: in Death in Damascus the state of the world is fixed before the agent moves, so his choice is a symptom of it rather than a cause of it, and that's exactly why both evidential and causal reasoning shrug and why the fatalistic conclusion is *right* there. The Delphic oracle looks like the same setup from outside, but I don't think it is, and the difference is the whole ballgame — Oedipus running from Corinth isn't evidence about where he'll end up, it's the mechanism that puts him at the place where the three roads meet. The prophecy isn't read off a prior state; it gets fed back through the agent's own response to hearing it. So sort it out for me, stopping at the scenes where it matters: is the play a genuine no-free-lunch problem, a self-fulfilling prophecy problem, or something backtracking and Newcomb-ish where the oracle's accuracy is doing the causal work? Be willing to say it mixes them and to say where the mixing looks deliberate rather than sloppy.

There's also a wrinkle I want you to run with, and fair warning, it's my own coinage so you won't find it in anyone's paper: call it the Jocasta update. She argues that oracles are worthless by pointing at the prophecy that Laius would fall by his son's hand, plus the fact that (as far as she knows) he was killed by a band of robbers instead, and the baby died of exposure on the mountainside three days in. That reads like a clean Bayesian downgrade on a predictor that missed — except the evidence is contaminated twice. Once because the exposure she and Laius ordered *in response to* that prophecy is a link in the causal chain that eventually puts a grown Oedipus on the road out of Corinth, and again because the sole survivor's report of robbers, plural, is testimony from a man who ran and left his king bleeding in the dirt, so whether he miscounted, lied to save his skin, or was just too far gone in shock to see straight is worth teasing apart on its own. She's revising her credence in the oracle's reliability using data that the oracle's earlier output helped produce. Is that a distinctive failure, or is it the ordinary feedback problem you get in macro forecasting and in clinical trials where the study changes what it studies? Tell me what a decision theorist would say she got wrong, and whether Sophocles is endorsing her mistake or just profiting from it.

Two more asks. Be honest about where the frame strains the play: flag the places where talking about acts and states and credences flattens something Sophocles is doing with the gods' will, with moira, with the pollution of the city, and with dramatic irony as the *audience's* evidential situation rather than the characters'. That last one I'd especially like developed, because it strikes me that we in the seats are structurally the man in Damascus — we already know where the appointment is, nothing we can do alters it, and yet our foreknowledge is what makes watching all this deliberation excruciating rather than tedious. Is that a real philosophical payoff or just a pretty thing to say at a book club? And end by telling me what the play adds to the fatalism debate that the decision-theory literature doesn't — in particular whether Aristotle's point in the Poetics about recognition and reversal arriving together, with Oedipus as his example of the best case, is itself decision-theoretic (the discovery of the truth just *being* the bringing-about of the disaster) or whether thats me overreaching. Don't quote the play or the Greek at me and don't hand me a reading list; I want the argument itself. American English please.
