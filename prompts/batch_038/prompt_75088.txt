I've been put in charge of the ethics section of a design doc for an agentic system that's going to make actual allocation decisions (think ICU resource triage, plus some content-removal calls), and before I hand it in I want someone to try to break the position I'm planing to defend. Here's the package:

1. I'm not a moral realist. I don't think there are stance-independent moral facts sitting out there to be tracked; on my view morality is best understood as the systematization of what people actually care about, once you clean it up.

2. Even so, I want the system to handle moral uncertainty the way the moral-uncertainty people recommend: put credences over the main candidate normative theories and have it maximize expected choice-worthiness across the mixture, rather than betting the whole deployment on one theory happening to be right.

3. Nothing gets hand-written as a principle. Its values have to come out of learning from what people choose and approve of — partly because any principle you write down will get gamed, and partly because I'm a particularist, so I don't think there are exceptionless moral principles for it to learn in the first place.

4. That learning can't take raw revealed preference at face value, though. Preferences shaped by addiction, by manipulation, or by people adapting downward to what they were denied have to get filtered out or discounted somehow.

5. Architecturally it has to be a scalar optimizer — I don't think there's a real alternative if you want planning over long horizons — but I'm also committed to there being things it flatly won't do. In the transplant-style case (one healthy visitor, five failing organs) it has to refuse, and the refusal has to fall out of the objective rather than being a special-coded rule bolted on top.

6. It has to be non-manipulative and corrigible. Non-manipulative in the sense that it should never be able to do better by changing what the human wants or what the human does than by changing the world; corrigible in the sense that it takes a shutdown command seriously and doesn't resist or route around it, on the usual line that an agent genuinely uncertain about human values has reason to let the human pull the plug.

7. And every morally loaded decision it makes should come with an account of what counted for and against, in terms a lay review panel can actually check — not a confidence score.

I keep feeling like something in here doesn't cohere but I can't pin down which pair, and I don't have a favorite I'm protecting — I'd drop whichever one has to go. So: go through these and tell me which ones genuinely can't all be held at once, and rank those clashes by severity instead of treating them all as equally worrying. For each real one, say what would have to be given up or weakened and what that costs me downstream. Some of these probably only look like they clash, and I'd like those called out as fine, with the reason — don't flag everything just to be safe. Then finish with the single revision that buys the most coherence for the least damage. I'm not after a tour of machine ethics here, just the audit.
