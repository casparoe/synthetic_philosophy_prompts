Our group is drafting the epistemic specification for a system that issues both flat factual assertions and numerical confidence estimates for them; at present the two outputs come from separate components, and nothing constrains one by the other. Before we choose among the accounts of outright belief, I want the requirement list any such account must meet in order to serve as that bridge. Give me: the cases it has to get right — a long conjunction of individually well-supported claims, one ticket in a large and genuinely fair lottery, a stream of testimony from a source that is reliable item by item but unreliable in aggregate — with the point of each; the distinctions it must not collapse, such as between believing and being confident, between what the evidence supports and what the stakes license, and between statistical and individualized support; and the structural principles it must either accept or explicitly refuse, namely closure under conjunction, consistency of the belief set, a threshold linking credence to belief, and stability of belief under further evidence, in each case saying whether the principle is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp and why. Note any formal results the account must be consistent with. Then tell me which single requirement eliminates the most candidates, and whether some subset of the non-negotiable ones is jointly unsatisfiable — if it is, say which one we should surrender, given that the system cannot drop either of its outputs. Close with a few lines, no more, on how a threshold view, a credence-only view that abolishes flat belief, and a stability-based view fare against the list.
