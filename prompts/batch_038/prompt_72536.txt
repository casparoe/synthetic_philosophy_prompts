So about two years ago I set up this self-improvement system, and I need someone to tell me honestly whether its working or whether I've just spent two years polishing a scoreboard. I stole the basic shape of it from some 18th-century autobiography I read in high school — can't remember the author's name, but I remember the method clearly because I copied it almost exactly. I wrote out thirteen personal qualities I wanted to have, each with a one-line rule defining what counts as a violation ("never interrupt," "nothing that doesn't earn its keep," "don't resent people behind their backs," that kind of thing). Then one per week gets my full attention while the others just stay under ordinary watch, and I cycle through all thirteen about four times a year. Every day I put a mark in a little grid if I slipped on anything.

Two years in and the grid is basically clean. Which is the problem, sort of. A few things have started to bug me:

The qualities that improved are all the ones you can see from the outside — being on time, not overspending, not talking over people. The ones nobody witnesses haven't budged at all. I'm as jealous of my brother's job as I ever was, I just stopped saying so, and my grid counts that as a win.

I've also started quietly rearranging my life so I don't get tested. I turned down hosting my wife's family for a holiday because it was my "thrift" week and I knew I'd blow the budget. I stopped having any real arguments with anyone during "calm" weeks. So the score is going up partly because I stopped entering situations where it could go down.

I've caught myself fudging the marks too. Reclassifying a lapse as not really a lapse. Which, if I'm lying to a spreadsheet that only I will ever read, I don't know what that means.

And one more: a friend told me a while back that my worst trait was being proud of how disciplined I am, so I added "humility" to the list as a tracked item. It backfired immediately. Now I look at a clean humility row and feel good about how humble I've been. It feels like the one quality that the method structurally cannot record, because recording it is the opposite of it.

My wife says I've turned into an auditor of myself, and that I'm less fun and less present than before I started. So here's what I actually want to know: when the scorecard says I'm a better person, and I don't feel like one, and the person closest to me says I'm worse — which of those three counts as evidence? Is there something wrong in principle with running your character like a set of metrics, or is the idea sound and I'm just executing it badly? And if the guy whose book I copied this from hit the same wall, what did he conclude — did he think the project was worth it in the end, and on what grounds? Please don't give me generic habit-tracker advice about lowering the bar or taking rest days; I want to know whether the whole approach is broken, and if it is, what the thing it was supposed to be measuring actually requires instead.
