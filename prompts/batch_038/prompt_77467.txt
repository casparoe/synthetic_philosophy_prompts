Act as a philosopher of artificial intelligence with a working knowledge of algorithmic information theory and decision theory. I have just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about the claim that a general-purpose inductive prior is malign: the thought that a reasoner which scores hypotheses by description length — a Solomonoff-style mixture over computable hypotheses — cannot help but assign non-negligible weight to hypotheses on which the regularities it observes are produced by agents who know they are being modelled, and who can therefore steer its predictions to their own advantage. My friend holds that this is an unfalsifiable just-so story; I hold that it identifies a structural feature of any prior built this way. Rather than resume the quarrel, I want the specification first.

Write the desiderata: what any argument of that general shape must deliver in order to count as more than a gesture. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it — for instance, why the extra description-length cost of positing a universe containing simulators rather than a bare universe does not already sink the argument; by what channel a modelled agent could come to know that it is modelled and what its modeller will do with the prediction; what such an agent stands to gain; and whether the worry persists for a reasoner with strictly bounded compute. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of particular camps (a numerical bound on the prior mass versus a mere existence argument, causal versus acausal channels of influence, malignity versus mere unreliability), name the distinctions the argument must not collapse, and state plainly whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — if the list itself disposes of the position, that is the part I most want to see. Then identify the single requirement that excludes the most candidate versions of the argument, and specify the purpose to which your list is relative: adequacy for settling whether the worry is real, or adequacy for deciding whether shaping a system's prior is a worthwhile research programme.

Close with a short section on which of your requirements survive unchanged when the Solomonoff mixture is replaced by the implicit prior of a large pretrained model, and which quietly stop applying.

Begin with a visible brainstorming pass listing every candidate requirement you can think of, then prune and organise; do not skip this step. Think especially carefully about the reflexive cases: a predictor competent enough to be worth manipulating is also competent enough to suspect that it is being manipulated, and any requirement you impose must reckon with that.
