**Question.** In the *Principles*, Berkeley challenges his materialist opponent to frame an idea of some body — he instances a tree or a house — existing unconceived and unperceived, and holds that the attempt defeats itself. Reconstruct the argument, identify the view it is meant to undermine, and assess whether the objection that Berkeley equivocates between conceiving of a thing as unperceived and conceiving of a thing while one is not perceiving it succeeds.

**Script.**

Berkeley's target is not the sceptic but the philosopher who holds that bodies subsist in an unthinking substance outside the mind. He thinks that view rests on an act of abstraction: the materialist supposes he can separate in thought the existence of a sensible thing from its being perceived, and thence conclude that a tree may exist unperceived. Berkeley converts this into a challenge. Try to conceive of a tree existing unconceived and unperceived; if you succeed, you have conceived of it, and so it was not unconceived after all. The materialist cannot escape by pointing out that other people perceive the tree, since Berkeley's question concerns existence apart from any mind whatever, and the matter the materialist posits is by definition unthinking and so cannot perceive anything. The natural reply is that Berkeley equivocates: to conceive of a state of affairs in which no one conceives of the tree is not to fail to conceive of the tree; the content of a conception is one thing, the act of conceiving another. That reply is sound as far as it goes, but it does not defeat Berkeley, who has an independent route to the same place. Bodies persist through unperceived intervals because they are permanent possibilities of sensation: the table in the empty room is not annihilated but is simply what anyone entering would perceive. Berkeley can therefore concede the equivocation objection and still secure the continuity of the world. Finally, this argument is what drives Berkeley to God. Since nothing can exist unconceived, and since things evidently persist when we do not perceive them, some infinite mind must perceive all things at all times; the argument thus yields a proof of God's existence that requires no further premises about the passivity and involuntariness of our ideas of sense. Throughout, Berkeley presents himself as the defender of common sense: what he denies is not that trees and stones exist, but that they exist in an unthinking substance.
