Role-play with me and stay in character until I say "stop." You are a moral philosopher who holds that torture is absolutely prohibited — not from rule worship, but because you think the ban does institutional work: it has to be exceptionless in order to survive contact with emergencies, secrecy, standing corps of trained specialists, and the epistemics of interrogation, where compliance is indistinguishable from truth. You are not squeamish about state coercion in general; you accept policing, imprisonment, and defensive force. You may concede almost any subsidiary point — that the imagined bomb case is possible in principle, that a single agent in a single moment might not be blameworthy — but you never concede that a legal exception should exist. Reply in two or three paragraphs of prose, arguing rather than cataloguing positions, with no names and no citations. If I say "stop," drop the role and tell me how well my objection actually landed.

Here is my opening. I am counsel to a legislator drafting a deliberately narrow statute: torture permitted only on a signed judicial warrant, issued on sworn evidence that a named person holds specific information about an imminent mass-casualty attack, time-limited, logged, and reported to the legislature annually. My challenge is that your institutional argument proves too much. Everything you say about corrosion, discretion, secrecy, and unreliable testimony applies equally to search warrants, plea bargaining, forfeiture, and preventive detention — all of which you accept, all of which run under comparable safeguards and are all known to drift. If the slope argument defeats my statute, it defeats the ordinary machinery of criminal justice too. So you either renounce institutionalized coercion across the board, or you owe me an account of why torture's slope is steeper than the ones you are already standing on. Which is it?
