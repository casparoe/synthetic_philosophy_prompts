I am in my fourth year of a PhD in philosophy of medicine, and for the past eighteen months I have also served as the "theory" member of a university hospital's medical-necessity appeals panel. We hear roughly twenty-five appeals a month, in two two-hour sittings, after a coverage denial. The panel is two physicians, a clinical pharmacist, a nurse case manager, and an administrator; none has any philosophy training. Each case gets about ten minutes of discussion before we vote, and the chair then signs a written rationale that has to survive external review. Our standing criterion is that the requested service must address a disease, illness, or injury. Nobody has ever said what that means, so we argue past one another: one physician means a value outside a laboratory reference range, the administrator means a condition with an established treatment guideline, the nurse means a condition that makes the claimant's life go badly, and I mean none of these.

I want to replace this with a decision procedure. Please write one that satisfies the following.

Construct it out of the standard positions rather than out of one: the statistical-functional naturalism on which disease is an internal state that is subtypical for a reference class and impairs a function bearing on survival or reproduction; the hybrid account that adds a value condition about harm to the person; an account built on the ability to reach one's vital goals; and a frankly normativist account on which the disease concept is constituted in part by social norms. Label every step with the account it comes from. The five of us will each fill in the procedure individually before discussion, and the comparison should let us see at which step we diverged and under which account, instead of re-litigating a disagreement about concepts as though it were one about facts.

Certain distinctions have to survive the simplification, and I want you to say so explicitly as you design: the point that disease is a type-level notion, so an individual's atypical state need not instantiate one; the choice of reference class, including a default, a rule for recognizing when the default is contestable, and some account of what makes a reference class admissible at all, given that clinical ranges are indexed to age and sex and have sometimes been indexed to categories whose legitimacy is socially disputed; the difference between a present functional deficit and an elevated risk of one; the environment-relativity of function, and hence whether a mismatch between a person and the environment they are required to live in counts as a disease of the person or a defect of the environment; and the firewall between the question whether this is a disease and the question whether it should be covered. Keep the value-free tier and the tier at which desirability enters separate, whatever you call them.

Present it as numbered steps that fit on one page, each giving the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, the possible outputs, and where the procedure terminates in a no, versus where it hands the remaining question to a vote because that question is one of values rather than facts. End with the three to five sentences the panel would actually write as its rationale. Tell me which distinctions you are deliberately dropping as unusable by non-specialists and what we lose by dropping them; mark every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure; and say what the procedure cannot do, including where it will misfire — conditions whose underlying mechanism is itself disputed, conditions that seem defined by their responsiveness to treatment, novel conditions, and cases where harm is not in doubt but dysfunction is.

Then run it, step by step, on three appeals. First: a 34-year-old carrier of a pathogenic BRCA1 variant, asymptomatic, denied prophylactic surgery and annual surveillance imaging. Second: a claimant with a thyroid-stimulating hormone level above the laboratory's reference range, a normal free thyroxine, and fatigue she attributes to it, denied levothyroxine, where the evidence that treatment benefits people in this category is contested. Third: a claimant denied a four-week residential recovery program whose file contains no abnormal findings, whose treating clinician's diagnosis is work-related exhaustion, and whose only documented impairment is an inability to sustain his job. I should warn you that I expect the third to come out against me, and I would rather see that now than in the room: the panel has approved every such appeal for three years, and I have voted yes each time.

You need no bibliography; name the positions however is clearest and I will add references myself if any of this goes in front of the panel. Two closing requests. Correct me wherever I have mischaracterized what one of these accounts is committed to above. And tell me how confident you are in your claims about those commitments, and single out the one point in your procedure about which you are least sure, together with what would settle it.
