i'm auditing a grad seminar on classical islamic philosophy and we just wrapped two sessions on the seventeenth discussion of al-Ghazali's Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, the causality one — fire and cotton, the claim that observation hands us sequence and never connection, so the burning is created by God at the moment of contact and the necessity the philosophers insist on isn't anywhere in the things themselves. i came away more sympathetic than most of the room, and i especially liked it when the seminar leader pushed back on the usual framing and said Ghazali arguably stays uncommitted between flat-out occasionalism and a view where creatures really have natures that produce their effects, just not with any necessity that could bind God. keep that fork in mind because it might matter below.

then a guy in the cohort who works on philosophy of religion built this argument to show the position eats its own tail, and i cant tell whether it's sound or whether he's smugling something in. he lays it out like this:

(1) On the occasionalist account, nothing in an event makes its usual sequel follow; the pairing is fixed only by what God habitually does.
(2) The only access anyone has to God's habit is the observed regularity itself.
(3) If nothing in the past sequence makes the next instance follow, then that something has happened a thousand times is no ground for expecting it the thousand-and-first time; repetition as such licenses nothing.
(4) So the occasionalist is not entitled even to say God has a habit — only that things have gone a certain way so far, which is a report about the past rather than a warrant about the next moment.
(5) The same trouble hits miracles. A miracle is supposed to certify a prophet by being a departure from the ordinary course that only God could produce. But if every event is equally a direct act of God, and the ordinary course is just a running summary of what has happened so far, then a staff turning into a snake is the same kind of thing as a stone nobody has ever seen float, floating.
(6) So nothing marks the prophet's sign off from the sorcerer's trick, and testimony in general — including the transmission of scripture, which reaches us through ink, memory, and chains of ordinary human events that this view gives no reason to trust — loses its evidential standing.
(7) But Ghazali's reason for attacking the philosophers' necessitarianism is precisely to make room for miracle and prophecy, which he thinks their system cannot accommodate.
(8) So the premise he needs does the opposite of the work he wants it to do: either the philosophers are right that the usual course is necessary, or nothing is knowable and the Tahāfut undercuts itself.

the thing that wont leave me alone is that Hume looks like an occasionalist about necessary connection — custom and habit, no glue in the objects — and yet he keeps induction and treats miracles as something to argue about rather than as unthinkable. so either Hume is inconsistent or my friend's argument is resting on something more than he's admitting, and i genuinely don't know which. i also don't want the way out to just be Ibn Rushd's charge in the Tahāfut al-Tahāfut that taking away causes takes away knowledge, since knowing a thing just is knowing its cause — that feels like it concedes the whole point rather than answering it. and there's the Deliverance from Error, where Ghazali describes his certainty coming back not by composing a proof or arranging words but by something like a light God put in his chest; my friend calls that a cop-out, and i can't work out whether he's right or whether it's actually load-bearing for Ghazali's side, which is part of what i want you to sort out.

if i had to pick, i'd keep (1) and (7) — i like the anti-necessitarianism and i like that it's supposed to be doing theological work. so: which step actually goes wrong, why did it look fine when you first read it, and what am i paying to give it up? and say whether the diagnosis changes depending on which Ghazali you read him as, the committed occasionalist or the one who stays uncommitted about secondary causes. if the argument survives your first repair attempt, tell me that instead of letting him off easy — i'd rather know it's a real problem than get a comfortable answer.
