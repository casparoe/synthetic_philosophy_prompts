I am in the second year of a master's programme in philosophy, and my seminar on free will and moral responsibility has just spent four weeks on Frankfurt's 1969 attack on the principle of alternative possibilities. Our assignment is a paper on the state of a debate rather than a contribution to one, and I have chosen this one precisely because it strikes me as the clearest case in the field of an argument that has stopped moving. I want a diagnosis of why, and I should say at the outset that I am not asking you to settle who is right.

Here is the shape of things as I understand them. The principle says that an agent is morally responsible for what she did only if she could have done otherwise. Frankfurt's counterexample is a case of counterfactual intervention: Jones is deliberating about taking something that is not his, and Black, who wants him to take it, has arranged to intervene if Jones shows any sign of deciding otherwise; in fact Jones decides on his own and Black never acts. The claim is that Jones is responsible and yet could not have done otherwise. The incompatibilist reply that has dominated the literature since is a dilemma: either Black's trigger is causally sufficient for Jones's decision, in which case the case presupposes determinism and begs the question against anyone who thinks determinism excludes responsibility, or it is not sufficient, in which case Jones retains some alternative, however thin, and the case fails as a counterexample. Libertarians such as Kane have tried to escape between the horns by making the trigger indeterministic. Separately, there is the response that denies the case eliminates alternatives at all: Jones could still have chosen at a different moment, or begun the act rather than completed it, or decided without Black's help — the flicker-of-freedom strategy — which compatibilists answer by insisting that what matters is robust alternatives, ones for which the agent would herself be responsible.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than accepted, is that these are not two sides of a single disagreement but two different disagreements sharing a name. One concerns the structure of the actual sequence: which features of what actually happens make an agent a responsible one, and whether access to alternatives is among them. The other concerns the standing to blame: whether it is fair to hold a person to a standard she had no opportunity to meet. If so, the Frankfurt case can defeat the second only by way of the first, and each side keeps answering an objection it was never given, which would explain the stalemate better than any failure of cleverness. A rival hypothesis I cannot rule out is that the impasse is methodological: a counterexample cannot function as data when the modal structure of the scenario is itself what is contested, so the two sides are really disagreeing about the evidential role of intuitions concerning cases whose very possibility is the question.

To make this concrete, my reading group built a variant and could not agree about what it shows. Call it the Delayed-Monitor Case. Black does not watch for a sign of a contrary decision; instead, if Jones's deliberation has not settled by a fixed time, an indeterministic process in the monitor fires, and that process produces in Jones the decision to take the money with probability just under one — but not with probability one. In the residual sliver of cases, Jones's own deliberation wins the race. The trigger is not causally sufficient, so the first horn of the dilemma does not bite; yet the alternative Jones retains is so improbable that half the group said it is not an alternative in any sense that could bear on responsibility, while the other half said the case reveals that could-have-done-otherwise is gradable and that the whole debate turns on a threshold nobody has been able to state.

So: diagnose the stall. Tell me which of my two hypotheses, or which third possibility I have not thought of, best explains why the positions have not converged, and support the diagnosis by reference to what each side actually claims rather than to a survey of the literature. Say where my variant sits — whether it advances the debate, merely relocates the dispute to a question about probabilities, or is useless for reasons one side or the other should be able to state. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that has not been drawn, a formal result (a precise account of what makes an alternative robust, perhaps, or a modal or decision-theoretic analysis of ability), a change of question, or something empirical. If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, argue for that, and say what the debate is doing for its participants instead.

Finally, I would like the answer to be candid about its own standing. State plainly which part of your diagnosis you are least confident in, what argument or consideration would change your mind about it, and where you think you are most likely to have misread one of the two sides.
