Three weeks ago I became chair of my hospital's infection-prevention committee, largely because no one else wanted the job, and my first task was a decision two previous chairs had deferred: whether to adopt a new whole-body decolonization protocol across our ICUs. Rather than convene another round-table discussion that would consume an afternoon, I tried something I believed would be cleaner. I posted the evidence summary in the committee's messaging channel and asked our eleven voting members to react to the post with a thumbs-up or a thumbs-down as they read it, on the theory that the reactions would give me an immediate tally and the written opinions could follow at leisure.

The design error at the center of what happened next is obvious to me now, but its consequences are not obvious at all, and that is what the argument below was about. Two reactions arrived within ten minutes, both thumbs-up, both from our most senior physicians. Seven more followed within the hour. By the next morning the tally stood at ten up, one down, and not one member had posted a word of reasoning. The recommendation, in substance, was unanimous.

Two members then argued about it in a call I sat in on: Naomi, a biostatistician, and Dev, a health economist. I abstained from the tally and have no result to defend, so I am reporting this as a bystander — but I have to write the recommendation next month and decide whether to rerun the vote under a different format, and I could not tell you who has the better of it. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

1. Naomi: the 10–1 is not eleven judgments; it is two judgments and eight followers. Once the second thumbs-up appeared, each subsequent member faced a tally that outweighed anything they personally knew, so they rationally set their own reading of the trials aside. The committee did not aggregate information; it stopped gathering it after vote two. Whatever else we say, we cannot treat the margin as evidence of anything.

2. Dev, three replies at once. First, the herding models assume the later mover has *nothing* but the public tally, whereas here all eleven had the same two trials in front of them, so most of them had a real private reason to vote as they did. Second, our signals were never independent anyway — everyone read the same literature — so Naomi is indicting the vote for failing a standard no such committee could ever meet, and on that standard every guideline panel ever convened is defective. Third, several members told him afterward they had agreed with the protocol on the merits.

3. Naomi: correlated inputs make aggregation worse, not better, and Dev's second point is a non-defense, since the requirement is conditional independence given the state of the world, which is much weaker than independence of reading lists. As for what members told him afterward, those reports are precisely what one expects from people reconstructing reasons for a choice they made by following; asked before the tally appeared, most would have been considerably less certain. Post-hoc testimony is not evidence about the process.

4. Dev: he grants the cascade for argument's sake and says Naomi's conclusion still does not follow. An uninformative cascade is not an incorrect decision. If the tally adds nothing beyond the first two votes, then the probability that the committee is right is just the probability that those two were right — and given two reasonably large randomized trials pointing the same way, that probability is high. Her argument shows the margin is worthless as evidence; it does not show the recommendation is likely wrong. He adds that she cannot have it both ways about blame: on her own account every member voted rationally given what was visible to them, so there is no one to indict.

5. Naomi: that is a distinction with no practical weight, and Dev has slid from "what does this vote license?" to "how likely is the recommendation correct?" Committees exist not to be right by luck but to be right in a way that survives accidents of sequencing. Had the two senior physicians opened their phones in the other order, or had the first reaction been a thumbs-down, the same evidence could have produced the opposite recommendation, and nothing about the trials would have changed. Worse, the unanimity is now public and entrenched: anyone with a private doubt has every reason to stay quiet, so the committee has destroyed its own capacity to revisit the question later.

6. Dev: the robustness demand comes with no price tag. A secret simultaneous ballot costs three weeks and political capital in a hospital that is already short of both, and if the aim is to be right rather than to be defensibly right, it is not obviously worth paying. He also objects that Naomi's line about the vote being worthless is being repeated on the wards as "the protocol is unsafe," which it is not, and that a critique of a procedure does not entitle her to have it laundered into a critique of the substance.

7. The final crux concerned the lone thumbs-down, cast by a charge nurse who told us she had voted against deliberately, having read somewhere that appointed dissent can break a cascade. Naomi says this vindicates her: the dissent reopened the thread and drew from a junior respiratory therapist the only genuinely new fact in the entire process — that our current stock of the antiseptic cannot be restocked for months. Dev says it proves the opposite. Because the protocol made the contrarian vote predictable — its content fixed in advance by its function — it could not carry information; and the nurse's datum emerged in spite of the manufactured dissent, not because of it. A proper ballot would have surfaced the supply problem just as well without the theatrics. Neither budged.

I would like a move-by-move adjudication rather than a verdict on who "wins." For each of the seven, rate it — clearly mistaken, weak, sound but limited, or clearly right — with a sentence of justification, and say explicitly whether any error is technical (a misdescription of what rational-herding models actually assume) or dialectical (a slide from one claim to a different one). Then take up four questions directly. Is Dev right that Naomi's argument, if sound, licenses no conclusion about the likely correctness of the protocol, or does the fragility she describes in move 5 bear on correctness itself and not merely on our warrant for believing in it? Is Naomi right that order-sensitivity is a defect in its own right, and if so, what is the value it points at — epistemic, or a matter of the decision's legitimacy and revisability? On move 7, was the nurse's dissent informative, and what follows for committees that appoint a known devil's advocate? And what are both of them missing — my suspicion is that they are arguing past each other about what a vote of this kind is even for, but I cannot articulate the missing frame myself. Finally, given all of that: should I rerun the vote, and in what format — and if a secret simultaneous ballot is the answer, does that concede Dev's point that the original tally was probably correct after all?
