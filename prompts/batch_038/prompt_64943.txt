so I've been trying to write down what I actually believe about the whole "does safety work slow you down" question, and before I put it in front of my reading group I want someone to check whether the package holds together. Here's the set:

(1) There's basically no alignment tax in the capability sense. A frontier model trained under all the safety-relevant constraints — refusal training, monitoring, interpretability gates, the lot — lands within a hair of what you'd get from an unconstrained run on the same budget. The costs are real but they're costs of engineering discipline and paperwork, not of ceiling.

(2) That said, competitive pressure absolutely does cause safety work to get skipped. Concretely: two labs close in capability, the one that spends six months validating before deploying loses the deployment race, and losing is expensive enough (the winner sets defaults, takes the market, buys the next cluster) that skipping is instrumentally rational. It happens because of competition, not because anyone is anti-safety.

(3) The marginal actor isn't a big lab. By the time capabilities are where it matters, whoever is closest to the frontier is doing the deciding, and that could be a mid-size outfit, a state program, or a small team running open weights. What any one frontier lab does moves the timeline only weakly.

(4) Safety research is capability-positive. Genuine interpretability progress buys you better training diagnostics, better data curation, cleaner RL signal. Over a long enough horizon the actor doing more safety work is more capable, not less.

(5) I'm against a broad slowdown and against heavy compute governance, because delay doesn't buy alignment progress. The field isn't bottlenecked on calendar years, it's bottlenecked on ideas, and ideas don't arrive faster because you got three more years. Delay mostly just reshuffles who builds.

(6) I think it's live that we get an unaligned frontier system which then has a decisive advantage, and that this is the main thing to worry about. I'm not a "market feedback will sort it out" person.

(7) Publishing safety results openly is almost always good, including results that let other people evaluate or replicate capabilities work, since the marginal bad actor is going to have those capabilities anyway.

Go through these and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other, and rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything as equally bad. For each real one, say which commitment has to give and what I'd lose by giving it up — and also tell me which pairs only look like they clash but are fine on inspection, since I suspect I've got at least one of those in here and I can't tell which.
