I spent thirty-four years as an experimental physicist, most of it measuring transport properties of materials at low temperatures, and I retired eighteen months ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy more systematically than a working life permitted, and I have become preoccupied with Zeno's paradoxes — not with whether Achilles catches the tortoise, but with how thin I find the standard assurance that the calculus disposes of the matter. I should like your help in the capacity of a philosopher of physics who also knows the history of the Eleatic arguments and the subsequent literature on motion, continuity, and infinite tasks. Please think carefully rather than quickly, and treat the ordering as the deliverable: a considered ranking will be worth far more to me than a comprehensive survey.

Before you rank anything, spend a few lines brainstorming. Which candidate responses am I missing? And is my list even a list of alternatives at the same level of description, or have I mixed together answers to different questions? If the latter, say so and re-cut the list yourself; I would rather be corrected at the outset than ranked within a muddle.

Here is the list, assembled partly from reading and partly from my own devising (item 8 is mine, and I flag it as such rather than pretending it is an established position):

1. The standard mathematical response: the convergent series, together with the reconstruction of the continuum and the at-at account of motion, according to which to move is simply to occupy different places at different times, with no further state of moving-at-an-instant.
2. The supertask response: the real difficulty concerns whether an infinite sequence of acts can be completed at all, so Zeno's arguments are continuous with the puzzles about lamps switched infinitely often and the like, and not with summation.
3. The intuitionist or finitist response: the continuum cannot be assembled from points, the dichotomy's decomposition is therefore illegitimate, and the paradoxes reveal the cost of accepting actual infinity.
4. The discrete or atomist response: there is a minimal structure to space or time, so the decompositions terminate; the arrow's frozen instants are then an artefact of a continuum we do not in fact inhabit.
5. The metaphysical response that takes motion as primitive: velocity at an instant is a derivative bookkeeping device, and the arrow paradox mistakes that device for a genuine instantaneous state.
6. The contradiction-accepting response: motion at an instant really is a matter of being both here and not-here, and the paradoxes expose a genuine inconsistency rather than a puzzle awaiting dissolution.
7. The deflationary response about how the arguments are worded: the puzzles trade on a misleading picture of traversing and reaching, and once that picture is dissolved nothing substantive needs answering.
8. My own operationalist gloss: every actual measurement of motion is a comparison across finite intervals, so instantaneous velocity is a regulative idealisation, and the paradoxes are artefacts of reifying that idealisation.

For each item, give me two scores from zero to one and a composite. The first axis is coverage: does the response dispose of all four of the arguments as Aristotle reports them, or only of the quantitative ones? My impression — correct me if it is wrong — is that the moving-rows argument is not about infinite sums at all, in which case a great deal of the familiar machinery simply does not reach it. The second axis is durability: would the response survive contact with the physics I know, including the relativistic structure of spacetime, the role of measurement, and the live possibility that there is a minimal length or a minimal interval? Then sort the items into four tiers by composite, with one or two sentences per item explaining why it lands where it does. I want decisive placements, not a discussion that concludes everything has something to recommend it.

Three further questions, answered separately and briefly. First, is item 8 a genuine alternative, or is it item 1 or item 5 in workmanlike clothing? I would like a verdict, not a diplomatic non-answer. Second, does the quantum Zeno effect belong on this list at all, or is its presence in popular discussions owed entirely to a shared name? Third, of these responses, which is most overrated and which most underrated among physicists who think they have settled the question in an afternoon?

Two constraints on form. I am not asking for a bibliography and I do not want one; where naming a proponent helps, do so, but if you are unsure of an attribution, describe the position rather than attach it to someone. And please mark explicitly the two or three placements about which you are least confident, along with what would settle them.

One last thing, and I mean it: if my framing rests on a confusion — if the two axes are not independent, or if coverage and durability are the wrong things to score a philosophical response on, or if the exercise cannot be done well at all without sources I have not supplied — I would much rather be told that plainly than accommodated politely. Treat my setup as a proposal to be tested, not a constraint to be obeyed.
