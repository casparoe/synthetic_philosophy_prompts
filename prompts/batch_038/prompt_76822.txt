You are a philosopher of science with a serious working knowledge of natural kinds, measurement, and the metaphysics of time, and enough familiarity with stratigraphic practice to reason about it technically rather than from press coverage. Here is the practice I want examined.

Since the 1970s the International Commission on Stratigraphy has defined the boundaries of the geologic time scale by means of single physical locations, informally called golden spikes. A committee selects one section of rock, ice, or sediment somewhere on Earth, designates a precise horizon inside it, and declares that horizon to be the lower boundary of a stage; the boundary of all planetary time is thereby fixed at a place you can walk to and drive a marker into. Candidate sections are judged against standing criteria: a primary marker (typically the first appearance of a fossil species) corroborated by secondary markers, minerals capable of radiometric dating, continuous sedimentation with no change of facies, adequate thickness, and global correlatability. The criteria also include openly practical ones — the site should be readily reachable, protected from development, and open to researchers of all nationalities. Proposals move up a chain of committees and are settled by supermajority votes. Where the record is too degraded to permit any of this — very roughly, before 630 million years ago — the commission abandons points altogether and defines boundaries by fixed numerical ages instead. The tolerance for imperfect markers is real: at one ratified early-Cambrian boundary the designated marker trace fossil has since been found several metres below the official point, and the boundary was left in place because nothing better was available.

The same machinery has now spent about fifteen years on the Anthropocene. A working group voted in 2019 to pursue a mid-twentieth-century start date, screened about a dozen candidate sites, and in 2023 proposed the varved sediments of a small lake in Ontario, with the boundary set at 1950 and the primary marker supplied by fallout plutonium from mid-century weapons testing, supported by plastics, fly ash, concrete, and altered carbon and nitrogen cycles. In 2024 the proposal was voted down by the relevant subcommission by a wide margin and the rejection was then confirmed nearly unanimously by the higher bodies, with critics objecting that the sedimentary record is too thin and the start date too recent to carry an epoch boundary. The term itself continues in general use. Meanwhile the competing start dates have never gone away: Neolithic farming, an early-seventeenth-century dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide associated with the depopulation of the Americas, the Industrial Revolution, or the postwar acceleration. And when the Holocene was subdivided in 2018 into three stages, the youngest of them pinned to an isotopic shift in a stalagmite from a cave in northeast India recording a drought some four thousand years ago, archaeologists and historians objected publicly that the units were useless for their own periodization and would corrupt their literature.

2. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it comfortably, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, where several rival theories would license exactly the same procedure.
3. Say which views of periodization the practice quietly rules out, and whether it rules them out by argument or only by institutional design.
4. Assess whether the parts of the practice presuppose consistent things. I am particularly struck by four apparent tensions, and I would like you to judge each rather than merely list them: a boundary of natural time is selected partly on whether researchers can fly to it and is ratified by a vote; the proposed Anthropocene marker was produced by the very species doing the marking, so its global synchrony is an artifact of human activity rather than of any natural process; the "too recent" objection seems to assume that a unit must be a closed interval whose upper boundary is already in the record, whereas the Anthropocene's upper boundary is the future; and the deep-time fallback shows the commission perfectly willing to define a boundary by stipulated number when a physical point is unavailable, so the refusal to do something similar at the recent end needs explaining.
5. Address the question of authority directly. What does a supermajority committee procedure presuppose about who is entitled to say when an age began? Can such a body be an epistemic authority about a fact it partly constitutes, or does the practice only work if the fact is entirely independent of it?

Describe what would have to change — in the criteria, the chart, the vocabulary, and the discipline's self-understanding — if the presupposed view were replaced by explicit conventionalism (a documented, revisable naming convention with published rationale) or by pluralism (several legitimate Anthropocenes indexed to different explanatory questions). Say what would be gained, what would be lost, and which objections from working stratigraphers the new regimes could and could not answer.
