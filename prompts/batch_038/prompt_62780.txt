I spent thirty-five years doing instrumentation on a synchrotron beamline — designing detectors, mostly, and arguing about calibration — and since I retired I've fallen into philosophy, which turns out to be the same activity with worse equipment. I run a Thursday-evening reading group at our public library, six of us, only one with any formal training. We did Dennett's piece about the fellow whose brain gets lifted out and put in a vat in Houston while his body is driven around by radio, and where a computer duplicate of the brain shows up later with a master switch for choosing between them. That set us off, and over the last couple of months we've voted our way through a stack of cases of our own devising. Our record so far:

1. Vat-and-radio, both in the same building: unanimous, I'm in the vat. The body across the room whose hand my wife is holding is not me.
2. Body kept alive on a ventilator, brain dead: unanimous, I'm gone. The organism lives, I don't.
3. Brain replaced by silicon equivalents one neuron at a time over several years, function preserved all the way through: five of six said I survive, and at the end I'm the machine.
4. Same thing done all at once — the scan destroys the brain, an artificial brain built from the scan wakes up in the same head two minutes later: majority said I survive. "Same as 3, only faster." Two of us thought a copy can't be an original and lost the vote.
5. Non-destructive copy — the original brain stays awake in the vat, and a copy boots up in the next room with all my memories and my temper: ruling was that exactly one of the two is me, namely the original. The copy is a well-informed stranger. We all agreed the copy would insist otherwise from the inside, and that this settles nothing either way.
6. Same brain, unbroken consciousness, twenty-five years, but memories and character progressively overwritten by illness and a wholly new life, so that none of the original psychology is left at the end: majority said I don't survive. So an unbroken stream carrying none of the content isn't enough.

Now here's the case I've built for next Thursday, and I honestly can't work out what we're committed to. Non-destructive copy again, like 5, except there's no standoff: the vat brain has a failing circulation loop that can't be repaired, so it will degrade over roughly ten days and stop on day eleven. The copy boots on day one in a fresh body, and we stay in contact the whole time — radio link, about a second of lag, enough that we can hear each other think. The degradation isn't a clean switch-off. By day six the original retains less of my pre-scan psychology then the copy does, and its fully aware of that. On day eleven it stops. The copy goes on another forty years with my name, my marriage, and my notebooks.

Two questions for the group: on day six, which of us is me, if either? And on day twelve, when there's only one candidate left, is there anybody who is me?

Here's what I want from you. Lay out the rules that are genuinely compatible with all six of our past verdicts — I suspect there's more than one and that we've been sloppily switching between them — and say what each of those rules yields for day six and for day twelve. Then tell me which of the six is the closest analog to my new case, predict what the group will actually rule, and say what it should rule if it wants to stay consistent with itself. If our record really does underdetermine it, spell out what principle we'd be adopting by ruling one way rather than the other, because I'd much rather pick that knowingly than drift into it. And finally — instrument man, sorry — give me one further case, short enough to put to six people in five minutes, that would force the surviving rules apart if mine turns out not to.
