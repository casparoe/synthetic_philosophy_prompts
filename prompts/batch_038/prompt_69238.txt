I am a doctoral candidate in political science writing the normative chapter of my dissertation on electoral-system choice in post-conflict constitutional design, and I intend to use the following as a worked teaching case in a graduate seminar. Please write a dialogue between two proponents of opposing views on one concretely specified design choice. All of the figures below are stipulated; the characters should treat them as common ground.

**The case.** The Republic of Valmara is five years past a civil war. Its constitutional commission must settle the electoral law for a 300-seat legislature, and two options remain:

- **Option A:** first-past-the-post in 300 single-member districts, equal in electorate at delimitation, no runoff, boundaries frozen for twenty years.
- **Option B:** closed-list proportional representation in ten regions of thirty seats each, no preference votes, a five percent national legal threshold, and regional seat allocations frozen for the same twenty years.

The parties are the Unity Party (centrist, largest), the Reform Coalition (centre-left), the Highland Alliance (the Ardach minority, roughly 15 percent of the population, heavily concentrated in two of the ten regions), the National Front (the legal successor to one of the belligerent movements, whose support is diffuse across the country rather than regionally concentrated), the Green Alliance (urban and diffuse), and a residual grouping the commission labels Others.

The commission's projections. Under A, assuming the coordination that plurality rules are expected to induce: Unity 39 percent of the vote and 152 seats; Reform 33 percent and 88; Highland Alliance 14 percent and 56; National Front 10 percent and 4; Greens 3 percent and 0; Others 1 percent and 0. Under B, assuming voters express first preferences: Unity 38 percent and 119 seats; Reform 27 percent and 84; Highland Alliance 14 percent and 44 (winning about 42 of them inside its two regions and none elsewhere); National Front 11 percent and 34; Greens 6 percent and 19; Others 4 percent and 0. The committee notes that a thirty-seat magnitude on its own implies an effective threshold near three percent, so it is the legal clause rather than the magnitude that excludes Others, and that dropping the clause would redistribute about twelve seats, taken mainly from Unity and Reform.

Governing rules. Confidence and ordinary legislation require 151 seats; a schedule of "vital-interest" bills reserved by the peace settlement requires 180. Unity and Reform have each pledged not to enter a government with the National Front; the Highland Alliance will join either; both Unity and Reform describe a grand coalition between themselves as a last resort only. The committee's arithmetic note records two consequences: under A, Unity's 152 seats suffice for confidence and ordinary bills but fall 28 short of the vital-interest threshold, where Reform and the Highland Alliance together (144 seats) can block; under B, Reform, the Highland Alliance and the Greens together hold only 147 seats, so no government is possible without either Unity or the National Front, while the National Front's 34 seats are needed for no majority that Unity and the Highland Alliance are willing to form (163) and cannot block the vital-interest threshold alone, with the Greens (53), or even with Reform (118, three short of the 121 required).

Demography. Over the twenty-year freeze the Ardach highlands are projected to lose 18 percent of their population while the Vale corridor around the capital gains 26 percent. Since district boundaries and regional seat allocations are alike frozen, by the end of the freeze the average highland single-member district would hold roughly a third fewer electors than the average Vale district. The committee has not agreed whether the same drift distorts results more or less severely under fixed regional magnitudes than under winner-take-all districts.

The settlement's Annex on Political Participation commits the drafters to arrangements under which "every significant community enjoys an effective voice in the national legislature and no community acquires the capacity to block ordinary legislation on its own."

**The characters.** Dr. Ines Marchetti argues for Option B; Dr. Samuel Oduya argues for Option A. Both sit on the committee. Marchetti has spent a decade advising on list systems in divided societies and is preoccupied by the fate of the 10 percent who would vote for the National Front under A, and by the disappearance of the Greens; Oduya works on legislatures in weakly institutionalized party systems and is preoccupied by what closed lists do when parties are personalistic machines, by the loss of a single petitionable representative per citizen in a country where the state has just been at war with part of its own population, and by the bargaining power that coalitional arithmetic confers. Neither is a strawman, and neither should be allowed to win on rhetorical flourish.

**Requirements.**

1. At least ten substantive turns each. Every turn must engage the immediately preceding argument rather than deliver a prepared speech.
2. At least two arguments on each side must arise only from the stipulated features of this case — the arithmetic of who can form and who can block, the interaction of the legal threshold with a magnitude of thirty, the twenty-year freeze meeting projected migration, the Annex's voice-but-not-veto clause, the successor-party question — rather than from trade-offs that would apply to any choice between plurality and proportional rules anywhere.
3. Where one character asserts an incentive effect, the other should test it against the projections rather than simply accept or dismiss it. They may challenge assumptions built into the simulation (for instance, whether plurality-induced coordination really occurs in a first post-conflict election with weak information) but may not dispute the arithmetic wholesale.
4. Each character must, at some point, propose a concrete modification to their own preferred design — a preference vote or a different threshold under B, a runoff rule or an earlier boundary review under A — and state what the modification costs them and whether it actually answers the other's objection.
5. Address explicitly whether a two-party blocking alliance on vital-interest bills satisfies the spirit of the Annex clause, and whether that question comes out differently under A and under B given where the Highland Alliance sits in each legislature.
6. No references, citations, named case studies, or reading lists. Named concepts may be used, but only where the character explains the mechanism in their own words rather than invoking it as authority.
7. Close without a verdict and without a compromise that papers over the disagreement: the two should instead converge on identifying the single question, empirical or normative, on which their remaining disagreement turns, and each should say what would settle it.

Please aim for 1,600 to 2,000 words, with speaker labels, in a register appropriate to two professionals who disagree sharply and respect each other.
