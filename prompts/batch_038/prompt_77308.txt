I'm drafting a chapter of my thesis on how to assign comparative "moral weight" across animal taxa for use in cost-effectiveness models of animal-welfare interventions, and my supervisor has asked me to write my position down as an explicit list of commitments before the department reading group tries to take it apart. I'd like a careful audit of the list rather than a general discussion of the debate — I know the literature on sentience and on animal minds well enough, and what I need is someone to trace which of these claims commits me to which others. Two useful bits of framing: I got interested in this because our group's models have to multiply a species weight by a population size, so a weight that is merely "small" can still end up dominating the calculation, and that worry is somewhere in the background of everything below. Also, I don't want a citation-heavy answer or a reading list; I want the reasoning.

Here is the package. (a) Sentience is the sole ground of moral standing: only the capacity for affective experience — for things to go well or badly for a creature — confers standing at all, and I reject any view on which a more intelligent or more autonomous being's suffering counts for more simply in virtue of the intelligence or autonomy. (b) Standing is binary but weight is scalar: once a creature is sentient it is fully inside the moral circle, but its interests can be outweighed, so I need a principled number for how much a given creature's welfare counts. (c) That number should track what I call affective bandwidth — the number of discriminably different affective states a system can occupy, multiplied by the rate at which it can move among them. My argument is that bandwidth, not cleverness, is what makes one life matter more than another: a wider and faster-moving affective repertoire means more distinct ways to suffer, more that can be felt at once, more continuity across time, and more that is lost when the creature is frustrated or deprived. (d) Bandwidth isn't measurable directly, so I operationalize it with neuron counts restricted to sensory-associative structures — pallium in mammals, the pallial equivalent in birds, mushroom bodies in insects — on the grounds that total brain mass largely measures how much body there is to control, and that non-associative tissue isn't doing the affective work. Concretely I'm working with figures like roughly 2,500 mushroom-body neurons for a fruit fly, about 170,000 for a honeybee, about 200,000 for a cockroach, around 6 million pallial neurons for a naked mole-rat and 14 million for a house mouse, against about 16 billion in the human pallium. (e) For the same reason I reject encephalization quotient as a moral measure: EQ is a body-size correction designed to isolate a cleverness residual, and cleverness isn't what matters. (f) Body size is nonetheless not morally inert — a larger body has more nociceptors and more tissue that can be damaged at once, so at equal neural architecture a bigger animal's injuries plausibly involve more simultaneous pain. (g) Weights enter my models multiplicatively and aggregate linearly: the expected moral value of a policy is the sum, over affected individuals, of weight times change in welfare. (h) Insects receive a small but strictly positive weight — I won't zero them out — and yet I think our resources should go overwhelmingly to vertebrates such as farmed fish, laying hens and pigs, and I take that prioritization to be an output of my framework rather than a prejudice smuggled into it. (i) I'm confident about three case verdicts: an octopus deserves substantially more weight than a honeybee, even though most of an octopus's neurons sit in the arm ganglia rather than in anything brain-like; an elephant deserves roughly what a human does, even though its brain holds more neurons in total than ours with the overwhelming majority of them cerebellar and only a fraction of ours pallial; and a human infant and a healthy adult cow should come out close together.

Please go through this and tell me which pairs or triples are in genuine tension, ranked by severity rather than listed as equally bad, and which merely look like tensions and dissolve once you say what each claim is actually about — I suspect some of them do, and I'd rather you separate those cleanly than flag everything. For each real tension, say which commitment has to give, what the cheapest available revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list; I'm especially interested in cases where fixing one joint breaks another. If some item isn't a tension at all but a gap — a claim my framework simply cannot deliver, so that I've misdescribed it as a conclusion when it's really an assumption — call that out separately. Finish by naming the single revision that would resolve the most damage at once. I don't know where the problem is, I haven't decided in advance what I'd sacrifice, and I'd rather not be told simply to abandon the neuron-counting project wholesale; tell me which specific connection fails.
