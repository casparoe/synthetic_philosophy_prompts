I cover science and ideas for a national paper, and I am drafting a column about the descendants of the ass that starved between two bales of hay: ordinary decisions between options that the evidence ranks as exactly equal. Before I commit to a thesis in print I want to know what my own verdicts add up to, since I suspect they add up to nothing coherent.

- A diner offered two dishes described in identical terms who orders one of them after a moment's hesitation: rational. The same diner who orders neither and goes home hungry: irrational — though I struggle to say what he did wrong beyond failing to act.
- A patient choosing between two treatments the evidence ranks as equally good: she may simply pick, and she owes nobody an apology afterwards for the arbitrariness of it.
- A judge who settles a dispute between two claimants with exactly equal claims by tossing a coin: I flinch here, and cannot say why, since the underlying indifference is the same as the patient's.
- Agonising for an hour over two identical parking bays and then driving off: irrational. Agonising for an hour because one suspects an unnoticed difference between them: entirely sensible. Yet if the bays really are equal, why should the agent's beliefs about them change what it is rational to do?
- Paying a penny to swap one equally good option for another: irrational. Swapping for nothing, even repeatedly: harmless, and I would not criticise anyone who did it. I expect these two cannot both stand.
- A friend who cuts through my dithering by saying 'take the left one': the resulting choice sits better with me than one I break the tie on myself. I recognise this as an embarrassment rather than a datum, but I have not managed to shake it.
- Someone who deliberately talks himself into preferring one of two indifferent options, so that the choice ceases to be arbitrary: I want to call this resourcefulness rather than self-deception. But a standing rule adopted in advance — always take the left-hand one — looks like the same trick, and I cannot see how a rule with no reason behind it improves on the tie it was meant to break.

Is there a principle about ties, reasons and choice that delivers all of this, or nearly all of it? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause is doing the work in each case above, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and must therefore overrule. If no single principle will do, give me the smallest set that comes closest and say exactly where its members conflict. Then tell me what the resulting account implies for two situations I have left undecided: an air-ambulance service with one helicopter and two calls it cannot rank, and a recommendation system forbidden by design from ever returning a tie. Finally, name one further case that my list has hidden from me, and give your verdict on it. I would rather be told that my intuitions are a mess than be handed a history of the problem.
