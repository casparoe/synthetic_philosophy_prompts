I serve on the research council of a national funding agency, and at next month's meeting we will vote on a change to our peer-review procedure. For the past decade, reviewers assigned to a proposal have been able to see the scores and written comments entered by reviewers who reported before them; because of staggered deadlines, this sequential arrangement applies to roughly a third of our portfolio. The recommendation before the council is to move the entire portfolio to simultaneous review: all reviews entered blind to one another, with scores released only once the full set for a proposal is complete.

The case for the change rests on the theory of information cascades — the result that a sequence of agents, each weighing private information against the revealed choices of their predecessors, can converge on an outcome that carries almost no information about the underlying quality, and can do so while every agent reasons impeccably. If that diagnosis fits our reviews, then exhorting reviewers to be independent-minded, or running workshops on scoring bias, cannot repair the problem, because the problem is not a defect in anyone's reasoning. Only the information structure can be changed.

We piloted the blind arrangement last cycle, and the result has split the council. Score dispersion in the blind arm roughly doubled relative to matched proposals reviewed sequentially, and the correlation between review score and the proposal's subsequent citation impact fell. The blind arm also produced several clear successes that the sequential process would almost certainly have filtered out, alongside a handful of conspicuous failures that a single enthusiastic reviewer had carried. One faction reads this as the cascade having been broken, with the genuine variance in quality finally showing through a measure that had been artificially smoothed. The other reads it as the pilot having dismantled a mechanism that was, on aggregate, tracking something real — and points out that we cannot have it both ways, since increased dispersion is exactly what one should expect from noisier review just as much as from more honest review.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the council in advance of the vote. It should recommend a definite course of action, together with the conditions and timeline under which it should be implemented; a recommendation to gather more data first is acceptable only if you specify what would be gathered, over how many cycles, and why the council cannot simply vote on what it has.

The opinion should also do the following:

- Distinguish informational herding, in which reviewers discount their own judgment because predecessors' scores are evidence, from conformity driven by reputational and collegial incentives — deference to senior names, reluctance to be the sole dissenter, the desire for a defensible consensus in a room. Say which of the two the proposed change actually addresses, which it leaves untouched, and whether blinding the scores makes either of them worse.
- Explain what the pilot's increased dispersion can and cannot establish, given that both readings of it are compatible with the data, and identify what further analysis or evidence would discriminate between them.
- Engage the strongest form of the opposing case rather than a straw version of it: if a cascade can converge on the right answer, then the old procedure's correlation with later impact is not obviously spurious; and there is a fairness argument that simultaneous review exposes applicants to idiosyncratic single-reviewer verdicts that discussion used to correct.
- Address the third option on the table, which several council members prefer: small standing panels that read independently but confer before entering a single score. Say whether the tendency of deliberation to manufacture consensus makes this better or worse than either of the other two arrangements, and for which subset of proposals.
- Flag explicitly each place where your recommendation depends on a contested empirical or normative assumption rather than on anything settled.

Do not include citations, references, or a bibliography; I will supply them myself. Write in American English, and keep the opinion to roughly 1,100 words, in prose suitable for circulation to non-specialists on the council.
