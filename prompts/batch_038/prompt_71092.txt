I'm in the middle of a dissertation chapter on Berkeley's economics — not the idealism, the money stuff, the Querist and the pamphlet he wrote a couple of years later backing the Irish bank scheme — and my friend Nadia, who did an econ PhD and thinks Berkeley is basically a monetary crank with good prose, walked me through an argument against him last week that I have not been able to get out of my head. It's supposed to show his own theory of money proves far too much, and that he'd have to disown most of it. Here's how she ran it:

(1) On Berkeley's account money isn't a commodity with value of its own; it's a kind of ticket or counter that records and passes on the power to command other people's labour and what that labour produces. Its worth is entirely a matter of the job it does. (2) If a thing's worth is entirely a matter of the job it does, then whatever does that job does it just as well, and it makes no difference what the tickets are made of — silver, paper, an entry in a bank's ledger. (3) The job is circulation: to keep passing hand to hand, so that whoever holds a ticket is pushed to turn it into work or goods rather than sit on it. His whole complaint about Ireland is that a want of circulating money is a want of industry, not a want of bullion. (4) More tickets moving at the same rate do that job better — more to hand over, more exchanges set going, more idle hands set to work — and there's no point at which the job is being done so well that doing more of it gets worse. (5) So a bank that issued paper with no limit at all, as long as the notes kept moving, would produce without limit exactly what money exists to produce: industry, and the wealth industry makes. (6) Therefore, on Berkeley's own premises, a country cannot have too much money; the only monetary failure is money that stops moving — hoards, chests of specie, notes stuffed under a mattress. (7) But if that followed, a national bank and a printing press would differ only in the paperwork, and no country could ever be made worse off by its notes losing their purchasing power, which nobody in the 1730s and nobody now is going to accept. (8) So either Berkeley didn't hold (1)–(4), or they don't carry (5) and (6).

Nadia says the way out is to dump (2), the indifference of the material, and that doing so collapses Berkeley into just another writer who thinks paper is fine so long as it's convertible, which makes the chapter not worth writing. I'd much rather keep (2) — that's the bit I actually find original, and it ties into his instrumentalist stuff about language not always standing for ideas — so tell me what keeping it forces me to pay. Which step do you reject, why did it look innocent going in, and what does the rejection cost him? I'm especially interested in whether he has any resources for blocking (4) that don't quietly reintroduce a quantity-and-prices story sitting awkwardly next to the pure functionalism of (1) and (2), or whether a token theory of money just can't pin down what fixes the purchasing power of a token and has to borrow that from somewhere else. And be honest about the bigger question: is this a hole in Berkeley specifically, or in any account that makes money's value out entirely out of what money does? Don't bother with references or a lit review, I've got that side covered — I want the diagnosis of the argument, step by step, plus a line or two on what I'd have to argue to save (2).
