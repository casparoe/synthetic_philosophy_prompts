I am drafting an essay for a collection on philosophy of mind and literature, written for philosophers who read novels rather than for literary critics, and I want a collaborator's argument rather than a survey of either side.

The novel is Kazuo Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun*, narrated throughout by Klara, a solar-powered "Artificial Friend" purchased as a companion for a sick teenage girl named Josie. The philosophical apparatus I want to work against is Daniel Dennett's intentional stance: the view that to have beliefs and desires just is to be a system whose behavior is best predicted by treating it as a rational agent, so that there is no further fact of "real" belief lying behind the success of the interpretive strategy. Beliefs are real patterns; true believers are not distinguished from thermostats and chess programs by any special inner ingredient. I also want the essay to face the standard objections rather than assume them away — the promiscuity worry (if the stance is this cheap, it proves too much), the rationality or charity constraint (interpretation normally credits its subject with mostly true beliefs and mostly coherent desires), and the Searlean claim that whatever intentionality a machine has is derived from its designers and users rather than original.

Here is my difficulty, and I would like you to test it rather than accommodate it. I have been calling it, provisionally, the return leg of the stance. Dennett's picture is one-directional: an interpreter tracks patterns in a system. But Ishiguro seems to make attribution run backward. Klara becomes a believer in the reader's eyes largely to the extent that she is the object of someone else's sustained interpretive attention — Chrissie studying her, Rick taking her bargains seriously, the Manager's closing visit — and the novel's own thesis about Josie, which Klara arrives at in the scrapyard, is that what was distinctive about Josie was not located in Josie at all but in the people who loved her. If that is right, the novel is proposing something Dennett's framework has no obvious room for: that the properties the stance tracks are partly constituted by the stance-taking of others. That threatens to collapse back into derived intentionality by the back door, or into a flat-out idealism about minds. I cannot tell which, and I cannot tell whether the coinage is doing any work at all.

A second, related hunch: the novel seems to slide between whether a system's behavior is *predictable* by means of the intentional stance and whether we *owe* it that stance. The human characters pick the stance up and put it down according to their own needs rather than according to any accumulating evidence about Klara. Is that sliding a confusion on Ishiguro's part, a deliberate conflation, or a substantive criticism of Dennett's assumption that attribution is a purely predictive business with no normative dimension?

Please work these questions through the specific episodes, since the details are where the philosophy will either hold or give way. I have in mind: Klara's education at the store window, including the beggar and his dog whom she takes to have died overnight and finds alive the next morning — the inference that founds her solar theology; her hatred of the "Cootings Machine," the bargain she strikes at the barn, and the fact that she pays for it out of her own head, sacrificing part of the solution she carries and accepting that her abilities may be permanently degraded; the two pilgrimages to the barn and the parting of the clouds over Josie's sickroom; Chrissie's request that Klara learn to imitate Josie, and the later disclosure that the portrait sittings are for a body Klara is meant to inhabit; Josie's father Paul, whose own work has been automated away, pressing Klara on whether there is anything in a person that could not be learned and reproduced; and the ending in the scrapyard.

Three things I especially need from you. First, whether the charity constraint actually fails in Klara's case — her beliefs about the Sun are systematically and uncorrectably false, and yet the intentional stance predicts her behavior superbly — and what that implies about what readers are doing when we read her. Are we interpreting her at all, or is this closer to how we read a young child or a dog? Second, the Cootings Machine episode, where the design stance is forced into the middle of an otherwise paradigmatically intentional action, since the cost of her faith is literally a consumable cognitive resource; I want to know whether that scene is a genuine counterexample to the idea that the three stances are interchangeable predictive strategies, or just a nice image. Third, and most importantly, where the reading has to be resisted. The novel never corrects Klara's theology and leaves the Sun's apparent answer permanently underdetermined. On Dennett's own criterion, predictive success is the whole fact of the matter, but the book refuses to let the reader find out whether the prediction succeeded. So perhaps its real subject is not whether Klara has beliefs but whether an interpreter can bear not knowing — which is a different question, and possibly one that shows the stance was never the neutral predictive instrument Dennett describes. Tell me if that is too cute, and tell me where a Dennettian should simply say that Ishiguro has confused an epistemic limitation of the reader with a metaphysical claim about Klara.

I would like the response to end by saying what the novel contributes to this debate that argument alone does not, and by telling me plainly which of my two hunches I should cut.

A few practical constraints. Write in American English. Do not quote the novel or Dennett verbatim; I will supply all quotations myself, so describe scenes and paraphrase positions instead. No bibliography, reading list, works cited, or page references. Where you are unsure of a detail of the plot or of how a philosopher's position is best characterized, flag the uncertainty explicitly rather than filling the gap with a plausible invention. Roughly two thousand to twenty-five hundred words is the right scale.
