I'm a software engineer working on ML infrastructure. I have no philosophy training, and about eighteen months ago I got pulled into a recurring argument about Goodman's grue that has stopped producing anything. I don't want a verdict on grue and I don't want another summary of the responses to it. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself: what kind of dispute this is, whether the sides are contradicting each other or answering different questions under one label, and what, if anything, would count as progress.

Here is the concrete situation that started it. My team owns a monitoring dashboard. Each record gets a status color derived from a rule, and that rule changed at a schema migration; call the migration date t. Define green the way the dashboard computed it before t, and define a second predicate, grue, meaning green if the record predates t and blue otherwise. Every record we had ever observed was green, so every observed record is also grue, and the two hypotheses about future records diverge exactly at t. This is not an analogy for the grue case that I constructed after reading about it; it is a thing that happened to us, and three of my colleagues and I each ran it in a different direction, which is where the argument wedged.

- Position A: the puzzle dissolves once you notice that not every syntactically well-formed predicate is a candidate for projection. The fix is a constraint on the hypothesis language — a whitelist of admissible predicates, or, in the more metaphysical version I only half follow, a preference for predicates that pick out real kinds rather than arbitrary disjunctions. Progress would mean getting that constraint right and showing it excludes grue without excluding anything we actually want.
- Position B: there is nothing special to constrain. This is an ordinary case of theory being underdetermined by data, and it is discharged by the prior. Two hypotheses that fit the evidence equally well need not be equally credible antecedently, and once the prior is written down the problem stops being a problem. Progress would mean defending or deriving the prior rather than assuming it.
- Position C: the whole framing is wrong because the object of evaluation is a method, not a belief. What you want is a procedure that performs well over time by some stated metric — fewest errors, fewest reversals, fastest convergence — and the correct response to grue is that adopting the grue-like hypothesis costs you extra retractions even though it fits the same data. Progress would mean proving that cost claim.

There is also a fourth reaction, from people who don't come to the reading group: that this is just concept drift, it has been handled operationally for a decade, and the philosophical version adds nothing. I have never gotten a straight answer about whether that is a position or a refusal to have one.

Where I made things worse. I ran an experiment. I encoded candidate predicates in a small DSL and measured description length, expecting to find that the grue-like predicate is objectively more complex. It isn't. Which predicate is shorter flips with the choice of primitives: if the DSL is given the migration timestamp as a primitive, grue-like predicates come out shorter than green; if the primitives are the ordinary dashboard columns, green comes out shorter. I reported this expecting it to settle something. Instead all three sides claimed it, and none of them seemed strained by it. A said it demonstrates that any complexity measure is vocabulary-relative, so the constraint has to be on the vocabulary. B said it shows description length is just a prior wearing an engineering costume. C said it is beside the point, because what matters is the error count of a method over time and not the size of a program that expresses a hypothesis. I also raised the obvious objection to A — that grue is only disjunctive because we happen to start from green — and was told Goodman anticipated it with a symmetric definition of green in terms of grue and bleen, which reproduces precisely the flip I measured and which I have never seen anyone in our group treat as a problem for their own view.

So: eighteen months, three positions, one experiment that everybody absorbed, nobody moved. From what I can tell the same thing has been happening in the literature for seventy years.

My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than accept, is that these are not three answers to one question. A is asking which predicates are eligible for projection, which sounds like a semantic or metaphysical question about the language and the world. B is asking how an agent's degrees of belief should be arranged before the data arrive, which is a normative question about priors. C is asking which procedure wins under a stated reliability metric, which is an engineering question about methods. On that reading, all three are compatible with every formal fact about the case — including my description-length result, which is exactly why each side could claim it — and the debate cannot be resolved by argument, because no argument in the vicinity discriminates between the three questions. If that is right, grue is not a hard problem that people have failed to solve; it is a name shared by three problems that have different standards of success.

Tell me whether that is right, partly right, or wrong. In particular:

1. State each position's argument as its holders actually make it, in the strongest form you can, and for each say what it is trying to establish and what would defeat it. If two of these positions turn out to be the same position described in different vocabularies, say so explicitly.
2. Locate the fault lines. Which pairs genuinely contradict one another, and which merely talk past one another? Is there any claim on which, say, A and B disagree in a way that an argument or a formal result could settle?
3. Address my description-length experiment head-on. Is there a hidden empirical or formal question that it bears on, or is it genuinely neutral among the three positions, as each of them claimed? If it is neutral, describe what would not be neutral — what kind of finding, if any, would force one of the sides to give something up.
4. Deal with the fourth reaction. Is the concept-drift dismissal a substantive position that competes with the other three, or is it a claim that the question is ill-posed? If the latter, is it right?
5. Given your diagnosis, say what progress would look like: a distinction, a theorem, a reformulation, a change of subject. If your honest view is that none should be expected, argue for that, and explain why the debate nevertheless persists — what work it is doing for the people who keep having it.

Two constraints on the answer. I cannot look anything up, so please give me no bibliography, no citations, and no attributions of specific wordings to specific authors; argue from the content of the views, and where you are unsure how a given philosopher phrased something, describe the view functionally instead. And keep it analytical rather than encyclopedic. I have read enough introductions to grue; what I am missing is an account of why the argument I keep having does not terminate.
