I should explain my situation before stating my request. I retired four years ago after a career in Quaternary geology, most of it spent on tephra sequences and lake-sediment records in the eastern Mediterranean, and I have since spent two winters auditing courses in the philosophy of history at our regional university. I am now applying for a one-year fellowship at a humanities institute that funds independent scholars, and the call asks for a two-page proposal. I would like you to write it with me. Please take on the role of a philosopher of history and of the special sciences who is equally at home in the metaphysics of causation and in how historical argument actually proceeds, rather than that of a well-read generalist surveying a debate; the committee reads a great many such surveys.

Here is my half-formed idea. There is a large and, to my mind, thoroughly stuck argument about whether non-human entities — an eruption, a multi-decadal drought, a pathogen — may properly be called historical actors, and about whether invoking them amounts to a disreputable environmental determinism. My suspicion, formed partly from having produced some of the data in question myself, is that three quite different questions are being argued at once, which is why neither side can land a blow on the other. First, a metaphysical question about causal selection: what promotes one among many genuine contributing conditions into 'the' cause of an event, and whether any principled answer is available that does not simply privilege whatever humans can be held responsible for. Second, an epistemological question that seems to me nearly absent from the philosophical discussion: a claim about past climate or past disease rests not on observation of the event but on a chain of proxies — sulphate in an ice core, tree-ring widths, pollen percentages, ancient DNA, dated inscriptions, papyrological series — and each link carries its own calibration assumptions, reservoir effects, chronological uncertainties and modelling choices, so that the strength of the conclusion depends, in a way nobody has articulated, on how many and how independent those assumptions are. Third, a normative and political question about what narrating a famine or a collapse as the work of a volcano does to the distribution of responsibility, and whether the worry here is really about determinism or about something else entirely. I want the proposal built around the second question, because that is where I have something to contribute, and I want it to use the second to reopen the first: specifically, to test the hypothesis that a good deal of the resistance to letting climate and disease into the foreground of historical explanation is in fact a resistance to weak evidential chains that has been misdescribed, by both its proponents and its critics, as a metaphysical objection.

Please turn this into the two-page proposal itself, containing: a precise statement of the research question, phrased so that it has a determinate answer and so that a reader could tell whether I had failed to produce one; an account of why the existing positions leave that question open rather than merely disagreeing about it; the method, including what I would actually do month by month over twelve months and what I would count as a result; an honest assessment of scope, saying what would have to be cut to fit the year; and a short section distinguishing failure from mere inconclusiveness. On method, I have candidate cases in mind — the eruption of Tambora and the harvest failures of 1816, the proposed climatic contributions to the collapse of the eastern Mediterranean palatial systems at the end of the Bronze Age, the seventeenth-century cooling and the general crisis debate, and the recent revisionist argument that the demographic weight of the sixth-century Mediterranean pandemic has been considerably over-read — but I would rather you argued with me about this selection than accept it. Tell me which of these genuinely stress the framework in different ways, which are redundant, and what I should substitute, bearing in mind that I need at least one case where the proxy chain is short and one where it is long and contested.

A few practical instructions. Think carefully before drafting, and begin with a half-page of brainstorming, clearly marked, which I will delete before submitting: consider at least three ways of framing the central question, say what turns on each, and explain why you settled where you did. Include no bibliography, footnote apparatus, or citations of any kind; the institute permits references to be added later and I will supply them myself, so refer to positions and arguments by description rather than by author and title. Define each technical term at first use, since two of the five readers have never encountered an INUS condition and one has never encountered a reservoir effect. And be blunt with me about the weaknesses of the idea: if my threefold distinction turns out to be a well-worn objection wearing new clothes, or if the project risks degenerating into a plea for better data rather than a philosophical argument, say so in the brainstorming section, and then write the proposal anyway with whatever can be repaired.
