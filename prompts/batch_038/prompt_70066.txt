I'm putting together a paper for a phil-of-med reading group and I'd love comments on a rough outline — mostly on whether the central move actually works or whether I'm cheating.

Setup: I think the naturalism/normativism debate about disease is stuck because both sides assume the word picks out one thing with one extension, and then fight about which facts fill it. The naturalist says disease is subnormal part-functioning relative to a species-typical design (Boorse-style, and Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction version is the hybrid attempt). The normativist says that can't be the whole story, because the same functional facts get labeled differently across times and places, and the labeling tracks what a community wants to treat, excuse, or insure. Both sides keep lobbing counterexamples: baldness, myopia, menopause, left-handedness, being short; on the other side drapetomania, the declassification of homosexuality, grief versus depression.

My move: treat disease as context-sensitive the way tall is sensitive to a comparison class, or the way weed is sensitive to what's being cultivated. A weed's competitive properties are entirely natural and mind-independent — but whether it counts as a weed depends on the garden. Same for a condition: all the functional facts are natural, and whether they amount to disease depends on a helping-context parameter, roughly what the medical institution around this patient is trying to do. That's supposed to dissolve the dispute without giving up naturalism about the biology underneath.

The part I'm least sure of: I claim the view can still condemn drapetomania as flatly wrong, not just differently indexed, because the helping-context is constrained by whether the classification tracks suffering and impairment that medicine can actually address. A context that licenses calling the desire to escape slavery a pathology is a bad context, not a rival one.

So, four questions. (1) Is the weed/tall analogy doing real work, or is it just relabeling the problem — does saying the extension is context-relative explain anything the two camps actually disagree about? (2) Does my bad-context-not-different-context line smuggle normativist values back in through the back door, so that I've collapsed into normativism with extra steps? (3) Am I missing an obvious objection — e.g. that context-sensitivity can't accommodate the fact that clinicians claim authority to tell patients they're wrong about being sick, or that insurance and sick-leave eligibility need a context that's fixed precisely so it can't be shifted locally? (4) Is any of this interesting, or does it just sound like a synthesis?

Please be straight with me where I'm wrong rather than encouraging, and seperate out what's genuinely settled in this area from what's live and contested among people who work on it — I don't want to walk into the reading group presenting one side of an open debate as background agreement.
