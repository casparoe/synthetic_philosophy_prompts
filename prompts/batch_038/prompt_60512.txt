**The question as set to the candidates**

Pain asymbolia is a rare syndrome. On being subjected to a noxious stimulus, the patient says that she feels it, identifies where it is, and can grade how intense it is; but she shows none of the ordinary response. She does not withdraw the limb, does not wince, does not ask for the stimulus to be stopped, sometimes smiles or laughs, and does not subsequently avoid the situations that produced it. Note that the standard clinical definition of pain treats unpleasantness as part of what pain is, rather than as a contingent consequence of it.

Some philosophers have taken the syndrome to show that a subject can feel pain without being in pain: the experience retains its sensory character while losing whatever it is that makes pain bad for its subject. Others hold that a less radical explanation is available — that the patients register and describe the noxious stimulus normally but fail to recognise or appraise what they are having as pain, and that the absence of suffering follows from that failure rather than from the loss of any component of the experience.

(a) Set out both explanations, and explain how they differ in what they imply about whether pain is necessarily unpleasant.

(b) Identify evidence, over and above the patients' verbal reports of intensity and location, that would help to decide between the two explanations, and say what each explanation predicts about it.

(c) Does either explanation, if it were correct, show that the badness of pain lies in something other than its felt character? Argue for your answer.

**The script**

(a) The syndrome matters because it appears to split pain in two. On the first explanation the patient really does have a pain: the sensory dimension is intact, so she knows that something is happening in her left hand and how intense it is, but the part of the experience that makes it bad has gone missing. Pain on this picture is a compound, and unpleasantness is one separable ingredient among others, so a subject can feel pain without being in pain and the claim that pain is necessarily unpleasant is false. On the second explanation nothing has been subtracted from the experience. She registers the stimulus and can describe it, but she does not categorise what she is having as pain, and it is this failure of recognition rather than the absence of a component that accounts for the missing suffering. Here pain remains essentially unpleasant: the asymbolic patient is not a case of pain without unpleasantness but a case in which the unpleasant state has not been properly identified by the subject, so that the whole complex — pain together with its badness — is not present to her at all.

Both explanations agree about the outward facts: she does not withdraw, does not ask for the stimulus to stop, and does not learn to avoid what produced it. They disagree about whether those facts are explained by a loss inside the experience or by a failure in what the subject does with an experience she still has in full.

(b) Verbal reports of intensity and location cannot decide the matter, since both explanations allow that the patient can give them. The useful evidence would be behavioural and physiological: whether she protects the affected limb afterwards, whether she shows the autonomic responses that ordinarily accompany a noxious stimulus, and whether she can be trained to avoid the place where she was stimulated. If only the sensory component is present and the unpleasantness is missing, these measures should be flat; if the experience is complete and merely unrecognised, at least some of them should register, since the badness is still in the experience even though the subject has not labelled it.

A more decisive test is available from the reports themselves. The recognition account predicts that a patient who does not take herself to be in pain will be unable to say how strong the pain is or where it is, since on that account she has no pain to describe. Asymbolic patients say both, reliably and without prompting. The recognition account therefore cannot be maintained, and the first explanation is the correct one.

(c) Suppose that it is. The conclusion is not merely a negative one about the concept of pain. If an experience can have all the sensory character of a pain and be entirely neutral to its subject, then what makes pain bad cannot be the way it feels, since here is something that feels that way and is not bad. Badness must instead attach to what the state signals — the threat to the body — and the patient's indifference simply shows that this signal has been cut off from her assessment of her own situation. Congenital insensitivity to pain supports the same conclusion from the other direction: those patients are born without any experience of pain at all, and although they injure themselves badly and die young, we would not say that they are suffering anything. Taken together, the two syndromes show that unpleasantness is a detachable property of a sensation and that the badness of pain lies in its functional role rather than in its phenomenology. Since the patient who smiles when pricked is not merely unmoved but mildly pleased, the sensation can even carry the opposite valence while remaining the same sensation.

This has consequences beyond the philosophy of mind. If the badness of pain is a matter of what it does rather than of how it feels, then hedonism about well-being — the view that a life goes well in virtue of the balance of pleasant and unpleasant experience — is false, because we have here experiences that make no difference to well-being in either direction despite being exactly the sort of thing that hedonism says matters.
