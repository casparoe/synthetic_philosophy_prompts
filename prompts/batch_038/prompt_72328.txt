I want to build a real thought experiment to test one specific piece of it — the subject combination problem, i.e. how a bunch of tiny subjects are supposed to add up to one big subject like me — and I want it engineered so nobody can weasel out of it.

Here's the design I want. Give me ONE physical system — something concrete, like a distributed nervous system (octopus arms, a slime mold, two hemispheres hooked up to an implant, whatever you like) — and then describe it twice, changing exactly one thing:

- Version A: the parts are coupled as tightly as physics allows. Perfect information sharing, synchronized dynamics, the whole thing behaves as one functional unit that a physicalist would happily call one mind. But nothing in your description gives any reason to think there's a *phenomenal* bond on top of all that causal glue.
- Version B: same system, but the parts are barely causally coupled at all — and yet somthing in the description makes the bonding view look genuinely attractive here.

What the case is for, so you know what counts as cheating: I want to find out whether "fundamental phenomenal bonding relations" is doing any real explanatory work, or whether its just a label we slap on whatever causal integration we already had. So version B is not allowed to smuggle the bond in — if the only thing making B attractive is that you wrote "and here they're bonded," the case fails. There has to be a feature you can point at that isn't causal coupling.

Constraints:
- each version is one paragraph, under about 120 words
- no zombies, and no "and then it just seems like no one is home" — whether there's a single subject has to fall out of the description, not get asserted
- the qualities involved should be things a room full of 16 year olds can picture (a color, a chord, a stab of pain), not exotic stuff
- no first-person reports from the macro level unless you tell me who exactly is doing the reporting

1. what a constitutive panpsychist who believes in bonding relations says about A and about B
