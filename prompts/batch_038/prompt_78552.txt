Stuck on a passage in Maimonides, Guide for the Perplexed, Book III ch. 32 (Friedländer's translation) — the one where he explains why the Torah kept animal sacrifice at all. The bit I'm working on starts with nature:

"On considering the Divine acts, or the processes of Nature, we get an insight into the prudence and wisdom of God as displayed in the creation of animals, with the gradual development of the movements of their limbs ... When such an animal is born it is extremely tender, and cannot be fed with dry food. Therefore breasts were provided which yield milk, and the young can be fed with moist food which corresponds to the condition of the limbs of the animal, until the latter have gradually become dry and hard."

then moves to the Law:

"It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed. ... For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner ... By this Divine plan it was effected that the traces of idolatry were blotted out, and the truly great principle of our faith, the Existence and Unity of God, was firmly established; this result was thus obtained without deterring or confusing the minds of the people by the abolition of the service to which they were accustomed and which alone was familiar to them."

He also puts the obvious objection in the reader's mouth — "How can we suppose that Divine commandments, prohibitions, and important acts ... should not have been commanded for their own sake, but only for the sake of some other thing: as if they were only the means which He employed for His primary object?" — and then drops this:

"it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action."

A mate of mine who writes software calls the whole thing a compatibility shim: same interface (temple, altar, priests, incense, blood), new backend, old backend deprecated — and shims get removed eventually. I'm not convinced the passage licenses that tho, and I'd rather read the text than my own theory into it. Can you help me with:

1. the actual structure of the arguement. Is the nerve-hardening and milk material doing real work — i.e. is it evidence that God legislates the way nature develops — or is it just decoration that makes the concession look dignified rather than arbitrary? And does the analogy risk proving too much, since nature's stages get outgrown?
2. "allowed these kinds of service to continue" versus "commanded us to serve Him in the same manner". Is there a live difference between tolerating a form and positively commanding it, and does he anywhere here claim the sacrifices are good in themselves rather than instrumentally?
3. that counterfactual prophet sentence. Does it swallow prayer and fasting too, putting them on the same spectrum as sacrifice with pure thought as the real ideal? Or is it only a point about how shocking change feels to people?
4. who the accommodation is addressed to — the generation in the wilderness, or every later generation that inherits the cult without ever having lived inside a sacrificial culture? If it's the latter, in what sense is it still a concession?
5. and bluntly: is there anything in the passage suggesting the arrangement is temporary, or does the shim stay in forever?

Where you think his framing or mine is carrying weight the text won't bear, just say so. Answer in British English, please.
