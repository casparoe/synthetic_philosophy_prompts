Origin essentialism — the claim that no person could have come into existence from a different gamete pair than the one they in fact came from — is typically supported by an intuition about transworld identity and typically attacked from the direction of vagueness: there is a continuum of nearby possibilities in which the originating gametes differ by ever-smaller amounts, and the thesis appears to require a sharp boundary somewhere along that continuum.

My credence in it is about 0.35, driven almost entirely by the vagueness worry, and I want to know whether that is a defensible place to sit or a failure to weigh something. Give me your number, but above all give me the derivation. Decompose the question into sub-claims — for instance: whether a sharp but epistemically inaccessible boundary along the continuum is acceptable or whether it disqualifies the thesis; whether the essential origin is the gamete pair, the zygote, or the nuclear genome, and whether the choice among them is substantive or verbal; whether facts of transworld identity need a criterion at all, or whether a counterpart-theoretic paraphrase of the claim dissolves rather than answers the objection; whether the intuition tracks a genuine metaphysical constraint or is an artifact of a biological-lineage concept of personhood that we have no reason to think is joint-carving — plus whatever other terms you think belong in the product. Assign each a rough probability with a sentence of justification, show how they combine, and identify which single term your total is most sensitive to and what argument would move it.

Fold in one complication that I think the standard statement of the debate does not handle. In mice, functional oocytes have already been produced in vitro from reprogrammed somatic cells — including from a male animal — and have given rise to live offspring after fertilization. If that technology were used in humans, a child's origin would lie in what had been, weeks earlier, an ordinary skin cell of a person who is not her genetic father in any ordinary sense. Tell me whether the essentialist claim survives this case: if it does, explain why deriving a gamete from a somatic cell leaves the origin unchanged; if it does not, say whether the claim fails only in such engineered cases or was never secure to begin with.

Finally, if you think the proposition as I have framed it is not the sort of thing that can have a credence — that "could have come from a different gamete" is too indeterminate for the number to mean anything, or that my decomposition smuggles in the answer I want — tell me that plainly rather than producing figures that look like an argument.
