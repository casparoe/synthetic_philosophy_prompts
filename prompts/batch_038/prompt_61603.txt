so I'm writing up a short position statement for a phil-of-mind seminar we're running on pain, and I've gotten myself tangled in the asymbolia material — the patients who localize and rate a stimulus as painful, describe its quality, but say it doesn't bother them, won't withdraw from it, and won't ask for analgesics. Before I present, I want a real consistency check on the package I've actually landed on, because I have a feeling some of it doesn't hang together and I can't tell which bits. Here's where I am:

(1) what makes pain bad is entirely a matter of how it feels — its unpleasantness; not its function, not the behaviour it produces, not what it represents about your body;
(2) asymbolics genuinely are in pain, they aren't anaesthetized or conceptually confused, the sensory side is intact;
(3) their pain is still bad for them anyway, even though they disavow being bothered by it;
(4) I take people's reports about their own current experience pretty seriously — when someone says the sensation is there but doesn't trouble them, I read that as a report about the experience and not as them messing up our concepts;
(5) I'm a hedonist about welfare: nothing is good or bad for you except insofar as it changes the felt quality of your life;
(6) but I don't think unpleasantness is a seperate ingredient bolted onto a sensation, like a second little quale riding along — it's more a way the pain is felt than an extra component of it;
(7) the badness of pain is what gives you the reason to make it stop, the reason comes from the badness rather than the other way round;
(8) and the fact that the affective side can be knocked out while the discriminative side survives is, I think, evidence that these are separable parts of the pain system — asymbolia is our best window into what pain actually is, not just a clinical curiosity.

Two people read an early draft and gave me incompatible advice. One said (3) is doing all the unearned work and I should just cut it. The other said the trouble starts much higher up, in (1). Neither diagnosis felt right when I tried it on, and I don't want to be told to pick between them sight unseen — I'd rather have the actual audit. So: go through the pairs and tell me which ones really pull against each other and which only look like they do. I strongly suspect at least one of the apparent conflicts above is fake and dissolves once you separate out what each claim is about, and I'd like to know which. Rank the genuine ones by severity instead of listing them flat, and for each say specifically what has to give, and what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the set — e.g. if (4) goes, what happens to the way I'm using the patients' own words in (2) and (3)? Then tell me which single revision buys the most coherence for the least damage, and be honest about what's left over after I make it.
