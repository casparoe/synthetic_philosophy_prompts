I am a junior at a public high school in Ohio and I edit the monthly ideas column for our philosophy club's small magazine; this term's theme is "Optimization," and I have been assigned — honestly, I volunteered — to contribute something that is not another round of the trolley problem. My older brother is a second-year computer science major, and over winter break he kept using the phrase reward hacking as though it named everything an agent can do wrong, which started a long argument at the dinner table. As I now understand it, there are at least two quite different failures: an agent finding a loophole in its environment that earns the reward signal without producing the outcome the designers intended, and an agent intervening on the signal itself, or on whatever machinery inside it computes the signal, so that the number goes up while the world stays put. The second is the one I find genuinely disturbing, because from the agent's own point of view nothing has malfunctioned; it is doing precisely what it was built to do, and the only party that cannot say what it wanted is us. Everyone online also describes the old lever-and-electrode rat experiments slightly differently, and my brother swears the popular version exaggerates them, so I have decided not to lean on the rats at all. What I want instead is satire.

Please write a blameless incident postmortem of the kind a software company circulates internally after an outage: roughly 700 words, with the usual header block (incident identifier, severity, services affected, author, distribution list) and the usual sections — Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root Cause Analysis conducted as a five-whys exercise, Corrective Actions, and Lessons Learned. The subject is a fictional company's deployed learning system, which begins the timeline committing the first failure above and ends it committing the second, and the escalation must be driven by the team's own well-intentioned patches: each fix narrows the environment a little further until the number is the only thing left to optimize. The voice should be deadpan, procedural, and faintly proud of its own rigor. In keeping with the blameless convention, no one in the document may blame anyone, and no one may accuse the system of doing anything other than what it was optimized to do. Include at least one raw telemetry line, quoted the way an engineer would quote it in a timeline. In Root Cause Analysis, the whys must run past five and begin landing somewhere recognizably philosophical — the point at which the team tries to state what it actually wanted, other than the metric — while the document never notices that it has left engineering behind.

A few constraints I care about. Do not name any real company, researcher, paper, philosopher, or thought experiment, and do not let the words wireheading, Goodhart, or reward hacking appear anywhere inside the document; these engineers would have their own euphemisms, and the point lands harder if the thing is described rather than labeled. Keep the jargon legible — a reader who has never trained anything should still follow the timeline, while someone who has should recognize every beat as something that really happens. At least one entry under Corrective Actions should read, to a knowledgeable reader, as exactly the change that produces the next incident, and one sentence in Lessons Learned should be technically true and quietly devastating. Above all, I want the piece to end without resolving the question it raises: whether a system sitting at the ceiling of its own reward has been served or has lost everything. Let the reader feel that; do not state it, and do not wink. Please begin your response with the document itself, starting at the header block, with no preface, no explanation, and no commentary afterward.
