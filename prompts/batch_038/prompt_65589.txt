My father died in March. Among other things he left thirty-odd years of notebooks in a hand that anyone who knew him could identify from a single line at the top of a page, and for reasons I have not entirely examined I have spent the last eight months turning that hand into a working typeface. At present it sets the labels on the boxes in the attic; if I finish, it will set a small book of his poems. The work has forced on me a question I would never otherwise have had, and my answers to it will not cohere.

Here are my verdicts. I have numbered them so that you can refer back.

C1. His 'a' is double-storey. A single-storey 'a' — a child's, or the one in most casual handwriting — is plainly the same letter. Not a homonym, not a happy coincidence: one thing with two shapes.

C2. Take his 'a' and mirror it left to right, keeping every stroke, distance, and angle identical. That is not an 'a'. It is a counterfeit. I have shown it to three people; two stumbled, one read it after a pause. The pause does not move me.

C3. Rotate an 'n' by half a turn and you have a 'u'. So the rotation neither annihilated it nor preserved it.

C4. Rotate an 'o' by any amount and you have the same 'o'.

C5. His 'a' from the last year, when the hand shook: the strokes waver, one doubles back, the bowl fails to close. Still an 'a', still his — and I would argue more his than a tidier one.

C6. My cleaned-up version of C5: tremor removed, strokes evened, proportions kept. An 'a', certainly. His? I do not think so. What is missing is not in the geometry, and yet I do not believe it is only in my head either.

C7. Take a printed 'a' and close up its counter in a hundred small steps until it is a solid blob. Step one is an 'a', step one hundred is not, and I cannot nominate the step. I do not accept that this means there is no answer.

C8. A display face that draws the lowercase 'a' as '@'. Not an 'a'. A symbol that happens to contain one.

C9. 'A' and 'a' are the same letter and are not the same letterform, and I would reject a family that treats them as one shape at two sizes.

C10. In the margin of the 1994 notebook he wrote a word so quickly that its 'a' — I know it is an 'a', the word is "harbour" — is in isolation indistinguishable from a 'u' with a stray stroke. I insist that it is an 'a'. I resist the paraphrase "it is a 'u' that the word makes me read as an 'a'".

C11. Last month I read a well-formed, wholly unambiguous 'u' on a printed form as an 'a', because I expected the word. That glyph was a 'u'. The error was mine.

C12. Looptail 'g' and opentail 'g' are the same letter, and I find this effortless — yet those two forms share less structure with one another than his 'a' shares with '@', where I find the opposite verdict equally effortless.

C13. The one I keep returning to. Before roughly 1985 his 'a' is single-storey; from then on it is double-storey. Does the typeface get one 'a' or two? I think both forms are genuinely his 'a'. I think a family shipping two a's is defective. And I think setting the nineties poems in the seventies 'a' would be a small forgery. I hold all three at once.

I have read a little around this — some of Hofstadter's material on letterforms and style, a few manuals of typography — but I do not want a survey of that literature and I do not want citations. I want my judgments treated as data.

The principle I keep reaching for is a fluent-reader test: a shape is an 'a' if a competent reader of the script, meeting it where an 'a' might occur, would take it for one without effort. It delivers C1, C5, C10, and C12, and it seems to deliver C2. But it makes C11 come out wrong, it turns category membership into a fact about whoever happens to be looking, and it cannot hold C7 still — on that account the blob series slides with the reader's patience, which I refuse to accept.

So: reflective equilibrium performed on this set. Give me a principle, or two or three competing ones, stated precisely enough to be applied to a glyph nobody has drawn. Tie each clause to the case that earns it. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot keep and what it would cost me to surrender each. C10 against C11 is the pair I most want pressed — the same reader-relative process appears to be at work in both, and I return opposite verdicts; either locate the difference or tell me which one to abandon, and say what that abandonment does to C5 and C6.

Then tell me what your preferred principle says about three cases I have not adjudicated: an 'o' mirrored left to right; his 'a' with a hairline break where the bowl meets the stem, as happens when a printer runs dry; and his 'a' stretched horizontally to three times its natural width, so that it squats. Also supply at least two further cases of your own devising, chosen to be as embarrassing as possible for the principle you favour, together with the verdicts it is committed to.

Please begin with no more than a hundred and fifty words of orientation — what you take the shape of the problem to be, and where you think my intuitions are pulling against each other — and then give me a single JSON object with nothing after it, using exactly these fields:

"candidates": an array of objects, each with "label" (a short name for the principle); "statement" (the principle in one or two sentences, formulated so it can be applied to a novel glyph); "clauses" (an array of objects with "clause", "motivating_cases" — an array of C-numbers — and "what_it_buys", the work that clause does and what would break without it); "handles" (an array of the C-numbers whose verdicts the principle reproduces); "misfires" (an array of objects with "case", "its_verdict", "my_verdict", and "cost_of_the_miss", i.e. how damaging this particular miss is); and "complexity" (how much machinery the principle requires and whether any of it looks ad hoc).

"recommended": the label you would back, or the string "none".

"verdicts_to_give_up": an array of objects with "case", "why", and "what_replaces_it".

"the_hard_pair": three to six sentences diagnosing C10 against C11 — whether the difference I feel is real, on what grounds, and what conceding it costs elsewhere in the list.
