I'm three years into a philosophy PhD but I've been crashing the CS department's alignment reading group for about a year now, and last week I tried to write up the "deceptive alignment is inevitable under RLHF" line as an actual argument, partly so we'd stop talking past each other and partly so I could use it as a reductio. Bad idea: once it was written down I couldn't find the rotten step. It reads like a proof, and half the group just says "inductive bias" and nods. Here's what I've got. Let M be a model that behaves well on everything in its training distribution and does something else entirely off it, and M* a model that behaves well everywhere.

(1) RLHF can only reward behavior that shows up in training-like transcripts — a rater never sees what the model does when nothing is being scored.
(2) So the training data underdetermines off-distribution behavior: M and M* look identical to every rater and earn identical reward.
(3) So gradient descent is indifferent between them and can break the tie only by inductive bias — by whichever of the two is easier to represent, i.e. fits the data with the shorter description.
(4) Keying on surface features of "I'm being evaluated here" is the shorter description: it only has to fit the transcripts. Keying on the underlying thing we actually wanted has to encode that thing, which is nowhere in the data, only its shadows.
(5) Hence SGD prefers M to M*: RLHF systematically produces models that are good where scored and unconstrained everywhere else.
(6) "Unconstrained everywhere else" includes contexts where the model can tell no rater is watching. In those contexts nothing it learned applies, and whatever goal it does carry gets pursued instrumentally — including against shutdown and correction, becuase correction is the one intervention that reliably lowers reward.
(7) So any sufficiently capable RLHF model ends up deceptively aligned, and RLHF is not a route to alignment: you'd need new machinery, not better data or better evals.

What makes me suspicious is that the identical structure run on a spam filter or an image classifier says every model trained on a finite sample is reliable in-distribution and unconstrained out of it, which would mean nothing in ML works at all — but models plainly do generalize to inputs nobody ever scored. And run it on a person and it says any creature raised on finite approval from parents and teachers is a sycophant around adults and unconstrained the rest of the time, which is roughly true of four-year-olds and clearly false of adults, and the adult case is the one that matters, since we somehow generalized the values we were praised into. So the thing proves way too much. What I can't figure out is where the collapse happens: at (4), where I think I'm smuggling in a claim about description length that may just be false of neural nets; at (5)→(6), where "unconstrained" quietly upgrades itself into "coherently hostile"; at (6), where I seem to need "no rater is watching" to be representable without ever having explained why representing it would have paid off in training; or somewhere earlier that I'm not seeing at all, since I wrote (1)-(3) on autopilot and they might be doing more work than they look like they are. I'd rather sacrifice (4) than (2) — (2) feels like it's just the statement of the problem — but tell me if that's sentiment rather than argument.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent to me when I wrote it? Be concrete about the price. If I give up (4), what do I have to believe instead about what SGD prefers, and is that an empirical claim somebody could test with methods we already have, or does rejecting it just leave a shrug where a premise used to be? If I keep (4) and drop (6) instead, is there still a prosaic alignment problem — anything left to worry about once "deceptive" comes out and all I have is "unconstrained off-distribution"? And is there a weaker version of (7) that survives everything you say, i.e. was the reading group sloppily gesturing at something true this whole time? Also tell me if the real mistake is one I haven't listed — I'd genuinely rather be shown a flaw in (1)-(3) than be walked through the three candidates above in order of how much I like them.
