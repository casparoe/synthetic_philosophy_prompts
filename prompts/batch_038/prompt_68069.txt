His section runs roughly as follows; I have retyped it rather than transcribing the manuscript, but the argumentative content is his:

"Take a three-judge court that has to decide whether a defendant is liable for breach of contract. The doctrine says the defendant is liable just in case the contract was valid and the defendant breached it. Judge 1 holds the contract was valid but not breached. Judge 2 holds it was breached but not valid. Judge 3 holds both. Now ask the judges about the outcome and the defendant walks free, two to one; ask them about the two premises instead, take a majority on each, and the court finds the contract valid, finds a breach, and holds the defendant liable. Same judges, same opinions, opposite verdict — the only thing that changed was the order in which the questions were put. This is the discursive dilemma, and it is Arrow's impossibility theorem turning up in a courtroom: that result proved decades ago that no voting system can faithfully aggregate individual preferences, and what these judges have run into is that proof in action. Notice what it means. In the second case the court ends up committed to a flat contradiction that not one of its members believes, which is simply irrational. The remedy is obvious in principle: always vote on the premises, because the premises are the reasons, and a group that ignores its own reasons is not reasoning at all. Once this is visible, the fashion for multi-question referendums looks like a fraud, since whatever combination of answers wins, nobody actually voted for it."
