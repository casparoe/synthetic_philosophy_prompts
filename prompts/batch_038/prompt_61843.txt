Quick situation, and I'd rather get pushback than agreement here.

I work at a small nonprofit — four of us, no academics — that does oversight work on the automated systems welfare and disability agencies use to make eligibility decisions. The stuff that scores applicants, flags inconsistencies, queues people for re-verification, sets review priority. We've been leaning on Foucault for a couple of years now, mostly Discipline and Punish, because the vocabulary feels like it fits: surveillance, normalization, the docile body. But we're about to commit roughly one person-year — basically me plus some of a junior analyst's time — to turning that vocabulary into something we can actually put in a report or say out loud to a legislator, and I've started worrying we grabbed the wrong end of the stick.

Two things make me doubt it. First, our systems don't train anybody. No one is being shaped into a docile body through months of timetabled routine; a claimant gets classified, scored, and sorted. What actually harms people is a decision made under scarcity about which category you belong to, and I'm not sure discipline is the right concept for that at all — it may be closer to what Foucault was on about later with governmentality and security, populations and distributions, letting a phenomenon run its course within tolerable limits instead of imposing a norm on individual bodies. Second, within the disciplinary material the idea that keeps jumping out at me isn't the panopticon or visibility, it's the examination — the ritual that turns a person into a documented case in a file, comparable to others, and thereby manageable. That is a suspiciously good description of a case note system. But I honestly can't tell whether I've found something real or whether I just like the less famous idea because it feels like a discovery.

So: if you were allocating that year, what goes into it, in what order, and what gets cut? I want the trade made explicit — is it worth three months settling whether the security/governmentality frame is the right one before we write a word, or is that a graduate-seminar detour that produces a footnote and changes nothing about what we recommend? Say what you're ranking by (which concepts are load-bearing for a concrete oversight recommendation and which are just atmosphere, what's tractable for people who aren't academics, what's neglected versus what everybody already says) and where those criteria pull against each other. And be blunt about which uses of Foucault in this kind of work are basically decoration, including ours. I'd much rather hear that now than in a review somewhere.

No reading list please, we're not going to get through the secondary literature and it wouldn't change the decision. And write it as continuous prose, like an email — no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, I want to read it straight through once.

Last thing: tell me what would change your mind about the ordering. Which consideration, if I'm right about it, reshuffles the whole thing — and where do you think your own reasoning here is most likely to be off? My guess is that you'll be assuming our audience cares about conceptual precision when what they mostly care about is who got denied and why, so if that's an assumption you're making, flag it rather than papering over it.
