Our departmental mathematics magazine runs a proofs column whose stated aim is that a strong third-year undergraduate can check every published argument line by line. To that end the editorial board has adopted two standing rules: nothing in the column may depend on the full Axiom of Choice, though countable choice and dependent choice are permitted; and every appeal to a choice principle must be announced explicitly, rather than absorbed into a passing remark about a standard maximality argument. I am the column's editor. Two graduate assistants and I referee the submissions — roughly eight a month turn on a maximal-element, a chain-closure, or a transfinite construction of some kind — and none of us is a set theorist. We can give any one submission about half an hour. The single faculty set theorist who reads our escalations replies on a monthly cycle, so escalation is costly and must be reserved for questions we genuinely cannot settle.

What we lack is a procedure. Please write one that my assistants can run on a submission of this kind. I would like it to contain:

- A fast triage at the top: three or four questions, in order, that dispose of the majority of submissions inside ten minutes, together with the answers that clear a paper and the answers that send it to the full check.
- The full check itself, as an ordered list of questions, each with an explicit statement of what counts as passing. At minimum it should cover whether the family under discussion is genuinely partially ordered by the relation the author names; whether it is nonempty, including the issue posed by the empty chain; whether every chain has an upper bound, with attention to chains longer than countable; whether that upper bound is itself a member of the family rather than merely a superset living outside it; whether the maximal element Zorn's lemma delivers is the object the author wants, as opposed to a maximum, or a maximal element with some further property still to be proved; how to recognise a well-ordering or transfinite-recursion argument that has been described as Zorn's lemma; and how to notice choice being spent elsewhere in the paper, unflagged, through selections of representatives, compactness of infinite products, or the existence of ultrafilters.
- The standard failure modes, each paired with a short diagnostic an assistant can apply without knowing any set theory, and a repair step saying what to try when a check fails.
- An escalation rule stating which questions the procedure must not pretend to answer — in particular any claim about how much choice a given theorem really requires, or that a theorem is equivalent to some choice principle — and what an assistant should write on the escalation slip so that our set theorist can answer it in one pass.
- A one-page version suitable for taping above a desk.

Then run the procedure on three submissions and report the verdict it produces, the check that produced it, and the note we would send the author. The first is a colleague's elegant two-paragraph proof that every vector space has a basis, obtained by applying Zorn's lemma to the family of linearly independent subsets; I argued for accepting it, and I would rather the procedure tell me now than have the board tell me later. The second is a student's proof that an infinite-dimensional space contains a maximal linearly independent set, obtained by applying Zorn's lemma to the family of finite linearly independent subsets. The third extends the familiar maximal-ideal argument to commutative rings that may lack a multiplicative identity.

Finally, be candid about the procedure's limits: which errors it will wave through, which correct papers it will needlessly delay, and where an assistant who follows it mechanically will still come out wrong.
