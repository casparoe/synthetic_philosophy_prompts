I'm reworking the supertasks week of my metaphysics seminar and I want to open with a variant I cooked up, then rank the standard diagnoses against it rather than against the original puzzle.

The variant: lamp A is your usual Thomson lamp — on at 11:59, off at 11:59:30, on at 11:59:45, and so on, with the toggles piling up at noon. Lamp B is hard-wired to copy A's state at every instant, but the copying wire is cut at 11:59:59, after which B just freezes. So at every moment before noon A and B are in exactly the same state, B uncontroversially has a determinate state at noon, and I can flip that state between on and off just by nudging the cut-off. Question hanging over it: if they share every pre-noon state, does A get a determinate noon state too, and if so, which?

Rank these five responses to Thomson's lamp by how well they cope with the two-lamp case — not by how plausible they sound in the abstract:

1. Thomson's own line that completing such a sequence is logically impossible.
2. Benacerraf's line that the pre-noon facts simply don't entail anything about noon, so we have a gap, not a contradiction.
3. The view that it's a physics question, not a logic one: if the device could exist, the noon state gets settled by physical continuity.
4. The deflationary view that no such device can exist anyway (energy, timing precision), so the whole thing is idle.
5. The bite-the-bullet view that the noon state is genuinely indeterminate in the world, not just in our description of it.

Give me a 1–5 ordering, plus a score out of 10 for each on how much of the two-lamp case it can actually absorb, and say explicitely which ones the case refutes outright versus which ones it just fails to help. Then tell me which one I should build the rest of the week on, given that the point of the week is to generalize past lamps to other supertasks.
