I am one of three markers for a third-year course on the philosophy of the social sciences; we set a timed in-class essay on Popper's *The Poverty of Historicism* and his companion essay on prediction and prophecy, and I have been given scripts to mark before our moderation meeting. The question the students answered, worth 25 marks and to be completed in 45 minutes under exam conditions, was the following:

> **Question 3 (25 marks).** Popper holds that the social sciences cannot predict the long-run course of human history, and that the search for laws of historical development is consequently misconceived. Explain the argument that turns on the growth of knowledge, and explain how it differs from an ordinary inductive or probabilistic objection to forecasting. Does that argument commit Popper to any position on free will or determinism? Illustrate your answer by reference to Popper's treatment of Marx.

Our department's rubric bands are: 21–25 excellent, 16–20 good, 11–15 adequate, 6–10 marginal, 5 or below a fail to address the question. Last year the external reviewer complained that our marking rewarded fluent prose out of proportion to its accuracy, so I want a careful independent read of this script rather than my own impression. Here is the answer, transcribed exactly, including its slips of phrasing:

> Popper's case against historicism is not that history is chaotic but that historicists mistake trends for laws. A trend — say, the growth of a city's population over thirty years — is a singular statement about a particular place and time, and it may stop; a law is universal and exceptionless, and it is only laws, together with initial conditions, that license genuine predictions. The historicist sees a trend of development in the past and reads it as a law of development, then prophecies where society must end up. What Popper objects to is this unconditional prophecy, not prediction as such: he allows that social science can make conditional or technological predictions of the form that if we adopt this policy, this consequence follows, which is enough to support the piecemeal social engineering he recommends in place of holistic reconstruction.
>
> The central argument turns on knowledge. The course of history is strongly influenced by the growth of our knowledge; we cannot predict, by rational or scientific methods, the future growth of scientific knowledge, since to predict a future discovery in detail would be to have already made it; therefore we cannot predict the future course of history. This is not an inductive worry about extrapolating from limited data, nor a probabilistic point about wide confidence intervals. It is supposed to be a logical limit: the prediction would have to contain its own ground. Because the argument depends on our inability to foresee what we shall later come to know, it presupposes that intellectual activity is genuinely undetermined. So Popper's conclusion shows not merely that the future course of history is unknown to us but that it is objectively undetermined, and a historicist who accepted determinism could reply that history remains in principle predictable even by a mind unlike ours. The argument therefore binds only those who already reject determinism about human thought.
>
> Popper's treatment of Marx illustrates the same point. Marx's theory of history was framed in terms flexible enough to absorb any outcome, so it never issued a prediction capable of failing; historicism is thus unscientific in the same sense as the theories Popper contrasts with Einstein's, and the argument from the growth of knowledge is really a special case of the demarcation criterion rather than an independent objection. One difficulty Popper does not escape is reflexive: if we cannot know our future knowledge, we cannot know that we shall never know the course of history, so the argument seems unable to establish even its own conclusion as more than a conjecture.
