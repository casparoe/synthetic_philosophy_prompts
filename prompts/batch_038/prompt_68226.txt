I am a novelist — four published books, a fifth in progress — and I have arrived at a view about well-being that my own profession makes uncomfortable to hold. That discomfort is exactly why I want the view stress-tested rather than reassured.

The view. I hold that contact with reality is a non-instrumental requirement on a person's well-being. If the central goods of a life are supplied by a mechanism that fabricates them, that life is worse for the person living it than an otherwise identical life in which those goods are real — and it is worse in a way that is not reducible to any bad experience the person has, not to any frustration of their informed desires, and not to any mere failure to have actually done the things they seem to have done. The deficit lies in the disconnection itself. I take this to be the strongest reading of the lesson people draw from Nozick's experience machine: not that we want to do things rather than have the experience of doing them, and not that we want contact with a deeper reality because contact serves other goods, but that the contact is itself a constituent of a good life.

Two refinements, which I want examined at least as hard as the core claim.

First, a transparency condition. I do not think fabrication as such is the problem. A reader who enters a novel knowing it is invented, is genuinely moved, and emerges better for it has not, on my account, had her well-being diminished. What damages a life is opaque fabrication: goods delivered by a mechanism whose operation the subject does not know about or cannot keep track of. Knowledge of the fabrication is, I believe, a real repair rather than a cosmetic one.

Second, a scalar condition. Contact is not all-or-nothing. A life that is thirty percent fabricated is, other things equal, worse by some determinate amount than one that is ten percent fabricated, and there is a fact of the matter about where the boundary falls between a tolerably real life and an intolerably synthetic one.

Now a case from the book I am writing, because I need to know whether my own protagonist is a refutation. Ilsa is told at twenty that from that moment on every experience she has will be generated by a system she fully understands and consents to without reservation. Her knowledge is periodically refreshed, so she is never deceived about her situation; the people she loves are inside with her and know it as she does; the work she finds meaningful involves problems she sets herself and solves, though the problems are generated. She lives to ninety, lucid and by her own lights fulfilled. Two questions I want answered rather than diplomatically dodged: does my view commit me to judging Ilsa's life badly deficient, and if the transparency condition spares her, what remains of the claim that contact matters non-instrumentally — given that the very feature which motivated the claim, undetectable fabrication, is now licensed?

Please take the role of a philosopher who specializes in theories of well-being and in the methodology of thought experiments in ethics, and who has thought carefully about what a hypothetical case can and cannot establish. Think slowly and seriously. Begin with a page of unranked brainstorming in which you canvass the space of possible defeaters — including ones you already suspect are weak — so that I can see the search was wide rather than the first three objections you reached for. Then give me the deliverable:

1. A ranked list of what would count against the position, ordered from fatal to merely awkward. Do not pad it to appear thorough; five genuine defeaters ranked honestly are worth far more than twenty.

2. For each, state plainly what a committed defender of the view would concede as counting against her and what she would dismiss as beside the point, and tell me which of those dismissals are legitimate and which are evasions.

3. What the view predicts that its rivals — hedonism, desire-satisfaction accounts, objective-list theories, achievement-based views — do not predict, and whether any of those predictions could in principle be checked: against people's reports of their own lives, against how lives actually go, or against anything else. If some of them are permanently beyond reach, say so and say what follows.

4. Whether the scalar condition can be made determinate at all, or whether I am entitled on reflection only to a vaguer comparative claim. If only the latter, tell me how much of the view survives the downgrade and what work the original claim was doing.

5. Whether the transparency condition and the core claim can both be retained, or whether one of them has to absorb the cost of the other. If they are reconcilable, show me the mechanism rather than asserting it.

6. An honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, separately, whether that is a defect for a thesis of this kind, given that a claim in value theory was never going to be falsifiable in the way a claim in physics is. Be willing to conclude that the view is well insulated and that this is not a problem, if that is where the reasoning leads.

I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or names-and-dates attached to arguments; I cannot check any of it and it would only dress up the reasoning. Argue from the ideas themselves, and where a real objection has a well-known shape, describe the shape rather than citing its source. Write for an intelligent general reader who happens to make up lives for a living: precise, unhedged, and unafraid to tell me that a view I find congenial to my work does not survive.
