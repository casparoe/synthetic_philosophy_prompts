Retired comparative physiologist here — forty years on insect nervous systems, only started reading philosophy properly in the last couple of years. At a public lecture last month a speaker arguing that we should treat insects as morally considerable leaned on the old line that absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence: we haven't found good evidence that insects are sentient, she said, but that gives us no reason to think they aren't, and given how many of them there are (I've seen 10^18 bandied about, so take it as order of magnitude) the stakes add up to something we can't ignore. I want to press on that one sentence. What are the different precise claims it could be making — something about logical entailment, something about whether a search would be expected to turn up evidence if the claim were true, something about how much a failed search is worth as evidence, maybe something about what prior you're allowed to start with? Sort them into the platitudes, the ones that hold only under conditions worth stating, and the ones that are just false, and then tell me which version she actually needed. Does that version survive contact with the science, given we have looked fairly hard at nociception, opioid modulation, avoidance learning and the rest — is a null result there really neutral? And separately, is the numbers move the same argument as the slogan or a different one: does a huge population change what a low probability of sentience is worth morally, and what would have to be true for that multiplication to be legitimate rather than a trick?
