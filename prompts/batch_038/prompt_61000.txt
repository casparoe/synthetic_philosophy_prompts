I am a second-year history student, and this semester I am enrolled in a seminar on perpetrators, bystanders, and rescuers that pairs Holocaust historiography with the moral-psychological debate over whether situations or stable character traits explain cruel behavior. Our department runs a double-blind peer-assessment scheme alongside the seminar: each of us is sent an anonymized practice exam answer, written under timed conditions by a student in the other section, and we must assign a mark and justify it in writing. Our instructor has said that the marks we give will be recalibrated against her own grading of the same answers, and that the point of the exercise is to train us to separate fluent, well-organized prose from accuracy about what an author actually argues. I want an independent assessment before I commit to a mark, since I am conscious that a confident-sounding answer can carry errors that read smoothly on the page, and I do not want my own grade to be the reason a bad mark survives calibration.

The practice question, worth 25 marks, was as follows.

Exam question: Both Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men and Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners examine the men of Reserve Police Battalion 101. Explain how the two accounts differ in what they treat as the primary explanation for the battalion's participation in mass shootings, and evaluate which account better explains why almost all of the men took part at Jozefow in July 1942.

Student answer: Browning and Goldhagen both work from the same body of evidence, the post-war investigations into Reserve Police Battalion 101, a Hamburg order-police unit of roughly five hundred middle-aged reservists drawn largely from working-class and lower-middle-class backgrounds, but they read it in opposite directions. Browning's title deliberately echoes Hannah Arendt's banality of evil, and he takes over her central insight that the men who staffed the killing apparatus were not fanatics but unremarkable people for whom killing had become routine; Arendt had argued at Eichmann's trial that the perpetrators' evil arose from the depth and pervasiveness of Nazi racial ideology, which made murder feel like administrative procedure. Browning's own explanation is situational. At Jozefow, Major Trapp told the battalion that it had been ordered to shoot the town's Jews, including women and children, and offered anyone who did not feel up to the task the chance to step out of the line; a dozen or so of the five hundred did. Browning takes that as proof that those who stayed were not acting from conviction, because a man who genuinely believed that what he was doing was right would have needed no outside pressure at all to do it. What held them to the line, on his account, was the pressure of comradeship: the fear of appearing weak and of leaving the filthy work to one's friends, together with the deference to authority measured in Milgram's obedience experiments, wartime brutalization, and a diffuse racism accumulated over the preceding decade. Goldhagen rejects this. For him the decisive factor is cultural rather than situational: an eliminationist antisemitism, a cognitive model of Jews embedded in German political culture well before 1933, which made the elimination of Jews imaginable and acceptable to broad sections of the population. Perpetrators killed willingly and would have gone on killing without Trapp's order, because the conviction was already there; Goldhagen therefore treats Browning's situational apparatus as an evasion of the question of motive, and argues that the battalion's later conduct, including men volunteering for further shooting actions and the cruelty some showed their victims, reveals enthusiasm rather than reluctant conformity. Evaluating the two, Goldhagen explains Jozefow better. The men of Battalion 101 were too old for the Hitler Youth and had never volunteered for the SS or undergone its racial schooling, so Browning's appeal to accumulated racism and situational pressure cannot account for why so few of them declined; Goldhagen's appeal to a pre-existing cultural conviction can. More broadly, the exchange is a strong argument for situationism in moral psychology: it shows that stable character traits do no explanatory work whatsoever, since the same individuals behaved very differently according to the situation they were placed in, though here the situation was itself a cultural one, so the two explanations ultimately converge. End of answer.
