> Student answer: Street begins from a premise that even her realist opponents mostly grant: that natural selection exerted a pervasive and systematic influence on which evaluative judgments human beings find intuitive. If moral truths hold independently of our attitudes, as realism says, then the realist has to say something about the relation between those Darwinian influences and the independent truths. Only two answers are available, and both are costly. Either the evolutionary pressure pushed our judgments toward the true ones, or it did not. If it did not, then our judgments and the truth have simply drifted apart, and the realist has no basis left for trusting her own moral beliefs; the result is skepticism about moral knowledge. If it did, the realist still owes an account of why a process that is causally blind to normative truth would nonetheless land on it, which makes the match look like a miracle rather than an achievement. So the realist must choose between an unexplained coincidence and an unexplained correlation. It is worth noting that the familiar objection from the genetic fallacy — that the origin of a belief says nothing about whether it is true — does not get a grip here, because what Street concludes is that the realist is not entitled to rely on her judgments, not that realism is false. Her own constructivism escapes the dilemma by a different route: it is a non-cognitivist position on which moral claims lack truth values altogether, so there is nothing for our judgments to track and no correlation to explain.
>
> Joyce's argument runs on the same genealogical machinery but pushes further. He too says that our moral judgments are the product of adaptive pressures that were indifferent to their truth, which is why he thinks those judgments have no evidential support. But for Joyce this is the empirical premise of an error theory: he concludes that positive moral claims are all false, since nothing answers to the categorical, inescapable demands they express. Where Street leaves realism standing but untrustworthy, Joyce takes it to be refuted, and the genealogy is what does the refuting.
>
> The realist's best reply is the third-factor strategy developed by Enoch. It concedes that moral facts are not causally efficacious, but denies that this leaves the correlation unexplained, because the explanation need not run through causation: survival and reproductive success are themselves good, evolution selected for traits that promoted them, and so a common factor links the direction of selection to the content of the moral truths. On this reading the second horn is blocked, since the match is no longer miraculous but a consequence of the shared factor. Street concedes as much, which is why the debate has moved on from the dilemma itself to the narrower question of whether moral knowledge survives it. Two worries remain. One is that the third factor seems to explain at most a general link between selection and survival, and not why we should have arrived at the specific normative claims we actually make. The other, pressed by FitzPatrick, is a scope problem: the debunker assumes that selection shaped even our reflective, reason-governed moral reasoning, whereas our capacity to step back on our own evaluative attitudes is not simply one more adaptation, so the argument needs a further premise that its advocates have not supplied.
