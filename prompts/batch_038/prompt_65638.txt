**The question**

Augustine is standardly said to hold that lying is never permissible.

(a) What, for Augustine, makes an utterance a lie, and what makes lying wrong?
(b) In his treatise on lying he distinguishes kinds of lie and orders them by gravity. What does that ordering show about his absolutism, and what does it fail to show?
(c) Someone is hiding in your house from a man who intends to kill him, and the man asks you where he is. What does Augustine permit you to do, what does he forbid, and is his treatment of such cases internally consistent?

Draw on at least one of his two works on lying.

**The answer submitted**

Augustine's view is that lying is never permissible, and that this admits of no exception, whatever good is expected from it. The first thing to see is that for him the falsity of a statement is beside the point. A man may say what is false and not lie, provided he believes it; and he may say what is true and lie, if he believes it to be false and says it anyway. What makes an utterance a lie is a mismatch between the mind and the speech that expresses it — the doubled heart — together with the will to deceive the hearer. Whether the hearer is in fact deceived is irrelevant. Lying is therefore defined entirely by the speaker's own state, not by how things stand in the world.

As to why it is wrong, Augustine's argument is in substance that lying cannot be universalised. If it were generally permitted to lie whenever a good end required it, no one's word could be relied upon, the practice of assertion would collapse, and the liar would be acting on a rule whose general adoption destroys the very practice his own act depends on. To this he adds a self-regarding consideration: the liar misuses a faculty, employing speech for a purpose contrary to that for which it exists. In both respects he is a clear forerunner of Kant, and I think the two positions stand or fall together.

On (b): in the earlier treatise Augustine distinguishes eight kinds of lie and orders them, the gravest being lies in matters of religious teaching, then lies that injure someone for no one's benefit, lies told from the sheer pleasure of deceiving, and lies told to be agreeable in company, down at the mildest end to lies that injure no one and are told to save a person from death or from violation. All eight are sins; the ordering grades their seriousness and nothing else. That is where I think the difficulty lies. If the mildest lie injures no one, prevents a grave evil, and is nonetheless a sin, then Augustine is plainly weighing outcomes — kinds and degrees of harm — in order to fix his scale, while his official doctrine denies that any weighing is admissible. The ranking is doing consequentialist work that the absolutism refuses to acknowledge. At the least he owes an account of why the mildest lie is not a lesser evil but a positive wrong.

On (c): he forbids the lie; you may not tell the killer that his victim is elsewhere. He does not, however, require you to hand the victim over by saying nothing at all. You may refuse to answer, you may decline to disclose what you know, and you may say something true in a sense of your own choosing which you are aware he will take in another sense, since on his own account there is no lie where the words accord with the mind. In the later work, written against the Priscillianists, who held it lawful to lie in order to conceal their faith, and specifically against the proposal that one should feign heresy so as to expose heretics, he applies the same rule: a lie may not be prosecuted by means of a lie, even where the truth itself would be served by it.

Is this consistent? Only partly. Refusing to speak, or declining to disclose, sits comfortably with the doubled-heart account. The permission for true-but-misleading speech does not, and I do not think Augustine can have both. If the wrong of lying is the will to deceive, then a true sentence uttered with the intention that the hearer be deceived is as much a lie as a false one, by his own definition. If the wrong lies instead in the mismatch, the misleading truth escapes unscathed. He appears to want the definition both ways at once, and that is why the doctrine looks least stable precisely where it is most likely to be tested.
