So I've been an SRE for about eight years, currently at a ~400 person SaaS company, and I keep running into something I cant quite articulate. We run a very strict blameless postmortem practice: after any sev-1 outage someone writes up a doc, and there's an actual style checklist for it that I helped maintain for a while. The checklist flags sentences that pair a person's name with a verb of fault — Dan forgot to rotate the cert, the on-call ignored the alert — and makes you rewrite them as descriptions of system state and information flow. Names are allowed in the "who did what next" section but not in the timeline, and the handbook line everyone repeats is basically that the engineer did the best they could with the information they had available at the time. Meanwhile, in the exact same building: we run stack ranking at calibration, there's a regrettable-attrition number that managers are held to, the level ladder rubric tells people to demonstrate ownership of their failures, our CODEOWNERS files hardcode specific individuals as answerable for specific paths, and the incident commander role is always one named human who can override everybody. And the git history, of course, records exactly who wrote the line that took down prod.

I brought this up with a friend who did a philosophy minor, mostly to complain, and she said something that stuck with me. Her take was that the blameless stuff only makes sense if you already hold a particular view about what a person is — that the self isn't a discrete origin point for actions but something constituted by its relationships and its position inside a web of practices. She said there's a debate in African philosophy, ubuntu and related work, about whether personhood is something you simply have from birth or something you acquire through your relations with others and can also lose, and that our postmortem ritual is quietly running on the second option while our HR process is running on the first. I didn't follow half of it and I'm not at all sure she wasn't just pattern-matching onto a course she liked.

Can you do the reconstruction for me? What I'm after: what theory of agency or responsibility or personhood does the blameless postmortem practice strictly presuppose — the minimal set of commitments needed for the checklist to be more than theater — and which rival views does it rule out without saying so? Then the harder part: do the two halves of the company presuppose consistent things, or are the postmortem ritual and the stack ranking making incompatible claims about whether an individual person is the right unit of causal and moral accounting? I especially want to know whether I'm overreading it. Maybe the practice presupposes nothing metaphysical whatsoever and it's purely an incentives claim — people hide their errors if they'll be punished for them, so you suppress the punishment and keep the record — in which case the whole personhood framing is just decoration on top of a pretty ordinary engineering-culture argument. If that's the right answer, make it, and lay out the argument rather than assuming I've read anything. And then tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, i.e. where several different theories of the person would all license the exact same checklist, and what would concretely have to change in how we write these docs if we actually adopted the acquire-and-can-lose-personhood view. Would we start naming names? Would the ranking get more brutal or dissolve?

Please push back if the framing is broken, by the way. If my friend's move is a category error, or if the practices I described are too generic and industry-standard to bear the weight I'm putting on them, say that plainly instead of humoring me — I'd much rather be told the question is confused than get a nice-sounding answer to a bad one.
