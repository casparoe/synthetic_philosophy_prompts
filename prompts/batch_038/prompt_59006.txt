I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine. For the past year I have kept a private log of every moment in a story where I decided that I knew something, as distinct from merely believing a source, and after a stretch spent reading Nyāya epistemology I have been testing whether that framework's account of the pramāṇas — and especially the conditions it places on perception, inference and testimony — captures the pattern of my own verdicts. Mostly it does, which is unnerving. In several places it seems not to, and I cannot tell whether the framework needs a clause I have failed to find or whether my verdicts need to go.

Here are the cases, with the verdict I actually reached at the time.

1. I stood in a lab and watched a technician pipette samples and watched the wells change colour. I knew the reaction had occurred. Nothing to think about.
2. The same week I read that group's figure — a bar chart whose vertical axis began at 90 — and had a clear, vivid, wholly unforced visual impression that the treated bar was twice the control. I did not know that. But at the moment of having the impression, my epistemic position looked to me exactly as it had in case 1.
3. In the field with an ornithologist who was fifty metres away, I listened to a recording and heard at once, without any reasoning I could report, that this bird was not the species I had been hearing all morning. I count this as knowledge. My ear is far less trained than hers and I would defer to her if she disagreed — but she did not, and I do not think my verdict was waiting on her.
4. A climate modeller I have known for a decade, with no stake in the outcome, tells me an attribution result is robust. I know it; I will print it as fact without checking anything.
5. The same man, equally candid and equally unconflicted, tells me the size of an effect produced by a model he assembled but does not fully understand, and says he would have to ask a colleague before he could defend the details. I do not know it, and I write it as a claim.
6. A colleague I trust tells me what an investigator's lab notebook says. She did not witness the work, cannot evaluate the methods, and has no opinion about whether the result is true; she read it and reported it accurately. The investigator was there, is competent, and has no reason to mislead. I know the result. This is the one that troubles me most, because my informant plainly does not know what she is telling me.
7. A large, pre-registered, well-powered replication comes back empty. I take myself to know the effect is not there. A thirty-subject pilot comes back empty. I take myself to know nothing at all. Both are non-apprehension; my verdicts differ by the quality of the search, which is a fact about the apparatus rather than about my impression.
8. Two honest, competent papers reach opposite conclusions. I do not simply hold the claim at low confidence; I am in a state that behaves differently. I cannot plan a story around it and I cannot even assert its negation. I suspect Nyāya is right to treat doubt as a kind of cognition in its own right, with identifiable causes, rather than as a position on a scale of confidence, and I would like that defended or demolished.
9. The case I keep returning to: a transient that no human being saw, flagged by a trained network searching telescope data and then corroborated through independent channels. I write that astronomers observed it. Asked whether I saw it, I say no. I cannot tell whether what I have is a perception mediated by an instrument, an inference from the network's output, or testimony from a source with no person attached to it — and if it is testimony, who occupies the place of the trustworthy speaker? I half-remember a device for cases where contact looks mediated, in which a cognition itself serves as the medium, the business about seeing sandalwood from too far off to smell it and yet taking oneself to smell it; I do not know whether it is any use here.

One further observation. Of the four sources, the one that concerns knowing what a word denotes from being told what it resembles has never once fired in my log. Tell me whether that is because my log is incomplete, and show me the kind of episode from my beat that would fill it, or because it is not a genuine source at all.

What I want is the smallest rule, or set of rules, that delivers every verdict above — or all but a named few — stated in terms I could actually run at my desk, with the Sanskrit apparatus optional rather than obligatory. Build the rule in front of me: for each clause, say which of my cases forces it. Then do three more things. First, apply the result to two episodes I have not listed: a confident assertion produced by a large language model with no identifiable human standing behind it, and my own recollection seven years later of exactly what a source said to me off the record — on your account, does memory generate knowledge or only preserve it? Second, tell me plainly whether cases 2 and 3 can both survive. They look to me like the same thing twice over, and if the only difference is that I happen to have learned that charts lie and have not learned that my ears do, then either say so and show me the clause that encodes it, or tell me which verdict I must surrender. Third, tell me whether my standing habit — before printing an inference, asking what else would have to be true for that evidence to be misleading — is simply a hunt for a hidden condition that rides along with my reason but not with what I am trying to establish, the sort of thing that undercuts a pervasion, and whether that means my rule is really a rule about defeaters rather than a rule about how often two things have gone together.

I do not want a reading list, a bibliography, or attributions to particular texts and thinkers beyond what you can state with confidence. I want the rule and the argument for it. Please also say where your own reasoning is most likely to break: which of my cases you suspect you have mis-described, which clause is load-bearing but under-motivated, and what argument or consideration, if someone pressed it against you, would move you to endorse a different rule altogether. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning after it, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
