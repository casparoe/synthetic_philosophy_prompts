Question was worth 25 marks, ~45 minutes of a 2-hour paper:

**Question:** Smith holds that we judge the propriety of an affection by asking what an impartial and well-informed spectator would feel in the situation of the person concerned. Explain how Smith gets from ordinary, self-involved sympathy to that standard. Does the standard amount to anything more than the internalised approval of the people actually around us?

**Student answer:**

Smith starts from a claim about human nature: however selfish we suppose people to be, there are principles in their nature that interest them in the fortune of others, and that make other people's happiness necessary to them even though they get nothing out of it except the pleasure of seeing it. The mechanism is sympathy, which for Smith is not pity — pity is only sympathy with sorrow. Sympathy is fellow-feeling with any passion whatever, so we can sympathise with someone's joy, their resentment, their embarrassment.

Sympathy works through the imagination. We can't directly feel what another feels, so we place ourselves in their situation in fancy, we conceive ourselves enduring all the same things, and we ask what we would feel. If what we would feel matches what they appear to feel, we approve of the affection as proper; if not, we disapprove. So propriety is a concord between the passion of the person concerned and the emotion the spectator feels when he imagines himself in that person's place.

Smith then splits moral judgment into two seperate objects. Propriety concerns the relation between the affection and its cause or object. Merit and demerit concern the relation between the action and the person it affects — we judge merit by sympathising with the gratitude or resentment of the person acted upon. An action is fully approved only when it clears both tests.

Mutual sympathy matters because it pleases. When the spectator goes along with me I'm delighted; when he can't, I'm mortified. So I bring my passion down to a pitch the spectator can enter into, and this is the origin of self-command. Repeated experience of what people approve and disapprove generates general rules of morality, and from these we get the sense of duty: we come to act on the rule even when our actual feelings don't cooperate.

Out of all this Smith builds the impartial spectator. Because actual spectators are biased — partial to us, ignorant of the details, too far away — we learn to imagine a better-informed and less interested one, the supposed impartial and well-informed spectator, the man within the breast, who is the real judge of our conduct. This is basically Hume's general point of view under a different name. Hume also says that because our sympathies vary with proximity we have to fix on a common standpoint, so that moral language doesn't shift from person to person, and for Hume what wins approval from that standpoint is whatever is useful or agreeable to the person himself or to others. Smith's spectator does the same job: its a bias-correction device that lets us track the usefulness and agreeableness of characters and actions from a common point of view. The differences between them are matters of emphasis rather than substance.

Which brings us to the worry in the question. The impartial spectator isn't discovered, it's constructed: it's built out of our experience of the approvals and disapprovals of the people actually around us and then idealised by supposing those same people better informed and less partial. But idealising doesn't add new content, it only re-weights content that was already there. So the verdicts of the man within the breast are the verdicts of my society, filtered for bias, and Smith turns out to be a conventionalist in disguise. In a society that admires duelling, the impartial spectator admires duelling, and Smith has no standpoint from which to criticise it, because the only standard available to him is the one the society supplied. His own remarks about how much custom and fashion influence our moral sentiments look like a concession of exactly this. The most Smith can say is that some societies' spectators are better informed than others', which is a difference of degree, not of kind.
