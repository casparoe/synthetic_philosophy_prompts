I am preparing a written response to a colleague's argument for a graduate seminar on the ethics of migration, and I have reached an impasse. Each of his steps strikes me as acceptable when I read it on its own, but the conclusion is one I cannot accept, and I have been unable to find the point at which the chain breaks. He states it as follows.

(1) A legitimate state has a right to determine the composition of its political community. This is what separates a self-governing association from a mere administrative unit: a body that cannot settle who is in it does not govern itself.

(2) Determining composition is a single power with two faces — deciding whom to admit and deciding whom to retain. A principle that permits the first while forbidding the second discriminates between two exercises of the same authority on no ground other than the direction of the change.

(3) Nor can the prohibition be grounded in the fact of belonging. Long-settled non-citizen residents belong to the community only in virtue of its continuing determination to include them; their membership is an output of that power, not a constraint upon it. Treating it as a constraint would allow the output of a decision to veto the decision that produced it.

(4) Therefore a state that may refuse admission at the border may, on the same authority and for the same kind of reasons, withdraw inclusion from those already resident — that is, it may remove them.

(5) Any attempt to block (4) by appeal to the claims of the residents concedes the point: if the claims of people inside the community can override its determination of who is inside it, then the right asserted in (1) was never a right of self-determination at all, but a privilege held at the sufferance of those over whom it is exercised.

(6) Hence the restrictionist must accept the removal of settled residents, and the open-borders advocate must give up the right to exclude. Nothing lies between these positions.

My own instincts run the other way. I think (1) is defensible, and I think the wholesale removal of people who have built their lives inside a state is clearly impermissible, so I want both halves of the middle that (6) says is empty. I would like a diagnosis rather than a restatement of the two sides.

(a) Is the argument valid? If it is not, identify the exact step at which it fails, distinguish the two readings between which that step slides, and explain why the step looks unobjectionable on a first pass.

(b) If it is valid, tell me which premise I should surrender and what the surrender costs. In particular, price the alternatives: what does the freedom-of-association account of the right to exclude, what does the national self-determination account, and what does Carens's case for open borders each have to give up in order to escape (6)?

(c) Finally, say whether there is a non-question-begging way to hold the middle I want — a right to refuse admission coupled with a bar on removing the settled — and if there is, identify the principle that separates the two cases without collapsing back into (2) or (3). If no such principle exists, say so plainly and tell me which of my two commitments I should abandon.

Please write your answer in British English.
