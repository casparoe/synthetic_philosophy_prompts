Okay so I help put together a small zine that's basically jokes-with-footnotes for grad students, and this issue's theme is mental causation. The piece I need is a parody of an appellate court opinion — so, full mock-judicial-register, case caption, "Before: ..." line, numbered paragraphs, the occasional Latinism used slightly too confidently — around 900 words total, with a short dissent at the end (say 200 words) that's clearly written by someone who has thought about this too much.

Here's the setup I want you to invent within, and please stick to it: in a jurisdiction you make up, the supreme court adopted some years ago a rule of decision called the Causal Exclusion Principle, which says that a defendant is liable only for events caused by something that is not itself fully caused by prior physical events, and that the law therefore may not treat a belief, desire, or intention as a cause whenever the body's movements already have a complete physical cause. The case on appeal is a mundane tort — something small and petty that you should pick and describe in a paragraph, ideally involving an object being destroyed on purpose, because "on purpose" is exactly what's at issue. The plaintiff lost below and is appealing; the defense brief argues, entirely in the register of a serious tort lawyer rather than a metaphysician, that the defendant's rage and his reasons did no causal work and are therefore legally inert. The majority affirms. The crucial thing: the majority has to state the argument *correctly*, not as a strawman — it needs the causal closure premise, the claim that under a non-reductive view the mental is not identical with the physical cause that's doing the work, and the rejection of the "well then everything is just doubly caused" escape hatch, since the court finds systematic overdetermination as offensive as it finds epiphenomenalism. If a metaphysician reads the opinion and thinks the judges have misunderstood Kim's argument, the piece has failed.

What I actually want the jokes to be is the collision, not the metaphysics: the court has to run into tort law's own machinery for handling overdetermination and preemption (substantial-factor tests, the concurrent-causes stuff, the old cases where two fires each sufficient to burn the barn down both get blamed) and discover, without ever admitting it, that its own doctrine is more permissive than its metaphysics — that the law has all along been assigning causal credit to things that don't look like sufficient physical causes. Let the court paper over this with procedural language, and let a reader who knows the literature see exactly where the seam is. Same with the dissent: it should push the proportionality line and the determinable/determinate analogy — the mental as a higher-level variable that's a better fit for the effect than the neural detail — and get far enough to notice that this reply buys mental causation at the price of making the mental do something the court finds uncomfortable, which the dissenting judge should trail off on rather than name. Both opinions stay deadpan to the last sentence; nobody winks, nobody says "and of course philosophers disagree," and the piece should end on the disposition line and cost allocation like a real opinion would. No footnotes citing actual papers and no bibliography — you can allude to real positions and even drop one or two real philosophers' names in passing if you're confident, but every invented case, statute, and judge is yours. Target here is the practice of piping a metaphysical argument straight into legal doctrine, plus a bit of the exclusion argument's own shape, so it should read as fair rather than as a hit job: an actual non-reductive physicalist should be able to laugh at it.
