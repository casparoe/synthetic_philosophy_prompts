I am at work on a novel whose central consciousness is a dead Dutch painter and forger, closely modelled on Han van Meegeren, and I have concluded that the most honest way to think him through is to ask what the real man would have made of the decades since his death in 1947. I am not after a biography. I am after a prediction, disciplined enough that I can see which of my inventions are load-bearing and which are merely decorative.

Here is the device. In one chapter he is brought back and shown four objects, one at a time, and asked to respond to each. Before I write a line of his dialogue I need to know what you take his responses to be — and, more urgently, which of the four would actually wound him.

The exhibits:

1. A text-to-image system, prompted with a paragraph, produces in under a minute a plausible hitherto unknown Vermeer: the light, the pearls, the domestic interior, and — this is the point — an invented iconography of exactly the kind van Meegeren practised, since he did not copy existing pictures but manufactured new ones to fill apparent gaps in the catalogue. The output is printed, textured, aged, and put before a connoisseur, who pronounces it genuine.

2. A recent sale in which one of his own forgeries — a canvas once acquired as a seventeenth-century masterpiece and praised at the time by the most respected authority on that painter — is offered under his own name, with a certificate, in an auction devoted to the work of forgers, where the hammer price is now a price for a van Meegeren.

3. A conceptual work by a living artist who reproduces a canonical photograph, signs her own name, and is exhibited in a major museum beneath a wall text arguing that the reproduction is the work.

4. The detection laboratory that ended his trade: pigment chemistry, canvas weave and thread-count comparison against documented pictures, radiometric analysis of the lead in the white, and the institutional habit of requiring scientific clearance before any attribution is published.

For each, tell me what he would say and what he would think, and keep the two sharply distinct. Then answer the question I actually care about: which of the four does the most damage to the account he gave of himself — the account in which he was a serious painter slighted by the critics, who set out to demonstrate that the experts were credulous, and who was converted by his own exposure from criminal into national hero. Press hard on a structural oddity in that account: it required the fraud to be discovered in order to be satisfied, and the discovery is also what killed him. Given that, does the machine in exhibit one complete his argument or annihilate it?

Two further matters, both bearing on craft rather than scholarship. First, my character must want something. Tell me which motive the record best supports — recognition, retaliation against the critics, money, or the pleasure of the deception as such — and how that motive fares under each of the four exhibits. Second, and relatedly: he proved his confession by painting a Vermeer under official observation, an undertaking of months that was, whatever else it was, a demonstration of skill. Exhibit one passes the identical test in seconds with no skill anywhere in the chain. I want your judgement on whether he would regard that as a rival, an insult, an instrument he would have adopted without a moment's hesitation, or a vindication so total that it would leave him nothing to do.

Mark clearly wherever you are extrapolating past what the record supports, and tell me which of those extrapolations you consider most likely to be wrong, since I would rather build the chapter on a joint you think sound. Resist the obvious vocabulary of the subject: I do not want aura, or the death of the author, or a general meditation on authenticity in a technological age, unless some particular claim of that sort is genuinely doing work. Close with no more than four or five sentences of what he would actually say on being shown the first exhibit — not as a period performance, but as a test of whether your analysis yields a man I could put on the page.
