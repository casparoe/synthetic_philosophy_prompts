Been reading Hume's economic essays for fun (mainly the one on money and the one on the balance of trade) and I keep running into an argument shape I can't find a word for. Hoping someone can tell me whether it's a known thing.

Setup as I read it. Hume insists over and over that the quantity of money in a kingdom, taken by itself, is of no consequence — prices just scale with it, so a crown in Henry VII's time did the same work a pound does now, and a nation's real strength is its people and its commodities, not its coin. Fine. But in the same essay he says manufactures migrate: they leave the countries they have already enriched, fly off to wherever provisions and labour are cheap, enrich those places in turn, and then get banished again by the very dearness they created. And he floats a public bank that locks up every penny it takes in and never returns any of it to circulation — a bank that by construction can't budge the money supply — and then claims a real national advantage from it, part of which is just that labour stays cheap. (The other part is the killing off of paper credit, so maybe that leg isn't clean, but the cheap-labour leg looks like it is.)

The pattern I want named: a variable that has been explicitly declared to have no lasting real effect, only a scaling one, nonetheless leaves the system permanently somewhere else after it has passed through — and leaves it there even once the variable is back where it started or has been switched off entirely. I know the stock line is that money is neutral in the long run but not in transition, and that's not what I mean, because the change I'm pointing at is still sitting there after the transition is over. And it isn't just "history matters" or sunk costs: the bite is that the thing you were told is a mere measuring rod turns out to have rearranged the furniture.

Second instance, modern, so I can check I'm not just misreading Hume. A regional tax break pulls a factory to a town; the break expires after five years and wages and prices fully adjust; by every neutral account the town should be back where it was. But the factory stays, the suppliers who followed it stay, and the town is permanently different. Nobody claims the break still exists. Its absence is compatable with the effect persisting indefinitely.

The wrinkle that has me stuck, and which I don't think is in Hume at all — probably my own addition, treat it as a separate question if you like: the durable rearrangement seems to depend on the *order* in which the extra money reaches different pairs of hands, not on the amount and not even on how long the whole thing takes. Hand it to the merchants first and they spend it into new habits and new working connections among the second group, and those connections outlive the money. Hand the identical sum to the second group first and nothing durable happens, because they just buy existing goods at existing prices and it all washes out. Same total, same duration, opposite residue.

So — does this have a name? I'm after the carving, not just a label. What exactly is the phenomenon, what are the closest existing concepts and how does each one fail to cover it, and where are the edges: does it still count when the residue is a habit or a network rather than a physical asset? does it count when the trigger was never declared neutral in the first place, or does the declared-neutrality bit matter to what's interesting here? If nothing established fits, say so plainly and coin something with conditions attached.

No bibliography or reading list, please — I don't want to chase sources, I want concepts. And if you're unsure whether a term is genuinely established versus half-remembered, flag that instead of dressing it up.
