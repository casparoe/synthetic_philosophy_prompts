Act as a philosopher of biology who's spent years working on what 'information' talk in the life sciences actually commits you to, and please think this through carefully rather than giving me a two-paragraph shrug — start by brainstorming a couple of candidate principles and noting what each one gets wrong, and only then commit to one. Context: I'm a computational biologist, I build cell-free translation systems for a living, so this isn't an armchair question for me, and I keep getting pulled into arguments on both sides — with science journalists who think DNA is a book and with friends in philosophy who think the whole vocabulary is borrowed mush. I've realised I don't have a single account of when DNA talk is legitimately about codes and when it's just borrowed words, so here's me going through cases and reporting what I actually believe, including the ones that obviously don't fit together:

- The codon table — 64 codons mapping onto ~20 amino acids plus stops — is a code, no hesitation. Nothing else in the cell comes close.
- 'The gene for curly fur', 'your DNA contains the instructions for building you': not fine, I actively correct this in outreach talks.
- The ribosome plus the tRNAs working through an mRNA: yes, that is translation, in the same sense that a person translates French.
- When my lab reassigns a stop codon to a synthetic amino acid, I say with zero irony that we rewrote the genetic code. Strong yes — and yet it's the exact same molecules as the case two above, where I'm a hardliner. That pair is the one I can't explain to myself.
- Tree rings carry information about past rainfall. True in the indicating sense, but there's no alphabet and nothing reads them, so not a code.
- An ant pheromone trail: other ants demonstrably read it and adjust behaviour. Still not a code. So readership can't be my criterion, since I denied it to the tree rings for having no reader and to the trail despite having one.
- Methylation marks that get read by protein machinery and change expression: I want to call that a second channel, but then genome-plus-epigenome becomes the code and I've quietly conceded most of what I deny in the 'instructions for building you' case.
- An engineered toggle switch on a plasmid: that is a program in the engineer's sense, full stop, I write its state table on the whiteboard. Same DNA as case two again.
- mRNA secondary structure changing how efficiently translation proceeds: just physics and chemistry, definitely not a code, even though the sequence determines it.
- The codon assignments being arbitrary or conventional: I think arbitrariness is what earns the word 'code', but I also think people oversell it — there are stereochemical affinities and error-buffering patterns in the table, so 'arbitrary' isn't clean either.
- Calling the genome a recipe rather than a blueprint: recipe is fine, blueprint isn't. But if recipe is fine I don't see why 'instructions' isn't, and that bugs me.
- DNA carrying information about the environments its lineage was selected in: genuinely informative about the past, but that's not coding for the organism, and I think people slide between the two senses constantly.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of genuinely different candidate principles, that captures this pattern? My own half-formed guess is that it has to be about arbitrariness of the mapping plus something about who's doing the reading, but those two criteria pull apart in exactly the cases I flagged (the pheromone trail, the rewritten codon, the toggle switch), so I don't trust it.
