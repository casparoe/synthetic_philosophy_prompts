For several years I have been sketching an institutional design that I believe answers a standing complaint about holding people to account for their own risky choices, and I have now written down the views underneath it so that someone can tell me whether they hang together. The design, briefly. Catastrophic injury and disability cover is automatic for every adult, funded by a flat percentage of income that does not vary with anyone's health, genetics, or activities. Above that sits a supplemental rider required for a schedule of designated high-risk pursuits — riding a motorcycle on public roads, backcountry skiing, amateur fighting — and the price of the rider is set by a pre-registered mechanism rather than by an administrator: the participant stakes money against her own assessed probability of serious injury, and the odds she is willing to accept determine her premium. Someone who declines the mandate altogether and is later badly injured receives the social minimum and nothing further, on the ground that her position is then genuinely the product of her own choice rather than of her luck.

The commitments behind it:

1. Inequality as such has no disvalue, and I take the levelling-down objection seriously. What is objectionable is that some people end up far worse off than others through no doing of their own.
2. The dividing line runs through the decision, not the outcome. A cost belongs to a person if she accepted the risk knowingly in advance; misfortune that arrives unchosen does not.
3. Universal automatic cover converts misfortune into something chosen, since afterwards nobody is exposed to a catastrophe she did not sign up for.
4. Self-reported probabilities are worthless, because people are overconfident and strategic — but that is a problem about incentives, not about the standard. Given a well-designed mechanism, the odds a person actually accepts are a faithful read on what she chose.
5. Nothing in a person's brute-luck endowment — her genome, her upbringing, her native appetite for risk — should show up in what she ends up with. This is the whole point of the exercise.
6. A taste she identifies with is hers to pay for. A temperament she was born with is not.

I would like an audit, not a lecture. Which of these genuinely pull against one another, and which only appear to? Two places where my confidence has slipped: the rider price leaves me uneasy and I cannot yet say which commitment it threatens, and I have been deploying (3) as my reply to people who say this whole approach lets the imprudent drown, though I am no longer sure it is a reply rather than a restatement of my own conclusion. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad; for each, give the smallest concession that repairs it and say what that concession costs; and then tell me which single revision buys the most coherence across the set. If some commitment can only be saved by making the mechanism counterfactual rather than real — priced in imagination instead of in money — say so plainly, and say what is lost by that retreat.
