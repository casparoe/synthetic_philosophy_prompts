so, context: I'm two years into a history PhD and my dissertation is a comparative thing about why two mid-nineteenth century port cities ended up with quarantine regimes that diverged pretty sharply even though they were dealing with roughly the same disease threats and had similar commercial interests. My supervisor made me write down, in advance, what I actually think historical explanation *is*, because she said the methods chapter always goes wrong when people write it after the fact and then retrofit a philosophy to justify what they did. So here's my package, and I'd like to know whether it holds together.

(1) The covering-law model doesn't work for history. Hempel's sketch reply fails too: when you try to fill in the missing generalization you either get something trivially true ("states under fiscal strain sometimes retrench") or something the historian would actively dispute, and neither can be doing the explanatory work.
(2) But I'm not going over to the other side — I don't think narrative is a sui generis form of understanding with its own logic. There's just causal explanation, and what we call a narrative is a description of a causal process unfolding in time. One kind of explanation, not two.
(3) At the same time I do hold something like a "following" criterion: a narrative explains to the extent that a reader can follow it, each step landing as unsurprising given what came before. And I think you can have two accounts with the *same* causal content where one explains and the other doesn't — the well-configured story versus a chronicle of the same events in the same order.
(4) Historical explanations answer contrastive questions, why-this-rather-than-that, and the contrast class is set by the historian's interests, not by the world.
(5) I don't think explanation in terms of what agents believed, wanted and intended needs to be cashed out in non-mental terms to count as causal; but I do think the mechanism vocabulary I want to use has to be able to house mental states rather than treat them as a special case.
(6) Individual historical explanations can't be confirmed or refuted in isolation — they get support as part of a larger body of interpretive claims, and I'm okay with that.

The one I actually lose sleep over is (4) sitting next to (2): if the question is partly up to me, haven't I snuck subjectivity into a position that's supposed to be naturalist about explanation? I suspect I'm wrong about that, but tell me either way. What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature — I know roughly who says what, so please don't spend words re-explaining the debate. Go through the pairs, tell me which ones genuinely conflict and rank them by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each real conflict say what the cheapest revision is and what that revision costs me — i.e. whether buying consistency means giving up something I'd no longer recognise as my view. Where a pair only looks like it clashes, say so explicitly and say why it dissolves, because I'd rather not tear apart (2) over a non-problem. And if the whole package is fine as it stands, I'd like to hear that too, with reasons, but I doubt it.
