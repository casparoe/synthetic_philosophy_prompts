I'm a TA for a philosophy of medicine course and I've got a stack of short-answer responses to grade. Here's the question and one of the answers.

Question (worth 10 points): In roughly 250 words, distinguish naturalism from normativism about disease, and say whether Boorse's biostatistical theory counts as a naturalist view. Then explain what Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis is supposed to add to the picture, and whether adding it costs Wakefield his naturalism.

Student's answer:
"The debate is about whether disease is a value-free biological kind or whether you can't identify it at all without appealing to what a given society counts as bad. Naturalists like Boorse hold that disease is an internal state that drops a person's functioning below the statistically typical level for their reference class, where reference classes are set by things like age and sex. Normativists hold that a deviation only becomes a disease once it's judged undesirable, which is an evaluative call, so theres no value-free concept to be had. Boorse's theory is clearly on the naturalist side — he wants to ground disease in biology rather than in what we happen to disapprove of. But he doesn't really get away from values. Since normality is statistical and always relative to a reference class, and the theorist is the one who picks out which classes to use, his account turns out to be normativism in disguise. He also treats disease as the value-loaded concept tied to suffering and social roles, with illness as the purely descriptive biological one, so he's conceding the point anyway.

Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis tries to hold both together: something is a disorder only if an internal mechanism fails to do the job it was naturally selected for, and that failure harms the person by the lights of social norms. So the dysfunction half is science and the harm half is values. But once you let values in like that you've basically given up on naturalism and become a normativist, which I think is also why clinicians like his account better. Both views run into trouble with mental disorder, where it's hardest to say what a mechanism was for."
