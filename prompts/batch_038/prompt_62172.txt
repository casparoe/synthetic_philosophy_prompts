You are advising me in the capacity of an experienced teacher of the political economy of famine — someone who has taught Sen's entitlement analysis both to economists and to students with no economics at all, and who has watched the same misunderstandings form in the same order every time. I am not asking you to explain the material to me. I am asking you to design how I should explain it.

**The setting.** One eighty-minute session in a second-year interdisciplinary course on poverty and public policy. The students have had a single introductory economics course (supply, demand, equilibrium, a little welfare economics) and no history or political philosophy; roughly two thirds are policy majors. The session's title question is: are famines natural disasters, or political crimes? The content I need them to leave with is Sen's entitlement analysis — that a famine can occur without any significant decline in aggregate food availability per head, because what kills people is the collapse of particular groups' ability to command food through the legal means open to them — together with the related claim that it is the absence of electoral accountability and of an uncensored press, rather than the absence of food, that allows a famine to run its course.

**The complication.** My department has asked me to build the session around the Chinese famine of the Great Leap Forward (1959–61) rather than around Bengal in 1943, on the grounds that Bengal lets students conclude too easily that famine is always a matter of food existing somewhere and being withheld from the people who need it. I accept the constraint, but I want to know what it costs me: the Chinese case involves a genuine collapse in production alongside the procurement, the restrictions on movement out of the countryside, and the silencing of internal critics. Tell me whether a case in which both mechanisms are present can still carry an argument whose whole point is that they come apart. Note also that Bengal is itself empirically contested — there is a substantial literature holding that per capita availability did fall there, and that wartime colonial policy caused much of the fall — so I cannot simply retreat to Bengal as the clean example.

**What I want from you.**

1. *Brainstorm first.* Sketch two or three candidate architectures for the session — concept-first, case-first, or a deliberate collision between the students' incoming view and a case chosen to break it — and say what turns on the choice. Then commit to one and justify it briefly.
2. *The dependency structure.* The components in the order they must go in, each with one line on what must already be in place for it to be intelligible. I need your judgment on three sequencing questions specifically: where the availability/entitlement distinction must land relative to the first narrative of what actually happened, and what goes wrong if it comes after; where the natural-trigger-versus-avoidable-deaths point goes; and where the press-and-elections claim goes, given that students will hear it as a claim about the goodwill of politicians.
3. *The carrying case.* What it must exhibit and must not exhibit, including whether I should run one case all the way through or bring in a second as a foil and, if a foil, at exactly which moment. If the Chinese case cannot do the work alone, say so plainly and tell me what supplement it needs.
4. *The misconception map.* The errors students predictably bring or form, in the order they will surface, each with the point in the sequence where it must be headed off and one sentence I could actually say there. Cover at minimum: the inference from "the food was available" to "the famine was therefore intended"; the belief that Sen's claim is that food supply never matters; the slide from "this was political" to "identifiable people are guilty of murder"; the assumption that once a trigger such as drought or blight is named, the question of avoidability is settled; and the tendency to hear the press-and-elections claim as a story about which regime is nicer rather than about how knowledge of suffering reaches whoever could stop it. Then add any misconception you judge more damaging than these that I have not thought of.
5. *The check for understanding.* One question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that separates students who have absorbed the analytical distinction from those who have only acquired a slogan. Say what a good answer sounds like, what a slogan-answer sounds like, and what the most likely wrong-but-interesting answer is and what it reveals.
6. *What to refuse.* The parts of the apparatus I should explicitly decline to teach at this level — I assume the formal machinery of exchange entitlement mappings is one — with the line of justification I can give students for each omission, so that leaving it out reads as a decision rather than a gap.

Think carefully about the relation between the causal question (why did these people starve?) and the normative question (who, if anyone, is to blame?). I suspect nearly all the damage in this session comes from students running the two together, and your plan must keep them apart without ever suggesting that the line between facts and values is neat. Do not give me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations: I want the plan, not the sources.
