I'm a fourth-year PhD student in wildlife ecology and I'm the graduate-student member of my university's Land Stewardship Committee — six of us: two faculty ecologists, the campus sustainability director, a trustee, the woods manager, and me. I've been asked to write the recommendation memo for Thursday because apparently I'm the only one who reads everyone's emails all the way through. The case: the university owns a 46-acre tract behind the ag campus, roughly half of it an old pasture that's closing in under autumn olive and multiflora rose, the rest young oak-hickory. Our charge says we manage for ecological integrity, biodiversity, and teaching/research use, consistent with the donor's restriction of the gift to "native habitat restoration" (counsel says brush-hogging counts). The woods manager has a five-year plan: brush-hog about 12 acres on a rotating four-year mosaic and herbicide the cut stumps, to hold open early-successional shrubland for a state-listed warbler — his estimate is we go from about four breeding territories to maybe twenty-five, plus a suite of other shrubland species. The trustee, who's a birder, loves it. The sustainability director likes it because it moves our biodiversity metric. Then someone forwarded the plan to a student animal-ethics group and now I've got two members saying opposite things at volume. The senior ecologist thinks welfare considerations are sentimental and outside our mandate — ecosystems are the unit, individuals are fungible. The student rep thinks the right answer is to do nothing, because shrubland management means mowing through nests, killing voles and invertebrates by the ton, and because if you actually follow the logic through, creating habitat creates animals, and most animals that get created die as nestlings or larvae of starvation, cold, or predation, so a bigger population is a bigger pile of suffering rather than a win. The woods manager's counter is that doing nothing isn't neutral either, it just means an invasive monotype with lower total abundance and more nest predation, but I don't think anyone's actually argued about whether that's true here.

And the annoying thing is I think she's half right and I can't say which half. I don't know how to count the animals who would only exist because of our decision — cost, benefit, or just not the kind of thing you add up — and I'm even less sure about the invertebrates, which on this tract is where like 95% of the individuals are, and whose welfare we can't measure at all. There's also a second item on Thursday's agenda that's made the whole thing worse: an adjacent developer wants to buy a 8-acre corner of the tract for a stormwater basin, which would pave and grade the densest shrubland we have, and the student rep said in committee, to everyone's visible discomfort, that on her reasoning we should say yes to the basin because it removes animals. Nobody answered her.

So — write the memo I'll circulate Wednesday night. About 900–1200 words, addressed to the committee, with a bottom line the six of us could actually vote on: approve as written, approve with specific modifications, defer, or reject. If it's modifications, name them concretely (mowing windows relative to nest timing, mosaic size and rotation, whether to swap herbicide for repeated cutting, whether the riparian strip stays untouched, whether we need a wildlife-vet or an animal-ethics person signed off on the schedule) and give a timeline that fits the five-year cycle and the donor restriction. Don't duck the hard parts: take the student rep's argument seriously enough that she'd recognize it and still feel argued with, and give the senior ecologist's mandate objection its strongest form rather than treating "outside our charge" as a way to stop thinking. Say explicitly what the memo's decision rule is under uncertainty — how much weight goes on nestling and invertebrate welfare given how little we know, and why that weighting rather than another — and tell the committee what we'd monitor and what result would make us reopen the call. And it has to handle the basin parcel consistently: whatever principle the memo uses on the brush-hogging plan, it should say what that principle implies about letting 8 acres get graded, including if the implication is uncomfortable. No citations or bibliography, I'll bolt on references myself if anyone asks, and don't spend words explaining what the committee is or defining basic terms — we all know the site. Honestly its not clear to me a good answer exists, so if the memo concludes that the defensible move is a narrower version of the plan plus a real monitoring commitment, argue for that rather than splitting the difference to be polite.
