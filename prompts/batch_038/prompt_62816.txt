For eleven years I have run a free Thursday-evening discussion group in the back room of our town library. Fourteen regulars, ages roughly twenty-five to seventy, none of them with philosophy training beyond what I have picked up myself, which is a small amount and unsystematic. We have a long table, a wall we are permitted to pin things to, and a colour printer. I never lecture. I hand people something to do and we argue afterwards about what happened.

This month's subject is forgery, and I want help designing the session: ninety minutes, and about forty dollars' worth of printing and materials.

Here is where my own thinking has got to, and it is the thinking I want the group to arrive at for itself rather than be told. Three things I cannot reconcile.

First, when I was twenty-six I paid real money for a small painting that turned out to be a later copy. I had admired it for over a year. From the afternoon I found out, I could not look at it the same way — and nothing about the object had changed. I want that shift to happen to the group in the room, to them, inside ninety minutes.

Second, I have come to suspect two different questions are being run together: whether a thing is good to look at, and whether the making of it was good. A convincing forgery might score perfectly on the first and nothing on the second. What I cannot explain is why the second should matter to someone who will never meet the maker and cannot see the difference.

Third, I cannot explain why an identical copy seems better or worse according to why it was made. A sign-writer's copy done as a gift for a friend's child feels harmless; the same brushwork aimed at a buyer's wallet feels like a theft, though the paint is the same. That pushes me toward saying the value sits somewhere in the history rather than on the surface — but if it sits in the history, then "look at it more carefully" cannot be the answer people keep giving me.

The practical asset I have is a local sign-writer who does faux finishes and mural copies and says he can copy a small painting I own closely enough that the pair would beat most people standing side by side. If he cannot, my fallback is a stack of identical colour prints of the same image, differing only in the story attached to each. Tell me plainly whether that fallback still does the job or quietly destroys the point.

What I need from you:

- A full running order with minute allocations, a materials list, and the actual wording of every card, label, and instruction sheet the group will handle.
- A mechanic in which the group must commit publicly to a stated rule about what does and does not change a work's value, and is then handed cases it has not seen, so that its own rule can be turned against it. Include a scoring scheme that needs no money.
- At least one segment confronting the most common escape — the claim that the good forgeries were never really as good, that connoisseurs could always tell, that the Dutchman who painted fake Vermeers is a special case — arranged so the group discovers what is and is not left of its rule if that claim is true.
- A segment on why the whole trick seems not to work for a poem, a piece of music, or a recipe, where the group notices the asymmetry on its own.
- A debrief tying each phase to the idea it was built to produce, in plain language; no vocabulary introduced before the group has earned it.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it with this particular group, and what I should do at that moment. Two of my regulars will insist from the outset that a good picture is a good picture and will refuse to budge; I would rather the design use that than argue with it.
- An honest note on which part of the problem the activity cannot reproduce, and that I will have to supply in words.

Two constraints. Do not let the session slide into market value: I am not interested in what a thing would fetch, and the design should make price the wrong currency rather than an appealing one. And do not give me a reading list or references — I want the session, not the syllabus, and I have no way of chasing books down.
