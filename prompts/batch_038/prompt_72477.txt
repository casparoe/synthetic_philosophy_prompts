I've come to hold that the legal category of genocide should be retired, and I want a map of that position's exposure rather than a defense of it.

Here is the case as I see it. The Convention's definition turns on a specific intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part, and that mental element is the hinge of the crime. In practice it can be established only in the paradigm cases and only after years of litigation about questions that seem beside the point — whether the men killed in one municipality amounted to a substantial part of a group — while the very same conduct is chargeable, sooner and with fewer contested elements, as a crime against humanity, as persecution, or as a war crime. The label then produces two further distortions: atrocities that fail to qualify are treated as lesser, and officials acquire an incentive to fight about vocabulary rather than act, precisely because the word carries a duty to prevent that the alternatives do not. Hence my view: keep the concept's content as a moral and sociological category, including the broader Lemkin-style idea of destroying a group's pattern of life by means other than killing, and let prosecution and diplomacy run on crimes against humanity, which reach political and social groups the Convention excludes and require no purpose to destroy.

What would have to be true for that to be wrong? I'd like the objections sorted by how decisive they would be against me, not merely listed. Which facts about how the category has actually been used — convictions secured or unavailable, the way governments have avoided or invoked the term, what the alternative charges have and have not delivered, the one case in which a state was found to have breached the duty to prevent genocide while escaping responsibility for the genocide itself — would be fatal, and which merely awkward? What does my view predict that a defender of the legal category does not, such that the two could come apart on evidence? Is there a harm, or an obligation attaching specifically to this crime, that crimes against humanity cannot articulate, and does any such uniqueness survive scrutiny? And which of these would a serious proponent of my position concede as genuinely damaging instead of absorbing with a qualification — if the view has been made compatible with any outcome, tell me that, and tell me whether it matters for a claim of this sort.

Please commit to a judgment about whether the position survives, and rank the defeaters from fatal to inconvenient, rather than presenting a balanced survey. If you think my framing is itself mistaken — that I've assumed what the category does, or that retiring it isn't a coherent proposal — say so plainly instead of working politely inside my assumptions.
