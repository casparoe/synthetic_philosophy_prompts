I run a monthly reading group of about eight people — two political theorists, a city-planning doctoral student, and a clutch of software people, myself included, who arrived at the philosophy of technology by way of the rationalist blogging ecosystem rather than by way of a department. Next month's session is on whether artifacts have politics, and I have volunteered, somewhat rashly, to put my own view on the table in writing beforehand so that the group can spend the evening attacking it rather than reconstructing it. Below is that statement. I have read more on both sides of this question than is entirely healthy, and my standing worry about my own positions applies here: that a package looks coherent only because every item in it is vaguely pro-technology and vaguely anti-authority, and that the coherence evaporates the moment someone pushes on the joints. I would like you to push on the joints.

My commitments, numbered so they can be targeted individually:

(1) I accept Langdon Winner's first sense of the thesis. A design decision can be a political act, and my paradigm case is the deliberately low overpasses on Long Island's parkways, which kept buses — and hence poorer and Black New Yorkers — away from Jones Beach. What makes the case political for me is that a live contest over access was settled inside the artifact instead of in a legislature.

(2) I reject hard determinism, including the Marxian register in which a given kind of mill hands you a given kind of society. Artifacts do not produce social orders. I think interpretive flexibility and the role of relevant social groups in fixing a design's meaning is broadly correct, and that histories of technology written as though the outcome were inevitable are just badly argued.

(3) I nonetheless accept Winner's second sense: certain technologies are in practice hostable only by certain political arrangements. Large-scale, capital-intensive, expertise-gated energy or weapons systems push toward centralized and hierarchical governance in a way that small-scale alternatives do not. I would defend this with something like Hughes's technological momentum rather than determinism — the technology narrows the feasible set of political forms without selecting a unique member of it.

(4) Metaphysically, I hold that the political properties of an artifact supervene on its physical and functional properties together with the social context of its deployment. Nothing about a design considered apart from how it is built and how it is used carries politics intrinsically.

(5) At the same time I am an intentionalist about the paradigm cases. What makes the parkways political rather than merely consequential is that someone resolved a conflict by building the resolution into the structure. Two physically indistinguishable overpasses — one built to exclude, one built to a standard clearance for engineering convenience — differ politically, even if they distribute access identically.

(6) I want to use political narrowly. A state of affairs is political only if it involves an actual contest over power that has been resolved. Uneven distributions of burden that nobody ever fought about I would call socially significant, or normatively loaded, but not political. I think this narrowness is what keeps the concept from swallowing all of ethics.

(7) Case verdict: a mid-sized city replaces its coin-operated parking meters with an app-only payment system. There was no contest; a procurement officer chose it for revenue and maintenance reasons; the effect is to price out residents without smartphones or bank accounts. I want to say this is a political design choice of exactly the same kind as the parkways, and I would defend that in the room.

(8) I have also read — from a European sociologist of technology whose name I cannot now recover, and whose details I have not checked — that the parkway story is shakier than it is usually told: that the clearances matched the standards of the day and that buses did in fact reach the beach. I do not think this embarrasses me. Even if the exclusion was never anyone's plan, the parkways remain political, because the exclusionary effect was foreseeable, was eventually noticed, and was permitted to stand for decades. Foreseen-and-uncorrected is, for me, sufficient.

(9) On responsibility I am a cheapest-avoider rather than a first-cause theorist: the political effects of a technology fall on whoever could most cheaply have altered the design or the deployment. I am drawn to this partly because of (2) — repurposing seems to me close to unlimited, so given enough time and ingenuity almost any artifact can be turned to purposes its designers never contemplated, and no design permanently locks in a politics. It follows, I grant, that original designers frequently lose the cheapest-avoider contest to later operators, users, and regulators.

(10) My practical program is nonetheless design-side. Institutions should audit the political content of artifacts the way they audit bridges for load: ex ante design review, and treating defaults, form factors, and technical standards as proper objects of public scrutiny rather than as engineering trivia best left to engineers.

Please treat this as an audit and not as a tutorial. Concretely: (a) identify which pairs or clusters genuinely pull against one another and rank them by severity, saying for each what would have to go and what I lose by giving it up; (b) identify which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and name the distinction that dissolves them, since I would rather not spend the evening defending those; (c) tell me whether either of my case verdicts is actually deliverable from the principles I have written down, or whether I am helping myself to a principle I have not stated — if there is such an unacknowledged lemma, state it explicitly and say whether it is compatible with (6), or whether adopting it quietly empties (6) of the narrowing work I want it to do; (d) close with the single revision that repairs the most damage at once, and be honest about how much of the position survives it. I am deliberately not telling you which of these I would most hate to surrender, and I would prefer that you not assume it. I also do not want a survey of the literature, a reading list, or the standard objections to Winner that I have already absorbed; where you need material beyond the ten items above, argue it out of those items rather than importing it.
