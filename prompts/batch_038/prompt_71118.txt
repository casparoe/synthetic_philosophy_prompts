I run a weekly proof clinic for our first-year structures course and at this point I've read something like forty student arguments that invoke Zorn's lemma, and I'd guess two thirds of them are broken in one of maybe four predictable ways. What I want is a one-page triage protocol they run on their own proof *before* they bring it to me: fifteen minutes tops, no set theory beyond what the course covers, nobody signs off on it, its purely diagnostic. I'd hand it out Monday.

Things it has to do:

- Give them a fixed sequence of slots to fill in, in the order you actually have to check them, with a hard STOP whenever a slot can't be filled. Most of the damage happens when somebody skips straight to "therefore there is a maximal element."
- One of the early slots has to be "is this collection actually a set," since Zorn doesn't reach into class-sized posets and students never notice.
- Make them write the chain-closure argument out in full instead of nodding at it: if you're taking unions of chains, why is the union still in your poset. The same slot should catch that the empty set is a chain, so the poset can't be empty either.
- There should be a step *before* they reach for Zorn at all where they check whether something cheaper does the job — plain induction, a diagonal argument, König's lemma, countable choice, dependent choice — and then a column at the end where they record how much choice the finished proof really used: none, countable, dependent, or full. Be straight with them that this last column often isn't something the protocol can settle and sometimes you just have to go ask a set theorist.
- Keep maximal vs. maximum alive. I've lost count of proofs where the student gets a maximal element and then claims it does something maximality doesn't give you.
- Ban the word "obviously." Half of them have been burned by that word already.

Then run it on four things, showing every slot as you go: the usual maximal ideal in a nonzero commutative ring with 1 proof, as a calibration so I can see it working on an argument that's actually fine; the Baire category theorem for complete metric spaces, where I expect it to tell me Zorn was overkill; a specimen I keep around, which is the poset of all finite subsets of N ordered by inclusion, where the student argues every chain has an upper bound because the union of a chain of finite sets is finite and concludes there's a maximal finite subset of N; and my own handout on bases for vector spaces, where I've been telling the class that countable choice is plenty because you only ever add one vector at a time. If the protocol says otherwise I'd rather hear it from the protocol than from a colleague at a talk.

Close with what the protocol can't do — where it'll wave through a bad proof, where it'll flag a good one, and which of the steps are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
