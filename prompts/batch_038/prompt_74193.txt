**Question 3.** Explain what the Dutch book argument and the money pump argument are each supposed to show about rational belief and rational preference. In each case, say what extra assumptions you need to get from "the agent is incoherent" to "the agent ends up worse off", and name one important thing these arguments do not establish.

**Student's answer:**

Both arguments are pragmatic rather than logical. They don't show that an incoherent agent believes a contradiction or prefers A and not-A; they show that incoherence makes you exploitable, and thats why its irrational.

The Dutch book argument starts from the idea that your credences show up in the bets you'd accept: if your credence in p is r, you'll pay up to r for a bet that returns 1 if p. So if you'd pay 60 cents for a dollar on heads and 60 cents for a dollar on tails, the bookie sells you both and takes 20 cents off you however the coin lands, because your credences add up to more than 1. And the theorem runs both ways: if your credences obey the axioms, no guaranteed-loss combination exists. There's also a diachronic version — if you don't update by conditionalising, a bookie can string bets together across time that are each acceptable when they're offered and jointly guarantee a loss.

The money pump is the preference version. Suppose your preferences run in a circle, A over B over C over A. Someone offers to swap your C for B if you hand over a small fee, then your B for A for another fee, then your A for C for a third. Each swap looks like an improvement by your own lights so you take it, and you finish where you started minus three fees. So intransitivity gets you pumped for the same reason incoherent credences get you booked: your own attitudes guarantee you end up worse off whatever you do. This works for any departure from expected utility maximisation, incompleteness included — if you can't rank two options, the pump just routes you round the gap and you still lose out, so the very same argument requires completeness.

On the limits: these arguments only show that you can be exploited. They don't show an incoherent agent will actually be worse off, since a sharp agent who sees the trap coming can just refuse to trade, and someone who refuses everything can't be booked or pumped whether they're coherent or not. And they don't get you the whole of decision theory: the representation theorems only say a coherent agent behaves as if she has a unique probability function and a unique utility function, not that her mind is literally built that way, and they say nothing about unbounded payoffs.
