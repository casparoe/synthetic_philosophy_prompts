Much of the modern Stoic revival answers the charge that the dichotomy of control licenses passivity with a single analogy: life is like poker — you don't choose your cards, only how you play them, and a serious player still plays hard for the pot. The same image then gets recruited to gloss the ancient practice of pursuing preferred indifferentials with a reserve, and to argue that Stoicism is fully compatible with ambitious worldly projects. I've spent years on rationality forums, so expected-value talk is my native tongue, and I'd rather audit the analogy than be reassured by it. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the cards, the dealer, the opponents, the chips, the pot, the decision to fold — then rank the disanalogies by how much damage each does to that anti-quietist use, separating load-bearing failures from decorative ones. I'm particularly suspicious that chips are fungible and cashable in a way moral worth is not, and that stack sizes make the game path-dependent in a way each moment of choice is not; tell me whether those suspicions are on target or beside the point. Does the conclusion survive? And would Cicero's archer or Epictetus's ball game carry the same work better — or is there a third image that would?
