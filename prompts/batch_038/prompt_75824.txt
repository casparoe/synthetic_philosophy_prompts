I am a second-year PhD student in political theory, and I am trying to turn a seminar paper on Rousseau's 1758 reply to d'Alembert into something that could stand as a dissertation chapter. My seminar is on Rousseau and his Enlightenment critics, and my disagreement with a classmate is what pushed me toward the reading below: she thinks the letter is essentially a piece of Genevan factional politics dressed up as moral philosophy, and that its arguments about the stage have no content that survives the removal of the polemical occasion. My response was to argue that there is a real theoretical core, but that it is not where readers usually look for it. I would like critical comments on the draft, not encouragement, and I am explicitly not after a bibliography — I have the secondary literature under control and would rather hear where the argument fails.

Here is the draft.

**Spectator-positioning: Rousseau's institutional argument against the stage**

Standard readings of the letter to d'Alembert place it inside the long Christian anti-theatrical tradition running through the seventeenth-century French moralists, or else explain it biographically, as an expression of Rousseau's quarrels with the encyclopedists and his own anxieties about appearing in public. On both readings the substantive charge concerns content: plays that depict vice license it, plays that ridicule virtue discredit it, and the pity one feels from a seat in the stalls is a cheap counterfeit of the pity one owes to actual sufferers.

My claim is that the load-bearing premise is not about content at all but about the role-structure of the institution. The theater assigns three standing positions — the one who pays, the one who performs, the one who watches — and Rousseau's objection is that a polity which routinizes this threefold division trains its members to experience public life as something staged for them by professionals. Three features of the text support this. First, the discussion of the actor is an objection to the position rather than to any role the actor happens to inhabit: Rousseau's complaint is that the actor sells his person, exhibits himself for money, and makes of himself a public instrument, and nothing in that complaint depends on which characters are performed. Second, the contrast Rousseau draws between the ancient spectacles — outdoor, religious, national, free, open to everyone — and modern paid entertainment in a closed room is a contrast in participation-structure, not in repertoire. Third, the alternative he offers at the end is a festival whose defining property is precisely that it has no audience: the assembled people are, in effect, a spectacle to one another, and the proposal is that the organizer need do no more than gather them around something in the middle of a public square. On this reading Rousseau is not a censor proposing to police representations but a theorist of what I am calling attentional infrastructure: institutions are to be judged by the participation-structure they impose, and content is secondary because content is downstream of who does what to whom.

The reading earns its keep by explaining three things a content-based reading cannot. It explains the asymmetry of scale: Rousseau concedes, roughly, that a theater in a great and already corrupt capital does comparatively little damage, whereas the danger is acute for a small republic whose citizens are supposed to exercise sovereignty themselves. If the harm lay in what is represented, that asymmetry would be inexplicable, since a bad representation is equally bad in Paris; but if the harm lies in displacing the citizen from an active position, then a place where no one is expected to act anyway has nothing to lose. It explains why comedy is attacked harder than tragedy: ridicule does not merely depict vice, it recruits everyone present into the act of laughing at virtue, an act performed from the spectator-position and repeated nightly. And it explains the otherwise baffling appearance of the Genevan cercles — those small face-to-face associations for talk and public business — in a letter about the stage. If my recollection is right, Rousseau defends them against d'Alembert's insinuation that they breed intrigue, and even acknowledges the resentment of the women excluded from them. On the structural reading they are the natural positive counterpart to the festival: another institution that generates no audience.

Two problems I cannot yet solve. The first is Rousseau's own position as a supplier. He wrote plays, at least one of them performed before the court, and in the preface to an early one he defends letters and the arts in a voice not far from his opponents'. If the objection is to the spectator-position, what about the author-position? Writing for the stage makes one a participant in the very institution being condemned, and Rousseau plainly did not think so; the structural reading therefore owes an account of authorship that does not look like special pleading.

The second problem is worse. Scale may be doing the work I attributed to structure. A festival in a city of twenty-odd thousand is not the same institution as a festival in a metropolis, and the absence of an audience may be an epiphenomenon of small size rather than an independent property. If so, Rousseau's real thesis is a thesis about scale, with the theater as its most vivid symptom, and the careful institutional argumentation of the letter is decoration. My reading would then have an attractive story but no distinctive content, since it would predict his verdicts only where the structural and the scale readings already agree.

What would settle the matter is a case where the two readings diverge. My candidate is the cercles: they are small-scale, yet they also generate something like an audience — some men talk, others listen — and they exclude half the population. If Rousseau's endorsement survives their exclusivity, structure is not the whole story; if he qualifies it precisely on grounds of who is admitted and who is left listening, structure wins. I suspect the second, but I am aware that I may be reading the answer off my own theory.

My questions:

1. On accuracy. Please check the attributions the argument rests on: that Rousseau treats a great corrupt capital differently from a small republic; that the actor discussion is about the position rather than the roles played; that he defends the cercles and what exactly he concedes about the women excluded from them. Flag anything I have misremembered, anachronized, or overstated, including the claim that Rousseau departs from the seventeenth-century anti-theatrical tradition rather than continuing it.

2. On whether the structural/content distinction is genuinely available in the text or is my imposition. Is there a rival reading — in terms of transparency, sincerity, appearing versus being, amour-propre — that does the same explanatory work with less machinery? If so, should I argue against it explicitly rather than leaving it unstated?

3. On the two problems in the fourth and fifth paragraphs. Is the authorship problem fatal, or is there a principled reply that does not depend on excusing Rousseau's own career? And is scale-versus-structure a genuine crux, or have I misdescribed the relationship — that is, are scale and participation-structure necessarily linked in his thought, so that trying to separate them is a category error?

4. On the proposed test in the final paragraph. Does the cercles case actually discriminate between the two readings, or is it too weak? If it is weak, suggest a better divergence case.

5. On priorities. The chapter will run to about seven thousand words. Which of the three explanatory payoffs is the most vulnerable to attack, and which of the two problems should be promoted from an admission of difficulty into the centerpiece of the paper?

6. Finally, a worry about register. Does framing all of this as attentional infrastructure make the chapter sound like contemporary media theory wearing an eighteenth-century costume? If it does, tell me how to keep the conceptual purchase without the anachronistic vocabulary — my classmate's charge of presentism is one I expect to face in the viva.
