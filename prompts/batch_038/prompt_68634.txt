im TAing a section of formal epistemology next week and we're getting to the accuracy-first argument for probabilism — the one where, if your degrees of belief break the probability axioms, some coherent alternative beats them in every possible world by the lights of basically any strictly proper scoring rule (brier is fine). my students keep mushing two seperate arguments together and i want something to read aloud at the top of section that keeps them apart:

(1) the dominance one: there's an alternative credence function more accurate than yours at every single world, so switch — and this needs no opinion whatsoever about which world you're actually in, no expectation, no weighting.
(2) the expected-accuracy one: a proper scoring rule makes truth-telling optimal by your own lights, so reporting your real credences is what maximizes your expected accuracy given your own probabilities. this one obviously does ride on your estimates.

so: write me a pantoum, four quatrains, and make the form do that work rather than just the words. the lines each stanza hands down to the next stanza — the inherited ones — should be the dominance claims, the stuff that stays true no matter what world the stanza happens to be set in; the fresh lines each stanza contributes should be the ones that lean on the agent's own expectations, so a reader can feel which material is portable across worlds and which is not. if you can pull it off, let one inherited line mean something different the fourth time it comes back around, same wording, without the poem pointing at the trick. and land the ending honestly: dominance gets you coherence and nothing more — it doesn't say which coherent function to move to, and it says nothing about how to respond to evidence — so the last stanza should feel like the argument stopping short instead of wrapping up neat. if you use the usual move where the poem's own first and third lines come back to close it, make the first line the one that changed.

no preaching, no "so be rational, kids," no naming philosophers in the verse itself. someone who knows epistemic utility theory should be able to point at specific lines and say which premise is which; someone who's never heard of it should just think it's a good poem about not knowing which world you're in.
