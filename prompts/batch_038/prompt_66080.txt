I have been working through a chapter of a recent institute report on evaluating the mental lives of deployed language models, and its policy recommendations appear to rest on a particular account of Dennett's intentional stance. The relevant passage runs as follows:

"Dennett's intentional stance is the fallback we adopt when a system cannot be made sense of any other way: unable to predict it from its physics or from its design, we treat it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires and ask what such an agent would do next. Two consequences matter for artificial intelligence. First, the stance is a predictive instrument and nothing more. To say that a system has beliefs is to say only that the instrument happens to work on it; it asserts nothing about the system's inner states, and beyond the success of our predictions there is no further fact of the matter. Dennett's later appeal to real patterns does not soften this: patterns are features of our descriptions rather than of the world, and a description that works is not thereby a discovery. Second, because the stance is empirical, it can be tested directly. We accordingly propose a doxastic audit. Administer a battery of probe pairs whose correct answers stand in entailment relations to one another, compute the system's conflict rate across paraphrases of each probe, and declare the system a true believer just in case that rate falls below a fixed threshold. Ascriptions are made to the trained parameters rather than to the model-in-context, since the context is merely a device for eliciting beliefs the system already has. Present-day models fail this audit routinely, contradicting themselves across trivially reworded questions. The intentional stance therefore licenses, rather than blocks, the conclusion that no such model has beliefs, and talk of what a model believes should be dropped from technical documentation."

I am inclined to think this is an unfair account, but I cannot locate the unfairness, and a part of me suspects that its closing sentence may be right even if the argument for it is not. Could you perform an accuracy audit of the passage? Specifically:

1. Work through it claim by claim and sort each into one of three categories: what a careful defender of the intentional stance would accept, what is a simplification that loses something load-bearing, and what is simply a misstatement of the view.
2. The passage presents the stance as a resort adopted only once the physical and design stances have failed. Is that its actual status, and does anything in the rest of the argument depend on getting this right?
3. Ascription on this view is said to be idealizing. If so, what exactly does a system's self-contradiction establish -- and does the proposed audit measure what the view says belief attribution consists in, or something else?
4. Consider the gloss on real patterns, namely that patterns belong to our descriptions and not to the world. Is that a fair rendering, and does the report's instrumentalist reading survive its correction?
5. The report ascribes beliefs to the trained parameters rather than to the model-in-context. On this framework, what would the corresponding desires or purposes be ascribed to, and can the two be prised apart in the way the report assumes?
6. Finally, does the closing recommendation follow from the corrected premises? If some version of the audit could be made to work, what would it have to look like, and what would it be unable to show even if it succeeded?

Please do not give me a reading list; I am interested in the analysis, and I would rather you reason from the concepts themselves than gesture at further literature.
