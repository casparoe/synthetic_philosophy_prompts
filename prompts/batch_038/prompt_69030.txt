I run grantmaking at a smallish foundation (~$25m a year) that switched over to scoring every proposal in wellbeing-adjusted life years per dollar, and its gone about how you'd expect. Twice a year some proposal comes in with a cost effectiveness number so far off the chart that it makes the rest of the portfolio look pointless — usually something serving a few dozen people in genuinely awful states, where the self-reported swings are enormous. Half my board wants to follow the numbers wherever they lead. The other half says we're getting mugged by whoever is loudest about their own suffering, and that this is just the utility-monster objection wearing a spreadsheet. Neither side will move and I'm the one who has to write the memo.

So: build me a triage procedure I can actually run on a proposal in an afternoon, with no philosopher on staff and one part-time health economist on retainer. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it outputs "the number stands, fund it," where it outputs "discount or cap the wellbeing estimate" and by roughly how much, and where it stops and hands the thing to the board because it's a values call dressed up as a measurement. Be explicit about interpersonal comparison: when is a 9-point reported improvement in one person's life worth more than a 0.3-point improvement in two thousand people's, and what evidence would distinguish a real monster from an artifact of the scale, of hedonic adaptation, of who bothered to answer the survey, or of the baseline we picked? Flag every step that's a fudge rather than a finding.

Then run it on three: (a) a palliative program for ~40 people a year with a rare, extremely painful degenerative condition — huge reported relief, ruinous cost per person; (b) an immersive wilderness program giving a small number of young adults what they describe as life-defining peak experiences, big short-term spikes, no evidence of durable gains at two years; (c) a dirt-cheap app-delivered CBT program, tiny measured gain per user, massive reach.

Be straight with me — at least one of those three should come out in a way my board will hate, and I want you to say which and why. And tell me where the procedure sneaks in prioritarianism or some other fairness constraint that total-wellbeing maximization was supposed to make unnecessary. If Nozick were looking at my flowchart over my shoulder, which box would he point at?
