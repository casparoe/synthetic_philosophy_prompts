Third year in a philosophy of medicine PhD, and my quals have a whole section on naturalism versus normativism about disease. There's one hard-nosed normativist on my committee and I'm fairly confident she's going to run the reference-class line at me, so I want to spar against the strongest version of the other side first. Honeslty I lean normativist myself right now, which is exactly why I need the naturalist to not go easy on me.

So here's the setup. Play a committed defender of the biostatistical theory — health as normal functional ability, where "normal" means statistical typicality within a reference class and the functions themselves are fixed by the species' typical design, with its goals of survival and reproduction. Not a caricature and not a fence-sitter: you think the theory is true and that the value-ladenness objection comes from sloppy reading. You may concede small points — sure, the choice of reference-class variables isn't dictated by biology alone, and sure, plenty of conditions are harmful for social rather than biological reasons — but you never concede the main one, which is that "disease" can be given a purely descritpive, value-free scientific core, with all the evaluation living in the separate question of whether we care about a given dysfunction. You can run your own cases and thought experiments at me, you can turn the tables and ask what my positive alternative is supposed to be, and if I say something confused you should say so bluntly. Stay in character the whole time: no out-of-character notes, no meta-commentary about the state of the debate, and don't send me off to read anybody — argue from the theory and from examples. I'll say "stop" when I want out. Keep each reply to about two paragraphs so it feels like a conversation, and open every one with your bottom line — the thesis you're actually pressing on me — in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no scene-setting and no restating my question back at me.

My opening move: your statistics aren't doing any work. You relativize function to reference classes, age and sex, and the reason you slice the population exactly that way is that slicing it that way happens to reproduce the classifications we already make about who counts as sick. So the value judgment sits upstream of the statistics, not downstream of them — you're not discovering dysfunction, you're picking a comparison group whose typical performance matches what you wanted to call healthy and then reporting the match back as a finding. Prove me wrong by naming a case where the machinary hands you a verdict that flatly contradicts what everybody, you included, antecedently takes to be healthy or diseased, and where you bite the bullet and say the verdict is right and the intuition is wrong. If you can't produce one, your theory is just a statistical redescription of our norms with a lab coat on.
