My friend does a lot of apologetics reading and every time I bring up the Euthyphro dilemna he waves it off with the same example: asking whether God measures up to some standard of good above him is like asking whether the standard metre bar in Paris is a metre long. The bar just is the measure, so the question never gets a grip, and there's nothing arbitrary about it either — both horns collapse at once. He adds that God's commands stay informative the way the bar is informative: you can lay your table against it and learn something, so finding out what God commands tells you real things even though "God is good" turns out to be empty.

I don't think that works but I can't say cleanly why yet. Can you lay out the mapping first — bar for God, unit for goodness, measuring for obeying, and whatever else has a counterpart — then sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually damage his argument and the ones that are only surface? Two I keep circling: whether "God is good" really comes out trivial in the same way "this bar is a metre long" does, since nobody stipulated the bar into existence the way he's stipulating God, and whether a standard you can't physically lay alongside the case can do the epistemic work the bar does when readings of it are contested. And if the measurement picture fails, is there a better analogy for the relation he's after, or is the honest verdict that no measurement analogy can carry it?
