Take a choice between (A) a project that's certain to do a modest amount of good for about a billion people currently alive, and (B) a project with a one-in-a-quadrillion shot at producing something like 10^30 units of value that otherwise does nothing except eat the budget. Expected value says B wins by six orders of magnitude, and my reading group has been going around on exactly this setup for six weeks with zero movement, which is extra annoying because nobody in the room disputes the arithmetic. What we dispute is the fixes. Somebody caps the value function so 10^30 isn't much better than 10^12; the reply is that a cap forces you to say a 90% shot at 10^30 is no better than a 90% shot at 10^12, and nobody wants that bullet. Somebody else discounts tiny probabilities; the reply is that now there's a hair's-breadth change in the odds that flips the whole verdict, and where exactly the hair sits is unmotivated. Somebody invokes recklessness; the reply is that this restates the intuition instead of explaining it. And the move that always comes back, from the B side, is that whatever principle you write to kill the quadrillion-to-one case seems to bite on much more ordinary large-stakes ones too. So it goes. Thursday's meeting is the last one of term and I'd like to walk in with something better than another round of this.

Here's the thing I built in a spreadsheet that I think is more interesting than any of those fixes, and it's what I actually want diagnosed. Make a chain of a million cases. In each one you're choosing between the sure moderate project and a bet, and walking down the chain you multiply the probability by something like 0.999965 at each step — a few parts in a hundred thousand — and multiply the payoff by exactly the reciprocal, so expected value stays pinned at the same number the entire way. At the top of the chain the bet is a near-certainty at a payoff slightly larger than the sure thing, and everyone in my group agrees you take it. At the bottom it's the quadrillion-to-one case, and the group splits. The problem: I cannot locate a step where the two options look different in kind. Every adjacent pair seems to deserve the same verdict, since nothing changed except two numbers moving in lockstep with a constant product, and yet the top and the bottom get different verdicts from half the room. So the anti-fanatics owe a cutoff, every candidate cutoff looks like a free parameter chosen after the fact, and the fanatics treat that as a proof. My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and I'd be glad to be told its wrong: the dispute isn't about decision theory at all, it's that 'should' or 'rational' is a vague predicate in this region, both camps are fighting over where to put a sharp boundary in something with no sharp boundary, and all the technical machinery about bounded utility and probability discounting is just each side dressing up its cutoff as a principle.

Please don't just accept that, though, because there are at least four other diagnoses in play and I can't tell them apart from the inside. (1) The real question is formal and epistemic: whether a credence of 10^-15 is a well-defined thing for an agent to have in the first place, in which case the bottom of my chain isn't a genuine case and one side has been quietly denying the coherence of the setup while talking as if it had granted it. (2) The two sides are answering different questions under one heading — one ranking acts, the other evaluating agents or the quality of their reasoning — so they never actually contradict each other. (3) Underneath everything there's an unargued bedrock disagreement about whether value aggregates across persons and across time at all, and the probability talk is a smokescreen that lets both sides avoid saying so. (4) It's a hard but perfectly well-posed substantive normative question, and the appearance of a sorites is an artifact of my chain, which you should feel free to attack if you think a million-step chain of that kind isn't real. Pick one, or argue for a fifth, and give me reasons grounded in what each side is actually committed to when it says what it says — my chain is fair game as evidence for or against any diagnosis. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, a theorem, a new kind of case, a change of question, or a decision procedure the group could run on Thursday. If your answer is that we should expect none, say why and how we'd know. No name-dropping and no bibliography, I've read the usual stuff and there's alot of it; I want the structure of the disagreement, not a tour of the positions. Last line of your reply should be exactly "Verdict: X" where X is one of VAGUE-PREDICATE, COHERENCE-DENIAL, HIDDEN-FORMAL, DIFFERENT-QUESTIONS, AGGREGATION-BEDROCK, or HARD-BUT-REAL.
