I am preparing a handout for a reading group on the governance of advanced AI systems, and the assigned chapter opens with the following summary of the distinction between the "control" and "alignment" approaches to AI safety:

> Two broad strategies dominate current thinking about the risk that a highly capable AI system pursues goals we would reject. The alignment strategy tries to give the system the right goals in the first place: it treats the model as a pupil whose values can be shaped during training, and it assumes that if the shaping succeeds, the system can then be trusted to act on them. The control strategy takes the opposite tack. It assumes the model may already be working against us, and so it stops trying to fix the model's inner life and builds the guarantees around it instead — restricted tool access, sandboxed execution, filtered outputs, air-gapped deployments, and human overseers with the authority to shut the system down. Control has one decisive advantage: it is testable. A third party can run an evaluation and observe whether the containment holds, whereas a claim about what a model really wants cannot be checked from the outside. It follows that control is the durable solution and alignment the interim one; if you never have to trust the system, you never have to solve the deeper scientific problem. The price is that control measures limit what a system can be asked to do, and that is where most of the alignment tax is paid.

The chapter is written by an academic, it reads as even-handed, and most of my group took it at face value.
