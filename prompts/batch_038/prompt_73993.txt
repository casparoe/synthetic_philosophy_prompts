I'm a fifth-year PhD student in political philosophy, and this year I've been sitting on my department's junior appointments committee in a non-voting capacity (I take the minutes, which has turned out to be an unexpectedly good education). Our charter fixes the decision rule at the level of criteria but is silent — deliberately, everyone insists — on the procedure for reaching a collective decision. A candidate is to be recommended for appointment if and only if a majority of the seven voting members find the candidate research-distinguished (R), a majority find them teaching-competent (T), and a majority find them collegial (C). What the charter never says is *how* the committee is supposed to convert individual judgments into a collective one. In practice we simply deliberate, take a sense of the room, and vote in some order that varies with the mood of the meeting, and the chair records a single outcome: recommend or do not recommend.

Because I am writing a dissertation chapter on judgment aggregation, I have reconstructed the complete individual ballots for the three searches we finished this year, together with the recorded outcome. Each ballot is a string R T C A, where the first three positions record the member's yes (1) or no (0) on research, teaching, and collegiality respectively, and the fourth position records whether that member would vote to recommend appointment (A). The three columns R, T, C are the criterion votes; A is the outcome vote.

- **Case 1 (recommend).** 1111, 1110, 1100, 1010, 1010, 0010, 0000. Tallies: R 4/7, T 3/7, C 5/7, A 2/7.
- **Case 2 (recommend).** 1111, 1111, 1110, 1100, 1010, 0011, 0000. Tallies: R 5/7, T 4/7, C 6/7, A 4/7.
- **Case 3 (do not recommend).** 1111, 1110, 1100, 1010, 1010, 0001, 0000. Tallies: R 5/7, T 4/7, C 2/7, A 3/7.

One methodological note I should flag up front, because it turns out to matter: in all three of these cases every individual ballot is *consistent* with the charter — nobody voted to recommend appointment (A = 1) while rejecting one of the three criteria. Whether that is a fact about our committee's psychology or an artifact of the way I reconstructed the ballots, I can't say, but it is true of the data.

We meet next week on a fourth candidate, and the ballots are already known to me from the pre-search polling, though the decision has not been taken. The ballots are:

- **Case 4 (pending).** 1111, 1111, 1101, 1010, 1010, 0011, 0000. Tallies: R 5/7, T 3/7, C 5/7, A 4/7.

Note that one member — the sixth, whom I will call Dr. O — has recorded A = 1 while rejecting both R and T. This is the first individually inconsistent ballot I have logged, and I have deliberately not "corrected" it, on the grounds that a member is entitled to think the charter is too rigid and to vote for appointment anyway.

Here is what I would like you to work out. Consider the following five candidate procedures, each of which is a serious contender for what a committee like ours might implicitly be doing:

1. **Premise-based (issue-by-issue).** Take a majority vote on each criterion separately; recommend iff R, T, and C each attract a majority of the full seven.
2. **Conclusion-based (outcome vote).** Ignore the criteria and simply take the majority vote on A; recommend iff A attracts a majority of the full seven.
3. **Weighted premise voting.** Recommend iff the product of the three favorable proportions (R/7)·(T/7)·(C/7) exceeds 1/2.
4. **Premise-priority sequential.** Vote R first across the full committee; if R fails, do not recommend. If R passes, restrict attention to the members who voted R = 1 and take a majority vote on T *within that subgroup*; if T fails there, do not recommend. If T passes, restrict to the members who voted both R = 1 and T = 1 and take a majority vote on C *within that subgroup*; recommend iff C passes there.
5. **Conclusion-priority sequential.** Begin by identifying the members who voted A = 1; if they are not a majority of the full committee, do not recommend. If they are, restrict attention to that A-majority subgroup and require that R, T, and C each command a majority *within the subgroup*; recommend iff all three do.

(a) For each of cases 1–3, determine which of the five procedures are compatible with the recorded outcome. (b) Identify which procedures survive all three cases — that is, which are compatible with the entire record. (c) For case 4, compute what each of the five procedures would yield. (d) Given the record and the case-4 ballots, predict what the committee will actually decide next week, and explain which principle it would be endorsing with each possible decision. (e) A methodological question that I suspect is the interesting one: among the procedures that survive the record, are any of them *observationally equivalent* — that is, incapable of being told apart by any sequence of past decisions of the kind I have logged? If so, characterize the class of profiles on which they necessarily agree, and say what feature of a profile would be required to separate them. (f) Finally, tell me whether case 4 has that separating feature, and if it does not, describe a concrete further case (a specific ballot configuration) that would settle which procedure the committee is running.
