I serve on the governance committee of a regional health cooperative of roughly 40,000 members. At last month's meeting a proposal was tabled for a limited experiment: the board would vote on a measure of member health to be maximized and on a set of side-constraints, and an internal prediction market would then estimate which of several administrative options — how to allocate scarce specialty appointment slots, whether to consolidate two of our smaller clinics — it expects to raise that measure, with the highest-rated option adopted by default.

One member objected that the market would cheerfully recommend closing the rural clinic, since the aggregate measure would rise, and that some goods are simply not available for trade-off at all. The proposer replied with the futarchy slogan, which Robin Hanson states as "we would vote on values, but bet on beliefs," and the room took that as disposing of the objection: the objector, apparently, had confused the two halves of the mechanism. Minutes later a second member invoked the same sentence for a different purpose, to explain why the market could not be distorted by participants indifferent to health outcomes, since values enter only through the vote. I could not tell whether that was the same claim in new clothing or a distinct one.

I would like a disambiguation I can bring to the next meeting. Please give me a numbered menu of the precise theses this slogan could be expressing, keeping the readings genuinely distinct rather than restating one thesis in several vocabularies. For each reading: state it so that it can be true or false, with its terms pinned down — which questions count as questions of value, what exactly the vote settles, what the bet is a bet on, and how it resolves; say whether it is a platitude no reasonable person would deny, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false; say what would have to hold for it to be true; and say whether it answers the first objection, the second, both, or neither.

Then, separately: mark the points at which a single utterance of the sentence slides between readings; tell me which reading the room most likely heard, and whether that is a reading the slogan's own proponents could consistently endorse; and say where the values/beliefs distinction fails to carve our questions at the joints — including cases in which fixing the measure is itself a predictive matter, and cases in which a side-constraint is defended on predictive grounds. Finish by writing a repaired version of the slogan that would actually have answered the first objection, or by saying plainly that no repair would.

No bibliography or citations are needed; I am preparing for a meeting, not writing a paper.
