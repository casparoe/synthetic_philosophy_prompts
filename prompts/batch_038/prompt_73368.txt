So my book club finally got through a chunk of the Pensées, which was mostly me being confused and one guy (a retired accountant, very intense about Jansenism for some reason) doing all the talking. Afterwards we ended up at a diner and my sister-in-law, who's a nurse and who says she used to be devout and then just slowly stopped without ever deciding to, mentioned that a podcast she listens to had spent an episode on the divine hiddenness argument — the contemporary one, Schellenberg's. Her version of it: a perfectly loving God would want a personal relationship with each of us, and you can't have a relationship with someone you don't think exists, so if such a God existed nobody who wasn't actively resisting him would end up failing to believe; but there plainly are people like that, earnest non-resistant nonbelievers, so there's no perfectly loving God, so no God. She liked that it isn't about suffering, it's about the silence itself, which is basically her actual complaint. Anyway I said "Pascal would eat this alive" without thinking, and now I have to defend that.

Can you do the reconstruction for me, and do it honestly rather than just handing Pascal an easy win? I want to know, roughly: would he even accept the terms of the argument — given how dismissive he is of the philosophical proofs and how much he leans on the heart rather than reason, does he think belief is the kind of thing an evidential gap could produce? He seems to think God's hiddenness is deliberate and somehow appropriate, a way of sorting those who seek him with everything they have from those who don't, so is his reply that the argument begs the question by assuming God's aims are ours? Does the wager count as an answer to Schellenberg or does it actually concede the point, since it's pitched at someone who can't be argued into belief? And what about the tension I keep tripping over: on one hand his Port-Royal side, grace given to some and not others, and on the other the whole apologetic project of winning over the sceptical salon crowd — which of those drives his answer when someone asks about a person who genuinely looked and found nothing? Would Pascal be willing to grant that such a person exists, or is he committed to saying that anyone who sought with their whole heart got an answer and therefore didn't really seek? Give me the three or four moves he'd lead with, in the order he'd lead them, and say for each whether it actually blunts the argument or just relocates it.

I know he died in 1662 and this argument is a twentieth century thing, so I'm not asking you to pretend he read it — I'm asking for a careful counterfactual. Which means please flag where you're extrapolating versus where you're anchored in something he actually wrote, and call out the anachronisms that matter. I suspect at least one of them is that he'd never accept the setup where theism is treated as a hypothesis that evidence should have confirmed by now, and another is that seventeenth century France didn't have anything like our population of people who grew up with no religious frame at all, so he might not even grant the datum at scale. Tell me if I'm wrong about that too.
