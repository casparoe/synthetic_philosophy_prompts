I spent thirty-eight years as an experimental physical chemist and came to philosophy only after retiring; I now help run a recorded conversation series at my former institute, in which a working scientist and a philosopher talk through a single disputed question. Our next guest defends dialetheism. As I understand her position: some contradictions are true; the semantic paradoxes are best handled in a paraconsistent logic of the LP family, which blocks the principle of explosion at the cost of disjunctive syllogism; and the resulting truth-value gluts are strictly confined to self-referential sentences and to the set-theoretic paradoxes. She explicitly rejects the more Hegelian extension on which change and motion themselves harbour true contradictions. Her pre-circulated abstract makes one further claim, which I would like the conversation to test: that the classical logician owes us an account of why the liar sentence is not true, and that any such account can only breed a revenge paradox of its own.

Please draft nine or ten questions. I have read enough to know that letting her restate the view would fill the hour and go nowhere, so each question should engage a specific pressure point; immediately after each, note the answer I expect from her and the follow-up that answer should trigger. At a minimum the set should cover:

- the exclusion problem: in a logic where the negation of a contradiction can hold alongside the contradiction itself, what resource is left for flatly refusing a claim, and in what does her rejection of a sentence differ from mine — including what she does with the distinction between a sentence's being true and its being true only;
- revenge: what becomes of the sentence that says of itself that it is false and not true, and whether treating it as yet another glut obliges her to a fresh expressive device at every stage;
- Curry's paradox, which is standardly blamed on contraction rather than on negation, and what her account of the conditional costs in ordinary mathematical argument;
- the confinement line: what principle keeps gluts inside self-reference and out of motion, given that she disowns the Hegelian strand, and whether that principle is independently motivated or reverse-engineered to fit the desired conclusion;
- her abstract: whether the revenge objection she presses against the paracomplete alternative is not equally damaging to her own description of the liar's status;
- belief: does she believe any contradiction, or merely accept that her theory entails one, and what that difference amounts to;
- closing, and in my own idiom: what argument or observation would count against the view, and how she would tell a genuine glut in the world apart from a glut in our bookkeeping of it — whether, say, complementarity in quantum theory is a candidate for the latter and why it is not a candidate for the former.

House rules for the deliverable: our audience is largely scientists with no training in logic, so no question may carry more than one piece of technical vocabulary, and that term must be glossed within the question itself. No warm-up questions about how she came to the subject, and nothing that requires her to cite, name, or recommend literature — the questions must stand on their own content. Finally, order them so the exchange builds: I would rather see one topic escalated across three successive questions than seven topics touched once each.
