I am in my fourth year of a philosophy PhD, working on the ethics of assertion and testimony, and this year I have ended up doing two things that have collided in an unhelpful way: I have been reading through several hundred anonymous peer-review reports as part of a digitization project in my department, and I am the graduate student member of a faculty search committee. In both settings I keep meeting the same conversational move. I have not been able to find anything that describes it precisely, which makes me suspect either that I am missing an obvious term or that the phenomenon sits in a gap between literatures that do not talk to each other.

Here is the move. In a search committee discussion, a colleague is asked what he makes of a shortlisted candidate. He says: "Her letters are enthusiastic. I have read them twice." He adds no qualification, and when asked directly whether he has a reservation he says that he has only reported what the letters are like. After the meeting, half the committee treats the candidate as someone whose referees do not really know her work, and the other half treats the colleague as having made an insinuation he will not stand behind. Neither reading is anything he intended: asked afterward, he is genuinely unsure what he meant by it, and says only that he was struck by the letters and thought it worth mentioning. Similarly, one of the reports I read consists of a careful summary of a paper's methods, followed by: "Readers will want to keep in mind the recent discussion of this laboratory's data." Nothing is claimed about the laboratory, nothing about the paper, and the referee, if pressed, would presumably say he was flagging relevant context.

What puzzles me is that the tools I have been trained on seem to have nothing to grip here. This is not lying, obviously. Nor is it the familiar case of literally-true-but-misleading speech, or misleading by implicature, at least on any account I know of that requires the speaker to intend the hearer to come to believe some particular proposition — because in these cases there does not appear to be any such proposition. It is not paltering, as I understand that, since paltering deploys a true statement to induce a specific false belief the speaker has already fixed on. It is not quite innuendo either, at least as that word is ordinarily used, which suggests someone who knows what they are implying and is being coy about it. And I do not think it is Frankfurtian bullshit, because these speakers are not indifferent to the truth of what they say: what they say is true, and they care that it is true. What they seem to be doing instead is changing which possibilities the hearer treats as live, while remaining agnostic — sometimes, I believe, sincerely agnostic — about what belief results. Two hearers can come away with incompatible suspicions, both of which the speaker would disown, and yet the hearers are in some sense I cannot articulate epistemically worse off than they were, and the speaker appears responsible for that.

So: is there an established concept, in pragmatics, in the ethics of speech, or in law, that captures this configuration — an utterance that is true, carries no determinate intended content, and operates by shifting what a hearer takes seriously rather than by inducing a belief? If there is, I would like to know how it is standardly defined, and where the disagreements about it run. If there is not, I would rather you said so plainly and proposed a term, with a definition and — more importantly for my purposes — with the boundary conditions: what has to be true of the speaker's intentions for the category to apply at all; whether the category survives the discovery that the suspicion was in fact well founded; whether it makes a difference that different hearers supply different contents; and how to distinguish this from raising a genuinely relevant consideration in good faith, which is of course exactly what my colleague would say he was doing. A bare label will not be much use to me. What I need is the carving.
