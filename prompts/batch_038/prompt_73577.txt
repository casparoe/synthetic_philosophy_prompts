Okay, this is a weird request but I need it.

I work as a functional assessor for a public long-term care scheme — I'm the person who decides, from a file plus one home visit, which tier a claimant lands in, and the tier sets how many hours of care they get. Six weeks ago my dad died, and because of some backlog reshuffle his file, the one he'd opened a year before he died about no longer being able to live on his own, got routed to me. I've recused myself now, obviously, but I had it for three days and I havent been able to think about anything else since.

The field that's doing it to me isn't the six items — bathing, dressing, the rest — those you can more or less score. It's the date field: "date on which claimant ceased to be capable of independent living." One date. The system won't take a range, won't take "approximately," won't take "unclear." And payment is backdated from that date, so the date is worth money, actual months of an actual person's care. My dad kept four hives and he kept a notebook about them, an entry every few days, and reading it you can watch both declines at once, his and the colonies', and there is definately not one entry you could point at and say there, that's the day. It just gets worse a notch at a time, and every notch is small enough that the day before and the day after look like the same kind of day.

So: I want a short story. Not an essay, and not a sad one — I've had enough sad this year. Roughly 1200–1600 words. Here's what I need:

- The protagonist is an assessor holding their own parent's file. Don't build to that as a reveal, just start there.
- The story has to make a reader hold two things at once without resolving them: that there really was such a day, a particular Tuesday, and that there was no such day and the date on the form is a fiction with legal force. I've been up at 2am reading about vagueness and I know there are views that say the first thing, views that say the second, and one that tries to say something like both. Don't name any of them, don't have a character explain any of them, and don't have anyone make a little speech about heaps or bald men — those words shouldn't show up at all.
- Include the notebook. I want one passage that's basically a run of dated entries, a dozen or so, one line each, in the old man's voice, about the hives, written so that no entry in it reads as more obviously the answer than any other. That passage has to do real work in the plot, not sit there as texture.
- The bees need to be more than sad-parallel material. The old man has a rule about when a colony stops being a going concern and starts being a dead one, and it is not the rule the assessor would apply to a person, and the story should set the two rules side by side without telling me which one is correct.
- Last line: the assessor entering a single date into a field that only accepts one. Don't say what the date is and don't tell me how they feel about it.

Keep the setting invented — don't try to match any real country's care system or any real assessment instrument, I don't want this fact-checked against something. And no real philosophers, no real court cases, nothing I'd have to go look up. I just want to read something that gets this exactly right, because nobody I can actually talk to about it gets it.
