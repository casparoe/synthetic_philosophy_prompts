I retired four years ago after thirty-seven years in paleolimnology, most of them spent splitting lake-sediment cores, counting varves, and arguing with colleagues over whether a given laminated couplet represented one year or two. My discipline had one luxury that I did not appreciate at the time: it could date its evidence. A core gives you an answer, and if you dislike the answer you may re-cut the section, take a different proxy, or wait for a better mass spectrometer — but you cannot ask the sediment to come back in a century when the field has improved. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science, and I have become preoccupied by the inverse predicament: theories whose decisive evidence does not merely lie in the future, but may lie permanently beyond the reach of any finite observer.

The case that holds me is Malthus. In the first edition of his Essay, in 1798, he argued that unchecked population increases in a geometrical ratio while the means of subsistence increase only in an arithmetical ratio, so that population is forever pressed back against the level of food by what he called the positive and preventive checks. That edition was short, confident, and thin on data; the greatly expanded edition that followed in 1803 was padded with parish registers, mortality tables, and accounts of voyages — an author converting his own rhetoric into evidence, at length. Two centuries of yield improvement, of fertility falling in precisely the countries that grew rich, and of no observed global subsistence collapse have left the theory neither buried nor vindicated.

I have a crude formulation of my own, which I would ask you to treat as my coinage rather than as an established term in the literature. A Malthusian prediction is a *limit-prediction*: it concerns where a sequence tends, while we can only ever observe a finite prefix of that sequence. Prosperity therefore does not refute it — it postpones it. Catastrophe, on the other hand, is immediately hailed as decisive. The evidential ledger runs in one direction only, which is why such a theory manages to be at once immune to falsification and unusually cheap to confirm.

Please write a short story of 1,800 to 2,600 words that dramatizes this problem without ever lecturing about it. I would like the following apparatus and constraints.

**Setting.** A records office — call it the Registry of Extended Horizons, or something better of your devising — which accepts long-range predictions and holds them as instruments with stated maturity dates. An instrument that has not been settled by its maturity may be rolled over, but each rollover must be secured by an auxiliary hypothesis entered as a clause, and each clause carries its own maturity date and a cost against the original claim. Set the story in a near future or in a deliberately unlocatable present; choose whichever lets the bureaucracy feel mundane rather than satirical.

**Protagonist.** A junior adjuster, not trained in philosophy, who has been assigned the instrument first filed in 1798 for its periodic review and must recommend either another extension or settlement.

**Documents.** Include, as a set piece within the fiction, the rendered schedule of that instrument's extensions across roughly two centuries. The clauses should be specific and recognisable — for instance, one excusing an unanticipated acceleration in crop yields, one asserting that affluence itself suppresses fertility, one permitting the substitution of new energy sources for arable land, one deferring the question to an era of off-world supply. Show, through the wording of the schedule rather than through commentary, that each clause rescues the prediction by narrowing what it predicts.

**Complication.** At least one clause in the schedule has already silently matured and failed. The instrument is being held up by scaffolding that is itself rotten, and the auditor is the first person obliged to notice. Decide for yourself whether this discovery is inconvenient, liberating, or merely procedural for him, and let that choice shape the story.

**Other characters.** Give him a senior colleague who defends the practice of rollovers in good faith and with a real argument — that a limit-prediction cannot be judged by its prefixes, and that an institution which settles such instruments prematurely is worse than one which never settles them at all. This colleague should not be a caricature. There should also be someone outside the office, however briefly rendered, on whom the recommendation bears.

**Tone and method.** Malthus may appear only as an archival presence — a signature, a portrait in a stairwell, a marginal note in a ledger — never as a speaking character. Do not quote his text; paraphrase if you need to invoke it. No real living persons, no named contemporary economists or politicians, no bibliography, and no footnotes. Keep the prose concrete and restrained, and let the procedures, the paper, and the light in the room carry the ideas rather than the dialogue. The ending should not resolve the question: I want to finish the story unable to say whether the instrument was refuted or merely patient.

After the story, add a single paragraph of no more than one hundred and twenty words identifying the one passage where you think the fiction most risks collapsing into illustration, and saying what you did, or would do, to keep it from doing so.
