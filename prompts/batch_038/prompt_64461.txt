I'm on the benefits team at a company of about 4,000 people and I've been asked to rewrite our parental leave policy before it goes to the exec committee in the spring. Reading up on how other places do this, I hit a comparison I can't make sense of, and I want help articulating what's actually going on.

Our current policy: 16 weeks for the primary caregiver, 3 weeks for the other parent, with HR deciding which is which off a one-page form you fill in with your manager. It's meant to be neutral, but something like 90% of the people claiming primary end up being mothers, which is why we're rewriting it.

Sweden, roughly: the entitlement is a big pool of days the two parents can split however they like, except a chunk (I think 90 days each) belongs to each parent personally and can't be handed to the other one — if the father doesn't take them, the family just loses them. As far as I can tell you can even give some of the transferable part to a grandmother who's doing the childcare, but the reserved days stay stuck to that specific parent. Same stated goal as ours: get fathers actually doing care.

The two designs look like they're built on opposite pictures of what leave is *for* and I can't say what those pictures are. Can you reconstruct the theory each one only makes sense under, and what each quietly rules out? Specifically: who's the beneficiary in each case — the child, the parent, the household, or gender equality treated as a good in its own right? And when a family forfeits the reserved months because the dad's boss can't spare him, the cost lands on the family rather then on the state or on fathers: is that meant to be a tax on a bad outcome, a subsidy for a valuable activity, or a judgment that these parents' own preferences don't count because they were shaped by the very inequality the policy is attacking? Does the quota design have to pick one, or would several quite different theories license exactly the same rule?

Same question for the no-cash-out part. We pay cash in lieu of almost every other benefit — unused vacation, transit, gym — and Sweden pays a child allowance in straight cash and subsidizes daycare. But nobody proposes just buying out the reserved months. What does refusing to monetize this one benefit presuppose about care, given the same states happily monetize other family supports?

Finally, tell me what I'd have to change in our policy if I decided an employer's job is only to hand parents resources and get out of the way, versus if I decided it's to shape who does the caring — and flag which of those two commitments our 16/3 split already makes, because I suspect it makes one without admitting it.
