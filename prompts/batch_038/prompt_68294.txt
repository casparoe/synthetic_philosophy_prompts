**Too many causes**

Ask for an explanation of a large historical event and you almost always get a list. The war came because of the alliance system, the mobilization timetables, imperial rivalry, naval panic, nationalism in the provinces, a fiscal crisis, a harvest failure, and a wrong turn taken by a driver in Sarajevo. Critics call this a cop-out: a list isn't an explanation. Historians reply that the event really was overdetermined. Neither side says what overdetermination actually costs, and I think that's the mistake.

The standard way to rank causes is counterfactual: ask which of them, if absent, would have changed the outcome. But in a dense causal web this test doesn't discriminate. Almost every candidate sits inside a set of conditions that was jointly enough for what happened, so each is needed given the others, and each passes the but-for test equally well. The test filters out irrelevancies; it produces no ordering among the survivors. Any ordering has to come from somewhere else.

The usual answer is that ordering comes from the historian's interests, which makes it look arbitrary. My claim is that it comes from somewhere less arbitrary and less factual: the thing being explained isn't fixed in the first place. Historians slide between descriptions of the same event to suit the story. "The war that began in August 1914," "a general European war," "Germany's bid for hegemony," and "the breakdown of the July system" are not the same explanandum, and each has its own cause-set with its own determinate counterfactuals. The list looks endless and unrankable mainly because the description is sliding mid-paragraph. I've been calling this description-sliding for want of a better term.

Same thing further back. "Why did the palatial states of the eastern Mediterranean collapse around 1200 BCE?" gets drought, raiders, internal revolt, and systemic fragility, and all four are defensible. "Why did the palace script disappear?" and "why did Pylos burn when Tiryns didn't?" get different answers from the same evidence. A book that lists fifteen causes of the collapse is usually not over-explaining; it is answering four questions under one heading and never saying which is which.

If that's right, the practical upshot is that "why did E happen?" and "why E rather than F?" are not two ways of asking one thing. The first is often unanswerable because E's description is unfixed; the second is usually answerable because the contrast fixes it. So the discipline shouldn't be ranking causes but pinning the foil. It also explains why the inevitability arguments never terminate: inevitability and contingency are properties of an event-under-a-description, so two historians can be simultaneously right about everything they assert and still disagree, because they're sliding.
