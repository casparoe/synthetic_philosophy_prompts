I am trying to settle my own view on the ethics of abortion by working through concrete cases rather than by picking a camp, and I have reached the point where my verdicts no longer look like they hang together. Some context that may or may not be relevant: my spouse and I went through several rounds of IVF before our daughter was born, and we still have two frozen embryos in storage in another state, with an annual fee attached and a consent form asking what should become of them if we die or divorce. I am also auditing a graduate applied ethics seminar this term, in which we have done Thomson's violinist and people-seeds cases and Marquis's future-like-ours argument, and I have found that the seminar has left my intuitions exactly where they started. Here are the verdicts I actually have, stated as flatly as I can.

1. The violinist as Thomson describes him: unplugging is permissible even after nine months, and I feel this strongly. But in a variant where he can be safely disconnected into a portable dialysis rig that keeps him alive without your kidneys, yanking the plug is wrong. What I have a right to, on my intuitions, is the end of the imposition, not the death of the person imposing it.
2. An ectopic pregnancy: removing the tube is permissible, and I do not think this is because the embryo has low status. It is permissible because both cannot be saved. If a technique existed that saved the embryo at no additional risk to the patient, I think we would be required to use it, or very close to required.
3. Contraception that prevents fertilization: clearly fine, not close to a hard case. Discarding our own two frozen embryos by joint decision, with nobody's consent violated and nobody's body involved: not fine, or at least a real loss that we would have to justify rather than shrug at. I cannot explain the asymmetry except by noting that one of these is a labeled vial we have been paying to store for four years, which does not sound like a principle.
4. Anencephaly: termination is permissible, and I think it is permissible for Marquis's reason, that there is no future like ours to deprive anyone of. But a fetus whose predicted life would be brief and dominated by suffering, though hardly empty of the goods Marquis lists: I also want to say termination is permissible, and here the reason cannot be that there is no future, since there is one. It would have to be that the future is not worth having, which seems a different and much more dangerous principle to let in.
5. Emergency contraception taken within three days of intercourse, when implantation may not yet have occurred: clearly permissible, and part of how I justify this to myself is that at that stage the embryo can still split into twins, so there is no determinate single individual whose future is being taken. A day-five embryo in a clinic's discard tray, at the same developmental stage, destroyed rather than transferred: a loss. The stage-based reason I just gave cannot separate these two, so either my twinning argument is doing no work at all or something else is doing it.
6. A person who stopped using contraception with the intention of conceiving and then changed her mind at six weeks: I think her claim to terminate is weaker than a rape victim's, though not extinguished. Someone who conceived through a lapse in a method she had knowingly chosen: weaker again, perhaps? I flip-flop here. The people-seeds case is supposed to settle the question and does not settle it for me; it makes the risk-taker's position look worse than I want to endorse.
7. A fetus at twenty-two weeks where transfer to an artificial womb is available at no risk or cost to the pregnant person: she may end the pregnancy, but she may not have the fetus destroyed, and must accept the transfer. At thirty-four weeks with no transfer available anywhere: she may still terminate, and I think this is because, in the absence of the alternative, ending the pregnancy and ending the life have become a single act. But if they are a single act there, then in case 1 the right to end an imposition would also have to be a right to end a life, which I have just denied.
8. A pregnant person given months to live who aborts a healthy fetus: I want to say this is no worse than the same act by someone in ordinary health, but the future-like-ours account appears to measure the wrong by the size of the future taken, and the future is the same size in both instances. If anything my instinct says the dying person's act is less bad, and I cannot ground that instinct anywhere.

My tentative formulation, which I suspect is merely ad hoc, is this: an act that ends a fetus's life is permissible only if either (a) the fetus has no future like ours, or (b) the fetus is, without the pregnant person's ongoing consent, imposing on her body and no transfer out of that imposition is available to her at reasonable cost. Clause (a) does the work in cases 4 and 5, clause (b) in cases 1, 2, 6, and 7, and I can already see that case 3 is a disaster for the whole thing, since a frozen embryo has as much claim to a future like ours as anything in case 4 while imposing on no one whatsoever. What I would like from you is a principle, or if you think two incompatible principles are the honest answer then both, stated precisely and clause by clause, with each clause tied to the verdict that motivates it; an explicit statement of which of the eight verdicts above it cannot accommodate and what it would cost me to keep them anyway; and a diagnosis of cases 3 and 5 in particular, saying whether the tension between them is real or whether there is a distinction I am fumbling at. Then tell me what the resulting principle implies about three situations I have not listed: whether anyone would be obligated to bring a discarded but viable frozen embryo to term in an artificial womb, if the technology existed and no one's body were involved; how to resolve the case of a frozen embryo whose genetic parents disagree, one demanding destruction and one demanding transfer to a willing gestational carrier; and what happens in a jurisdiction where transfer is available at modest but nontrivial cost, say a few thousand dollars and a week of recovery, so that the pregnant person declines it for financial rather than bodily reasons. Please do not survey the literature or map my intuitions onto named positions and camps. I want the reasoning and the price of holding it.
