Electoral law in many jurisdictions resolves an exact tie for a single office by chance: names drawn from a container, or a coin tossed, and the seat goes to whichever candidate the device selects. Deliberative assemblies in the same legal culture do the opposite: when a vote on a motion divides evenly, nobody reaches for a coin — the motion simply fails and the status quo stands. What account of choice without preference does this pair of rules presuppose? Does the draw concede that the tied candidates are genuinely equally entitled to the office and that either may permissibly be chosen, or does it deny there is any such fact and treat the device as a way of manufacturing a decision the community can live with? Why is chance legitimate in one case and unthinkable in the other — because an office cannot be left vacant while a motion can be left unpassed, or because the rules quietly treat inaction as though it were not itself a choice with consequences? Distinguish what these practices strictly require from what they merely sit comfortably with, say whether they commit us to treating an exact balance and a one-vote margin as radically different kinds of thing, and explain what would have to change if we instead held that exact ties between candidates never in fact obtain.
