I am a junior at a public high school, and for an independent-study seminar I owe a short argumentative essay of about twelve hundred words rather than a research paper, so I cannot lean on sources and need the argument to carry itself. My topic is W. E. B. Du Bois's claim, put forward in an address on Black art in the 1920s, that all art is propaganda and always must be, and that an artist who claims to stand outside politics has merely declined to notice whose side his work is already on. My seminar partner thinks this is an easy position to hold: art is made by people with commitments, it circulates in a world already structured by power, and so it does political work whether or not anyone intends it. I find that reading too comfortable, because Du Bois does not stop at the descriptive claim. He goes on to insist that Black artists make their propaganda deliberately, and make it good propaganda, which seems to presuppose that they have a choice about whether their art is propaganda at all. If every work already is, the exhortation looks empty, the distinction between engaged art and neutral art dissolves, and the view becomes something no conceivable counterexample could dislodge.

Please write the essay on this controversy. It should take up, at minimum: whether the universal claim is true, and what would have to be true about art in general for it to be true rather than a rhetorical flourish; whether a thesis that applies to everything thereby stops saying anything, or whether the real content lies not in whether art is propaganda but in whose interests it serves and how openly it acknowledges them; and how the position survives the aesthetic objection that art written to persuade flattens its subjects into types, idealizes its heroes, and forbids the unflattering or ambivalent portrait — including the further question of who gets to decide which messages are fit to be advanced, given that Du Bois also edited a magazine and had to decide in practice what got printed. I would like the essay to weigh against Du Bois the objection made by a poet of his own generation, roughly that the young Black artist's first obligation is to his own individual voice and that writing as a representative of the race is its own kind of surrender, and then to weigh the reply that the ideal of pure, uncommitted art is available mainly to those whose mere existence is not already treated as a political question. If the dispute is partly verbal — if Du Bois means something closer to "no art is politically inert" than "art should be instrumental" — say so and explain whether the two readings can be held at once.

I do not want a survey or a biography, and no list of works or citations; assume the reader knows who Du Bois was but nothing about this debate. State your verdict in a plain sentence early, then defend it, and take the two strongest objections to your own conclusion seriously enough to show what would have to change for you to give it up. Do not end by splitting the difference or by saying the question remains open — if the position can only be saved by narrowing it into something much less interesting than the slogan suggests, that is a legitimate verdict, and I would rather have that argued clearly than hedged.
