I'm in my final year of high school, and my school offers a seminar in comparative political philosophy that I've been taking this term. A few weeks ago we were reading Mencius in translation, including the passage where he sets Yang Zhu's each-for-himself principle against Mozi's principle of impartial care for everyone, says that both are defective, and adds that a man who holds a middle course between them is better off than either extremist but is still in error if he holds that middle course rigidly, without the ability to weigh the circumstances in front of him. Afterward two people in the seminar, Noor and Elias, began arguing about what that passage shows about the individualism-versus-collectivism distinction, and they went several rounds while the rest of us listened. I'm reporting the exchange because I honestly cannot tell who was right, and neither of them conceded anything. Here it is as I noted it down.

1. Noor opened by saying that the individualism/collectivism distinction is a Western projection that distorts East Asian thought rather than describing it. Her evidence was the passage itself: Mencius rejects both poles, and the picture behind his rejection is not of an individual who holds rights prior to society but of a person constituted by particular relationships and roles. So scoring societies shaped by that picture as "collectivist" is a category mistake — it forces a foreign vocabulary onto a framework that never asked the question the vocabulary exists to answer.

2. Elias said that is an escape hatch available to anyone. Rejecting two extremes is still taking up a position relative to those extremes, since the mean between them lies on the axis they define; and if Noor genuinely thinks no common dimension exists, she cannot even state her own contrast, because saying that this framework differs from Yang Zhu's egoism already assumes a scale on which the two come apart.

3. Noor replied that the axis is not neutral in the way he assumes. It is built on a picture of pre-social individuals who subsequently form a society and then need to be ranked against it. On the relational picture that question cannot arise, any more than one can ask whether the hand or the body came first. She added that the passage proves her point independently of that premise: Mencius's complaint about the man holding the middle course is not that he is insufficiently extreme but that he holds it without adjustment. Rigidity and adaptability are a second dimension, and a single axis with two poles has no way to represent it.

4. Elias answered that relational constitution is precisely what a Western individualist denies, so using it to show the axis is inapplicable assumes the thing in dispute. He also said that granting extra dimensions does not defeat the axis, since nobody claimed it was exhaustive — calling one economy more market-oriented than another does not pretend there are no other economic differences. Then he made two further points: the same objection to an atomistic conception of the person was pressed inside the West by the communitarian critics of Rawls, so this is not an East-West divide at all; and since Yang Zhu and Mozi are themselves Chinese thinkers, both poles of the distinction were available within the tradition, which means the distinction is not an import.

5. Noor said his last point cuts against him. If the poles were available internally and Mencius still refused both — and refused them for the same reason, namely that each takes a single principle and makes it exceptionless — then what is at issue is not a location on a scale but the structure of the principle, that is, whether it can be adjusted to the case. That is a structural difference, not a scalar one.

6. Elias's final move was that the structural/scalar split is unstable. A theory that can be wrong anywhere along a rigidity-to-sensitivity range is simply another axis; and in any event the two frameworks come out the same way in practice, since discretionary role obligations and collectivist demands tend to override the individual's own claims in the same sorts of situation. So he suspects the difference between Mencius's position and collectivism is at bottom verbal.

Could you go through this move by move? For each of the six, tell me whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and say briefly why. Then tell me which specific sub-claims each of them actually established — for instance, whether Noor is right that the axis is a category mistake, whether Elias is right that the communitarian parallel dissolves the East-West framing, and whether his point that Yang Zhu and Mozi belong to the tradition really shows what he thinks it shows. I would also like to know whether any of the moves depend on running together a metaphysical claim about what a person is with a normative claim about whose interests take priority, and which moves collapse once those two are separated. Finally: who is more right overall, and what are both of them missing — in particular, is there a dimension of the individualism/collectivism question that neither of them named? I am not looking for reading suggestions, only your judgment about the argument as it was actually made.
