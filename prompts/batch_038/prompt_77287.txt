Last week, in a graduate seminar I run on the ethics of wild animal suffering, two of my students got into an argument about a proposal to drain a coastal marsh for a logistics park, and I could not resolve it. Here is the exchange as I noted it down afterward.

Marcus: The draining is a moral disaster. It kills the marsh's current residents by drowning, exposure and starvation, and those harms are concrete and datable. Whatever one says about the animals who would otherwise have been born, nobody is made better by their nonexistence — so the only real entry in the ledger is the killing.

Iris: Only on a person-affecting view. If an average wild life is net negative — the juvenile mortality, parasitism and predation in a marsh like that one are hardly marginal — then a habitat is a machine for producing bad lives, and removing one makes the world better even though it benefits no one in particular.

Marcus: That picture of nature is an artefact of salience. Predation is dramatic; a vole's uneventful weeks are not. So the visible suffering gets counted and the invisible welfare doesn't. You've dressed an availability bias as an empirical finding.

Iris: Grant the uncertainty — it doesn't restore your position. Your restriction is ad hoc: if nobody can be benefited by existing, then no population-reducing intervention can be good either, including ones you'd endorse, and the only losses that count are losses of beings already here. That is an intuition about human-scale cases doing the work of a principle.

Marcus: Your conclusion still doesn't follow, because the drained site doesn't become a void. It gets colonised by generalists with higher fecundity and shorter lives than the marsh specialists, so on your own premises the development may multiply exactly the kind of life you say is worst. And separately, you are implicitly weighting actual agonising deaths far below the non-occurrence of speculative future ones — a weighting you've asserted rather than argued for.
