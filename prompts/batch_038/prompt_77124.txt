The college research committee awards one semester of teaching relief each year for a single, clearly bounded project, and applications are capped at five pages. I intend to use mine for a question in the metaphysics of laws that I do not think has been properly posed, and I would like you to draft the proposal. The readers are mixed — some metaphysicians, some philosophers of physics, one historian of science — so it must be exact without becoming technical.

Here is the idea, which I want developed into a project rather than restated back to me. The dispute between Humeans and anti-Humeans about laws is ordinarily staged as a contest between two accounts of what laws are: summaries of a mosaic of local matters of particular fact, systematized by a trade-off between simplicity and strength, versus irreducible nomic structure — relations of necessitation among universals, or fundamental dispositional properties. Yet both sides lean on a prior notion of which properties are natural enough to belong in the fundamental description of that mosaic. The Humean needs naturalness to fix the vocabulary in which simplicity is measured, and can hold it prior to the laws only at the cost of a quidditistic primitivism about properties; the anti-Humean usually individuates properties by their nomic roles, which makes naturalness law-dependent. My question is whether those two directions of dependence can be satisfied at once: is there a stable fixed point consisting of a privileged description of the mosaic together with the laws that best systematize that description and thereby select exactly those properties as privileged? And if such a fixed point exists, is it unique, and does its existence favour either side?

The proposal should contain:

1. The research question in one sentence, precise enough that one can imagine a paper answering it, together with a statement of what an answer would consist in — a result with the shape of a theorem, a dilemma, or the articulation of a third position.
2. Why the established positions leave the question open. Address explicitly why the familiar charges against the Humean — that laws cannot explain their own instances without circularity, that the Best System Account cannot accommodate objective chance, that accidental generalisations are not excluded — do not already settle the matter, and where they do bear on it.
3. The method, laid out month by month across the semester. I expect the first stage to be a toy model: a finite mosaic with a small number of candidate properties, in which the fixed-point condition can be checked by hand for uniqueness. Say what such a model could establish and what it manifestly could not.
4. Why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives — dissolving the issue by reading the Best System as an account of our inferential practice rather than of the world, accepting quidditism outright, or abandoning the Humean framework for a dispositionalist metaphysics of properties.
5. The strongest objection an anti-Humean would press. I anticipate this one: if a unique fixed point exists, it just is the necessitation relation under another name, so the project either concedes the conclusion or proves nothing. The proposal must engage that objection head-on, not sidestep it.
6. Failure conditions. State what I would have to find by the third month to warrant abandoning or substantially redirecting the project.
7. A feasibility paragraph. The award supports one semester and one paper of roughly nine thousand words, not a monograph, and the committee is alert to overreach.

I will supply the references myself: include no bibliography and no literature review organised by citation, and speak of positions and arguments rather than of particular papers. Open with a working title and an abstract of about 150 words.
