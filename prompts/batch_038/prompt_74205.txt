Trying to write something about how much morality is allowed to demand of a person, and every time I look up supererogation I get the same two-line gloss — good, but not required — and then a pile of people arguing past each other about cases. So before I read one more article I want the spec. What does an account of supererogation actually have to deliver to be worth the name? Give me the requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable versus which are just one camp's tastes.

Corners I want covered, since I suspect most accounts quietly dodge them:

- why the better-but-permitted option isn't simply required — the account has to say something here, not just re-label the gap
- whether the category can be more than residual ("whatever's good and not obligatory"), or whether that's all it ever was
- the same act flipping from supererogatory to obligatory because of something the agent did yesterday — a promise, a job, a relationship. what's doing the work, the act or the agent's situation?
- asking for one: can it ever be legitimate to request, pressure, or reward a supererogatory act, or does the account have to make all such requests off-limits?
- an act that costs the agent nothing subjectively (the billionaire for whom the donation is trivial), and the same act done from a motive you'd otherwise blame — does the category need sacrifice, or purity, or neither
- whether admitting a mirror class, bad but still permitted, is forced on you or optional
- agents who can always do a bit better, with no ceiling on "the best"

Then: which single requirement knocks out the most accounts, and is any pair of them jointly unsatisfiable? If the list itself turns out to be a result, that's the part I most want to see. Keep the weight on the criteria — a couple of sentences at the end on how the main existing approaches do against it is plenty, I'll do my own scorecard.
