For several years now I have worked out, on my own and without any training in the area, a position about uncertain belief that I would like properly stress-tested. In brief: attaching a single number to your confidence in a claim is legitimate only when something like a long, stable run of observations licenses it; otherwise the honest representation of a belief is a range, and the width of that range carries information the midpoint destroys. I further hold that you can get from ranges to action without ever collapsing them into a number — you compare options by asking which holds up across everything the range permits, and if none does, you say so rather than inventing a tie-breaking figure. Those who insist a point value must always be there are, I think, smuggling in a false precision they cannot justify.

Tell me what would count against this view. Which arguments, formal results, or consequences ought actually to move me, as opposed to merely annoying me? I am most interested in failure modes that arise from inside the position: situations where holding a range leaves you worse off or less informed than holding a single number would, where the range quietly turns back into something precise, where a rule for acting on ranges gives verdicts I would find unacceptable, or where having a range makes some kinds of learning impossible. Say also which of these the view's own defenders would concede as telling against them, and which they would wave off — and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Finally, give me a straight verdict on whether the position risks anything at all, or has been hedged into untouchability, and whether that hedging is defensible for a view of this kind.
