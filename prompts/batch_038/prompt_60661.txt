Novelist here, about 200 pages into a book that's built around the many-worlds idea — the version of the physics where nothing gets selected, every outcome happens, the world just forks. I've been reading about it for a couple of years for research, and I think I've hit a problem that isn't really about my book, though I can't tell.

The plan was to write both sides of every fork, in full, with equal seriousness. Chapter 7a: she gets on the train. Chapter 7b: she doesn't. Neither one marked as a dream or a regret or a what-if — both just as real as the other, which is the whole point.

It doesn't work, and I want to be exact about why, because the why feels like it might be the same reason people keep attacking the physics. Two things happened across three drafts. First, when both branches are equally full, nobody worries about anything. Nothing is given up, so nothing is at stake, and the whole thing reads like an inventory. Someone in my workshop said it felt like reading two weather reports back to back. Second — the interesting one — the minute I fixed that by making the branches uneven, giving one more pages and more interior life and more attention, readers immediately started treating the fat branch as the real one and the thin ones as imagination or memory or a character talking herself into something. I had smuggled the single outcome back in through the back door just by caring about it more on the page. Every attempt to make the forking legible made it not-forking.

So is there a name for this? Not for "branching stories are hard to read" — I mean the specific shape of it: representing all the branches equally destroys the thing you're trying to represent, and the only way to make it representable at all is to weight them, and weighting them is just picking one with extra steps. It feels like the literary version of an objection I've half-absorbed from the physics side, something about how if everything happens there's nothing left to be uncertain about, or nothing left to lose, and separately something about why the branches should be weighted by one particular number rather than another. But I dont know whether what I've hit is those objections, or a cousin of them, or something that only exists because there's a reader.

Where's the edge? Does the physics have this problem in a form that doesn't depend on anyone paying attention, or is mine purely an artifact of the medium — like how a film can't show two things at once without splitting the frame, which is a cost of screens and not of the world?

I'm not after a label to sound clever in an interview. I want the carving: what the nearest established ideas are, how this differs from each one, and if nothing actually covers it, coin something and tell me what would count as a case of it and what would merely look similar.
