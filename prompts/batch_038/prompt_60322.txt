**The question as posed:**

Recall Putnam's Twin Earth. The liquid in the lakes and rivers there is not H2O but XYZ, a different compound macroscopically indistinguishable from it, and Putnam asks us to consider the year 1750, when neither planet has the chemistry needed to tell the two apart.

Now modify the case. In 1750 on Twin Earth, a naturalist investigating a vein of ancient ice exposed in a mountain pass — ice deposited there by a comet in the distant past, and in fact H2O — isolates a clear liquid from it, establishes that it is a compound of two simpler substances, and introduces the name *waterium* by ostension to her sample. The rest of the planet's surface liquid remains XYZ. Because the two are macroscopically indistinguishable, the community at once applies *waterium* to the liquid in its lakes, rivers and rain as well, taking itself to have identified the substance the naturalist baptized. The vein is exhausted within a few decades, no further sample is ever obtained, and nothing afterwards forces a correction. In 2025, Twin Oscar says that waterium is wet.

(a) What does *waterium* as used by Twin Oscar in 2025 refer to: H2O, XYZ, both, or neither? Say what a causal-historical account of reference owes by way of an answer here, and what resources it has for supplying one.

(b) Putnam's argument is that Oscar and Twin Oscar can be internally alike in 1750 while their terms differ in extension, so that meaning is not fixed by what is in the head. Does the modified case still support that conclusion? Bear in mind that in this version H2O occurs on Twin Earth as well as on Earth. If you think the conclusion survives, identify which external difference is now doing the work; if you think it does not survive, say what the case shows instead.

**The student's answer:**

> Putnam's argument depends on a causal-historical account of reference, on which a natural kind term receives its extension at a baptism, typically by ostension to samples of the kind, and is then handed down a chain of speakers each of whom intends to use the term with the same extension as the person they learned it from. Reference is therefore not fixed by whatever description a speaker associates with the term, and this is what allows Putnam to set his case in 1750: at that date neither Earthlings nor Twin Earthlings can chemically distinguish water from XYZ, and yet the two terms have different extensions. The division of linguistic labor belongs to the same picture. A lay speaker can have a term with a determinate extension that he himself could not identify, because the work of identification is delegated to experts in his community.
>
> In the modified case I think *waterium* refers to H2O. The baptism in 1750 was an ostension to the ice sample, and the ice sample was H2O; every subsequent use stands in the chain of transmission to that baptism, and each speaker intends to refer to whatever the term was introduced to name. That the community went on to apply the term to the lakes does not alter this, because in doing so they took themselves to be applying it to the very substance the naturalist had baptized. Reference can of course shift — Evans's *Madagascar* is the standing example, where a name that had been transmitted as naming part of the African mainland came to apply to the island once the mistaken usage took over — but on Evans's account a shift requires that some source come to play the dominant role in speakers' use of the name, and the dominant source here is the baptism, since the ice sample is what the term was anchored to and every later use runs through it. One thing the division of linguistic labor cannot do is settle the case in the other direction. The experts are themselves wrong about the identity of the two liquids, and deferring to experts who have misidentified the kind only transmits the mistake; deference cannot supply an extension that the people deferred to have got wrong.
>
> Suppose instead that two centuries of ostension to the lakes counts as a second grounding of the term, and that the chain leaves it open which of the two governs. Then *waterium* has two extensions, H2O and XYZ, in the way that *jade* has two, applying as it does both to nephrite and to jadeite. Twin Oscar's sentence would then be ambiguous between two readings, though in context it is plainly about the liquid in his glass, and speakers disambiguate it in that direction every time they use it.
>
> This is why I do not think the modified case supports Putnam's conclusion. In the original version the two planets differ in their physical environments, and that difference is supposed to carry the semantic weight. In the modified version H2O is present on Twin Earth too, so the environments do not differ in the relevant respect, and what fixes the reference of *waterium* is instead the naturalist's intention together with what the community has taken itself to be talking about all along. That is the lesson Burge draws with arthritis: what determines the content of a speaker's term is the practice of the community rather than the physical environment. So the modified case shows that content depends on facts about other speakers, but it does not show that content depends on the physical environment, and to that extent it fails to support semantic externalism in the form Putnam argues for.
