I am in the third year of a doctoral programme in philosophy; my thesis concerns the epistemology of perceptual knowledge, and I have hit a wall in the chapter on scepticism. My supervisor has asked me to bring a finished diagnosis of the following argument to our next meeting, and I have not been able to produce one. The argument is meant to show that Moore's proof of an external world and the bootstrapping objection stand or fall together, and hence that the proof fails as a refutation of the sceptic. My instinct is that it moves too fast somewhere in the middle, but each step looks defensible when I examine it in isolation, and I cannot say which of them I ought to sacrifice.

(1) Moore's 1939 proof proceeds by holding up his hands and claiming to know, of each, that here is one; from those two premises it follows that there are at least two external objects — external in the sense he specifies, of being things to be met with in space — and hence that there is an external world.

(2) A proof is rigorous if its premises are distinct from its conclusion, if the premises are known rather than merely believed to be true, and if the conclusion follows from them; on his own account the proof meets all three conditions, the only gap he concedes being that he cannot prove the premises.

(3) What entitles him to those premises is not any antecedent verification that his senses are reliable: requiring such verification before one may know anything perceptually would generate a regress that could never be completed, so perceptual knowledge must be available without it.

(4) Knowledge is closed under known entailment: if one knows that p, and knows that p entails q, one is in a position to know that q.

(5) Therefore the sceptic is refuted, and refuted by an argument whose premises are more certain than anything the sceptic can offer against them.

(6) The same machinery certifies any instrument I consult. Looking at the fuel gauge, I come to know that it registers the tank as full; looking into the tank, I come to know that the tank is full; hence I know that on this occasion its registration matched the facts — and by (3) I was entitled to both premises without independent grounds for trusting the gauge.

(7) Repeating the procedure on enough occasions, I come to know that the gauge is reliable.

(8) Generalising: whatever source I consult, and whatever its actual track record, I can come to know by consulting it that it is reliable; so no test of a source can be independent of the source tested, and the difference between a well-calibrated instrument and a useless one is not something I could ever detect.

(9) But (8) is unacceptable. It would entitle a man whose gauge has been fixed permanently to register a full tank to know that the gauge is reliable, and it renders unintelligible the ordinary practice of checking an instrument against a second, independent one.

(10) Therefore (3) and (4) cannot both be true; and since the proof depends on both, it does not refute the sceptic after all.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a survey of the literature. Which step should I reject, and why did it look unobjectionable when I first set it down? I should say where my own loyalties lie, though I do not want that to settle the matter for you: I am attached to (4), because without closure I cannot see how anyone could know that he has hands and know also that he is not a handless brain in a vat, and I am reluctant to disturb (1) and (2), which seem to me to be simply what Moore says. Please price each of the exits available — rejecting (3), rejecting the move from (6) to (7), rejecting the generalisation at (8), rejecting (9), or rejecting (4) after all — and tell me which of them leaves the least damage. If one of those steps is not false outright but salvageable in a restricted form, give me the restriction and say what work it can still do. And if the honest conclusion is that the proof survives while the sceptic remains unmoved, explain to me what kind of failure that is, since it is not a distinction I have managed to make precise.
