You are a philosopher of language who has also written, and delivered, a good many ceremonial speeches, and you are good at both. Please think carefully before drafting: the difficulty in this brief is not the prose, but the fact that several of the constraints pull against one another, and a speech that satisfies all of them on paper can still fail in the room. Begin with a short piece of planning — no more than about 200 words, not to be confused with the speech — in which you identify which of the restrictions I am imposing genuinely bite on ceremonial prose and which were formulated for a different purpose entirely, and where you think two of my constraints collide. Then give me the speech, then the note described at the end.

**The occasion.** Each spring a local foundation and our university's journalism school jointly award a prize for public writing, where "public" is defined in the charter as writing produced for readers who did not choose to read it. This year's honoree is Ruth Kelleher, seventy-four. For twenty-six years she worked in the correspondence unit of our state's disability and unemployment agency, writing the letters that went out when a claim was denied, delayed, or reopened. Somewhere around the middle of that period she began rewriting them; then she began teaching other people to; then she wrote a short handbook that is still used in three states. She has no academic training and no interest in being taken for a theorist. I have been asked to present her in four minutes — about 550 words — at a dinner next Thursday, before the check is handed over.

**The room.** Two hundred and fifty people. Her three children and a sister, in the second row. At the head table, the foundation's trustees and, among them, the man who ran the correspondence unit before she did: eighty-one, here with his daughter, universally described as decent, and — so far as anyone can tell — a man who read the old form letters and thought them perfectly clear. One table of linguists and copy editors who know Orwell's essays well and were invited because Kelleher's handbook leans on them. And about a dozen people who came because she wrote to ask them: recipients of the notices.

**The discipline I have imposed.** I want the speech written under the restrictions Orwell set out in his 1946 essay on English prose — not as a stunt to be announced, but as working conditions. Specifically: no metaphor or figure of speech of the kind that turns up in every prize citation; short words where short words will do, and no word kept that can be cut; the active wherever the active is available, which in this material is not a stylistic preference but the whole question; no pretentious diction, and no large political word used without a meaning a listener could state and then contest. Concretely: the speech must not use "clarity," "accessibility," "empowerment," "voice," "impact," "legacy," "dedication," "integrity," or "champion" as terms of praise, must not call her an inspiration, must not quote Orwell, and must not name him more than once. She would hate being introduced by way of a famous man, and I agree with her.

So that you know which strand of him I mean — not the catalogue of tricks so much as the account of how the habits reinforce themselves: "It becomes ugly and inaccurate because our thoughts are foolish, but the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts." I do not want that sentence in the speech. I want the speech to be consistent with it.

**What has to be in it.** Kelleher has let me read aloud the pair of sentences she used to open her night class. The first is from the form letter as it stood when she arrived:

"It has been determined that the documentation provided was insufficient to establish continued eligibility. Accordingly, benefits have been terminated pending further review."

The second is her replacement:

"We stopped your payments on March 4. We stopped them because the doctor's letter we asked for on February 1 had not reached us by March 1. If you send that letter to the address below, we will look at your case again and give you our answer within thirty days. If the answer is no, you may ask for a hearing, and a person who does not work in this office will decide. Here is how to ask."

I want the speech's centre of gravity to be the difference between those two, and I want that difference described as what it is: not a matter of tone or manners, but of who can be answered. The first letter cannot be argued with, because there is nobody in it and nothing in it that a reader could check against a rule. The second can be disputed by someone with a calendar and an envelope. Say that, or something that amounts to it, in words a listener could contradict if they thought them false.

**The problem you have to solve.** The man who approved the first letter is at the head table, and he is not a villain. Euphemism is precisely the offence under discussion, so I cannot take refuge in the register that lets the room off — the warm generality about how far we have all come and how much better things are today. But a speech that names the old letters as instruments of unanswerable power, delivered within ten feet of the man who signed them, turns a party in her honour into an indictment. I need the third thing, and I think there is one available in the essay's own claim that these habits spread by imitation and infect writers who intend to conceal nothing: the form letter was inherited, sentence by sentence, by everyone who touched it, and the man at the head table stood inside the same inheritance as the woman who got out of it. If you take that route, take it honestly and not as a diplomatic dodge. It has to be something I would say if he were not in the room.

**The objections I expect from my department chair,** who has given a hundred of these and whom I respect. First: those restrictions were written for argument and reportage, and a ceremony is neither; a ceremony's job is to make two hundred and fifty strangers into one room, and the shared formulas are how it does that, so a speech that refuses them goes cold and the family feels short-changed. Second: an audience at a dinner cannot hear an absence, so a speech assembled out of refusals simply sounds like a thin speech. Third: a performance of not using stock phrases is the most self-regarding thing one can do at somebody else's prize, and the room will end up watching the speaker instead of the honoree. I think the third is the real danger and the first is partly right. Do not answer her in the speech; write the speech so that she is wrong, or so that whatever is right in her objection survives inside it.

**Deliverables.** (1) The speech: roughly 550 words, four minutes aloud, readable by a non-actor from a lectern, containing the two letters, addressed to that room and not to a seminar. (2) Then a note of at most 250 words, for me and not for the audience: which of the chair's three objections you accommodated and which you refused, and where in the text; the two places you came closest to breaking your own rules and what you decided; and any sentence you kept that you would concede is a stock phrase, with one line of defence for keeping it. No citations, no bibliography, no suggestions for further reading.
