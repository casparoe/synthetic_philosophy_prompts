I'm putting together the handout for the last session of my grad seminar on the philosophy of set theory (co-taught, somewhat uneasily, with a working set theorist who thinks I'm uncharitable to the pluralists), and I want the closing exercise to be an audit of an analogy that keeps doing unearned work in the room. Every time we get near the independence results, somebody reaches for non-Euclidean geometry: the parallel postulate is to Euclid's other axioms exactly what CH is to ZFC, both were treated as different in kind from their neighbours, both were shown independent by building models, both leave you with several equally respectable extensions, and therefore — this is the punchline my students want — asking whether CH holds is like asking whether space is Euclidean, i.e. not a question with one answer but a choice of framework, and anyone still hunting for new axioms to settle it is like someone insisting there's a fact of the matter about which geometry is really true. I should say I half-sympathise, which is why I need it taken apart rather than applauded.

One student then argued the analogy cuts the other way. In geometry the framework question did eventually get an answer, from general relativity, and it turned out to be no to Euclid; so the moral of the parallel postulate story is not "shrug and pick a framework" but "frameworks get adjudicated from outside, by something nobody thought was relevant" — which is a reason to stay hopeful about CH rather than to give up. Same analogy, opposite conclusion, and I want the handout to make that tension the centrepiece. There are also two worries of my own that I can't quite sharpen. First, in the geometry case there seems to be an ostensible object everyone can point at — the space we live in, the plane on the blackboard — and its not obvious to me that the universe of sets, or the power set of the reals, is pointed at in anything like the same way, so I don't know whether the two independence results are even about the same shape of question. Second, in geometry the model-building settled a question about relative consistency, whereas the question people actually cared about in set theory seems to be which alternative is right, not which is consistent; I suspect my students are sliding between those two and I want the slide named.

So: can you write me the audit, in a form I can hand out and defend in an hour? Start with the mapping, ideally as a table, and fill the slots deliberately — base axiom system, suspect statement, the rival extensions, the intended model, the community of practitioners, the long history of unease (or its absence), the model-building episode, and the eventual adjudication — marking for each one whether the correspondence is tight, loose, or simply vacant, and saying what fills the vacant ones if anything does. Then sort the disanalogies into those that bear on the students' conclusion and those that are surface: I have a hunch that "Euclid's axiom was doubted for two thousand years and nobody doubted CH until 1963" is doing far more rhetorical than logical work, but tell me if I'm wrong. Address my two worries explicitly and say whether either is a real disanalogy or just me confusing myself. Then the payoff: which reading of the analogy survives once the disanalogies are on the table, the shrug or the hopeful one, and whether the fact that a single analogy can be made to carry both is itself the best evidence that it's a picture and not an argument. If you think there's a better analogy for the CH situation — a Gödel sentence, the axiom of choice, determinacy, whatever — sketch it and say what it licenses that the geometry one blocks. And please flag anywhere I've garbled the history above; I'm reconstructing Beltrami and Hilbert from memory and my recall of who convinced whom of what is probably too tidy. Assume the reader is a first-year grad student who knows what forcing and inner models are and has never opened a book on the history of geometry.
