I write novels -- three published, one overdue -- and last week I made the mistake of reading Augustine's two treatises on lying, where he holds that every lie is a sin and there are no exceptions, not even to save a life. Naturally I then built an argument that I'm a professional liar, and now I can't tell whether it's sound or whether it just proves too much.

(1) To lie is to assert something you believe is false, intending your hearer to take it as true.
(2) Every sentence of my books asserts what I believe is false. There is no such woman as my narrator; there is no such town.
(3) And I intend readers to take it as true. That's the craft. A reader who keeps telling herself it's all invented hasn't been given a novel, she's been given a puzzle.
(4) So I lie continuously, four hundred pages at a stretch.
(5) Every lie is a sin.
(6) So writing fiction is a sin, and a long one.
(7) But fiction isn't a great evil -- some of it does real good.
(8) Therefore (1) is false: being-meant-to-be-taken-as-true can't be what makes a lie.

Honestly I'd rather surrender (5) than (3). I dont want to concede that I'm not after my reader's belief, that sounds like giving up the whole game. But (8) also seems to let off the fake memoirist and the roman à clef marketed as reportage, and I'm not willing to let those off.

So: which step is the actual break, and why did it look fine to me when I wrote it down? What does the repair cost -- what's left to separate a lie from a joke, an actor's line, sarcasm? And did Augustine have a category for what I do (I vaguely remember jokes and parables being allowed somewhere)? Does his account save me, or is there a sharpened version of (3) that sinks me anyway?
