I'm the volunteer coordinator for a small river town's emergency management committee, and every February through May we have the same argument. Someone has to decide whether to spend about forty grand pre-positioning sandbags and closing the low road, and later whether to recommend an evacuation for the bottomlands, and the whole thing hangs on how likely the levee is to overtop in a given storm. Trouble is our people won't hand over one number, and honestly I've stopped wanting them to. The county hydrologist says she's somewhere between 10 and 30 percent for the scenario in front of us, a retired guy who's read this river for forty years says more like 40 to 60, and the actuary on our committee says averaging those two is a sin and gets red in the face about it. I've been reading on my own in the evenings about the idea that a belief can be a whole set of probability functions instead of a single number — ranges, credal sets, that sort of thing — and it feels like it's describing our situation exactly, but everything I find is written for people who already have the math, and none of it says what to do at seven o'clock on a Tuesday.

So could you write me a procedure my committee can actually run? Constraints: it's a two hour meeting once a month during the season, nobody in the room can do math past an average, we have no software budget, the county manager signs off on the recommendation and has to be able to defend it in the paper afterwards, and we cannot ask these experts to sit through a statistics course first. I want plain steps in order, what we write down at the end of each one, what counts as a good enough answer to move on to the next, and which steps hand the thing over to the county manager because it's a values question and not a fact question. Specifically it has to tell me: how to ask each person for a range so that all the ranges mean the same thing (is there some betting-flavored question that gets an honest low and high out of someone?); whether we should combine them into one group range at all or carry the separate ranges forward, and what combining throws away; what to do when two experts' ranges don't touch; what to do when someone's range is effectively 0 to 100, meaning they told us nothing; how to pick between planning around the worst number in the range and planning around the most likely one — I gather there are a few named rules for this and I want to know which suits a body like ours, what it forces us to do that we'll resent, and why the others are wrong for us; and what to put in the single box on the state reporting form that asks for the probability of overtopping, because I suspect putting the midpoint in there is a lie but I can't say why.

Then run it on cases, with numbers, and don't go easy. First, our real March meeting: hydrologist 10–30, river guy 40–60, and a weather service forecaster on speakerphone who says 20–25 but keeps hedging — take it all the way through to what we write down and what we tell the county manager. Second, a case where we paid for a sonar survey of a scour hole under the levee and the results come back, and afterwards the committee's range is *wider* than it was before, not narrower — someone told me that's supposed to be possible with ranges and isn't a screw-up, and I need to know whether to trust the wider range and how to say so publicly without looking incompetent. Third, a case where the procedure gives an answer I personally won't like — your pick, whether that's it ordering an evacuation we've already told people won't happen, or it counting our forty-years-on-the-river guy for much less than I think he's worth, or it saying the call isn't ours to make at all. Finally, be honest about the seams: flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, where this thing will misfire in practice, what it flat out cannot do, and what a version with real expertise and a real budget would add that ours is missing.
