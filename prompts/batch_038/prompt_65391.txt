I'm a professor — my own field is philosophy of language — and our German idealism person is on leave, so I've inhereted a three-hour graduate seminar slot on the recognition material in the Phenomenology. My Hegel is honestly not great, and the secondry literature I've been skimming mostly just repeats itself in different vocabularies. So I don't want the standard "self-consciousness needs another self-consciousness" gloss. I want to know what these particular sentences are doing. Here's the stretch I'm building the session around, quoted exactly from the old Baillie translation (paragraph numbers from my edition) — work from this, not from whatever translation you half-remember:

> ¶178: SELF-CONSCIOUSNESS exists in itself and for itself, in that, and by the fact that it exists for another self-consciousness; that is to say, it is only by being acknowledged or “recognized”. The conception of this its unity in its duplication, of infinitude realizing itself in self-consciousness, has many sides to it and encloses within it elements of varied significance.
>
> ¶182: This process of self-consciousness in relation to another self-consciousness has in this manner been represented as the action of one alone. But this action on the part of the one has itself the double significance of being at once its own action and the action of that other as well. For the other is likewise independent, shut up within itself, and there is nothing in it which is not there through itself. The first does not have the object before it only in the passive form characteristic primarily of the object of desire, but as an object existing independently for itself, over which therefore it has no power to do anything for its own behalf, if that object does not per se do what the first does to it. The process then is absolutely the double process of both self-consciousnesses. Each sees the other do the same as itself; each itself does what it demands on the part of the other, and for that reason does what it does, only so far as the other does the same. Action from one side only would be useless, because what is to happen can only be brought about by means of both.
>
> ¶184: …Each is the mediating term to the other, through which each mediates and unites itself with itself; and each is to itself and to the other an immediate self-existing reality, which, at the same time, exists thus for itself only through this mediation. They recognize themselves as mutually recognizing one another.
>
> ¶185: This pure conception of recognition, of duplication of self-consciousness within its unity, we must now consider in the way its process appears for self-consciousness. It will, in the first place, present the aspect of the disparity of the two, or the break-up of the middle term into the extremes, which, qua extremes, are opposed to one another, and of which one is merely recognized, while the other only recognizes.
>
> ¶186: …They are independent individual forms, modes of Consciousness that have not risen above the bare level of life (for the existent object here has been determined as life).

The knot I actually can't get straight is ¶182. One of my students has a deflationary reading I haven't been able to shake: she says the whole paragraph is a point about coercion — you can't get recognition out of someone by force, since "action from one side only would be useless" and the other has to do it too, and you can't make them. On her version it's a strategic claim about what works. My instinct is that it's a claim about what recognition is, that the double process is built into the concept, so that one-sided "recognition" isn't a botched or extorted version of the real thing but isn't that kind of thing at all. Which of us has the better claim on this text? Point me to the sentences that decide it, and tell me what would have to be true for the other reading to win.

Then some smaller things:
- "in that, and by the fact that" in ¶178 — is that a distinction with content (a ground/explanation split of some sort), or stylistic doubling that could collapse into "because" without loss?
- The middle term / extremes machinery in ¶184–¶185. I follow that the middle term is self-consciousness breaking itself into extremes, but what does "the break-up of the middle term into the extremes" add over and above just saying the two of them now stand opposed? Is the logical imagery load-bearing or decorative?
- ¶185's "one is merely recognized, while the other only recognizes": is the disparity a falling away from the concept just stated in ¶184, or a further determination of it? This matters for how I frame the struggle to the death that comes right after, though I don't want the answer to hinge on that.
- The parenthetical at the end of ¶186 — what work is it doing there?
