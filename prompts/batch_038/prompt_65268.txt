so the 1929 torches of freedom thing — bernays on american tobacco's payroll, hiring a group of debutantes to light up lucky strikes at a set moment in the easter parade down fifth avenue, his own photographers planted to make sure the pictures were good, the press tipped off beforehand but never told who had staged it, and a feminist writer publicly calling on women to join in and fight another sex taboo. run that through the manipulation vs. legitimate persuasion distinction.

i want the mapping first: who actually counts as the target — the paid marchers, or the women reading about it the next morning — and which of the two were manipulated as opposed to just employed, or just persuaded. then which tests it passes and fails. it hid everything that mattered about its own origins, and dressed a bought ad stunt as spontaneous protest; but it also traded entirely on values the targets already held, and there's an argument the underlying claim (a public-smoking taboo aimed only at women is unjust) was true. does that combination break the standard accounts, or does it show the accounts were about mechanism rather than content all along? and where does that leave the nudge-style line that influence aimed at the target's own good or her own ideals isn't manipulation?

one more thing worth pinning down: the textbook story that this stunt broke the taboo is disputed, and the trend was already moving before it. does the verdict change if it was mostly theater on top of an existing drift — i.e. is manipulation about what you do to someone or about whether it worked? i know rougly how the campaign played out, so skip the sales figures and the health stuff; i want the persuassion/manipulation boundary work.
