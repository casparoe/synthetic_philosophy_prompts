I run a six-week philosophy of mind course out of the county library. Twelve people, ages about 25 to 78, four of whom work in IT and have very firm opinions already. Week four is mine, 75 minutes, whiteboard only, no handouts. What I've promised them is the inversion Dennett takes from Darwin and then from Turing — the move where you account for competence using parts that have no comprehension at all, dumb stuff doing clever work. I know the material; don't explain it to me. Plan how I teach it.

Order first. Does the Darwin case have to land before the Turing one, or does opening on evolution poison the well, i.e. half the room spends twenty minutes thinking I'm having an argument about God before we ever reach machines? And what has to be in place before the inversion can even register as a claim rather than a slogan — my guess is that following a rule and representing a rule need prying apart first, but I don't know how much scaffolding that costs me at this level.

Then the carrying example. I'd planned to hang the whole session on AlphaGo's move 37, the one in the second Lee Sedol game that the commentators first read as a blunder, because it'll hook the IT people. A friend says that's exactly the wrong pick: with a designed program the comprehension just gets relocated upstairs into the engineers, so I'd be teaching them the reverse of the inversion without noticing. Is he right? If he is, what should carry it instead, and what does the carrier need to have and to lack — I want the criteria, not just a substitute.

Misconceptions I'm braced for: comprehension relocated to the designer; the inversion heard as mysterious emergence, wholes being more than the sum of parts, which is exactly backwards; and then the dismissal move, so its just a lookup table, a stochastic parrot, where they think they've agreed with me. Tell me the point in the sequence where each gets headed off, and whether one of them is actually worth letting happen and cleaning up afterwards, because 75 minutes isn't much. Also one guy will insist his chatbot genuinely understands — do I take that on in the room or deflect, and when.

One more thing to rule on. A colleague says without comprehension is ambiguous between the system not needing to represent the rule in order to follow it, and the system being unable to state the rule when you ask it. He thinks only the first is what the inversion claims. Is that a real distinction, does it matter here, and if so where do I plant it so it doesn't turn into jargon they repeat at each other?

Last: tell me what I should announce I'm skipping and why — I suspect the Turing test and Searle both, but I want the reason stated to the room. And give me one closing question that shows whether they got the inversion or only learned to say the phrase, ideally one where a specific wrong answer tells me which misconception is still live.
