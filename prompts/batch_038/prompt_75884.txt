I am writing the term paper for a graduate seminar that was advertised as being about fixed-point constructions in the theory of truth and has drifted, over eight weeks, into the general question of whether truth is a substantive property at all. Before I commit to a line of argument I want to know where I actually stand, and I find that my own view is a fog of half-formed convictions. So: here is the proposition I want a credence on, stated as tightly as I can manage it. Some deflationary account of truth is correct, in the specific sense that (a) all there is to a proposition's being true is captured by the equivalence schema together with a thesis about the function of the truth predicate — namely that it exists so we can endorse, deny, or generalize over claims we cannot enumerate one by one — and (b) accordingly, no substantive theory of truth, correspondence-flavoured, coherence-flavoured, pragmatist, or semantic, can carry any explanatory weight that the deflationary apparatus cannot carry just as well. I want a single number on that conjunction, not a survey of which deflationisms are on offer.

The number matters much less than the derivation, so please decompose it: list the sub-questions your credence depends on, assign a rough probability to each, say explicitly how you are combining them, and tell me whether you are treating them as independent — because I suspect several are not, and a naive product would understate the result. Then flag the term you are least confident in, and the term to which your final figure is most sensitive. Here are the pieces I have been turning over, and for each I would like a verdict on whether it is load-bearing or a distraction. First, the normativity worry: truth is supposed to be the aim of belief and the standard governing assertion, and a predicate exhausted by a schema seems unable to be the target of a norm in any robust sense, since saying that one ought to believe that p if and only if p is just restating the schema rather than explaining what aiming at it involves. Second, the paradox worry: the instances of the schema include, or can be made to include, self-referential sentences, so the collection cannot simply be accepted wholesale, and any principled restriction on which instances belong looks like it must be drawn by appeal to facts about truth of a non-deflationary kind. Third — and this is the one I cannot tell whether I have invented or half-remembered from a footnote — a stability worry specific to the minimalist implementation, on which the concept of truth is implicitly defined by our disposition to accept every instance of the schema. To know which sentences count as instances, you have to know what those sentences mean; but meaning does not look deflatable in the same cheap way, so the account of truth appears to borrow substance from a theory of content and to call the loan interest-free. Does that sink deflationism as such, or only that implementation of it, and how should it be weighted? Fourth, a meta-question I would like priced separately rather than folded into the total: the probability that the dispute between inflationists and deflationists is substantially verbal, so that a settled answer in either direction would be a kind of bookkeeping victory rather than a discovery.

Finally, a comparison. My own credence on the conjunction is about 0.70; a classmate who works on norms of assertion and is generally sympathetic to the whole deflationary program puts hers at 0.35, and neither of us can locate the source of the gap, since we agree on most of the individual arguments. Give me your number, then tell me which single sub-question most plausibly accounts for the difference between those two positions, and why someone might assign that sub-question a much lower probability than I do while agreeing with me everywhere else. Close with the one argument, piece of evidence, or worked example that would move your own figure the furthest, and say in which direction it would move it.
