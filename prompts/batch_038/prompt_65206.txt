I teach a 75-minute weekly seminar attached to an undergraduate course on the ethics of emerging technology. This term's section has 24 students, mostly second- and third-years: roughly a third are computer science majors, the rest are split between philosophy, public policy, and a handful of biology students who are considerably more comfortable with probabilities than the others. They have done the reading on decision-making under uncertainty, and I gave a lecture last week gesturing at the idea that the safety of a group's choices can depend on how many people are individually in a position to act — which is the reading and the lecture that flopped. Students nodded politely and then, in the discussion section, one of them said that if everyone is doing their own due diligence, more diligent people should obviously mean safer outcomes, and nobody in the room had an answer. I want them to run into the unilateralist's curse in their own behavior, before anybody names it: the way a group of individually careful, well-informed, well-meaning people becomes systematically more likely to do something catastrophic as it grows, not because anyone's judgment degraded but because the number of chances for one of them to be the most optimistic person in the room went up. I want them to feel three specific things. First, the scale effect itself — that six reasonable people are not six times as safe as one, and to see the arithmetic in what happened rather than in a formula on the board. Second, that the harm was produced by someone doing what looked locally responsible given what she alone knew, so that the debrief cannot degenerate into blaming a villain. Third, the asymmetry: the individual threshold for acting should be far more conservative than "it looks net-positive to me," precisely because a mistaken go and a mistaken no-go do not cost the same. I would also like the mirror image staged somewhere in the session — the same informational structure with the sign flipped, where the group's default caution is what kills people and one person's willingness to move alone is what saves them — because I do not want the takeaway to be the flat lesson that one should always consult. Consultation costs something, and I want them to have to say out loud what.

For the scenario, here is a seed you are welcome to improve on or replace, provided it keeps a hard deadline and permits any single member to complete the act alone. Each team is the on-call software group for a regional hospital network. A firmware bug in a widely deployed infusion pump is currently mis-dosing patients on one ward; the vendor's validated patch will not clear review for nine hours; an interim patch built in-house can be pushed to production by any one engineer holding a deployment key, with no second signature required, by design. Each engineer has seen something about the interim patch, and I want the information distributed so that a member acting alone is neither reckless nor careful enough: a shared public test report that all of them have read, plus one private observation apiece that is genuinely informative, genuinely ambiguous, and not fully recoverable from the others. Tell me how to distribute this with a single print run and no fiddling with envelopes mid-game, since I have no teaching assistant and cannot circulate. I need a payoff scheme that requires no money: a shared ledger of patients harmed and patients saved, plus some individual score that rewards being the engineer who fixed the problem and penalizes being the one who let patients die while waiting for consensus. I want that personal incentive pulling toward action, because it is realistic, and I want the debrief to be able to distinguish the student who moved for career reasons from the one who moved on honest evidence. The mechanic I am least sure how to build is the scaling. With 24 students I can field one solo player, one pair, two teams of four, and one team of eight, but each unit plays the scenario only once, so I will end up with a handful of independent observations per size and the group-size effect will be buried in noise — one team of eight might just happen not to push. Tell me how to make the effect visible anyway, whether through parallel instances, repeated rounds with fresh draws, a die roll standing in for many worlds, pooling results with the other sections, or something else, and be explicit about which of these options lets students *feel* the escalation as opposed to merely accepting my table of results. I would also like a variant in which the private observations are nearly independent and another in which they are strongly correlated — everyone has read the same study and differs only in how they interpret it — so that the class can discover that the curse is driven by the dispersion and independence of the information, not by headcount as such. Finally, build the deadline in as a real constraint, since "just pool your evidence first" is free only when time is free.

For the debrief, give me roughly twenty minutes of questions in the order I should ask them, each anchored to a moment that will actually have occurred, moving from "what did you know when you reached for the key" through to the naming of the idea, which should not happen before that point. Then tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each case: the student who refuses on principle and thereby short-circuits the demonstration for her whole team; the group that appoints a leader within the first ten seconds, quietly converting a scenario about unilateralism into one about hierarchy — I genuinely do not know whether to forbid this, since forbidding it may be less instructive than watching it happen and asking afterwards who authorized the authority; the students who reverse-engineer the pedagogy in minute two and fail on purpose to give me the result they think I want; and the run in which nothing bad happens at all and the room concludes the reading was hysterical. Close with a candid account of which feature of the phenomenon this kind of game structurally cannot reproduce. My guess is that it has to do with irreversibility — my students will know the patients are imaginary, that the facilitator is watching, and that the round can be replayed — but say where you think the real limit lies and what I have to supply by other means to make up for it.
