So I'm sitting in on an epistemology seminar this term (not for credit, I just like the arguments) and we've burned two sessions on the bootstrapping worry about easy knowledge. I went home and tried to write out the version of the argument that disturbs me most in fully explicit form, and I think I've managed to break it — my version now proves something so strong that it has to be wrong somewhere, but every individual step looks boring and I can't find the bad one. Maybe you can.

Base case. Roxanne drives a car with a gas gauge she's never tested and has no particular opinion about. Say a source is "basic-OK" for a person when the source is in fact reliable, conditions are normal, and the person can get knowledge straight from its outputs without needing any prior justification for trusting it. That's supposed to be the minimal externalist-friendly claim and I want to grant it for the sake of argument.
1. The gauge is basic-OK for Roxanne: when she looks and forms the belief that it reads F, she can thereby know that it reads F.
2. On the same look she forms the belief that the tank is full, and she can thereby know that too.
3. If S knows p and knows q, then S is in a position to know (p and q), and hence in a position to know (p → q), by trivial deduction.
4. So after one look Roxanne is in a position to know: if the gauge reads F, then the tank is full.
5. She drives for a year, does this a few hundred times in all sorts of conditions, and is in a position to know the same conditional for each of those episodes.
6. If S knows each member of a finite list of conditionals and knows that they are all the relevant cases, then S is in a position to know the generalization they support.
7. So Roxanne is in a position to know that her gauge is reliable, just by driving the car and never once checking it against a dipstick or a gas station receipt.

Up to here I'm sort of willing to shrug: sure, bootstrapping is epistemically cheap and a litte gross, but maybe that's the price of not being a skeptic. The trouble is what happens once you notice the argument never used anything specific about the gauge.
8. Nothing in 1–7 turns on which source it is, or on Roxanne having any prior evidence about it. All it needs is that the source be basic-OK for her and that she can read the outputs and conjoin them.
9. So: for any source whatsoever, if it is basic-OK for S, then S is in a position to know that it is reliable, purely by using it.
10. Now run the template on a source that Roxanne's mechanic has just told her, at length and with diagrams, is stuck permanently on F. Suppose the mechanic is lying, the gauge is actually fine, and conditions are normal. Then it is basic-OK for her, so by 9 she is in a position to know it's reliable — even though, by hypothesis, her total evidence tells her decisively that it isn't.
11. So knowledge that a source is trustworthy can come apart from the evidence about it in the strongest possible way: S can be in a position to know a source is reliable while everything she has to go on says it's broken.
12. But if that's possible, then misleading evidence against a source can never undermine the beliefs that come from it, because S can always bootstrap her way back to knowing the source is good, and the misleading evidence just gets outranked by her own knowledge.
13. And since the template applies to perception as a source, this also hands anybody a one-afternoon refutation of external-world skepticism with no independent checking required, which is not how that debate is suposed to go.
14. 13 is false. So something above is false, and it isn't 14.

My hunch is that the air leaks out between 5 and 6 — a year's worth of known instances turning into a known generalization feels like the biggest jump in the whole thing — but I don't trust my hunch, and I can see 10 being the culprit instead since I built that case myself. I'd really like to keep 1 and 3 if I can: giving up 3 puts me on a road I don't want to travel, and giving up 1 seems to leave me a skeptic about perception. So — which step do you reject, why did it look so innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? And separately: if you kill one of 8–12 rather than something in the base case, does the argument that stops at 7 survive your fix, or does it die too?
