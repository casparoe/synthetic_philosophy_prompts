I write about science and ideas for a couple of magazines and I'm putting together a piece on the social ontology of money. The episode I want to hang it on is August 15, 1971, when Nixon suspended the convertability of dollars into gold, i.e. the anchor that had held Bretton Woods up since the war. Nobody voted on it, the patched-up Smithsonian deal that December fell apart too, and by 1973 the major currencies were floating. And yet the dollar just... kept being money. Same pieces of paper, same role in everyone's accounts, still the world's reserve currency, even though the thing that supposedly backed the status had been switched off, and then a decade of ugly inflation.

I want to run this through Searle's account — money as a status function assigned by a counts-as rule and kept alive by collective intentionality. So: what does that account actually predict about August 1971? Which features of what happened does it explain, and which does it miss or misdescribe — especially whether collective acceptance was really present in any meaningful sense, or whether most people were just stuck holding dollars with no way to opt out. And does the episode confirm the theory, strain it, or sit outside its scope entirely: maybe Searle is answering what makes something money rather than why it keeps its value, in which case grading him on an inflation story is a category error I should avoid making. Feel free to bring in Hacking's interactive vs indifferent kinds if it earns its place — a currency whose value moves on what people believe about the institution looks awfully interactive, though I don't know if that's an argument or just a pun.

When you answer, be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who work on it and which are genuinely contested. I don't want to run a minority reading as if it were the consensus.
