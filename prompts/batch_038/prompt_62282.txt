For thirty-eight years I worked as a physical chemist, chiefly on reaction kinetics, and I have only lately taken to reading philosophy and the history of the social sciences with any seriousness. I find that I keep reaching for habits from my old trade — mechanism, confounding, mediation — in domains where those habits may not travel well, and I should like to test the suspicion that they do not. Our local philosophical society has asked me to present something on explanation in the historical social sciences, and I have chosen the hardest case I know.

The case is Weber's thesis about ascetic Protestantism and the spirit of capitalism. I take the claim, as a description of what he advanced, to be roughly this: that certain strands of Protestant religiosity helped produce a distinctive economic conduct — methodical labour in a calling, restraint upon consumption, the reinvestment of surplus — which in turn fed the development of modern capitalism; that the effect was largely unintended by the believers themselves; and that Weber cautioned against replacing a one-sided materialist account of history with an equally one-sided spiritualist one. What troubles me is the causal work supposedly done by the values.

To get clear on this I have drawn up, for my own purposes, five distinct routes by which the observed pattern might come about. The labels are mine, and I do not believe they correspond to any standard taxonomy, so please treat them exactly as I have stated them rather than attempting to match them to positions in the literature:

(a) Motive-channeling. The values enter the agent's own practical reasoning: he works and saves differently because of what he takes his faith to require of him, and the aggregate economic effect is the sum of those individual reasons.

(b) Selection and retention. The doctrine moves nobody much, but it sorts. Congregations whose teaching happens to retain the thrifty, the literate, and the mobile out-survive and out-reproduce others, and individuals self-select into and out of sects according to dispositions they already have. The professed value is a tag attached to a population that was already different.

(c) Signalling and coordination. A shared profession of belief functions as a costly and hard-to-fake marker that solves trust problems in credit, apprenticeship, and long-distance dealing. The economic benefit runs through the network the marker sustains rather than through the content of what is believed, and exit costs keep the marker honest.

(d) Reverse channel. Commercial and legal forms came first, and religious self-description adjusted to them afterwards. The ethic is a rationalization composed by people already living inside new economic arrangements, and the historian mistakes a record of the change for a cause of it.

(e) Archive artifact. The difference lies in the sources, not in the world. Ascetic communities generated introspective paperwork — diaries, spiritual autobiographies, account books kept as moral self-examination — so that their inner life is legible in a way that the inner life of more festive or more oral cultures is not. What is correlated with religiosity is documentation, and the evidence is drawn from those who wrote.

I should like the following from you.

First, rank these five routes, together with any sixth route you think my list genuinely omits and that is not reducible to one of them, by how much of the pattern Weber pointed to each could account for — the over-representation of Protestants among entrepreneurs and among the more highly skilled workers in confessionally mixed regions, and the distinctive attitudes toward work and profit visible in their self-descriptions — without already presupposing the economic conditions it is meant to explain. Give me the ordering, and for each item a score between 0 and 1 on two separate dimensions: how far the route is genuinely cultural, in the sense that it would fail if the values were replaced by purely economic or demographic facts about the same people; and how far it is testable in principle with the kinds of evidence actually available, such as parish and probate records, subscription lists, confessional censuses, and modern surveys. One or two sentences of justification per score, not an essay per item.

Second, tell me which of these routes, if it were the dominant one, would leave the thesis unfalsifiable or circular, and which would leave it intact but badly described. I suspect these are different items in the ranking, and I want to know why.

Third, a colleague of mine holds that (c) is economics wearing religious vocabulary, and that only (a) is a case of culture being a cause at all, since only there does a value figure in the agent's reasons for acting. I am inclined to the opposite view: (c) strikes me as the more interesting precisely because a shared profession of belief is doing real work through its meaning, and because the work it does would vanish if the belief were publicly disavowed. Please adjudicate between us. In doing so, give me a straight answer to the underlying question, which I take to be the philosophical point of the whole exercise: for values to be a cause of economic conduct, must they enter the agent's practical reasoning, or can they cause things, as it were, over the agent's head?

Fourth, close with a short verdict on whether any of the top-ranked routes earns the description that culture caused this, or whether that phrase should be retired from the debate in favour of something more specific that I could state in a seminar without embarrassment.

I want your own reasoning about these five routes rather than a survey of who has argued what; I am interested in the mechanisms, not in the historiography. And since I am conscious of being an amateur here, I ask two further things: tell me plainly how confident you are in the ranking you produce and in your answer to the third question, and single out the one point in your response where you judge yourself most likely to be wrong.
