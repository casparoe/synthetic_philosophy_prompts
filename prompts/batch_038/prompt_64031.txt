I run a four-person pricing and analytics team at a small specialty insurer that writes long-tail, first-of-a-kind exposures: product liability for newly approved medical technologies, aggregation-prone cyber and technology-error-and-omission covers, and a handful of parametric climate perils in markets where we are among the first writers. Roughly three times a month, underwriting sends us an exposure and asks for an expected loss figure, and the request comes with structural features that make the usual actuarial machinery misbehave — somewhere between zero and twenty-five years of relevant loss history, a generating process that is demonstrably not stationary (device design changes, fuel loads and building stock change, attack techniques change), and a mechanism for the bad tail that nobody has yet observed in the wild. Turnaround is expected in forty-eight hours for a renewal and about a week for new business. The output feeds two consumers who will not tolerate ambiguity: a capital model that ingests a single number per exposure, and a chief risk officer with a finance background and no actuarial or philosophical training, who signs the line. My team is numerate and statistically literate but has read nothing in philosophy of probability, so whatever I hand them has to be runnable without understanding the theory behind it.

What I want is a decision procedure — ordered steps, the questions to ask at each, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and an explicit specification of the possible outputs, including the cases where the correct output is not a point estimate but a range, a set of scenarios, or a documented refusal to quantify accompanied by an instruction about what to do instead. I would like it grounded in Keynes's work on probability and uncertainty rather than in generic risk-management lore: the treatment of probability as a logical relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion rather than a frequency or a degree of belief; the claims that some such relations are not numerically measurable at all and that some pairs of them are not comparable with one another even where both are measurable; the separate dimension he calls the weight of an argument, which tracks how much relevant evidence stands behind a conclusion independently of how favorable that evidence is; and, from his later work on employment and interest, the critique of the convention whereby practical people treat genuine uncertainty as though it were calculable risk, proceed anyway on the strength of a spontaneous confidence he likens to instinct or enterprise, and are only retrospectively revealed to have been guessing. Three specific translation problems I want you to confront head-on. First, my recollection — which you should correct if it is wrong — is that Keynes did not think accumulating more relevant evidence automatically strengthens an argument, since new evidence can be unfavorable or can reveal that the case was less one-sided than it appeared; if that is right, the procedure must encode it, and must not simply treat "we bought another data vendor's history" as a mechanical upgrade to the estimate. Second, the capital model requires a strict ordering of exposures by severity even when the underlying arguments are, on this account, not comparable; tell me what a defensible convention is here, and how to flag to the CRO that a particular ordering is manufactured rather than discovered. Third, the whole framework is obviously abusable — insufficient weight of argument is a very convenient excuse for declining to quantify, and for pricing too high, and for never being held to an estimate — so build in whatever discipline prevents my team from using Keynes as a shield, and tell me what to say to an underwriter who objects that a refusal to produce a number is itself a decision with a number attached.

Please then run the procedure end to end on three live files, showing the answers at each step rather than just the conclusions. One: general liability for a class of implantable neurostimulation devices approved six years ago, with United States claims data only, no long-term failure mode characterized in the literature, and a plaintiff bar that has begun advertising for claimants. Two: a wildfire-peril portfolio in a region where a credible loss record begins about twenty years ago, and where both the vegetation regime and the density of interface housing have changed substantially inside that window. Three: a large renewal of a technology aggregation exposure that our CFO has already quoted an indicative rate to the broker and that we need for premium volume — I want at least one of these three to come out badly for us, and I would rather it be this one than a soft finding I can ignore, so do not spare me if the procedure says the honest answer is that we should not be writing it at the price already mentioned. Finally, close with a short account of what the procedure cannot do: which distinctions from the underlying theory were dropped or flattened to make it usable, where it will systematically misfire, at which steps an analyst's judgment is doing the real work under a procedural label, and whether any part of the foundation I have asked you to build on is contested enough among commentators that operationalizing it involves taking a side in a scholarly dispute rather than applying settled doctrine.
