**Does the simulation argument dampen fanaticism? A different route than the usual one**

**1. Setup.** Fanaticism, in the technical sense, is the view that arbitrarily large payoffs dominate: a sufficiently tiny probability of a sufficiently astronomical good can swamp any ordinary consideration. The far-future case that drives it is roughly this: if humanity survives and spreads, the number of future lives dwarfs everything that has ever existed, so the expected value of what we do now is set almost entirely by our effect on that number, and near-term effects wash out.

The simulation argument shows up in this literature as a footnote worry. Bostrom's trilemma says one of three things holds: almost no human-level civilizations reach a posthuman stage capable of high-fidelity ancestor simulations; or almost no posthuman civilizations are interested in running such simulations; or almost everyone with our kind of experiences is simulated. The standard reading of the third disjunct, for longtermist purposes, is that it adds a termination risk — sims get switched off, sims are cheap, sims are run only until the interesting part is over — so the astronomical case is a bit shakier than it looks. That's the version I want to set aside. It treats "we are simulated" as a uniform hazard. I think it's not uniform, and the non-uniformity does more interesting work than risk does.

**2. The trilemma is a claim about posthuman psychologies, not just about counts.** Disjunct two is doing something people skip past. It says that among civilizations with effectively unbounded compute, near-zero of them want to run high-fidelity simulations of their own history. That's a claim about values, and it's a surprising one. A civilization that expands, optimizes, and converts resources into whatever it takes to be good — the profile the astronomical case assumes — is exactly the profile you'd expect to run ancestor sims in bulk, because ancestor sims are cheap, information-rich, and about themselves. So disjunct two being true needs an explanation, and the available explanations are all axiological: they think creating suffering-laden simulated ancestors is wrong; they don't care about the past at all and value only forward optimization; or they've concluded simulated beings aren't conscious so there's nothing to gain.

Each of those, if it's the actual explanation, tells us something about what posthuman civilizations are like — including, plausibly, ours. And two of the three shrink the far-future case. A civilization with a standing prohibition on creating vast numbers of beings who suffer is not going to fill the light cone with as many minds as physics allows; it's going to leave a lot of the budget unspent, deliberately. A civilization that has no curiosity about its own past is a civilization whose values are not the expansive, generative ones the fanatical calculation is quietly borrowing from the human case. So disjunct two, taken seriously, is evidence against the *shape* of the far future, not just its length.

**3. Conditional on disjunct three, the far future is capped by an agent, not by physics.** The expansionist case needs a future whose size is set by our descendants' resource acquisition. But if we're simulated, our future is set by whoever is simulating us, and sims are made for reasons — someone wanted this particular history to happen and is watching it. The ceiling on the future is then a function of a curator's continued interest. That's a smaller and much less controllable quantity than a light cone, and it's not something we can grow by being more efficient.

**4. The interestingness constraint (this is the part I'm least sure about).** In a curated sim, what buys you a long future is being worth keeping around. So conditional on disjunct three, the actions that maximize expected future value are not the actions that maximize expected future population; they're the actions that maximize expected continued attention. Call it the interestingness constraint. The punchline I want: the constraint and the expansionist program are in tension, not just orthogonal. A civilization that flattens itself into a single convergent optimizing process is, viewed from outside, a solved problem with a flat payoff curve. It has stopped generating the novelty that would justify the compute. So disjunct three doesn't merely add hazard to the far-future case; it points at a property of futures — generativity, irreducibility, unresolved structure — that the fanatical calculation has no term for, and that the calculation's own recommended strategy tends to destroy.

**5. Notes for the section I couldn't write:** need to say whether the interestingness constraint survives a curator who finds convergence fascinating rather than boring; need some story about nested sims (does the constraint iterate?); need to confront the obvious charge that I'm conditionalizing on a disjunct I have no independent evidence for, so the trilemma is doing no work beyond smuggling in my priors.

**6. My own worries, stated plainly.** (i) The trilemma's disjuncts are about frequencies across civilizations, and moving from "almost no posthuman civilizations do X" to "our descendants wouldn't do X" needs a homogeneity assumption Bostrom doesn't offer. (ii) Section 4 could be a just-so story dressed as an inference: any property I like can be declared the thing curators reward. (iii) The whole piece may be unfalsifiable in the bad way, since it gives no observable difference in what we should do *today* beyond a vague preference for keeping options and novelty open.
