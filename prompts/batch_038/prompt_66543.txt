I'm TA-ing a first-year seminar on philosophy in the movies and next week we're doing Back to the Future. I want to build the session around presentism vs. eternalism instead of the free-will-and-determinism stuff everyone defaults to with time travel films, because as far as I can tell the movie is committed to something ontologically much weirder than fatalism, and I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the metaphysics seriously rather than treating one as an illustration of the other. The detail that won't let go of me: the Hill Valley courthouse clock has been frozen at 10:04 ever since the lightning hit it that night in 1955, and the whole escape plan depends on that stuck clock plus a souvenir flyer that reports the strike in the past tense. That is a town treating a long-gone event as a standing, present-tense feature of the world. Then there's the snapshot — Marty carries a picture taken in 1985 in which his brother and sister get rubbed out one at a time, oldest first, and Doc's own gloss is that the alteration spreads as a kind of ripple instead of landing all at once. What ontology makes sense of a photograph that changes what it depicts? A presentist should be able to say the photo is just a present object with present properties, but then it's hard to see what it's a picture of anymore, and an eternalist should be able to say it depicts a fixed stretch of the block, which is exactly what it stops doing. Please walk me through which scenes carry the ontological weight and which view each one favors, and be blunt about where the film is just being sloppy and where a charitable reading turns into a stretch.

The part I actually can't resolve, and where I want you to push on me: at the end, Marty and Doc both remember the erased history, and Marty wakes up into an upgraded 1985 while the old one seems simply gone. So who is remembering what? If the original stretch of 1955-to-1985 no longer ever happened, their memories have no causal ancestry, and yet the film treats those memories as accurate and makes them plot-relevant — Marty's the only one who knows his parents used to be losers. Is the movie presentist (nothing persists to constrain the rewrite), or eternalist with the block edited after the fact — and does editing a block even cohere, since it seems to need a second time in which the block is first one way and then another? I've been privately calling the view the film appears to hold a rewritable block, and I have a distinction I can't test on anyone: an edit (the very same block, changed) versus a replacement (old block deleted, new one installed). I genuinely don't know whether either is a real position or something I made up on my couch at 1 a.m. Related: the erasure spreading gradually over days looks like a block that shrinks from the earlier end, not just one that grows at the later end, and I have never once seen anybody defend a shrinking block. Is that an actual view, a view that can be coherently stated, or nonsense — and if it's nonsense, why does the ripple effect seem to require it? Finally, what fact of the matter, if any, settles whether the world of the film contains one 1955–1985 stretch or two, and what would each answer cost the movie?

What I want back is criticism, not a summary: which readings survive contact with the scenes, where the honest critic has to say the film resists the interpretation, and — this is the part I most want — whether the movie dramatizes anything the presentism/eternalism debate as usually framed leaves out. My hunch is that it does something with memory and with traces that the standard arguments ignore, and also something structural, in that the audience is put in a position where 1955 and 1985 are equally vivid and equally real while no character can occupy that position; tell me if that hunch is worth anything or if I'm just describing what any cross-cutting film does. Please stick to the first film — half the class hasn't seen the sequels and I don't want to build a session on them — and don't give me a reading list or citations, I'll do my own hunting afterwards. American English, and if you can flag the two or three places where a first-year student is most likely to go wrong with this material, that'd be gold for my discussion questions.
