I am a science-policy analyst, not a philosopher, and my reading group has just finished a trade-press book about expertise, institutional trust, and the politics of evidence. The closing chapter turns on a philosophical claim about truth that I am not equipped to check, and a good deal of the book's argument leans on it. Here is the passage, in the author's own framing:

> Philosophers used to argue about what truth is: correspondence with the facts, coherence within a system, practical usefulness. The deflationists showed that the whole quarrel rested on a mistake, because "true" does not pick out a property at all. To say that a claim is true is just to repeat the claim, so that saying the sentence about the water sample containing lead is true is nothing over and above the water sample's containing lead. Truth, on this view, is a scrap of grammar we could strike from the language tomorrow without finding ourselves able to say anything less than we can say now — which is why the liar paradox loses its sting, since a sentence that says of itself that it is not true simply fails to say anything and therefore cannot generate a contradiction. It is also why the deflationists have quietly made common cause with the pragmatists: if truth is no more than a device for nodding along, then when scientists claim that their theories are converging on the truth, they are not describing a relation between theories and the world, they are cheering for their own side. Which is why the whole project of defending truth against the post-truth crowd is something of a trap. There is no deep property there to defend, only a habit of endorsement, and the habit is the thing worth arguing about.

A friend who did graduate work in philosophy read this over my shoulder, said that the opening is fine and that the trouble begins in the middle, and then declined to say more, on the grounds that I would enjoy hunting it down myself. I would rather not spend three months acquiring the literature, so I am asking you instead. Go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me what to trust. For each sentence, mark it as something a deflationist would sign off on as it stands, a fair simplification for a general reader that loses nothing important, a subtle misrepresentation that keeps deflationary vocabulary while changing the claim, or simply false. I would also like to know which strand of deflationism the author is drawing on at each step — redundancy in the manner of Ramsey, endorsement in the manner of Strawson, disquotation and semantic ascent in the manner of Quine, minimalism in the manner of Horwich — because my suspicion is that the chapter slides among several of them without noticing, and that part of what looks like a bold conclusion is an artifact of the slide. Please run the audit on deflationism's own terms rather than measuring the passage against a correspondence theory the author never accepted; I do not want it convicted of failing to be something else. Note in particular whether the passage's claim about what the truth predicate is *for* is the deflationists' own account or its opposite, since that seems to me to be where the argument starts to run downhill.

Finally, I want a committed answer rather than a diplomatic one. Where deflationists genuinely disagree among themselves — about the liar, about whether truth is a property at all, about whether deflationism can make sense of truth as a goal or a norm of assertion — do not hand me a map of the disagreement and leave me to choose from it. Tell me which position you think is correct and why, and then say plainly how much of the chapter's conclusion survives once the passage is corrected: does anything remain of the move from the claim that truth has no underlying nature to the claim that there is nothing there worth defending, or is that step a non sequitur dressed up as an insight? If you think some sentence of the passage is right for reasons the author has not stated, say so and supply the reasons.
