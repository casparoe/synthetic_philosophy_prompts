I'm applying for a ten-week summer studentship in a group that does formal modelling of cooperation between AI systems, and they want a two-page research plan with the application. My half-formed idea is below — I think its decent but I can't get it into the shape they're asking for.

Background as I understand it, correct me if I'm mangling it. A safe Pareto improvement is roughly a way of changing how the players of a game end up playing — usually by having them precommit to programs that can condition on each other, or delegate to representatives carrying instructions — such that (i) the outcome everyone lands on is better for each of them than the outcome they'd have got by default, and (ii) it's safe, in that going along with it can't leave you worse off than the default no matter what the other side does. The whole selling point is that it's supposed to rescue cooperation in one-shot mixed-motive games — chicken, prisoner's dilemma, bargaining and demand games — where you can't lean on repetition and where the agents don't have shared probabilistic beliefs about how each other will play. My worry: both clauses are indexed to "the default", and in precisely the situations the device is meant to help with, there may be no such thing. Multiple equilibria, or a default that itself depends on what each player expects the other to expect, means player 1 and player 2 can be working with different candidate baselines. Then a transformation that is safe and improving relative to my baseline might be neither relative to yours. So the concept seems to smuggle in exactly the shared counterfactual agreement that the miscoordination problem lacks. I dont know whether that tension is fatal, or whether it just means safety has to be redefined — subjectively, per-player, or relative to a set of baselines with some robustness condition across the set. One thing to engage with rather than ignore: I vaguely remember some of the program-game work sidestepping the choice of a particular improvement altogether and instead proving that, under mild conditions on players' beliefs, each player does at least as well as the worst payoff available in any efficient outcome. Is that a way of dissolving my worry, or does it just relocate the baseline problem into the belief conditions?

Turn all this into the proposal. I want: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, because "is the default well-defined?" as I've phrased it is far too vague to fund; an honest account of why the existing treatments leave it open rather than settled — including a straight answer on whether this is a real gap or something that just falls out of the definitions once you write them down carefully, and if the latter say so, I'd rather know before I submit; the method, i.e. what I'd actually be doing week by week, and why that approach instead of the obvious alternatives like just running more simulations on toy games or writing a philosophy paper about counterfactuals; what a positive result looks like (I'm imagining a worked-out definition of baseline-robust safety, conditions under which such improvements provably exist or provably don't, and at least one concrete example where the standard account returns the wrong verdict) and what would count as the project failing; and a realistic assessment of whether ten weeks is enough, naming the sub-question I should cut.
