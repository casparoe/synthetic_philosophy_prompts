Please write a short story of between 1,200 and 1,600 words, with a title, that dramatizes a problem in the philosophy of science entirely from inside an institution that has never heard of the philosophy of science.

The problem in abstract, for your orientation only and not for the page: two accounts of the world are both known to contain at least one error, and we nonetheless want to rank them, to say that one is nearer the truth than the other. The story must never use the words "verisimilitude," "truthlikeness," or "approximation to the truth," and no character may be a philosopher, a professor, or anyone given to generalizing.

Build the story on the following machinery, which you should treat as given and render concrete rather than explain:

The inland city of Vessa lives on snowmelt led down from the high country. A founding commission walked that country once, a hundred and forty years ago, and drew it; the country has never been fully surveyed again, and knowledge of it reaches the city only in fragments, through the sworn reports of drovers, prospectors, and repair parties who cross the pass. These reports are entered, one at a time, into the books of the Office of the Ledger, which keeps and ranks the competing maps of the high country and certifies one of them when the city must act.

Every map is scored against a division of the country into named districts — the Kilnfield, the Ashen Steps, the Nine Terraces, the Salt Road, Greywater Flat, the Threshing Plain, Long Bar. The reckoning is kept in two columns: what the map asserts, the rival does not, and inspection has borne out; and what the rival asserts, the map does not, and inspection has borne out. Article Nine of the Office's charter, written by a founder who distrusted the divisions his own surveyors had left him, permits certification only where the first column is non-empty and the second is empty, and only where that holds under every admissible division of the country into districts — not merely under the coarse division the founding commission happened to use.

The two maps currently before the Office are those of Anselm Ferrand, the Office's own surveyor of thirty years, and Dace Iles, who works for the water company. They disagree about where the water-bearing stratum lies beneath Long Bar. Each map already carries a recorded contradiction somewhere else in the country; neither mapmaker is a fraud, a fool, or a partisan, and the story must not let the difficulty dissolve into one of those.

Within six weeks of the thaw the Office must certify a map, because the certification decides which of two aqueduct cuts is begun: the short Long Bar cut, which the city can afford and which only the Iles map justifies, or the longer Greywater cut, which both maps permit but which will not deliver full flow until the third year and will leave the lower terraces short through two winters.

The discovery belongs to Odile Renn, a junior reckoner, and it has two parts. First, that dividing a district more finely can move an entry from one column to the other without either map having changed at all. Second, that once two rival maps are each known to be wrong somewhere, Article Nine can never be satisfied by either of them, at any division fine enough to isolate the districts where they differ — and therefore that no certification the Office has issued in living memory met the rule it cites, because every reckoner has quietly substituted the coarse founding division and written the result up as though it were the charter's. Make the chief reckoner's response not outrage but the weary knowledge of someone who has made the substitution himself for decades and can name the seasons the city would not have survived without it.

Observe these constraints:

- The ledger arithmetic appears exactly once, in a scene, in a form an untrained reader can follow: the named districts, the two columns filled in, and the single refinement of a division that turns the ranking over. It is never restated or summarized afterward.
- No character articulates the general lesson, and no scene exists to illustrate a thesis. The reader should be able to reconstruct the argument from what happens and what is said, and the story should also work, as a story, for a reader who never reconstructs it.
- End on something Odile Renn decides, or fails to decide, about the certification, with the aqueduct unresolved and the reader able to regard her either as the only honest person in the Office or as its vandal.
- Name no real philosopher, work, university, or institution. Prose should be restrained and concrete; no allegorical signposting, no dream sequences, no closing paragraph that lifts the camera.
