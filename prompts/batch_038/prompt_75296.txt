I am drafting a short paper arguing that factored cognition is best read as an idealization of *calculation* rather than of *deliberation*, and that the difference matters for how much work iterated amplification can be asked to do. Below is the argument in near-final form. Please give me critical comments in the specific ways requested at the end; you do not need to know the surrounding literature well, and I would rather have your assessment of the argument as stated than pointers to things I should read.

**Draft**

§1. In the iterated-amplification picture, the way to get cognitive work out of an agent that is individually too weak to do it is to factor the work: split a hard question *Q* into subquestions the base agent can answer reliably, then reassemble the answers. The stronger claim — call it factored cognition — is that this is not merely an engineering technique but the correct description of what a competent reasoner is already doing: successful human deliberation is the search for a factorization of the problem relative to which the reasoner's unaided judgment is trustworthy. Humans Consulting HCH makes the structure explicit, as a human consulting a helper that is itself a human consulting a helper, recursively.

§2. My objection turns on a property I will call factorization invariance. Let a decomposition be a finite rooted tree: the root is *Q*, internal nodes are subquestions, leaves are answered directly by the base policy, and each internal node carries a reassembly rule turning the answers to its children into an answer to itself. Call a tree admissible when the base policy is reliable at every leaf and every reassembly rule is truth-preserving. The standing hope is that admissibility transfers upward: an admissible tree for *Q* makes the answer to *Q* reliable.

§3. That transfer does not hold as stated, because two admissible trees for the same *Q* can yield different answers. Take *Q* = is this thirty-page argument valid. One decomposition checks each inference locally, one step at a time. Another reconstructs the argument's global shape and asks whether the conclusion follows from a précis of the premises. Each leaf is within the reasoner's competence and each reassembly rule is truth-preserving, yet the two can disagree, because the second decomposition has silently re-construed what the argument says. Invariance therefore fails wherever the choice of decomposition construes the question, and construal is not something the base policy can be assumed reliable about merely because it is reliable at the leaves.

§4. Nor can invariance be verified from inside. Checking that all admissible decompositions agree requires enumerating them, which is harder than *Q* itself; and deliberating about which decomposition to use requires a decomposition of that meta-question, and so on. Factored cognition does not eliminate un-factored judgment, it relocates it into the choice of tree. There appear to be three exits: an infinite tower, which is what HCH literally proposes, and which I take to be the honest rather than the evasive reading; a fixed point of self-trust; or truncation by something non-cognitive — taste, temperament, or the settled norms of a community.

§5. The regress is harmless in one situation only: where recognition is easier than production. If the base agent can reliably *check* a proposed answer, the tower is anchored at every level and no global choice of decomposition is needed, since the human at each level of HCH need only accept or reject what the level below offers. Where verification is no easier than generation, decomposition buys nothing: the subquestions are as opaque as the whole.

§6. Hence the thesis. Factored cognition is an idealization of calculation that generalizes to deliberation only by accident. Its paradigm cases are arithmetic and proof-checking, where the strategy of shrinking the trusted kernel succeeds precisely because a small checker can audit an arbitrarily large construction. Alignment work generalizes from these cases to value-laden questions — whether an outcome is good, whether an action deceives — where no comparable asymmetry is in evidence. There, the recursive tower does not remove the un-amplified judgment of the human at the top; it buries that judgment under a great deal of machinery. If so, the safety argument for iterated amplification must be indexed domain by domain to the existence of a verification asymmetry, and the domains we most care about are the ones where the asymmetry is least plausible.

§7. A corollary that worries me: even in mathematics the asymmetry is partly an artifact of formalization, and the choice of formalization is itself an un-factored judgment. My paradigm case may therefore be weaker than §6 grants.

2. Novelty. Is §5's verification/generation asymmetry simply an assumption already load-bearing in debate-style approaches? If I am claiming credit for something the literature treats as background, say so plainly.
