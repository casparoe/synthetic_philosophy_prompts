Second-year condensed matter PhD student here, and I need help putting something together for my group's journal club.

Last term a visiting philosopher of science sat in on our weekly journal club for a couple of months, and he said "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts" at least once per meeting — always at exactly the moment when somebody was trying to explain a collective behavior in terms of the microscopic model. The last time it came up it was used to shut down a discussion of whether the collective phase in an array of coupled oscillators counts as a real property or is just a convenient way of talking about a few hundred individual ones. For context: it's a chip with a couple hundred driven oscillators, each with its own natural frequency; you crank the coupling past a threshold, they lock, and now you can talk about one shared phase and an order parameter that used to be zero and isn't anymore — and no single oscillator has that property. His argument was basically: you can't get the shared phase out of the individual oscillators, so it's genuinely novel, so your reductionist program is never going to absorb it, case closed. Half my group now repeats the sentence approvingly and the other half says it means nothing, and I'm fairly confident nobody is disagreeing with the same claim anybody else is making.

So can you lay out the distinct precise theses that one sentence could be carrying? Be picky about both halves of it. What could "greater" mean — more properties, more causal powers, more ontological stuff, more information required to describe it, not derivable even in principle versus not derived yet versus not derivable by anyone working with a human-sized budget of time and paper? And what exactly is "the sum of its parts" — the parts sitting alone, the parts plus their intrinsic properties, or the parts plus every coupling, boundary condition and initial condition they're actually embedded in? Because if the sum is allowed to include the coupling term, a lot of the cases people call emergent stop looking interesting, and if it isn't, the slogan seems trivially true of literally any interacting system. Also tell me if some of the readings don't even have a truth value, on the grounds that "sum" is doing metaphorical work with no counterpart operation in the physics. Sort them for me into trivial-but-true, substantive-and-plausible-for-at-least-some-cases, and false-or-unintelligible, with a one-line verdict and two or three sentences of reasoning each, as a numbered list I can print out and hand around.

For every reading, say whether it would actually license the move he made — going from "we can't derive the shared phase today" to "a bottom-up account is impossible in principle" — and flag the spots where two different readings get swapped mid-sentence, since I think that's what makes the thing feel forceful and then evaporate the second you push on it. Finish with the single sentence you'd say out loud at the meeting if you were me, one that doesn't sound like I'm just handing him the point. American English, please.
