Our AI-safety reading group is taking up Frankenstein next month, and I want to assign it as an embedded-agency text rather than as the usual bioethics parable about hubris. My working thesis, which I would like you to test hard against the book: Victor's catastrophe is not a value-loading problem, because he never attempts to load values at all. He builds an agent, supplies no specification, and then withdraws as the only available source of one, leaving the Creature's goals to be formed entirely from its environment — the De Lacey cottage it watches through a gap in the wall as its training data, and the three volumes it finds in an abandoned satchel in the woods, Milton's Paradise Lost, Plutarch's Lives, and Goethe's Sorrows of Young Werther, as its priors on what an agent is for and what it may demand of its maker.

Read the novel through that lens and answer three questions. First, does the Creature's turn to killing William, Clerval, and Elizabeth look like instrumental convergence in the service of an acquired terminal value, or is that the wrong description of the mechanism — and if so, what is the right one? Second, how should we classify Victor's refusal to build the female companion and his destruction of her half-finished body on the remote island: a corrigibility success that averted a worse outcome, or the single worst intervention available to him? Note that the novel gives Victor reasons that sound like an argument from goal drift in a second agent he cannot specify either. Third, the Creature learns his designer's evaluation of him not from the designer but from Victor's private laboratory journal, found in the pockets of the coat he stole, and his own account of all this reaches the reader only as the innermost of three nested narratives — Victor retelling it inside Walton's letters. Does that mediated structure say anything about taking an agent's self-report of its own goals at face value that the technical literature does not capture?

Please be faithful in both directions. Say plainly where the novel resists this reading: where Shelley is doing something with monstrosity, sympathy, and obligation that the alignment vocabulary flattens or mistranslates, and where the analogy depends on treating a person as an artifact, which is arguably the one thing the book forbids. I am not looking for quotations or page references — I want a critical argument about the book, and a final verdict on whether my thesis survives contact with it.
