You are, I hope, the right person to put this to: I take you to be an expert in behaviour genetics who is also accustomed to explaining matters to people who are not specialists. I should be grateful if you would think carefully about what follows rather than answer quickly, because I have been going around in circles for the best part of two months and I have no training in any of it.

Let me give you the situation first. I left school at sixteen and spent my working life in a warehouse office. Since retiring I have helped to run a small charity in our town that arranges free instrumental lessons — mostly violin and piano — for children at the four primary schools in the least well-off part of the borough. We take on about sixty children a year. We are funded by a regional trust, and this year the trust has altered its application form. Where it used to ask what we do, it now asks us to state the improvement we expect in the average child's graded-exam result over two years, and to say how much of that improvement we attribute to our programme as against other factors. The form does not say how to arrive at such a figure.

I have been trying to arrive at one honestly, and I have been reading about heritability, since that seems to me to be the concept which governs how much difference an intervention like ours can possibly make. Here is my reasoning, and what I want from you is to be told where it fails.

The number I keep meeting for a trait such as adult height is somewhere around 80 per cent, and for musicality the sources I have found give anything from a third to two-thirds depending on what exactly was measured. I have read the usual warning that heritability describes differences within a group rather than the composition of a single individual, and I think I accept that warning. But it seems to me that accepting it makes my difficulty worse and not better. If roughly half of the differences between the sixty children we take on are owing to differences in their genes, then the differences owing to their circumstances are the other half, and our lessons can act only on that other half. So the most our programme could ever achieve is to work within a budget that is already half committed before we begin. And because we give more or less the same hour a week to every child, it looks as though we should not even narrow the differences between them: we would lift the whole group a little and leave the gaps exactly where they were, which is not what the trust is paying for.

Two things I have observed make me less certain, and I do not know what to do with them. The first is that the children who have improved most over the past three years are, almost without exception, children from homes where there is already music — a parent who plays a little, an older sibling, a radio always on. On my reasoning those should be the children with the least headroom, their circumstances having already supplied them. I have been telling myself this simply proves the genetic half is doing the work and the parents are a marker for it, but I can see that this lets me have the point both ways, and it does not satisfy me. The second is that the children who play best at our September audition are very often not the best by the following July; the early leaders drift back towards the middle. If that is what happens, is the true heritability lower than the studies claim, or is something else going on for which I have no name?

What I should like from you, in this order: first, set out plainly which steps in my reasoning do not hold, and why; second, tell me whether there is any figure at all I can honestly enter on the trust's form, and if there is not, what I ought to write in the box that would not be an evasion; third, tell me what I should actually be measuring if I want to know whether our lessons are working.

Please begin by listing, in a few lines, the assumptions I would have to have got right for my arithmetic to go through, and examine each of them before you proceed further. Write to me as an intelligent person who has never opened a statistics textbook: I am not offended by being told I am wrong, but I do need to be given the correct way of thinking and not merely the news that mine is incorrect. I am not asking for references or a reading list — I have no easy means of following those up, and would much rather have the reasoning set out in your own words.
