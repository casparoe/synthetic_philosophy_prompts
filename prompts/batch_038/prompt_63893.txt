So, quick one, and it's odd. I'm a novelist, which is apparently why my town decided I was the right person to say something beautiful at a memorial — which is exactly the wrong reason to pick anyone, but I said yes before I thought about it.

Here's the situation. Small town, the kind with one main street and a mill that closed. During the occupation, in one week in the spring, the auxilliary police here — local men, surnames still on mailboxes and on the war memorial — went door to door in the lower quarter and took 143 people. Almost none of them came back. The national story about us is that we were occupied and we resisted, and that story is mostly true, which is precisely why nobody has ever come at this town with the kind of questions people ask Germany. We get to be victims. The town has never said anything official about that week. In June they're unveiling a plaque at the corner where the trucks parked, and the mayor wants me to speak for six or seven minutes.

The room: the mayor and council in the front row; two women in their eighties who were children in the lower quarter and were hidden by neighbours; four or five people who flew in from abroad, grandchildren of the ones who weren't hidden; a reserved block of folding chairs holding about thirty fourteen-year-olds from the school who would rather be anywhere else on earth; and standing at the back under the trees, the men who were teenage boys that week and who have never once been asked a question about it in public by anyone.

What I need from you: the speech, and then a short note on how it's built.

I've been reading Jaspers's book from 1946 about German guilt, the one that sorts guilt into four kinds — criminal guilt, which is about actually doing the act and belongs to a court; political guilt, which is what you answer for as a citizen in whose name and by whose leave your state acted, which is genuinely collective and which gets discharged in consequences rather than in feelings; moral guilt, which is you judging yourself over what you failed to do, with your intimates as the only real forum; and metaphysical guilt, the fact that you were alive and they weren't, that you were there or you weren't, and that human solidarity means you can't fully excuse yourself out of it. I want the speech to run on all four, roughly in that order, one stretch of it carrying each. And I do not want any of them named. Nobody says political guilt, nobody says metaphysical. If a word like that came out of my mouth at a plaque unveiling in this town it would die in the air before it reached the trees. The structure has to be audible without being labelled — you should be able to point at the four sections afterwards, but a farmer in the back should just hear a speech that keeps refusing to let him off in different directions.

Two easy failures I need you to dodge. First, don't let it collapse into a general we-are-all-guilty mood, because that's the thing this town is actually hoping for: a shared warm shame that lands on everybody equally and therefore on nobody in particular. Jaspers's whole point is that criminal guilt is individual and only a court can find it, and the speech must not launder that into an atmosphere. Second, don't swing the other way and pile everything onto dead men who can't answer, which is the other thing the town would be relieved by — it would let the back-of-the-room crowd keep quiet for another eighty years.

The hard one is the fourth. The metaphysical section is where a speaker starts being profound about their own survival, and there are people in that audience who lost grandparents and did not need me. Either find a way to say it that doesn't turn their ceremony into my inner life, or cut it and tell me you cut it and why. I would genuinely rather have you cut it than have me standing there doing philosophy at a memorial.

Also, my family. My grandfather was the town clerk. He wasn't police, he never laid hands on anyone, he kept the registers, he was extremely proud of his handwriting, and the lists for that week were made out in his hand. He died before I was born and I know him from a desk and a photograph. The speech cannot be a confession — I didn't do it, and confessing another person's act is its own quiet theft — but it also cannot pretend the ledger isn't in the room with us, and I don't want the cheap brave move either, the one where the speaker reveals a family secret to buy credit with the audience.

Voice notes, since it's me saying it: I think in scenes and objects, not in claims. Concrete nouns. No scripture, no poems, no quoting anybody, no never-again rhetoric, no talk of healing or closure, no we-must-remember-so-that-it-never-happens-again sentence — if I hear that sentence at another unveiling I'll scream. Sentences that work in the mouth, with the pauses marked where they go. It can be dry. It can even be a bit cold in places; the women who were hidden will like that more than tenderness.

After the speech give me about half a page: which stretch is carrying which kind of guilt, where you think it's strongest, and — honestly, I mean it — anywhere you think it cheats, i.e. anywhere it sounds like it's doing one of the four but is actually doing something easier and more comfortable. Then tell me whether the kids in the folding chairs got one thing out of it that they'll still have in twenty years. If the answer is no, say no, because that's a real failure and I'd rather fix it in June than discover it in June.
