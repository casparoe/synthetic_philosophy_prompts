I write literary fiction, and I have been asked to contribute an afterword to the tenth-anniversary reissue of my second novel — six pages, in which I am expected to say what the book was arguing. My draft does that, and a friend who teaches moral philosophy read it and told me, kindly, that the argument quietly changes direction somewhere around the middle. She would not say where. I would rather have the diagnosis from you than from her, and I would rather have it as an audit than as advice about my prose.

The novel's central case, briefly, because the afterword leans on it. Vera is a civilian who keeps a list: the households sheltering in the cellars of the old quarter of a city under occupation. A requisitioning officer tells her — and she has no reason whatever to doubt him — that if she does not give him the name of one household before morning he will search the quarter street by street and take everyone on the list. She gives him one name. Eleven households remain. The family she names is no relation of hers; she chose them because the father walks with a cane and she told herself they would have been least likely to survive a search anyway, a reason she is later ashamed of having had. The book does not resolve this, but the afterword has to.

Here is what I say there, and what I believe. I have numbered them so that you can number them back to me.

1. There are situations in which what a person most ought to do, all things considered, is settled — one option and not another — and Vera's is one of them. I think she did the right thing in giving up a name. I am not a relativist about such cases, and the afterword does not leave the question open.

2. Ought implies can, and I mean it strictly. A requirement an agent cannot fulfil is not a requirement she has failed to meet; it is not a requirement at all. I would not want a novel in which a character stands condemned for not doing the impossible.

3. The demands bearing on a person at a time cannot genuinely conflict. Where two pull against each other and only one can be met, one of them is not, in the end, a demand.

4. Nevertheless something is left over in Vera's case, and I want to be exact about its status: it is not a mood. She owes the family she gave up something she would not have owed them had she been able to refuse the officer outright; and she is the proper object of a remorse that would be indecent in someone who had merely struck a prudent bargain.

5. That remainder cannot be the trace of an outweighed duty. Once a duty has been outweighed it is simply gone — that is what outweighing is. I do not want to say that Vera half-obeyed some second requirement, and I do not want the reader invited to say it.

6. Nor can the remainder be explained subjectively. The moral facts do not depend on anyone's feelings about them, so a remainder that lives only in Vera's conscience is no remainder at all.

7. Vera is not to blame. A person who deliberates well, attends to what she has reason to attend to, and does what she most ought to do has nothing charged against her. I hold this generally, not only about her.

8. And yet I hold that she is worse — not merely differently placed, but worse — than someone who never had to choose, and worse than she was before. What a person has done enters into who she is. A good person can be spoiled by the acts she has had to perform, and I do not think that spoilage reduces to the memory of them.

9. On the other hand, none of this counts against her in the eyes of anyone judging correctly. Moral assessment of a person tracks the quality of her will and her responsiveness to reasons, and nothing else about her is morally to the point. This is why I can hold 7 and still refuse to describe her as a justified wrongdoer.

10. Two things I expect will look like flat contradictions, and that I believe are not: that a blameless person should feel remorse, and that she should owe reparation to people she has not wronged. Tell me whether I am right that these are unproblematic. My friend's vague unease may have been about them, and I do not want to dismantle the book over nothing.

11. A note on method, which the afterword states in fewer words. When I cannot write a scene — when every resolution I draft reads as false and I sit with it for months — I treat that as information about the case rather than about my craft.

The sentence the whole afterword is built around, which has cost me a year, is this: There are situations in which a person cannot do what she is morally required to do, and in which she remains answerable — not to punishment, but to those she has cost something — for what she does instead.

What I want from you:

Audit the set rather than lecture me on it. Rank the tensions by severity, most serious first. For each, say precisely which numbered items cannot stand together, what the pressure actually consists in (not merely that there is pressure), what would have to be struck or weakened to release it, and what I would lose by striking it.

Separately, tell me which of the tensions are only apparent and why, so that I do not over-correct; I am thinking of item 10 in particular, but there may be others I have not spotted.

Then tell me the single revision that buys the most consistency across the whole set — the one change that would dissolve the most trouble at the least cost to the book.

And finally: does the sentence above survive? If it does not, tell me which clause must go and what claim is left standing once it is gone. I can rewrite a sentence. I cannot rewrite a novel.

Please argue rather than cite. I am not going to be sent anywhere in the literature, and I have no appetite for a survey of available positions; I want to know what my own commitments commit me to.
