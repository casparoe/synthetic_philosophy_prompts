I retired four years ago after a career in experimental soil physics, and I have taken up philosophy with the habits of my trade: before asking what a claim means, I want to know what would falsify it. The claim in question is Simone Weil's wartime argument that political parties ought to be abolished outright. As I read her, the case has three linked parts. A party is in the first instance an apparatus for manufacturing collective passion. Its operative aim, whatever its professed programme, is its own indefinite growth. And it is this structure, rather than the moral quality of its members, that makes a party systematically hostile to truth and to justice, since the demand that a person take a line on everything, including matters he has not studied, is incompatible with the slow, disinterested attention that truth requires. I find the argument unusually congenial precisely because it locates the defect in the institution and not in the sinners.

I have not been able to leave it there. For two years I have kept a private register of party statements of position on matters where the empirical record was subsequently and publicly corrected — a retracted dataset, a reversed expert consensus, a study withdrawn by its own authors. I record two things: whether the party ever revises its stated position, and if so the interval between the public correction and the revision. My working hypothesis, in Weil's spirit, is that the interval is governed by the party's competitive standing and is essentially uncorrelated with the epistemic event that prompted the entry. I have also come to suspect that her conclusion outruns her premises, and that the defensible claim is a weaker one: that the corruption she describes is a function of the need to grow and to compete, so that a party placed where growth is impossible — a chamber whose seats are allotted by lot, or a caucus forbidden to publish any common line — would fall outside the indictment altogether.

Please take the position as I have stated it and map its exposure. I want to know what would count against it, and I would like the following sorted out rather than blended together:

- The strong thesis (parties as such) and the weak one (parties under competitive pressure to grow) probably do not share defeaters. Separate what would defeat each.
- Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say what the world would have to look like, and which routes are live rather than merely logically available.
- Say which of these a committed Weil sympathizer would himself concede as counting against the view, and which he would dismiss as missing the point. In particular: if a party is found to have tracked a correction quickly, can the defender always reply that the party simply found a truth-conducive passion? Is that an immunizing move, and if so, is it fatal here or permissible given the kind of claim this is?
- Tell me whether my register could in principle deliver a decisive result, and what pattern in it would count against Weil rather than for her. I suspect there are patterns that would look confirming and would not be.
- Say what the position predicts that its rivals do not. I have in mind an iron-law-of-oligarchy account, which predicts capture but not this specific hostility to truth, and an account of parties as devices for managing coalition and pooling information, which seems to predict precisely the behaviour Weil says is structurally impossible. Which of these could absorb her evidence?
- Give me an honest verdict on whether the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect in a moral and structural claim of this sort or something else entirely.

I want the reasoning, not a catalogue of authorities; assume I have read the essay and little else. And since I am asking you to be self-critical on my behalf, extend the same treatment to yourself: close by saying which of your own conclusions above is most vulnerable, what argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to have failed.
