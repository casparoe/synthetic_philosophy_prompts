QUESTION (short answer, 10 points):
Explain how Boorse's biostatistical theory is meant to give a value-free account of disease. Then state one objection that turns on the role of reference classes, and say whether you think that objection is fatal to the theory.

RUBRIC (instructor's):

- 2 pts — states the reference-class objection as an objection, i.e. shows why it's supposed to bite from inside a naturalist program and not just that the classes look arbitrary.
- 3 pts — gives a reasoned verdict on fatality and considers what the naturalist could say back.
- 2 pts — at least one other account (Wakefield, Nordenfelt, Canguilhem) is used to do real work rather than name-dropped.

STUDENT ANSWER (typed, roughly 500 words):

Boorse wants a concept of disease that could be lifted straight out of physiology with no moral or social content in it. His trick is to split the ordinary words apart. Illness is the scientific term: an illness is an internal state that depresses some part-function below the typical level of efficiency for the relevant reference class, where the classes are carved out by things like age and sex, and where the part-functions themselves are picked out by their contribution to two goals that biology hands us rather than anyone choosing — survival and reproduction. Disease is the narrower word for the subset of those states that people find undesirable and therefore want treated, so it is disease, not illness, that carries the normative load. Because the statistical part is supposed to be purely empirical, Boorse can say that two people arguing over whether something is a disease are having a factual disagreement that more data could settle.

The pressure point is the reference classes. Statistical typicality means nothing until you say typical for whom. Take menopause: measured against the class "females, all ages" it is a large functional deficit, measured against "females over fifty" it is exactly what the class does, so the same woman is diseased and perfectly healthy depending on which box you put her in. Boorse's reply is that the boxes are natural, since age and sex are real biological divisions rather than conveniences, but the objection is that he hasn't earned that claim. Nothing in the statistics tells you where to draw the boundaries; they get drawn where it is clinically convenient to draw them, and clinical convenience is a value-laden notion, since it depends on which cohorts we think are worth treating and which deficits we regard as problems in the first place. So the naturalist has smuggled the norms back in through the parameter-fixing rather than through the definition.

Wakefield is supposed to be the naturalist repair job. His harmful dysfunction analysis drops statistical typicality and asks instead whether a mechanism is failing to do what natural selection designed it to do, which is a fact about evolutionary history rather than about cohort freqencies, so it looks like it dissolves the reference-class problem outright: whichever box you sort people into, the mechanism either performed its selected function or it didn't. My verdict is that the objection is fatal, and fatal to naturalism about disease in general rather than only to Boorse's version of it. Any account of disorder has to specify normal or proper functioning relative to some population or some environment, and picking the population is an interest-relative business, so the normativist was right from the start that disease is an evaluative concept and the naturalists have just been hiding it in different places. Nordenfelt's version — health is having the ability to reach your vital goals in standard circumstances — merely makes this explicit by building the person's own goals into the definition, but he is only being honest about a move everyone else in the debate is making quietly.
