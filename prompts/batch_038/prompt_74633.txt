I'm drafting the framework chapter for a comparative cognition handbook, and the editors want it to hand the whole volume a shared vocabulary — which is awkard, because roughly a third of the contributors work on artificial agents (LLM-based ones, a couple of embodied robots, one on evolved controllers) and the rest work on animals. The plan is to build it around a competence hierarchy in the spirit of Dennett's tower of generate-and-test — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian creatures — stretched so it covers machines on the same footing.

Before I commit to that I want the spec, not the taxonomy. Write the desiderata: what any proposed hierarchy of kinds of minds has to deliver to be worth making a field's shared vocabulary. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, because I want to be able to see why it's on the list and not just that it sounds sensible, and mark whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or a preference one camp has dressed up as a requirement. I'm thinking especially of demands like: the levels must be nested, each one presupposing the levels below; each level must correspond to a real evolutionary transition with a plausible selection story; the hierarchy must not smuggle in a ranking of worth or of how much mind something has; placement on the hierarchy must be decidable from behavioral data alone; the same level must mean the same thing whether the occupant is an animal or an artifact. I suspect several of those aren't requirements at all and I want to know which.

The purpose, so you can calibrate what counts as adequate: the hierarchy has to license inferences. A contributor who pins some animal or system to level N should be able to say what follows from that — which experiments are worth running, what they're entitled to conclude from a positive result, what they must not claim. If it can't do that work it's decoration, and I'd rather know now.

Here are the cases I already know will cause trouble. Treat each as stipulated — they're all in the volume in some form — and attach each to the requirement it actually stresses rather than to the one it most obviously breaks:

1. A plasmodial slime mould, no nervous system, that reliably finds the shortest route through a maze to two food sources, and that our author claims anticipates a periodic cold pulse it was previously exposed to.
2. An octopus. Something like two-thirds of its neurons are out in the arms, the arms do a lot of local problem-solving on their own, and the whole animal's behavior reads like a negotiation between a central controller and eight semi-independent ones.
3. A honeybee swarm picking a nest site. Individual bees do fairly simple things, but the colony accumulates and compares evidence about candidate sites and commits to one via a quorum rule. The author wants to say the swarm, not the bee, is the thing that sits at the higher level.
4. Bumblebees rolling wooden balls around when no reward is on offer at all, and doing it more in some conditions than others; the chapter argues this meets the behavioral criteria for object play.
5. A jumping spider that stalks prey, loses line of sight with it, and then takes a long detour that only makes sense if it has been tracking where the prey was and where it is now relative to that.
6. An LLM-based agent that reads a tool's documentation, follows a multi-step written procedure it was never trained on, and generalizes it to an unseen variant — but has no way to acquire a genuinely new skill from trial and error, because its weights don't change between episodes.
7. The reverse direction: a person with advanced dementia who loses language and tool use but keeps forming new emotional associations. Does a hierarchy have to permit downward movement, and if it does, what is it that persists and does the persisting thing count as the same kind of mind?

Then the two things I most want out of this:

- Which single requirement does the most work excluding candidates — the one where most proposed hierarchies actually fail, rather than the one people argue about most.
- Whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that "each level must correspond to a real evolutionary transition" and "the hierarchy must classify artificial agents on the same footing as animals" can't both survive, but I can't tell whether that's a real result or me conflating two different requirements. If it's real, say what should be given up and what the leftover hierarchy still buys you. If I've muddled it, untangle it explicitly.

Last, briefly and only briefly: how does the standard four-storey version do against the list — where does it pass, where does it need patching, where does it just get lucky — and if someone wanted to add a fifth storey for systems that can represent and revise their own selection criteria rather than merely their hypotheses, what would they have to show for that to count as a new level rather than a relabeling of the fourth?
