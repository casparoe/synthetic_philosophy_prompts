My friend and I spent most of a Saturday arguing about whether a sunk cost can ever rationally enter into a decision, and we got nowhere, so I want to test my verdicts rather than my arguments. Here are the cases and what I actually think about each.

1. Cinema. Twenty minutes into a film I paid £14 to see, I am miserable. Leaving is rational; staying to "get my money's worth" is not. I agree with my friend entirely here.

2. Fence. Two weekends into building one, I realize the post spacing will make it lean within a few years. Pulling the posts and relaying them costs materials and another weekend; finishing as designed yields a fence that fails. Relate them — that is, redo it. The two weekends do not figure in the calculation at all, and that seems plainly correct.

3. Venture. A firm has spent $40 million developing a product, needs $10 million more, and the finished product returns $12 million: finish it. The same firm in the same position with a $9 million return: abandon it. Neither verdict mentions the $40 million, and I think that silence is exactly right.

4. Doctorate. Four years in, I no longer want the degree or the subject. Quitting is rational, and "I have already given it four years" is not a reason. My friend agrees, which is why he thinks I am inconsistent elsewhere.

5. Marriage. Twelve years, no cruelty, no rival in view, but the relationship has gone flat and we would both probably be marginally happier apart. I do not think "because of the twelve years" is a bad reason to stay; I think it is a reason, and a respectable one. This is where the argument began. My friend's reply is that the twelve years are not sunk at all — they are what the marriage consists in, so I am counting a good, not a cost. He may be right about this one, which is why the next pair is what troubles me.

6. The hill. A commander has lost three hundred soldiers trying to take an objective of no strategic value; taking it now costs more lives and changes nothing. Continuing so that the dead were "not wasted" is not merely irrational but monstrous. I hold this more firmly than anything else on the list.

7. The garden. A close friend died halfway through building a community garden on a patch of wasteland. No one else will do it. Finishing takes two years of my weekends and yields a modest good; a different project of equal effort yields somewhat more. I think finishing hers is not irrational, and that most people whose judgment I trust would call it admirable rather than merely permissible.

Six and seven are, as far as I can tell, the same shape: an investment that cannot possibly benefit the investor, who is dead in one case and being counted in body bags in the other. My verdicts run in opposite directions. My friend says I am sentimental about one and squeamish about the other.

8. Piano. Three years of lessons, badly played. Switching to guitar now forfeits them, except that reading music and some dexterity carry over, so the first year of guitar goes faster than it otherwise would. The three years count — but they count as an asset with a future yield, which strikes me as a different phenomenon entirely.

9. The vow. A union leader has publicly pledged to strike rather than accept anything below a figure. An offer arrives just below it, and above what the members would get with no deal. Retracting the pledge costs her credibility in every negotiation for the next decade; holding to it costs the members something real now. I think holding can be rational, and again only because the past acts on the future.

10. The membership. I pay for a year of gym access I know I will not use, precisely because the wasted fee makes me go. I think this is rational, and I think it is rational by exploiting my own susceptibility to sunk costs — which suggests that susceptibility is not simply a defect.

If eight, nine, and ten are the whole story, my friend wins: the principle is that a past cost counts only insofar as honoring it changes what happens next, and cases six and seven are me dressing up a taste for my own constancy. I would rather not accept that, but I cannot yet say why.

The case I keep returning to: three hundred pages of a novel I now know is bad. Nobody has read them, nothing depends on them, they have no salvage value and no reputation attached. Finishing produces a bad book; starting over produces, plausibly, a better one, and whatever craft I have acquired travels with me either way. I cannot bring myself to delete them, and I do not believe my reluctance is mere weakness.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that delivers these verdicts? Put the one you would defend at the top in no more than two sentences. Then give me its clauses, each tied to the case on my list that motivates it; its verdict on the three hundred pages; and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I should consequently abandon. Prefer the simplest principle that gets the pattern right over one that rescues every verdict by bolting on machinery, and say which trade-off you are making. No theory names, no readings, no citations — I want the principle, not the literature. Please keep the whole response under 600 words.
