I direct curriculum for a network of thirty-four independent Islamic day schools in the United States. We publish our own high-school science sequence and the accompanying teacher handbook, and every manuscript page passes through a volunteer review board of three traditionally trained scholars before it is adopted. Last night I argued with a close friend of mine — a philosopher of science, and not himself a believer — until well past midnight about whether al-Ghazālī's account of causation in the Incoherence of the Philosophers can survive contact with a chemistry textbook. I had been insisting that our materials be revised so that causal claims carry an acknowledgment that the tie between a supposed cause and its effect is not a necessary one. He insisted that this misreads the seventeenth of the twenty discussions: on his account, al-Ghazālī is not denying that fire and cotton placed in contact are followed by burning, nor that observed sequence supports reliable knowledge and practice, but only that the fire burns by its own nature rather than in virtue of God's creating the burning on the occasion of the contact; and he pointed out that the quarrel is directed at metaphysics, not at the logical and mathematical sciences. He also accused me, repeatedly and I think fairly, of reaching past an eleventh-century Ashʿarite theologian to Malebranche and importing a seventeenth-century European occasionalism into the discussion. Neither of us budged, but the argument exposed a real deficiency: we have no procedure, so the decisions are being improvised by people with no training to improvise them.

What I need is a triage procedure, not an essay about the theology. A reviewer without training in philosophy or kalām must be able to run it on a flagged item in twenty minutes; a classroom teacher must be able to run a compressed version of it in the two or three minutes a live student question allows. We flag roughly fifty items a year — sentences in drafts, questions from students, written objections from parents — and two or three per term become board matters, where the consequences run to accreditation language and, in at least one recent case, to a family withdrawing three children.

Please give me: the questions to ask of an item, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a verdict the reviewer can act on; where it must hand the item up to the board because the question is doctrinal or a matter of piety rather than a question about what the sentence says; and what the default is when a teacher cannot resolve the matter in the moment and must say something anyway. Tell me which distinctions in al-Ghazālī's position have to survive the simplification — I suspect the difference between denying necessity and denying the observed connection is one of them — and which can be dropped for a fifteen-year-old without distorting the view. Mark every point where the procedure is really a judgment call dressed up as a step, and say where it will systematically misfire.

Then run it on three cases. First: a chemistry sentence stating that the oxidizing agent is what makes the fuel burn, which a reviewer has annotated with a demand that it be rewritten so that the burning is attributed to God. Second: a parent's written objection to our infectious-disease unit, arguing that teaching transmission models instead of reliance on supplication and trust in God undermines the religious formation of the students, with the underlying student question being whether medicine has any causal standing at all. Third: a physics chapter asserting that the regularities it describes are exceptionless laws, with no qualification anywhere in the text — which happens to be the sentence I like best in the whole sequence, and which I expect the procedure to damage. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not welcome, and you should say so rather than softening it.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: the questions it will get asked that it has no business answering, the cases where a school's science curriculum cannot be made consistent with a medieval theological account of causation and something simply has to give, and where the honest recommendation is to keep the theology in the religion classroom and leave the science texts alone. I do not want sources or a reading list; I want something my reviewers can pick up on Monday.
