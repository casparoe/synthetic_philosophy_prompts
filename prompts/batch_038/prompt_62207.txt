I am a graduate student in a seminar on distributive justice, and at the end of term I will be presenting my paper at the department's graduate workshop. My view is a positional variant of prioritarianism. Like the standard view, it weights a benefit by the recipient's level of well-being, but the weighting function does not run on absolute levels: it decays smoothly to zero between the fortieth and sixtieth percentiles of the well-being distribution within the relevant comparison group, so that a benefit to anyone above that band counts at its full sum-ranking value and no more. My motivation is twofold: it blocks the case in which a marginal gain for one very badly off person outweighs enormous gains for a great many people who are merely somewhat worse off, and, unlike a fixed absolute threshold, it stays calibrated to the society it is applied to. I also claim — and this is where I am least secure — that the view retains prioritarianism's immunity to the leveling-down objection.

The room will contain three luck egalitarians who suspect any decay band is either arbitrary or a covert surrender to sufficientism; two orthodox prioritararians who hold that a weighting function must be a function of absolute well-being alone; a social-choice theorist who will press on measurement, interpersonal comparability, and whether distribution-relative weights make the view non-separable; and a philosopher of disability who thinks percentile position is the wrong currency for identifying who is badly off.

Please prepare me for the question period rather than defend the view. Give me the eight objections this audience is most likely to press, in the vocabulary its members would actually use, ordered by how much damage each would do to my argument; for each, a reply I could deliver in about a minute, the concession I should make up front to keep my credibility, and an explicit note on where the reply goes soft. Finish with the single question I am hoping nobody asks, and an account of why it is harder than the others.
