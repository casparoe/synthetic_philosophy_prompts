I'm on the climate committee for my town — small place, about 4,000 of us year-round, plus maybe a thousand houses around the lake that belong to people who live in the city and come up summers and holidays. I'm not an economist and I never went to college, I'm the guy who reads every packet cover to cover because nobody else does, so somehow I always end up being the one who writes stuff down. We've got a problem and I need a document to put in front of people.

Here's the situation. The state made us do an emissions plan, and what the committee landed on is a fee on heating oil and propane — households pay it when they fill their tank — and then once a year the town mails the money straight back out in equal checks, per person, kids count too. Standard stuff, I think, except for one line buried in the draft: only registered residents get a check. The lake house people pay the fee on their fuel like everybody else and get nothing back. Not a cent. Which they noticed.

Two of them showed up at last month's meeting with a lawyer's letter. The argument, as far as I can tell, isn't that the fee is too big. It's that you can charge them but not count them — that they're inside the thing when it costs money and outside it when the money comes back, and that there's no line we can point to that says why. And honestly I don't think we have an answer. When the residency rule got put in, the reason given out loud was that including them would drop the average check from about $180 a year to about $40, and nobody wanted to mail out $40 checks after telling everybody this would be worth something.

The committee is now in three camps. One wants to give them a partial refund just to make it go away. One wants to say the quiet part out loud — that yes, this is on purpose, it's a transfer from rich outsiders to people here who actually need the money, and we should stop pretending it's about carbon. And the third one, which is closer to where I am, keeps asking what the fee is even for. Because if the point is cutting emissions, the money should probably go into insulation and heat pumps and electrical hookups at the town landing, and then the question of who gets a check mostly evaporates. But if you send checks, people just spend them, and next winter they heat the same houses the same way and the lake is exactly where it was — the tonnage doesn't care how we felt about it.

There's something else under all this that nobody will say out loud. A lot of the people voting on this don't like the lake house crowd. Not their carbon — them. They got mad when one of them put in a new boathouse, they roll thier eyes at the cars, and there's a kind of pleasure in making the successful ones pay a little that has nothing to do with the atmosphere. My mother was Danish and she had a name for it: she grew up being told not to think you're anyone special or better than anybody else, and she said whole towns run on that. I looked it up after she died and it's apparently from an old Scandinavian novel, a made-up little town where the rules amount to nobody being allowed to stand out, and the guy who wrote it said he wasn't inventing anything, he was just writing down what was already sitting there. My point isn't that the resentment is wrong. Some of those people really are among the richest folks in the state and there's a real argument that they should pay more. My point is I can't tell anymore which argument I'm voting on, and I don't think most of the committee can either.

The line problem is worse than it sounds. A bunch of those houses belong to people who grew up here and left. One of the two who objected taught at our school for eleven years. Somebody's going to inherit their parents' cottage next spring and become a non-resident overnight without moving anywhere. We've got a year-round business owner who files in the city because his wife works there. If we keep this rule we're going to be relitigating who counts every single year.

So: write me the memo I'll hand out before the second reading, three weeks out. It needs to actually recommend something — keep the rule, scrap it, or a third option you think is better, like tying the money to something instead of mailing checks, or splitting it two ways — and say what should go into the ordinance and what we should say back to the letter. Then the part I care about most: make the case that we can't have this be both things at once. If the fee is about the carbon, one answer follows; if it's about who belongs here and who doesn't, a different one follows; and right now the draft is trying to collect the benefits of both stories without paying for either. Give me the strongest honest version of the lake house owners' objection, steelmanned rather than knocked over easy, because I'd rather hear it from you than from their lawyer — and then explain why my recommendation is still the right call anyway. Last thing: give us some rule of thumb we could use the next time we fight about who counts, so we're not starting from zero every year.

Please write it so a normal person can read it. No jargon, no citing philosophers at me — the other eight people on this commitee would stop reading at the second paragraph if you did.
