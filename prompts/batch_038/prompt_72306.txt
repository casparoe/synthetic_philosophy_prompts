A friend of mine who teaches economics sent me the following précis of Marx on exploitation and alienation, in the middle of an argument about whether Marxism still merits serious attention:

'Marx's central claim is that a worker produces more value in a working day than the value of the wage he receives, and the capitalist pockets the difference, which Marx calls surplus value. The wage contract is therefore a swindle: the worker is paid nothing for the extra hours, which is why Marx treats profit as unpaid labour, and why the whole critique is a moral one — capitalism is unjust because it denies workers their right to the full product of what they make, a principle Marx himself endorses. Alienation is the softer, more philosophical face of the same complaint, chiefly a theme of the early writings, which the economist of *Capital* largely abandoned once he had a scientific account of exploitation to offer. And once marginalism displaced the labour theory of value in the 1870s, the derivation of surplus value lost its foundation, leaving only the moral complaint standing.'

I have read some Marx sympathetically and several of these sentences feel wrong to me, but I cannot say precisely where. Please go through the précis sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful Marx scholar would accept, what he would regard as a defensible popular simplification, and what he would call an outright misrepresentation. Three points I especially want clarified: whether Marx would accept the suggestion that the wage contract fails to be an exchange of equivalents, and if not, where he locates the appropriation of surplus value instead; whether the normative characterisation — injustice, theft, a right to the full product of labour — is settled or contested among interpreters, and what the main lines of that dispute are; and whether the concluding claim survives once the earlier sentences are corrected. Please write your answer in British English.
