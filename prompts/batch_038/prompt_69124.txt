A design team is building an agent whose actions are gated by a theorem prover — a consistent, recursively axiomatized extension of Peano arithmetic with an ordinary provability predicate. The intended policy, act on whatever the prover establishes, runs into Löb's theorem: if the system could prove, of some sentence, that its proving that sentence makes the sentence true, then it proves the sentence outright. So a blanket policy of self-trust cannot be adopted inside the system at all without its becoming inconsistent.

Write a dialogue between two members of the team working through one concrete case: the agent must decide whether to accept a self-modification whose consequences it can assess only by predicting what its successor would prove.

One of them holds that this is a structural barrier rather than a quirk of one formalization: so long as the agent can encode its own syntax and prove enough arithmetic, any device that lets it treat its own outputs as authoritative reconstitutes the reflexive step, and self-trust must therefore be abandoned, outsourced, or replaced by something that never amounts to an implication in the object language. The other holds that the barrier is an artifact of the specific provability predicate and of the diagonal lemma behind it — an arithmetic too weak to prove multiplication total, a nonstandard provability predicate that fails one of the derivability conditions, or a hierarchy in which the reasoner and the system reasoned about are genuinely distinct, each restore something worth calling self-trust.

Both should be technically fluent and neither a strawman. Have each press on the other's actual constructions, naming precisely where the reflexive step is reintroduced or genuinely blocked, and close with whatever each concedes. Argue from the logic itself; don't appeal to the literature.
