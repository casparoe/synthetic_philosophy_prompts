A friend of mine, Ilana, who is finishing a dissertation in philosophy of mind, and I were arguing on Friday evening about whether the split-brain literature tells us anything about the unity of consciousness at all. Partway through, her brother Dan — a neuropsychologist who assesses patients before and after epilepsy surgery — came in from the hallway, and from that point the argument was really between the two of them. I retired to the kitchen, so what follows is their exchange rather than mine, and I have no score to settle: I could not tell you this morning which of them was winning, only that neither gave an inch.

They were working from a case both of them knew. A patient has had the corpus callosum severed to control intractable epilepsy. In the laboratory, an image is flashed briefly to the left visual field, so that it is available to the right hemisphere but not, directly, to the left. Asked what he saw, the patient reports that he saw nothing. Asked to reach behind a curtain with his left hand and pick out the object, he selects it correctly. Asked why he chose it, he produces a fluent, confident, and entirely invented reason.

(1) Ilana began from the criterion itself. What makes an experience mine rather than yours is that it is co-conscious with my other experiences: they belong to a single phenomenal field, such that the subject can be aware of them together. In this patient, the right hemisphere's visual content is not co-conscious with the left hemisphere's verbal content. So by the only criterion that is actually about consciousness rather than about behavior, there are two subjects in that chair, one of which has never spoken.

(2) Dan said that criterion is operationalized entirely through report, and that the only reason to say the right hemisphere's content was not co-conscious is that the left hemisphere could not report it. The two hemispheres share one skull, one memory, one set of skills, one autobiography, and one body that they steer together, and outside the laboratory they cross-cue constantly: the right hemisphere sees what the left hand has just done, and the left hemisphere learns of it through the body it shares. Take away the tachistoscope and you have a single integrated agent who drives, works, and does not, in ordinary life, have one hand doing what the mouth denies. He added that the old reports of the hands working at cross purposes are rare, mostly confined to the early months after surgery, and in any case show competition for motor control, not competition between persons.

(3) Ilana said this concedes the point and then substitutes a different one. If integration by way of a shared body and a shared history is what makes one subject, then the claim that there is one subject is no longer a claim about consciousness but a claim about the coordination of a biological system. And Dan's own cross-cueing, she said, makes the count condition-relative: two subjects under lateralized presentation, one at the dinner table. A number that changes with the measurement arrangement is not the number of anything real.

(4) Dan replied that this is precisely his objection, not hers: if the count depends on the arrangement, the count was never tracking a substance. Then he pressed the interpreter against her. If the left hemisphere reliably confabulates — inventing a reason for behavior whose cause it cannot access, and remaining confident while wrong — then it is a witness impeached on the very topic in dispute. Ilana cannot use its denials to establish a silent second subject and simultaneously discard its affirmations of unity as noise. She has to pick one.

(5) Ilana said she discards neither, because confabulation is not the rival of unity but its mechanism. A system that cannot access the causes of its own behavior and nonetheless produces a single continuous first-person narrative is doing the very thing that unity consists in. What Dan has established is that narrative unity is cheap and unreliable, which is a reason to distrust the interpreter as evidence about the count — but it is not evidence that the count is one. It is evidence that we are in the dark.

(6) Dan said then notice where the darkness stops. In the fission cases philosophers trade in, one person becomes two because there are two separately embodied, separately ongoing successors, each with its own future. Nothing like that happens here: the patient was one subject before the operation and remains one continuously existing organism with one history and one future. There is an interrupted channel, not a division. And if functional lateralization plus the absence of immediate access to the other hemisphere's processing is enough to make two subjects, then there were two before the surgery as well, in everyone, in which case nobody has ever been one subject — a conclusion Ilana cannot hold while still asking him how many there are in this patient.

(7) Ilana then made a move I suspected was aimed at the room rather than at the argument, and Dan said so out loud. If there really are two subjects, she said, the operation produced a second person who has been unable to speak, unseen, and unconsulted for decades, which would make a celebrated therapeutic success into something considerably worse. Dan called this an argument from consequences: we do not select our metaphysics by how unpleasant it would be if true. Ilana said it was not a conclusion but a calibration — a hypothesis with implications that appalling should demand more evidence before acceptance, not less.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the seven numbered steps, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and give the reasoning rather than a bare verdict. Four things in particular I would like settled. Does Dan's dilemma in (4) genuinely bind, or does (5) escape it — and if it escapes, what does it cost Ilana to escape? Is the condition-relativity in (3) a real finding about the case or a restatement of her original claim with a different emphasis? Does the reductio in (6) actually follow: is the severing of the callosum comparable, for these purposes, to the ordinary lateralization of an intact brain, or does the comparison turn on a difference Dan has suppressed? And is (7) a fallacy or a legitimate evidential principle, and does Ilana's reformulation rescue it?

When you have scored the moves, tell me what neither of them said: whether the dispute is really about counting at all, or about what kind of thing a subject is; whether the correct answer here could be indeterminate rather than either one or two; and whether there is any evidence that could in principle have settled the matter between them at my kitchen table.
