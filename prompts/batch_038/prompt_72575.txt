DRAFT:

The question "is there really a Golden-Gate-Bridge feature in this model?" is usually answered pragmatically: we check whether the direction steers the model when we clamp it, whether it fires on bridge-related inputs and stays quiet otherwise. I want to say this is a real-patterns question in Dennett's sense, and that the sense in which such features are real is weaker, and more indexical, than interpretability people assume.

Dennett's criterion in Real Patterns is compression-based: there is a real pattern in some body of data when describing the data at a higher level buys you a description much shorter than any description at a lower level. His illustration is a bitmap. A grid of black and white pixels admits a bit-by-bit listing, but if what's on the grid is a handful of stripes, the stripe description is enormously shorter, and that's what makes the stripes real rather than a convenient fiction. Realism here isn't a question of what the pattern is made of; it's a question of whether the higher level earns its keep.

Applied to a trained model: the lower level is the weight matrix plus activation traces over some corpus. A candidate feature is a real pattern to the extent that describing the models behavior in terms of that feature, plus a small number of others, compresses those traces substantially better than a description mentioning only weights and arithmetic. That gives interpretability a metaphysics it mostly lacks. It also explains why feature claims feel unstable, because compression is a three-place relation: data, level, code. Change the code (different SAE architecture, different dictionary size) or change the data (shifted evaluation distribution) and you can lose the gain entirely.

Here's the difficulty. In the bitmap case the gain is so large and so distribution-insensitive that the relativity never shows up. With features it does: a feature that compresses on the training distribution may be worthless off it, and one that's real at dictionary size k may not survive at 10k. So either features aren't real, or reality is indexed to a distribution-and-code pair. I want the second horn, plus an invariance clause: call a pattern real relative to a family of distributions if the compression gain persists across the family. That slides the criterion away from compression and toward something like intervention-support, and it makes feature realism graded rather than binary.

Two further claims. First, that the three stances aren't three independent levels: the intentional stance is the design stance run under an assumption of rationality, and the design stance is itself a compression of the physical stance under the assumption that the thing was built to work. If that's right, most of interpretability is design-stance work — reverse-engineering a purpose nobody specified — which predicts why it keeps sliding into talk about what the model is trying to do. Second, that the obvious objection to the invariance clause (it makes reality hostage to which datasets we happen to care about) is answered by pointing out that interests fix the question, not the answer: given the distribution and the code, whether the gain obtains is an objective matter, just as the stripes are there whether or not anybody counts them.

END DRAFT
