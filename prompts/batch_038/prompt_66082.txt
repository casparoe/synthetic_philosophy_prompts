so my brother in law is one of those church-adjacent history buffs and he has a move he pulls out at every holiday dinner. it goes: Schliemann dug up Troy. Everybody was perfectly certain Homer was a fairy tale, and then some guy with a shovel found the city, complete with (he says) a burn layer and bodies in the streets. So when skeptics say the Exodus never happened, they're just doing to the Bible what everyone did to Schliemann, and they'll be embarrassed in exactly the same way. Then he adds King Arthur as a bonus round, something like "and didn't they dig up Arthur's fortress on some hill in England?"

the analogy is doing a *lot* of work here. its meant to convert a substantive argument about evidence into a story about the skeptic's priors: the skeptic gets cast as the armchair classicist who refuses to look, and the believer as the amateur with a shovel who turns out to be right. i want to know if it can actually carry that.

can you audit it for me? specifically:

- lay out the mapping explicitly, a table is fine. for each of the three cases, what plays the role of the poem, the excavator, the mound, the destruction layer, the contemporary written record, and the scholarly consensus that got overturned?
- where does the mapping break — and this is the part i care about most — which of those breaks actually matter for the use he's putting it to, versus which are just noise?
- what does the analogy quietly import from the Troy case that the other two don't have? i suspect the "mound" is doing most of the smuggling, since a mound is a thing that exists whether or not anyone believes in it.
- did Troy "become history," or did it become something else? what precisely got vindicated at Hisarlik — a place, a war, a poem, a date? and could that same thing get vindicated in the other two cases even in principle, or is the payoff structurally unavailable there?
- a classics tutor of mine once handed me a distinction i've never seen written down anywhere and i honestly don't know if it's real scholarship or something she made up over coffee: she said a legend can preserve two very different things, a *place* and an *event*, and that they have wildly different half lifes — place names are nearly immortal, events rot fast, and the trick is that a surviving place name lends unearned credibility to a rotted event. use that if it helps, tell me if you think it's mush.
- and does his conclusion survive? don't give me "it's complicated on both sides" — say whether the analogy can bear the weight, and if it can't, what a better analogy for the legend-to-history transition would look like and what conclusion *that* one would license.

no bibliography or reading list, i just want the reasoning laid out. where you're fuzzy about a fact, say you're fuzzy rather than filling it in.
