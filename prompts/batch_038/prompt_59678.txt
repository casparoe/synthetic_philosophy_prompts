I am seven years into a novel that intends to make an argument, and I have reached the point where I must know whether the argument survives contact with the evidence, rather than merely sounding convincing in my own sentences. The book is in three movements: a marcher settlement in the thirteenth century, a casualty clearing station in 1916, and a coastal city in the near future. Its thesis, stripped of the prose, is that the civilizing of the world — the long fall in the rate at which human beings kill one another — is substantially an achievement of bookkeeping: of what gets recorded, what gets classified as homicide rather than misadventure, whose deaths are counted as war and whose as statistics, and what the numbers are ultimately divided by. Before I commit six hundred pages to that, I want your honest credence, not your diplomatic summary.

Please give me a single number for the following conjunction, and then decompose it.

P: Between roughly 1500 and the present, the rate at which human beings die from the deliberate acts of other human beings — homicide, battle, execution, terror, and deliberately engineered famine — fell worldwide by at least a factor of five; and that fall reflects a real change in how people treat one another rather than a change in how such deaths are recorded, categorized, or normalized.

Assign a rough probability to each of the following terms, with one or two lines of justification apiece, and then show me the arithmetic by which they combine into your figure for P.

1. The baseline. How reliable is the pre-state casualty record? The ethnographic figures were gathered after contact with states that had already suppressed raiding, which biases them toward peace; the archaeological figures come from mass-burial sites preserved and excavated precisely because they are catastrophic, which biases them toward war. Tell me which of these two selection pressures you take to dominate, and by roughly how much.

2. The war series. If the sizes of wars follow a heavy-tailed distribution, then the mean is effectively estimated from a handful of observations, and a run of quiet decades carries little information about the tail. What is the honest confidence interval on the decline in battle deaths, and at that sample size is a genuine trend even distinguishable from sampling?

3. The homicide series. The European data showing a several-fold fall from the late middle ages onward are the strongest evidence available to either side. How much of that fall could be produced by things other than behaviour: improvements in surgery and antisepsis that convert what would once have been a killing into an assault; the shifting legal treatment of infanticide, duelling, and lethal punishment; and the fact that surviving early records come disproportionately from places with busy bureaucracies, which is not a random sample of the past?

4. The aggregate. Interpersonal homicide, interstate war, civil war, one-sided violence against civilians, and state violence inside prisons have not plausibly moved together. If the components diverge, is there a fact of the matter about the sum, or does violence merely name a convenience of aggregation?

5. The denominator. Here I want your own thinking rather than the literature's. I have been drafting a private measure, which I call the acquaintance burden: for an ordinary member of a society, the expected number of people she personally knows who die violently over the course of her life. Because the size of a human acquaintance set seems roughly constant across history, this measure is not per-capita in the usual sense, and it is highly sensitive to clustering — a single killing in a village of two hundred touches everyone, while ten times the per-capita number of killings in a city of four million may touch almost no one. Does P come out differently under this measure? If it does, which of the two measures is tracking whatever it is that the word violence does moral work for?

Then three closing deliverables: which of the five terms my number is most sensitive to — the one whose reversal would move it furthest; what single piece of evidence, as opposed to argument, would move it most, described concretely enough that a researcher could go and look for it; and a second number, your credence that if I published the thesis flatly an informed reviewer could identify a decisive error in it rather than a mere difference of emphasis. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I want the reasoning exposed, with its weak points marked where they are.
