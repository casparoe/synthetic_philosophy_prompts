I write a weekly-ish column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've talked my way onto the shortlist for a foundation fellowship that buys you a year off to go deep on one question and come out with a long essay plus a public lecture. The application wants a two-page research proposal, and I'm bad at these because I keep sliding into feature-article mode. Can you draft it for me?

Here's the half-formed thing I want to do. Everyone in my beats orbit repeats some version of "Solomonoff induction is Occam's razor turned into a theorem": you put a prior over programs, weight each one by two to the minus its length, and the math does the rest — short hypotheses dominate, and in the limit you converge on the truth. Then the contrarians turn up with two objections that I'm pretty sure are getting run together. One is the uncomputability point, which sounds devastating to a lay reader but seems to amount to "it's an idealization, so what." The other is the reference-machine point: Kolmogorov complexity is only pinned down up to an additive constant, so the prior is only pinned down up to a multiplicative constant. A theorist friend tells me that constant doesn't depend on your data, only on which pair of machines you picked — which is why the standard reply is that it washes out asymptotically, and why the less standard worry is that for any amount of data anyone will ever actually have, the constant may be doing all the work.

My hunch, and this is the bit I want the proposal to sharpen rather than just restate: "vindicates Occam's razor" is doing at least three different jobs, and the objections bite on different ones. There's the limit claim (as evidence piles up, the posterior concentrates on the truth), the finite-evidence claim (at the data you actually have, the simpler compatible hypothesis really is the favored one), and the guidance claim (a scientist should therefore prefer the simpler theory). A related worry I want folded in: shortest-program length may not be the notion of simplicity working scientists have in mind when they talk about fewer free parameters, fewer entities, or a theory not being propped up with ad hoc auxiliaries — a program can be short for reasons nobody would call elegant.

So the question I want stated precisely: which of those levels of vindication, if any, survives the machine-dependence objection, and does the answer change if the simplicity at issue is the scientists' notion rather than program length?

What the proposal needs: a crisp statement of the question; why the positions people already hold don't settle it (the enthusiasts, the "it's just an uncomputable idealization" shruggers, and the reference-machine skeptics); the method — what I'd concretely be doing month by month, given that I'm not a mathematician and the theorist has agreed to be a technical sounding board for a few hours a month, so spell out which parts are his and which are mine; why that approach beats the obvious alternatives (say, running simulations of toy priors, or doing intellectual history of the razor); what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest verdict on whether a year is realistic or whether I should cut the scope down. Also a couple of sentences on the public payoff, because this funder is allergic to projects with no audience.

Skip the bibliography entirely — I'll attach names to the positions myself, and I'd much rather you not invent any.
