For well over a millennium, Indian epistemologists have disagreed about how many pramāṇas there are: the Buddhist camp counts two (perception and inference), Nyāya counts four (adding comparison/upamāna and testimony/śabda), and Mīmāṃsā and Advaita count six (adding postulation/arthāpatti and non-apprehension/anupalabdhi). What puzzles me is that the parties seem to agree about every actual case. Take Nyāya's stock illustration of upamāna: a person is told that a gavaya is like a cow, later encounters one in the forest, and thereby comes to know that this animal is a gavaya. The reductionists do not deny that the knowledge is had; they insist only that it is a bundle of perception, memory, and inference. My hypothesis is therefore that the quarrel is not about the facts of any case but about the grain at which knowledge sources ought to be individuated — a taxonomic dispute with no substantive content, in which two, four, and six are equally adequate maps at different resolutions.

I want that hypothesis tested rather than repeated. Here is a criterion I have been toying with, borrowed from neuropsychology: count two candidate sources as genuinely distinct only if a double dissociation can be constructed — one case in which the first yields knowledge while the second provably cannot operate, and a matched case in which the second yields knowledge while the first cannot. Attempt to build such a pair for upamāna against inference, and then for śabda against inference, and report whether each attempt succeeds, fails, or only appears to succeed by smuggling in a description that already presupposes the answer.

Finally, say what would count as progress: a discriminating case, a criterion of individuation both camps could accept, evidence that the count is really downstream of metaphysical commitments about which relations are genuine rather than about epistemology at all — or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed and no movement should be expected.
