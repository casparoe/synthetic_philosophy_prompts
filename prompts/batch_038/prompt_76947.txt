Our reading group on the badness of pain ended up with an argument I'm pretty sure is broken, but two of us have been going in circles for a week and can't locate the break. Raw material first, since the whole thing hangs on it: pain asymbolia, the Schilder-Stengel business from the early thirties, mostly patients with insular damage. You prick them, pinch them, burn them, and they report feeling it — they localize it correctly, they can say sharp versus dull, they can rank intensities. What's gone is the aversive half: no withdrawal, no grimacing, some of them grin or laugh at the pinprick, they don't learn to avoid the source afterwards, and they'll tell you the pain is there but that it doesn't bother them. The case reports I read say they still flinch at a hand raised as if to strike them, so it isn't blanket apathy. And you apparently get a similar split from cingulotomy, and in a murkier way from morphine.

Here's what we built out of it.

(1) If an experience is bad for the person having it, it's bad in virtue of how it feels; nothing else about it could make it bad.
(2) Pain is the paradigm case of an experience that's bad in that way.
(3) In asymbolia, everything the patient reports about the feel of the pain is intact. What's missing is the minding of it.
(4) So there can be a felt pain that isn't bad for the person feeling it.
(5) So the badness doesn't come from the pain-feel. It comes from a further ingredient that can be switched off while the feel stays exactly as it was.
(6) And the same split should run through every unpleasant experience — nausea, itch, tinnitus, grief. In each there's a feel, there's the minding, and only the minding carries the badness.
(7) The minding is not itself a feel. If it were, the asymbolic would just be someone missing a quale, and we'd be back to (2) doing all the work.
(8) If you make the minding a further feeling instead, then its badness needs its own minding, and you're on a regress with no felt bottom.
(9) So either nothing is bad in virtue of how it feels, or the badness bottoms out in something that isn't felt. Either way (1) is false — and it took one lesion in the insula to knock it over.

What makes me queasy is that (9) seems to license the cingulotomy reading too: damp the minding and you've improved the patient's welfare without touching anything they feel. I don't want to land there, and I especially don't want to surrender (1), because I don't want welfare to collapse into preference-satisfaction.

So: which step do you cut, why did it look perfectly innocent going in, and what do you pay for cutting it? Tell me what each of the main candidate cuts costs, not just your favorite. And if you think the argument actually goes through and (1) does die here, say so plainly and tell me what's left of the idea that pain is intrinsically awful.
