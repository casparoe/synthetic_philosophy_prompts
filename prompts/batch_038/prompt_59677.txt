I spent close to forty years calibrating instruments — gas chromatographs for most of it, and standards work before that — so I have a permanent tic about whether a number means anything or is just a label we agreed not to argue about. Retirement pushed me into philosophy, mostly ethics, and there's a reading group at our library, three of us, all retired, Tuesdays. We've been chewing on Harsanyi's aggregation result: if every individual's preferences over the same set of lotteries satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and so does the social preference, and society is indifferent whenever all the individuals are, and the social ranking doesn't shift when you rearrange things among people who aren't affected by the rearrangement, then the social utility function has to be a weighted sum of the individual ones. I've put that in my own words and I'm sure I've garbled something, so please repair my statement of the theorem before you attack it — I'd rather be wrong about the right thing.

The position I've more or less talked myself into, and want stress-tested, is that this is the closest social philosophy gets to an actual derivation; that the equal weights come out of symmetry rather than being slipped in under a table; and that anyone who wants prioritarianism or egalitarianism has to name the assumption they're giving up and pay for it. So: what would count against it? I don't want the sociology — I'm not interested in who finds utilitarianism distasteful — I want the structural exposure. Which assumption is actually carrying the load? I suspect it's the seperability one, and that it may already be a moral claim wearing a technical costume, but I could easily be wrong about which premise is doing what. What happens when the set of individuals isn't fixed — a theorem proved for a given population, applied to policies that change who exists? And the comparability business: does the result need utilities comparable across people, or does it get by with something weaker, and if weaker, has somebody concluded more than the premises license? I'd also like to know what the theorem rules out that a prioritarian or a Sen-style rival can't rule out — whether it earns its keep.

One more thing I can't shake, from the measurement side. The theorem looks like it's about objective lotteries, everybody agreed on the probabilities. But nearly every real dispute is about the probabilities, not the payoffs, and I have a half-formed memory that if you try the same aggregation with people who disagree about how likely the states are, you run into trouble and something like a common-prior requirement drops out. If that's right, how badly does it cut? Rank all of this for me: fatal, awkward-but-survivable, and which objections a committed Harsanyian would happily concede and still think they've won. Then be straight about the meta-worry: a theorem can't be falsified, only misapplied, so is this view risking anything at all, or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability in practice — and if so, is that a defect or just what a formal result is? Last thing: tell me how confident you are in your main claims, and single out the one point in your answer you're least sure of. I'd rather have that flagged up front than guess at it myself.
