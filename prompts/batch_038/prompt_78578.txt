You're a philosopher who works on practical reason and you've taught Williams on internal and external reasons to grad students more times than you can count. Think carefully about this one. Before you commit to a scenario, do some brainstorming out loud — I want to see two or three candidate cases you considered and threw out, with the reason for each rejection. That part is not optional, I've found it makes a big difference.

Here's what I'm trying to build, and it's for a specific purpose, so keep the purpose in view because it fixes what counts as a successful case rather than a cheap one. The purpose: I want a case where what blocks an agent from reaching a reason is *conceptual*, not doxastic and not motivational. She isn't mistaken about any of the relevant facts, she isn't weak-willed, and she isn't missing a desire she could pick up by ordinary means — she literally lacks a concept, and the concept is exactly what would be needed for the consideration in question to show up for her as reason-giving at all. Fricker's hermeneutical injustice stuff is the rough neighborhood, but I don't want the case to be about a marginalized group in any obvious political register. Keep it small and domestic or workplace-scale so the philosophy is doing the carrying.

The specs:

1. Realistic. A family, a clinic, a school, a small office. No demons, no neurosurgery, no perfectly accurate predictors, nothing science-fictional.
2. The scenario itself: one paragraph. Then a control case, same length, that differs from the first in exactly one respect — and you name that respect explicitly.
3. The agent must not be blameable for lacking the concept, and the gap must not be closable by correcting a false belief. If you catch yourself writing "she just doesn't realize that...", you've broken the spec. She can be handed the complete list of true propositions about her situation and accept every single one of them.
4. She must also be a good reasoner — means-end, consistency, the works. In particular the case must not be one where she holds a general normative principle that she's merely failing to apply. If a consistency requirement by itself would walk her to the reason, the internalist wins by default and the case is boring.
5. This is the tricky one and I expect you'll fight with it: the case has to be such that if she ever did acquire the concept she'd look back on the gap as a loss rather than a relief. But that recognition must be conditional on acquiring the concept, and must not be something any current element in her motivational set can latch onto from where she stands. So if you satisfy 5 by giving her a standing desire for self-understanding, or a general disposition to hunt out new ways of seeing things, the internalist will point straight at that element and the case collapses. The conditional has to be genuinely unreachable.
6. The reason at issue should be unheroic — a reason to refuse, or to ask for something, or to stop doing one particular chore. Not a grand moral reason.
7. Write the whole thing so an advanced undergrad who's read the Williams paper once can follow it. No jargon dumps, and don't lean on terminology you haven't set up.

Show me the deliberation actually failing or succeeding; don't just declare it blocked.

For each, report whether it rescues internalism or just relocates the problem somewhere less comfortable.

I'm guessing it'll be something like: you described a conceptual gap and then relabeled it a reason-gap, and whether she has a reason she couldn't possibly reach is the very thing in dispute, so the case begs the question against internalism rather than testing it. Engage that head-on instead of just noting it.
- One line on what the main/control pair isolates.

And be honest about the design rather than defending it. Tell me where you think the case is most likely to have cheated — the place where you stipulated a verdict instead of generating it from the theories.
