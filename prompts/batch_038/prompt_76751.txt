For a term paper in a seminar on information and entropy, I tried to rebuild the modern resolution of Maxwell's demon from the ground up, and the reconstruction has run away from me. Every step looks like something I would happily defend in class, but the conclusion is a claim about the total quantity of thought available in the universe that I am not prepared to sign. This is the argument as it currently stands.

(1) Landauer's principle: any device that resets a memory register whose prior content it does not know to a standard state, while coupled to a single heat bath at temperature T, must dump at least kT ln 2 of heat into that bath per bit reset — on the order of 3 × 10⁻²¹ joules per bit at room temperature, which works out to roughly 3 × 10²⁰ bit-resets per joule.
(2) The bound attaches to the logical operation rather than to the hardware that realizes it, so it applies with equal force to silicon, to biological neurons, and to whatever substrate some later civilization builds its minds out of.
(3) A finite-memory system that keeps taking in new information must eventually overwrite old states; a machine that never overwrites runs out of room and ceases to be able to register anything at all.
(4) Overwriting a state whose content the machine is no longer tracking simply is resetting a register of unknown content, so by (1) and (2) it costs at least kT ln 2 of dissipated heat per bit.
(5) Hence any finite mind that runs indefinitely, in any substrate whatsoever, pays at least kT ln 2 for every bit it forgets, and can afford no more than about 3 × 10²⁰ bit-forgettings per joule of energy it spends.
(6) How much thinking a mind does is at least proportional to the number of bits it acquires and discards in the course of thinking, so (5) converts directly into a ceiling on total thought: lifetime thinking is bounded by lifetime energy budget multiplied by 3 × 10²⁰ bits per joule.
(7) No amount of ingenuity lifts this ceiling, because the ceiling is a statement about information and not about engineering: reversible-computing tricks, better thermal design, exotic substrates, and quantum emulation all still have to dispose of the bits they are finished with, and disposing of them is exactly what (1) prices.
(8) It follows that the total thinking available to any civilization is fixed in advance by its energy supply and its operating temperature, and that a civilization hoping to think forever has one move left — cool itself toward absolute zero so that each bit costs less, in the manner of those proposals about minds outlasting the heat death by slowing their clock rate and radiating into an ever-colder sky.
(9) But refrigeration costs work in proportion to how cold you are trying to get, so cooling only postpones the accounting; the total is finite either way. A warm universe therefore contains a finite and in principle computable quantity of thought, and minds that never stop are impossible.

The seminar's own resolution of the demon appears to require (1), and I would rather keep (1) than any other premise in the list, since it is what makes the Szilard-engine story close and what explains why the demon cannot run for free. What I distrust most is (9), and (8) makes me flinch as well, though I cannot tell whether the trouble is genuinely localized that far down or whether those are only the first visible symptoms of a bad move much earlier in the chain. So I would like three things. First, identify the step at which the argument actually fails — or, if you judge it valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to abandon and why that one. Second, explain why the offending step looked innocent to me when I wrote it, since I suspect it trades on something I was not distinguishing carefully enough. Third, price the alternatives: what does saving Maxwell's demon cost me if I have to weaken or reject (2), (3), (4), (6), or (7) instead, and does any of those options leave the demon-exorcism intact? I am especially unsure whether (4) and (7) can both survive contact with reversible computation, since that is the direction the reconstruction was heading before I noticed how large the conclusion had grown — and if they cannot, I would like to know what a defender of the demon has to say about erasure once it is no longer unavoidable.
