Got into a two hour arguement with a friend last night about Berkeley, and not the idealism — the money stuff, the Querist, the bits where he goes after the economists of his day. He read some of it for a seminar and has come away convinced Berkeley is basically a Bitcoin guy: money is just a ticket or a counter, there's nothing intrinsically valuable about it, so it doesn't matter one bit who issues it or what stands behind it, and a decentralized ledger with a hard cap is the honest endpoint of that view. I said he's reading 2020s crypto-libertarianism backwards into an eighteenth century bishop. My case was that for Berkeley the entire point of money is that it stirs up industry, and stirring up industry is something a public authority has to be deliberately aiming at — the reason he wanted a national bank for Ireland issuing paper was so that a shortage of coin would stop being an excuse for idleness. And he clearly thinks wants have to be manufactured in people on purpose; there's a query to the effect of asking whether peasants wouldn't be more industrious if they got used to eating beef and wearing shoes. Bitcoin doesn't manufacture wants in anybody, it just sits in a wallet appreciating. His answer was that I'm smuggling in chartalism, a twentieth century German doctrine Berkeley never heard of, and that the ticket-or-counter line genuinely does cut money loose from any particular issuer, which is exactly what I was trying to deny. Neither of us budged, and honestly neither of us knows the texts well enought to be sure who's bluffing.

So can you tell me what Berkeley would actually say about each of these? One at a time, and ground it in his own commitments rather than in what sounds plausible for a guy in a wig:

1. Bitcoin itself — fixed supply, no issuer, held overwhelmingly as a store of value rather than spent. Would he count it as money, or as a gaming counter from a game nobody is running?
2. A central bank digital currency. He asks somewhere whether, if the denominations were retained and the bullion gone, things might still be rated, bought and sold and industry promoted. Is that a CBDC, or just paper, which already existed?
3. Quantitative easing and interest on reserves: enormous quantities of money created that then mostly don't circulate anywhere. He says less money circulating fast is equivalent to more circulating slowly. What about more money barely circulating at all?
4. The modern monetary theory claim that a sovereign issuing its own currency can never run out of it, and that the real constraint is labour and resources rather than the ticket supply. Agreement, or agreement for the wrong reasons?
5. Cards, buy-now-pay-later, and the near disappearance of cash: money as pure bookkeeping entries moving between private ledgers.
6. Given that he denied we have abstract general ideas, and that he attacked the calculus for trading in vanishing quantities that are neither finite nor infinitely small nor nothing — fictions, ghosts of quantities that have already left — would he say that "the money supply" as a single measurable aggregate, and the marginal utility of a dollar, are the same species of mistake? Is there any economic magnitude he'd allow to be real?
7. A universal basic income. He wants wants created to drive industry; does a payment with no strings look to him like switching the engine off, or like the cheapest way to get everyone participating in exchange?
8. A gold standard revival — nostalgia for the commodity theory of money he seems to reject, or a legitimate complaint about who controls the counters?

Then tell me which of us had the better read, and where each of us is flat out wrong. And if I've misremembered or overstated anything about what Berkeley says up there, say so first — I'm working from a book I read four years ago and I'd rather be corrected than flattered.
