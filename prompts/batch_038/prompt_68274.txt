So, background: I write about computing and physics for a magazine — mostly AI hardware, occasionally the stranger corners of "post-silicon" computing — and every couple of years I get seduced by some startup claiming their analog widget can do something a Turing machine can't. I've stopped writing those pieces credulously, but the episode left me obsessed with why the claim is so hard to even pin down, which is not the complaint you usually hear from people who cover this beat. Anyway, a friend of mine teaches a fourth-year seminar on the philosophy of computation and she's got a semester of leave coming up, and she's asked me to set the take-home final, on the theory that I know which confusions actually matter in the wild. I want to do right by her.

Here's the scenario I've cooked up. It's entirely invented — there is no such lab, no such device, no such paper — so please don't go hunting for it, just treat the setup as stipulated. A group (call them the Aarhus people) build what they call a relaxation solver: an electrical network which, given an input encoding some Turing machine M and input n, is left to settle into an equilibrium, and whose equilibrium voltage V they claim is a real number whose binary expansion encodes the halting function. Specifically, for the pair (M, n) the answer is supposed to be readable off the k-th binary digit of V, where k grows with the size of the description of M and n — so bigger inputs demand more digits of precision out of the voltmeter, without limit. They publish readouts for a few thousand instances, each accurate to as many digits as that instance requires, and every one matches the known halting result. They then announce that they've built a machine that computes a non-recursive function, i.e. a physical counterexample to the Church-Turing thesis in its physical formulation. The objection that immediately gets raised is that any readout is finite-precision, so you can't tell whether the network is really tracking a hypercomputational function or just some fantastically complicated recursive one that happens to agree with the halting function on everything anyone has ever checked. Which sounds right to me, but it also seems to prove to much: on that standard no experiment whatsoever could falsify the physical thesis, which starts to make it look less like an empirical hypothesis about the world than like a rule we bring to the data — and if that's right, the whole hypercomputation literature has been arguing about a presupposition of measurement rather than a claim that could come out false.

What I'd like you to produce: (1) the exam question itself, presenting the case and asking something that cannot be answered by reciting that the thesis identifies effective procedures with Turing-computability — it has to force the students to say what a genuine falsification would have to look like, and to take a position on whether the physical thesis is empirical, definitional, constitutive of what counts as an experimental result, or some mixture; (2) a model answer of about 900–1000 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25. It's a closed-book 48-hour take-home for fourth-years who've read some Copeland and Piccinini and know roughly what accelerating Turing machines and Malament–Hogarth spacetimes are supposed to be, so the question can lean on that vocabulary but shouldn't require anyone remembering a specific argument from a specific paper. Students will be expected to write 1200–1500 words. Please don't put a bibliography or reading list anywhere in the model answer — they're not allowed to look things up and I don't want the model answer implying they should, so name-dropping should be minimal and only where it's genuinely load-bearing. In the marking scheme I want the moves that seperate a first-class answer from a solid 2:1 spelled out explicitly, with marks allocated — for instance whether the student distinguishes "this function is non-recursive" from "we could ever establish that it is"; whether they notice that agreement on any finite initial segment is consistent with infinitely many computable functions, so the Aarhus claim isn't merely unconfirmed but structurally unconfirmable; whether they think about what the device would have to be like for the objection not to bite (unbounded precision, noise, the fact that a physical equilibrium is only ever approached, never attained); and whether they can say what's left of the thesis's empirical content once all that is conceded, without just throwing up their hands and calling it a definition. And a short list of the wrong turns that should cost marks — concluding straight off that the thesis is analytic, treating the published readouts as even partial evidence for hypercomputation, or sliding between the claim about physical processes and the claim about what humans can compute by hand.
