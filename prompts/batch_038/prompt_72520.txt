A district curriculum committee has deadlocked over how to structure the opening six weeks of a seventh-grade unit on ratios and proportional reasoning, and its two external advisers have agreed to argue the matter out on the record. Write a dialogue between them. One takes her bearings from Whitehead's account of the rhythm of education — the phases of romance, precision, and generalization, and the danger of inert ideas, knowledge that is received but never used, tested, or thrown into fresh combinations — and holds that precision taught before the pupil has felt the subject matter as interesting produces exactly that kind of dead knowledge. The other argues from what the research on novice learning, worked examples, and cognitive load supports: that undirected exploration exceeds working-memory capacity, that novices learn more from studying a completed solution than from discovering one, and that procedural fluency is what makes later transfer possible.

Each must engage the strongest form of the other's position rather than a caricature. In particular, the direct-instruction advocate should take seriously the charge that her sequencing reliably manufactures the inert ideas Whitehead describes — pupils who can execute a cross-multiplication with no sense of what a ratio is — and say what in her own framework prevents this. The Whiteheadian should take seriously the charge that romance-first design quietly advantages the pupils who arrive already fluent from home, and that waiting for felt interest is not a plan for a child who has none. Let each concede what deserves conceding, including whether the expertise reversal effect and Whitehead's own insistence that the phases interweave and recur at every scale bring the positions closer together than their partisans admit.

Close with the two of them stating as precisely as they can where the residual disagreement lies — in the ordering of the phases, in the time allotted to each, in what counts as understanding a procedure, or in whose interests the design optimizes for — and saying what evidence about the unit's outcomes each would accept as bearing on it.
