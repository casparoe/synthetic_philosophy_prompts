I am a senior at a fairly demanding public high school, and for my capstone in a philosophy elective I have to write a ten-page paper that puts a primary text into contact with a modern policy case rather than just summarizing the text. Our unit this term was Plato's *Laws*, and we read the early books where the Athenian Stranger, the Spartan Megillus, and the Cretan Clinias get onto the subject of wine and drinking parties. What struck me is that the Stranger begins with a thought experiment about a drug that would reliably produce fear in whoever took it, and argues that if such a drug existed a city could use it to test and train courage cheaply and safely, the way one might test a colt or assay gold in a flame, instead of exposing citizens to real danger; and then he says wine is in fact something like that drug, except that it produces confidence rather than fear, which makes it dangerous to administer and therefore in need of an expert, sober, older leader of the drinking party. Alongside that he lays out an age ladder for wine — none at all for the very young, on the reasoning that you should not pour fire onto fire, moderate use through young adulthood, and fuller permission once a man reaches middle age and eats at the common messes, where wine is treated as the gift of Dionysus and something like a remedy for the sourness of old age. My paper's claim is that this is fundamentally a training-and-testing regime aimed at producing a certain character, whereas the American drinking-age regime is fundamentally a postponement-and-protection regime aimed at a certain body count, and that the two are not rivals so much as answers to different questions. I was arguing with a friend at lunch who insists the drinking age is just paternalism dressed up as safety, and I want to know whether Plato is actually on his side or not, because I suspect the answer is more awkward than either of us assumed.

Here are the facts of the episode I am using, so you do not have to guess at them. After the voting age came down to eighteen in 1971, roughly twenty-nine states lowered their minimum legal drinking ages to eighteen, nineteen, or twenty over the next several years, partly on the reasoning that anyone old enough to be drafted was old enough to vote and to drink. Studies then associated the lower ages with a rise in alcohol-related traffic deaths among young drivers, advocacy groups organized around that finding, and in 1984 Congress passed and President Reagan signed the National Minimum Drinking Age Act, which did not directly ban underage drinking but withheld ten percent of a noncompliant state's federal highway apportionment unless the state set its purchase age at twenty-one. South Dakota resisted and challenged the statute, and lost at the Supreme Court in 1987 on the spending power. Because the federal lever operated on purchase and public possession rather than on drinking as such, states retained a patchwork of exceptions for religious use, for private consumption in the home, and in several states for drinking with a consenting parent. By the mid-1990s every state had complied. What I want from you is a case analysis that runs in both directions, not a summary of either side. Please map the theory onto the episode carefully: who is the lawgiver in each case, who is the subject being legislated for, what counts as the harm, what counts as success, and what the enforcement mechanism is. Then tell me which features of the 1984 Act Plato's account genuinely illuminates, which features it simply has no vocabulary for — the federal structure that had to work through conditional money rather than direct rule, automobiles and the epidemiology of traffic deaths, a population that will never sit at a supervised common table, and the modern assumption that the state may not aim at the virtue of its citizens at all — and whether a Platonic might think the Act's real failure is that it hands young adults wine at twenty-one with no prior training in it, having forbidden precisely the graded practice Plato thinks is the point. Finally, turn it around: does the 1984 episode confirm Plato's scheme, expose an assumption in it that no longer holds, or reveal that his scheme was never a proposal about who should be allowed to drink in the first place?
