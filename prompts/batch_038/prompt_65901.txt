Consider the conjunction **J**: epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness is true — phenomenal states depend nomically on physical states but produce no physical effects whatsoever — *and* our judgments and reports about consciousness are genuinely about those phenomenal states and are substantially accurate rather than accidentally so. I would like your credence in J, as a number, together with the decomposition that produces it.

Please lead with a single sentence stating your final figure, then assign a probability to each of the following terms, with no more than two sentences of justification per term:

1. That there are phenomenal states at all, i.e. that illusionist and eliminativist accounts fail.
2. That epiphenomenalism is true, conditional on there being phenomenal states — I expect the causal closure of the physical and the conceivability of zombies to do most of the work here.
3. That the paradox of phenomenal judgment is genuinely fatal to epiphenomenalism rather than merely embarrassing. Break this into three sub-probabilities: that reference and knowledge of a state require a causal history with it at all; that some non-causal route — acquaintance, or a constitutional account on which the experience is a component of the judgment — could secure both; and that the objection is not equally damaging to physicalism, which would blunt its force as a reductio.
4. A term I think is usually skipped. Suppose the epiphenomenalist escapes the paradox by holding that content is fixed by nomic covariance rather than by causation. Covariance is symmetric, and in the relevant sense transitive, in a way that causation is not: the experience covaries with its neural correlate, and it is the correlate that causes the judgment. If nothing in that arrangement privileges the experience as the referent of our phenomenal talk, the escape route delivers a world in which our judgments are about neural states after all — that is, it collapses epiphenomenalism into a physicalism about the content of phenomenal concepts, and so fails to make J true. Assign a probability that this collapse argument succeeds.
