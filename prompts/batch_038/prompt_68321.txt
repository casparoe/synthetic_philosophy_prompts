You are to work in the role of a specialist in eighteenth-century French political thought whose competence extends beyond the two Discourses and the *Social Contract* to Rousseau's writings on culture and public spectacle — in particular the *Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre* (1758), written in reply to d'Alembert's article on Geneva in the *Encyclopédie*, which had suggested that a playhouse there could be conducted wholesomely if properly regulated. Treat what follows as a piece of disciplined anachronism rather than as an occasion for the usual op-ed reflexes. Think through the problem in stages before you commit to any verdict, and treat the intermediate reasoning as part of the deliverable, not as scaffolding to be discarded.

I am contributing a chapter to an edited collection on Enlightenment aesthetics and contemporary media institutions. My section poses the following question, which I want answered with attention to the actual architecture of Rousseau's argument.

Rousseau's positive alternative to the theatre is the public festival: a gathering with no stage, no ticketed seats, and no paid performers, in which the assembled people see one another and so become, on his account, actors in their own right — set against the darkened auditorium, where a collection of private individuals consumes the represented passions of professionals. Now suppose Rousseau were shown three present-day institutions, each of which advertises itself as the realization of exactly that ideal, by dissolving the barrier between spectator and performer:

1. **Commercial immersive theatre** of the *Sleep No More* type: a paying audience moves freely, often masked, through a large multi-room installation, arriving at scenes in whatever order it chooses, while highly trained professionals perform on a wage and occasionally draw individual spectators aside into private rooms for one-to-one sequences. The building is closed, ticketed, expensive, and the audience members are largely strangers to one another.

2. **Long charity livestream marathons** of the speedrunning-marathon type: a continuous multi-day broadcast in which viewers direct what happens by donating — selecting the game, imposing handicaps, triggering on-air events — while hosts read out names and amounts in real time. The audience is distributed, asynchronous in attention, mutually invisible, and the performers are the broadcasters, whose livelihood depends on the metrics the audience generates.

3. **A municipal amateur production** — a village pantomime or community musical: cast, crew, and audience are substantially the same local people, tickets are cheap, proceeds go to a local cause, the performance standard is visibly non-professional, and rehearsals occupy the participants' leisure for months beforehand.

**The task.** For each of the three, predict whether Rousseau would judge it an approximation of the festival or a more insidious form of the theatre he opposed; then state which he would tolerate most readily and defend that ordering.

**Constraints on the answer.**

- Do not let the participatory surface decide the case. I want to know which of Rousseau's commitments is actually doing the work. To fix vocabulary, let me stipulate two labels of my own devising — they are not Rousseau's terms, and you should not present them as his. Call the **mirror constraint** his claim that the theatre cannot reform morals because a playhouse must please the tastes already formed in its audience, and so reflects and intensifies the character of a city rather than altering it; call the **substitution constraint** his claim that spectacle displaces the citizen's own activity with the paid performance of another, and assembles people as an aggregate of strangers rather than as a public body. For each institution, say which constraint governs your verdict, and identify any case in which the two pull in opposite directions. I expect institution 1 to be precisely such a case, and I want to see how you resolve it rather than whether you notice it.

- Attend to the considerations Rousseau actually pressed and that modern readers tend to skip: the commercial character of the actor's trade, as a person who sells himself and performs another character for pay; the cost in time and money to a small working republic, and the luxury and inequality a playhouse imports along with its plays; his denial that spectacle purges the passions or does anything at all to conduct; the fact that his argument is calibrated to Geneva rather than to Paris, so that the harm he predicts is a function of a city's size and manners and not of the repertoire; his treatment of Molière's *Misanthrope* as a case in which comedy renders the honest man ridiculous and the complaisant worldly man sympathetic; and his remarks on the mixed audience and the display of women as a theatre of intrigue. Say which of these remain live in a world of distributed media and which lose their grip, and why.

- Where Rousseau's framework simply runs out, say so rather than papering over it. I anticipate at least these candidates: the absence of any bounded civic body that could be the subject of a festival; the fact that a distributed audience is not co-present and therefore cannot see one another; and the fact that participation is itself monetized and continuously measured. But go further and name the disanalogy you consider most damaging to any attempt to recruit Rousseau as an ally of participatory media, and explain what in his system makes it damaging.

- Finish with a short paragraph reporting your confidence in the overall ordering and identifying the single interpretive question about Rousseau's own position which, were it settled the other way, would most change your answer.

**Manner and length.** Roughly 1,400–1,800 words, organised under the three institutions and then the comparative ordering. Argue rather than summarise. Paraphrase throughout; I am interested in your reconstruction of the reasoning, not in your recollection of Rousseau's formulations, and I would rather you give me an accurate paraphrase than a confident-sounding quotation. No bibliography, no reading list, no suggestions for further work — this chapter has one question and I want it answered.
