I'm a staff engineer on the infrastructure team at a company that runs large-scale distributed systems; my formal education is in computer science, and the closest I have come to philosophy is a shelved intention to read something about minds and machines someday. Last month our executive offsite closed with a talk by an outside speaker — a physicist by training who now runs a quantitative fund — on the simulation argument. He had no slides, and because he is clearly intelligent and fluent, most of the room, me included, left persuaded. On the flight home I tried to reconstruct what he had said and found that I could not tell which parts were the argument and which parts were his confidence. There is a famous original paper behind all of this, which I have never read, and I would rather not guess at what it says, so I am giving you his version as faithfully as I can and asking you to audit it.

His reconstruction went roughly as follows, and I have kept his order because he said the order matters. Minds are information processing, and information processing does not care what substrate it runs on, so a sufficiently detailed simulation of a person would be genuinely conscious and would not seem simulated to itself. Now consider a civilization that survives long enough to acquire enormous computing power and becomes curious about its own past. If such a civilization can run ancestor simulations at all, it will not run one or two; it will run enormous numbers of them. So if such simulations are ever run at scale, the number of simulated people will dwarf the number of non-simulated people by many orders of magnitude. Given that, if you find yourself to be a person with what look like ordinary human memories in an ordinary-looking universe, you should conclude that you are almost certainly one of the simulated ones rather than one of the originals. He then said that this does not quite prove we are in a simulation, because it leaves three possibilities, at least one of which must be true: civilizations essentially always destroy themselves or otherwise fail before reaching that stage of technological maturity; civilizations that reach it lose interest in simulating their ancestors, perhaps for ethical reasons; or we are in a simulation right now. His parting line was that the third possibility is the one to bet on and that the only real question is which of the other two you want to give up.

He also offered three elaborations, and these are the parts I trust least even though they are the parts I liked most at the time. First, he said the computing cost objection is naive, because a good simulation does not need to model every particle everywhere: it can be lazy, rendering detail only where it is observed and only when it is observed, much the way a video game engine or a garbage collector does work, and he added that this is exactly how our own physics already looks to us, since the most careful measurements of nature come back quantized and limited in resolution. Second, he said you cannot test the hypothesis from inside, because any experiment you design, and any record of the result, is part of the simulation too — so the hypothesis is immune to evidence, which he treated as a mark of profundity rather than a defect. Third, he slid into a claim about consciousness: if we are simulated, then the regularities of our experience are being produced by something outside our physics, and that, he said, is what makes the whole thing a religious question wearing a lab coat, and it is a separate and stronger argument than the counting argument, because it explains why there is anything it is like to be us at all.

What I would like is an accuracy audit of all of the above, organized so that I can act on it. Take his numbered steps and tell me, for each, whether a professional working on this argument would accept it, accept it as a common simplification, or reject it outright — and I am especially interested in whether the move from the counting to the conclusion is licensed, since as I have written it out he seems to shift at some point between counting civilizations and counting people, and between a conditional claim and an unconditional one, and I cannot tell whether that shift is where the argument actually lives or whether I have simply misremembered him. Please tell me whether his two simplifications about lazy rendering and quantization are doing legitimate work or whether they quietly assume the conclusion — my own intuition as someone who writes systems for a living is that on-demand rendering is only cheap when you can afford inconsistency, and that a renderer that has to keep every observer's measurements mutually consistent, and consistent with a past nobody is currently inspecting, may be no cheaper than brute force, and I would like to know whether that intuition is a real objection or a category error. Tell me what the second and third disjuncts actually cost him, and whether the ethical gloss he attached to the second one is right. And tell me what to make of the consciousness material: whether it belongs in this argument at all, or whether he has spliced on a different argument that does different work and needs different premises, and if so what the splicing hides.

Two constraints on your answer. Please do not send me to any sources: I cannot fetch papers, and I have no way to check a bibliography, so I would rather you work entirely from the argument as I have laid it out and say plainly where you are unsure because my reconstruction may be at fault. And please end with a verdict rather than a shrug — specifically, what you think a careful person should now believe about the probability that we are in a simulation, whether his closing recommendation to bet on the third disjunct survives correction of his summary, and which single claim of his, if I had to strike one, would do the most damage to the whole performance.
