I'm a junior stats major, probably the only person in my political philosophy seminar who's actually taken survey methodology, and I keep having this problem where everything we read wants to turn into an estimation problem in my head. The seminar unit for the next couple weeks is the franchise: compulsory voting (we spent a while on the Australian model where the fine is basically a parking ticket), whether the voting age should drop to 16, and whether long-term resident non-citizens should get the vote. The readings are the usual spread — the legitimacy and fairness case for making voting mandatory, the argument that unequal turnout skews policy toward whoever actually shows up, the civic-duty and anti-coercion objections on the other side, competence worries about 16-year-olds, and membership or stakeholder arguments about non-citizens. I follow all of it, I just can't tell which parts are doing real work and which are vibes.

So here's what I want. Translate that whole debate into the vocabulary I actually live in: sampling frames, coverage error, nonresponse bias, self-selection, weighting and post-stratification, MAR versus MNAR, design effects, estimands. Treat the electorate as a sample drawn from the resident population and an election result as an estimate of something. Go issue by issue — compulsion, voting age, non-citizen votes — and for each tell me (a) what carries over cleanly, (b) what you can only render by bending or distorting something, and (c) what has no expression in that vocabulary at all. Please don't smuggle in assumptions from my side and present them as translation results; if a step only works because you quietly presumed something, name it.

The residue is the part I actually care about. My suspicion going in is that some of the moves people make in seminar — like the idea that compelling someone to vote is a distinct kind of wrong because voting is a form of speech, or that a 16-year-old is missing something that isn't just extra variance in the estimate — sound like they're reaching for things my framework literally can't state, and I want to know whether that's right or whether I'm just not being creative. Watch especially for the big one: if calling an election result an estimator quietly assumes there's some true population quantity out there that the vote is tracking, then the whole analogy might beg the question against people who think the vote constitutes the outcome rather than measuring it. Say so plainly if that's where it breaks, I'd rather hear the break than a prettier analogy. And finish with a verdict — is the stuff that refuses to translate evidence my vocabulary is missing something real, or evidence that some of these arguments are less substantive than they sounded when I read them?
