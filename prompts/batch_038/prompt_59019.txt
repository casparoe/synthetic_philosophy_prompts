I'm writing a seminar paper on the unity of consciousness and a friend in neuro sent me this bit from some popular book he's reading, saying it basically "settles" the debate:

> "Split-brain patients — people whose corpus callosum has been severed to control epilepsy — show that consciousness was never one thing to begin with. Once the two hemispheres can no longer talk to each other, each one has its own beliefs, desires and experiences, and neither knows what the other is up to. So there are literally two minds in one skull, and the single conscious subject of everyday talk turns out to be a myth: the self is just a story the left hemisphere's 'interpreter' tells after the fact. Gazzaniga's patients prove it. Flash a word to the right hemisphere and the patient says he saw nothing, but his left hand reaches out and picks up the right object. He has no idea what his own hand is doing."

I feel like this is overstated — something about the disunity being way narrower than the paragraph implies, and about coordination between the two hands surviving the surgery — but I don't know enought to say where it breaks.

Can you act as a philosopher of mind who also properly knows the neuropsychology here? Before handing out verdicts, brainstorm what's solid in the paragraph, then audit it claim by claim: what a specialist would sign off on, what's a simplification that actually changes the argument, and what's flat wrong. Pay special attention to whether it slides between two hemispheres, two streams of experience and two *persons* as if those were the same move, and to whether results obtained under restricted, lateralised stimulation can settle how many subjects of experience there are. Also — I vaguely remember someone arguing that neither "one mind" nor "two minds" fits these patients, and that's why the case is philosophically interesting rather than just decisive. Is that roughly right?

Finally: does the last line ("he has no idea what his own hand is doing") survive the corrections in some weaker form, or does fixing the errors take it down too? Think carefully and don't hedge everything into mush — where the book is fine, say so.
