You are to work as a professional philosopher with a specialism in distributive ethics and aggregation, writing here in a satirical register: someone capable of parodying a genre precisely because they understand its intellectual commitments from the inside. Before drafting anything, spend a short paragraph (no more than 150 words, clearly separated from the piece) identifying the genuine pressure points in your target — the places where an aggregative welfarist methodology is doing real work and is therefore vulnerable to being followed too faithfully. Then write the piece.

**The commission.** I am assembling readings for a final-year undergraduate seminar on the utility monster — the creature Nozick introduced in 1974, whose welfare-conversion rate per unit of resource so far exceeds anyone else's, and does not decline as resources accumulate, that a sum-maximising view appears to require us to hand it everything. The standard classroom treatment has become stale: my students can recite the thought experiment and then produce three objections from memory. I want instead a piece of comic prose that makes them feel the methodology operating on a case where it has actually been applied.

**The conceit.** Write a parody of a British grantmaking foundation's internal cost-effectiveness evaluation report — the document a senior analyst circulates to trustees ahead of a funding decision — which concludes that the foundation's entire remaining endowment should be transferred, unrestricted and in a single tranche, to a recipient the analysts have identified as a utility monster. The foundation is a registered charity in England and Wales, its idiom is managerial and unembarrassed, and nothing in the report is presented as unusual.

**Beats the report must hit, from within the fiction rather than by commentary on it.**

- The identification of the monster: how the analysts elicited the welfare-conversion coefficient, what instrument they used, and the report's frank acknowledgement that the entire recommendation rests on an interpersonal comparison it cannot verify. A short sensitivity analysis should show the recommendation is robust across every range the analysts regard as plausible and reverses only under values they dismiss as artefacts.
- Why the recipient is exempt from diminishing marginal utility, treated as a finding rather than a miracle, with a sentence on the discount rate applied to its future welfare and on the fact that the recommendation survives the foundation's standard rate.
- A boxed 'risks and mitigations' section carrying the dissent of one trustee who objects on prioritarian grounds — that the worst off are being left exactly where they were while one party receives everything. The dissent must be reasonable *within the funder's own framework* and must lose, answered in the foundation's characteristic voice.
- The separateness-of-persons objection, which the report should not merely mention but *answer*, because the recipient turns out to be a single incorporated optimiser rather than a fantastical individual: an entity that genuinely is one subject, so that the objection, on the analysts' own reading, does not apply. This should be the report's most chilling sentence and it should not announce itself as such.
- 'Room for more funding': the binding constraint is not the conversion rate but the recipient's appetite, and the report should recommend a supplementary grant for appetite expansion.
- A note on counterfactual impact, on whether other funders would have fed the recipient anyway.

**Craft constraints.** The humour must come from getting the ethics right: exaggerate real features of aggregative welfarism and of cost-effectiveness practice, and invent none. Sustained deadpan throughout — no winking, no narrator stepping outside the document, and no character permitted to observe that any of this is absurd except the dissenting trustee, whose objection has to be the reasonable one that fails. A reader who has never met the thought experiment should nonetheless be able to reconstruct, from the report's own reasoning, that something has gone wrong. Close with a 'lessons learned' paragraph recommending that the same methodology be applied retrospectively to the foundation's previous fifty years of giving, and let the reader draw the consequence.

**Form.** Between 900 and 1,100 words, with a document reference number, headings, one small table, the boxed sidebar, and footnotes. The footnotes may cite invented internal papers, memoranda and committee minutes; they must not cite real books, articles or people, and there should be no bibliography. Refer to positions and arguments by description only. Write in British English.
