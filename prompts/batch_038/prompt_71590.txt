Essay for a decision theory seminar, and I want you to actually pick a side.

There's a move people keep making: Simon showed we can't maximize because our information, time and processing are all limited, so we satisfice — take the first option that clears an aspiration level — and then somebody slides from "we can't optimize" to "optimizing isn't required of us, taking the good-enough option is rational." Press on that gap.

Set it up so the search-cost story is switched off entirely. An ER doctor has settled on a treatment thats clearly good enough for the patient, and then learns, at literally zero cost in time, effort, or risk, that a different protocol sitting right there would leave the patient modestly better off. Is sticking with the good-enough one irrational, permissible, or something else? Take the two lines seriously: the one that says a known-better option always beats an aspiration level that does no normative work of its own, and the one that says the level is fixed by something other than a comparison of the options on the table — her role, her standing commitments, how much of her attention this decision deserves at all — so stopping isnt a failure of anything. Then deal with the collapse worry: whether normative satisficing just turns out to be maximizing once you price in the agent's own time and attention, and what survives if it does.

Briefly say whether the verdict changes for epistemic satisficing — stopping evidence-gathering once your belief is good enough.

No citations, I'll add them. But land on a verdict: is there a real normative satisficing distinct from maximizing-under-costs, or not? Dont fudge it with "it depends what you mean by rational."
