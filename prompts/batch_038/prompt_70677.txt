so — I got out in March after nine years, and next Thursday the reentry center where I do intake paperwork is running one of those voter registration drives in a church basement, and for some reason they asked me to say a few words to open it. About sixty people, mostly men and women who got out in the last couple of years, some still on supervision, plus the staff, plus one reporter from the local paper who comes to all of these. Four minutes max, they said, maybe five.

Here's my problem. I don't want to say "your vote counts" or "every vote matters" or "it's your civic duty," because I dont believe any of those and everybody in that room has spent a decade being lied to politely by institutions. When I registered last month I sat down and actually tried to work out the odds that my vote changes anything in a city race here, and it isn't zero but it isn't much, and I'd be up there pretending otherwise. But I do want them to fill out the form. Not as a favor to the system, and not because anything got given back to them.

What I actually think, for what it's worth, is that the reason to do it has nothing to do with the outcome. It's that it's the one place where you get to go on the record about which side you're on and have it counted as you saying it, whether or not it lands anywhere. I want the whole speech built on that, and I want it to survive the three things people in that room will be sitting there thinking: that it won't change anything, that both options are bad so picking the less-bad one just makes you one of the people who picked it, and that they don't owe this place a damn thing after what it did to them.

And here's the part I can't figure out, which is the real reason I'm asking. Maybe a third of the room can't register Thursday at all. Some are still on supervision. Some are from states where it doesn't come back until you've paid off every last fine and fee, and they can't. And one woman I've been doing paperwork with is from a state where it never comes back, full stop, she will never vote in her life. So the speech can't end with "now go do it," because for a bunch of them that's just the door slamming again while everyone else stands in line. They need to come out of those four minutes with something that isn't a consolation prize, and I genuinely don't know what that something is. That's the knot I need you to untangle.

Two more complications. There's a guy on staff whose nephew is in college and reads all this political philosophy, and the nephew's whole position is that most people shouldn't vote because they'll vote badly and you shouldn't be adding a bad vote to the pile. I find that unbearable in this room — the sentence I served was the state formally declaring that I wasn't fit to participate, that was part of the punishment — but I don't want to just dunk on a kid I've never met either, and I don't want the speech to pretend the objection is stupid. And then there's a regular at the center who's told me he's voting for a candidate with no chance whatsoever, purely to make a statement, and a woman who won't vote at all because she says both parties were in on it. I want the speech to leave room for both of them without telling either one they're wrong, and without collapsing into a shrug where nothing is better than anything else.

Please don't quote anybody famous, no founding documents, no "democracy is not a spectator sport," no flags, no bullet points, no "in conclusion." One concrete image from my own first week back is welcome — the little card in the mail, the box on the form where you have to write your address after nine years of not having one, take your pick, I'll use whatever you give me. And it has to be sayable out loud by a person who is going to be nervous: short sentences, nothing I'd have to reread to get through.
