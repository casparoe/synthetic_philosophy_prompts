For three years I have co-taught a graduate seminar on predictive processing with a colleague from computational neuroscience, and the seminar has settled into a standoff that neither of us knows how to break. The formal apparatus is not in dispute: hierarchical generative models, prediction errors weighted by precision, perception as the updating of those models and action as the sampling that brings the world into line with them, and, at the outermost level, variational free energy as a tractable bound on surprisal that adaptive systems are said to minimize. What is in dispute is what any of this commits one to.

On one side are those who take the framework to be a vindication of representationalism: the generative model is about its hidden causes, descending predictions carry content, prediction errors have conditions under which they are satisfied, and the standard accounts of delusion or of bistable perception are accounts of content going wrong. On the other side are those — several of whom arrived at predictive processing from enactivism — who insist that none of this vocabulary is required: what descending signals do is specify what will happen if the organism goes on behaving as it is, motor commands are causes rather than descriptions, and the whole apparatus concerns the self-organization of a system coupled to an environment rather than inner states standing for outer ones. On this second reading, the principle is closer in kind to a variational principle in physics than to a theory of mental content, and the representationalist gloss is an optional extra smuggled in by philosophers. Between them sits an uneasy middle position, on which there are representations but only action-oriented, short-horizon ones, which each side recruits as an ally and neither accepts.

My working diagnosis, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that this is three distinct disputes wearing a single coat. First, a semantic one: whether "representation" is to be cashed out in terms of misrepresentation conditions, so that the question of whether the models represent is settled by stipulation rather than discovered. Second, a methodological one: whether expected free energy, with its decomposition into something like epistemic and pragmatic value, describes a mechanism or merely redescribes instrumental rationality in new notation — this is where the familiar dark-room objection does its work. Third, an empirical one: whether precision corresponds to anything measurable, such as synaptic gain and neuromodulation, or functions as a free parameter fitted after the fact, which would decide whether the framework licenses novel predictions or merely accommodates data. My hypothesis is that the standoff persists because the shared equations are compatible with every combination of answers to these three questions, and so each camp is able to enlist every new result.

Is that diagnosis correct? I want you to say, for each of the three disputes, what kind of disagreement it actually is — verbal, substantive and empirically decidable, normative about what counts as an explanation, or a bedrock intuition neither side can argue for — and whether there is a single deeper disagreement underneath them that I have missed, perhaps about what it takes for a formalism to be about the world at all. Then tell me what would count as progress on each: a discriminating experiment, a theorem, a definitional settlement, a change of question — or nothing, in which case say so plainly and explain why the impasse is stable rather than merely difficult. Ground the diagnosis in what the two sides actually say, not in a survey of the positions or a list of further reading; I have read all of it. Finally, tell me where my own tripartite diagnosis begs the question against one side or the other, since I suspect it is not as neutral as I have presented it here.
