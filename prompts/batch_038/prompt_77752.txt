I've been running a monthly rationalist-ish meetup for about six years now — 20 to 30 people, maybe half of them have real stats backgrounds, none of them have any genetics — and I've made the mistake of letting heritability come up casually three separate times. All three turned into the same exhausting mess. So next month I'm doing a proper 75-minute session on what heritability is and isn't, and I want a teaching plan from you, not the content. I can read a textbook, that's not the bottleneck.

My hunch, tell me if it's wrong: the reason it goes sideways is that everybody walks in carrying the nature/nurture question, and heritability was never built to answer that question. So I want to teach it backwards from the job the number actually does — the breeder's parameter, the thing you multiply by a selection differential to predict how much the next generation shifts — and only then let them notice that a quantity designed for that job has no business holding the weight they want to put on it. If you think that's the wrong root for the tree, say so and give me a better one.

What I need from you:

- The order, with the dependency structure actually spelled out — what needs to be in place before what can land. In particular I keep flip-flopping on whether "it's a ratio of variances, not a percentage of causation" has to come before "it's relative to this population in these environments," or whether the relativity point hits harder first and motivates the ratio.

- One example that carries the whole thing, and what it has to contain and what it must not. I've been toying with the seeds-in-two-trays move — split a genetically variable batch of seeds between a tray of uniform rich soil and a tray of uniform poor soil, so the variation within each tray is essentially all genetic while the difference between the trays is entirely soil — but I worry it's a gotcha that teaches them the punchline instead of the machinery. Alternative: build a tiny population of eight plants they do the variance decomposition on by hand. Which one, or neither?

- The mirror-image pair I want them to walk out with: a trait that's about as genetic as it gets and completely fixable by changing the environment (the PKU situation), and a trait that's genetically built in developmentally but has near-zero heritability because the actual variation is all accidents (fingers, eyes). Tell me where in the sequence those go so they land as consequences of the definition rather than as fun trivia bolted on at the end.

- The misconception map. I'm confident about the dumb ones — that 0.8 means eighty percent of my height is genes, that a high number means environmental intervention is hopeless, that high within-group heritability licenses an inference about a between-group gap. I care way more about the ones I probably won't see coming: the reification slip where a high number makes the trait feel more real and more like a natural kind; the treating of a twin-study estimate as a measurement rather than an estimate resting on assumptions that are themselves what's contested; broad-sense and narrow-sense getting collapsed so that later somebody reads a GWAS-derived figure as if it were the twin figure. Which of these do I head off in real time, which can wait for the debrief, and which should I deliberately let them get wrong so I can walk it back?

- The IQ conversation. Some fraction of the room drags us there within ten minutes and one or two people have already-read-the-internet positions they're attached to. I don't want to spend the whole session on it, and I don't want to look like I'm routing around it either. Give me a plan for handling the derail in the room, including whether there's a legitimate slot for it in the sequence or whether it belongs entirely after.

- What I should refuse to cover, out loud, with a sentence I can actually say. My candidates are gene-environment interaction, epigenetics, SNP heritability and missing heritability, and the Flynn effect — but I suspect at least one of those is load-bearing and cutting it costs me more than keeping it.

- One question at the end, preferably with numbers in it, that separates people who got it from people who are nodding along. Something in the shape of: here's an intervention that makes everyone in the population healthier, which direction does heritability move and why — but sharper than that, and ideally one where the intuitive answer is wrong for an interesting reason.

Constraints: whiteboard only, paper handouts are fine, no projector, 75 minutes, and about a third of the room will happily do arithmetic while two thirds won't touch it. Don't give me readings or citations, I'll fill those in myself. And be blunt where my plan is bad — the entire point of asking is that I'm probably wrong about something structural.
