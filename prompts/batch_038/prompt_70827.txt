I have no training in law or philosophy, but one contrast has left me stuck. The rules of armed conflict ban bullets designed to expand or flatten inside the body, and treat firing them at an enemy soldier as a war crime. Police forces in many countries issue precisely those rounds deliberately, on the ground that they are the humane option because they stop in the target instead of passing through into a bystander. The same wound is an atrocity in one setting and a recommended practice in another.

What account of harm does each presuppose? Is the wartime ban really about how much the soldier suffers, or about fairness between people who are each permitted to kill the other, or about the thought that he has done nothing to forfeit protection against being maimed as distinct from being killed outright? And how does either square with the same body of law permitting a pilot to kill that soldier with a bomb?

Reconstruct the view of harm, and of what makes someone liable to be injured, that each practice assumes; distinguish what each strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with; and say what would have to change in either practice if injuries were instead ranked solely by the pain and disability they cause. Please write in British English.
