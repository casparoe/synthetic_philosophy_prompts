I am putting together a problem set for an advanced undergraduate seminar in the foundations of decision theory, and the week in question is on the value of information. The students will have been handed the slogan that free information is always worth waiting for, together with the two-line argument that generates it: an agent who decides immediately gets the expected utility of the act that maximizes against her prior, an agent who waits gets the average, over the cells of the evidence partition, of the maximum against each posterior, and the second quantity cannot be smaller because maximization is a convex operation. Two of the students are pre-law and treat the slogan as a theorem of practical rationality that admits no exceptions; I would like a problem that shows them it is a theorem with hypotheses, and that the hypotheses are load-bearing. So: construct me a case, to specification, in which the result fails — a fully ideal Bayesian who is offered a genuinely costless, genuinely relevant piece of information before she must act, and whose expected utility from waiting for it and then choosing optimally is strictly lower than her expected utility from choosing now.

The specifications, all of which must be met simultaneously. The scenario should be a single paragraph set in ordinary professional life — a clinic, an engineering firm, an audit, a family business — with no exotic physics, no predictors better than a shrewd human being, and no neurotechnology. The agent must be a precise Bayesian who conditionalizes on her total evidence and then maximizes: no weakness of will, no forgetting, no foreseeably non-Bayesian response to what she learns, no uncertainty about her own updating rule. The information must be strictly free, in the sense that acquiring it costs nothing in money, time, attention, risk, or foregone opportunity, and it must be evidentially non-trivial: a test that could only confirm what she already believes does not count. Her utility function must be defined over states of the world that make no reference to what she believes or knows, so that the case cannot trade on bad news being painful or on the comforts of ignorance; likewise her tastes and values must not shift between the moment of decision and the moment of acting. And the numbers must be on the page: give a small partition of two or three hypotheses and two or three possible pieces of evidence, the prior, the utility matrix, and both expected utilities computed explicitly, with the inequality running the wrong way by a margin that is not a rounding artifact. The drop must be generated by the decision structure, not stipulated — if I could point to a line where you simply assert that waiting is worse rather than deriving it, the case fails.

Then do the diagnosis. Tell me which hypothesis of the theorem your case violates, and locate the exact step in the convexity argument that goes through only because that hypothesis holds. Give me a repaired version of the same setting in which that hypothesis is restored — changing as little else as you can manage — and show the inequality flipping back the way the theorem predicts. After that, play the adversary: state the strongest reply available to someone who insists the result is a theorem and therefore cannot be counterexampled at all, for instance that your utility assignments covertly mention the agent's epistemic situation after all, or that the two expected utilities are not comparable because the decision problem she faces after learning is not the decision problem she faced before, or that the option to wait should itself have been treated as an act in the original act set. Say which of these objections lands against your case and which does not, and if the case survives, say how the theorem ought to be restated so that it stops looking like a claim about the world and starts looking like what it is. Finally, note which feature of your construction is doing the real work, what happens to the verdict if that feature is weakened rather than removed, and what the most likely wrong diagnosis from a student who gets all the arithmetic right would be.
