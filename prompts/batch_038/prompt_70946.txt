I'm working through the closing chapter of a well-regarded general book on aesthetics, and the author devotes the last few pages to Tolstoy's late theory of art. I've read enough about that theory in fragments to be suspicious that the summary is sanding off the parts that make it difficult, but I can't say where. Here is the passage as I transcribed it:

"Tolstoy's aesthetic tract starts from a deliberately deflationary idea: art is a form of communication, nothing more exalted. Just as words carry thoughts from one person to another, artworks carry feelings. The artist recalls a feeling he once had, reawakens it in himself, and then fixes it in lines, colors, sounds, or words so that other people catch it from him. What marks off art from other kinds of transmission is that its quality is measured by the strength of the infection: the more vividly and clearly the feeling comes across, and the more completely the audience is joined to the artist, the better the work. Tolstoy therefore throws out the traditional definition of art in terms of beauty, on the ground that beauty just names what gives us pleasure, so a definition built on it can never yield a standard. His conclusion is radical. Because the worth of a work is entirely a matter of how widely and how powerfully it communicates, art that only a trained minority can appreciate fails by that fact alone — which is why he was willing to dismiss King Lear and Beethoven's Ninth Symphony along with the decadents. What is usually missed is that he is not moralizing here in the ordinary sense; he is applying a communicative test rather than a doctrinal one. A work succeeds insofar as it is sincere and infectious, and sincerity for him means being honest about what one actually felt — which is why he located the disease of modern art in professional artists who manufacture effects on commission."
