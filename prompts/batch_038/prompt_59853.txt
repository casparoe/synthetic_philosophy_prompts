I have just taken over as chair of our university's student hardship fund, and the first thing I inherited is eleven years of minutes plus a standing instruction from the dean that the committee, which has always insisted it applies one coherent standard rather than a bundle of sympathies, should publish a short memo this term explaining what that standard is. The fund is fixed and heavily oversubscribed: each round the committee ranks the applications and funds down the list until the money runs out, so a refusal is usually a ranking, not a judgment that an applicant is undeserving. The previous chair wrote every rationale line in the minutes herself, was fond of a particular vocabulary, and is no longer contactable; the two longest-serving members vote as a bloc and have never explained themselves. I know the general labels for the candidate standards — welfare, resources, capabilities, and whatever the responsibility-sensitive versions of each are called — but I cannot tell from the record which of them my committee has actually been operating, and I suspect the answer is that the record supports more than one.

Here is the whole of the relevant history. (a) A student with a diagnosed progressive condition, from a household with income well above the median, most of which was absorbed by treatment and equipment the national health service did not cover: funded at the top of the round, above two applicants with far lower incomes, on the recorded ground that what matters is what a student is left with once the condition has taken its share. (b) A single parent studying full time, unremarkable health, earnings near the median, most of them going to childcare: funded second, with the note that the same sum does not buy the same life for her. (c) An applicant living rent-free with family, no dependants, no health costs, and the lightest material burden in the round, whose statement was dominated by an account of persistent low mood and a sense that nothing much mattered: not funded, and a nominally better-off applicant with a real cost burden was ranked ahead of them; two members dissented, arguing the distress was the clearest hardship presented. (d) An applicant who had resigned a stable full-time post two years earlier to work unpaid on a community archive that subsequently lost its funding, leaving depleted savings and no references: not funded, the minutes recording that the fund insures against misfortune, not against plans. (e) A wheelchair user from a wealthy family who described their life as satisfying and expressly did not ask to be compensated for the disability as such, seeking only a specialised workstation and the additional transport costs of reaching campus: funded for those items and refused a general living-cost supplement. (f) Two applicants with near-identical earnings and identical rent, one in the city and one in a remote valley where fuel and travel costs ran to roughly double: the valley applicant funded, the city applicant not. (g) An applicant whose chosen course required a specific high-specification instrument they could not afford, having been advised of cheaper routes and declined them, whose statement described acute distress at the prospect of withdrawing: not funded, and when one member argued the choice was hardly free given family expectations the committee rejected that line explicitly, minuting that it does not inquire into how an applicant came to want what they want. (h) An applicant who developed a chronic condition in their late teens as a consequence of a voluntary high-risk activity they have since abandoned and who can no longer work full time: funded near the top, with the tersest rationale in the file, that the origin of the condition is not the committee's business while its cost is.

The application the committee has to decide in May is this. A postgraduate student, thirty-four, who six years ago — as the only family member within reach — left a well-paid job to care full time for a parent with a rapidly advancing illness; the parent died last year. The consequences, all documented, are that their savings are largely gone, that they have no recent professional references, and that the caregiving years left them with a diagnosed chronic condition that now caps them at roughly fifteen hours of paid work a week and requires equipment if they are to study from home. Their statement contains no regret whatever: it says the years were worth it, describes their life as settled and even good, and asks only for what is needed to finish the degree, expressly declining to claim lost earnings. The request as submitted covers a home workstation and a part-time study allowance to make up the shortfall in living costs.

What I want from you is an analysis of my own record, not a survey of the literature or a list of who holds which view. First: set out every standard that is jointly consistent with all eight outcomes, stated precisely enough that it generates each of them, including (g)'s refusal to look behind a choice alongside (h)'s declared indifference to origins, which I cannot reconcile. Second: for each candidate standard, say what it implies for the May application — fund, refuse, or fund in part, and if in part, which items — and note where a standard makes the applicant's own contentment relevant and where it makes it irrelevant. Third: give me your best guess at what this particular committee will actually do, with your reasons for preferring that reading over the others, and tell me which recorded rationales are doing no work at all or are contradicted by the votes. Fourth, and this is the part that goes in the memo: tell me what the committee should decide if consistency with its own record is the sole criterion, which single precedent is the closest analog to this application and where the analogy strains, and — if the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome — what principle the committee would be adopting for the first time by deciding either way. Finally, describe the one hypothetical application I should table next year that would settle which standard we have been running, and say which of the surviving candidates it separates from which.
