I built an argument for my grad seminar on the epistemology of maths — meant to push the students from Benacerraf's original dilemma toward Field's reliability version of it — and it seems to prove way too much, and I can't locate the flaw.

(1) To know that p, you have to be able to explain why your belief that p isn't lucky — why it lands on the truth rather than a falsehood.
(2) Any such explanation has to cite a causal, or at any rate a counterfactual, hookup between the fact that p and the process that produced the belief.
(3) Arithmetical truths, if there are any, are necessary.
(4) Nothing counterfactually depends on a necessary truth: if 7+5=12 couldn't have failed, no belief-forming process could have tracked its failing.
(5) So no explanation of the kind (2) demands is available for arithmetical belief.
(6) Hence no view on which arithmetic is true — platonist, structuralist, whatever — can deliver arithmetical knowledge.
(7) But we obviously know 7+5=12.
(8) So arithmetic isn't true, its a useful fiction.

Which step do I reject? I'd like to keep (7) and (1) if I can; I'm least attached to (2). Tell me why the bad step looked perfectly innocent when I wrote it down, and price the escape routes — keeping (2) but weakening it, denying (4), and going fictionalist. One more worry while you're in there: does (8) get anybody off the hook, given that the fictionalist still owes an account of why we're so reliable inside the fiction?

Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no wrap-up.
