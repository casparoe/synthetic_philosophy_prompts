I am co-teaching a graduate seminar on the thermodynamics of information, and a disagreement between two regular participants has now survived three meetings, several rounds of email, and a whiteboard covered in erasure protocols. Neither side thinks the other is being obtuse; they simply do not appear to be having the same conversation. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the dispute rather than a ruling on who is right.

Position A, held by a statistical mechanic: Szilard's insight is that one bit of information about a single-particle engine is worth kT ln 2 of work, and Brillouin's proposal that the measurement itself must dissipate is unnecessary, because measurement can in principle be performed reversibly. What cannot be avoided is the reset. A demon is a finite machine, so to begin a new cycle it must return its memory to a standard state, and any operation that merges previously distinct memory configurations is logically irreversible, which carries a minimum dissipation of kT ln 2 per bit. Gain and cost therefore cancel, and the second law is preserved by a general theorem about computation rather than by any claim about the demon's particular mechanism. On this view the problem is closed and has been for decades.

Position B, held by a philosopher of physics: the reset step is a red herring, imposed by a prior decision to make the demon cyclic. In the modern feedback-controlled formulation, the bound on extractable work is set by the mutual information between system and controller within a single cycle, and no erasure need occur inside that cycle; the Landauer–Bennett accounting is then a limiting case, recovered only when one insists on a machine that runs forever. More sharply, the claim that logical irreversibility entails a thermodynamic cost is not a theorem at all: every derivation of it seems to presuppose something like the second law, or the impossibility of a device that converts heat from a single bath entirely into work, so it is either circular or at best an empirical generalization about actual reset protocols. B adds that the cost can in principle be paid in a conserved quantity other than energy, and that a demon with a sufficiently capacious memory could run a very long time before needing any reset.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: A and B are answering different questions under a single heading. A is asking whether a finite machine can, in the long run, extract work from a single heat reservoir; B is asking what the information-work trade-off is in one feedback cycle, at the level of individual trajectories. If so, both can be right, and the appearance of contradiction is an artifact of the word resolution. But I suspect something substantive is buried underneath — a disagreement about what counts as a proof, namely whether a conceptual argument that a given computation is logically irreversible is sufficient, or whether one must exhibit a Hamiltonian, an initial ensemble, and a bound that holds either on average or trajectory by trajectory. I am also unsure whether a residual empirical question remains about whether the kT ln 2 figure is an average claim or a single-shot one.

So: diagnose the dispute. Is it verbal, substantive, a clash of evidential standards, two questions sharing a name, or evidence that asking whether the demon has been exorcised is itself ill-posed? Ground the diagnosis in what A and B actually assert, not in a survey of the field. Then say what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction, a theorem, an experiment, a reframing of the question — or argue that none should be expected and why. Finally, both sides seem to assume the demon's memory is finite and fixed in size: would replacing it with an unbounded, ever-growing record dissolve the disagreement, sharpen it, or reveal that the finiteness assumption was doing all the work?
