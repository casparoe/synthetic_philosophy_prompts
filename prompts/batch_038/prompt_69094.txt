I'm the operations director at a small regional seed bank in the Willamette Valley. We keep roughly eleven hundred heirloom and landrace lines in two walk-in freezers, and the entire collection fits into about four hundred packets the size of a deck of cards, which is either comforting or terrifying depending on the day. Last month our treasurer — a retired software engineer who does back-of-envelope analysis for pleasure — circulated a one-page memo to the board arguing that we should not spend $38,000 on a thermal buffer and a second generator. His method: he listed the four things that would all have to go wrong for the collection to be lost in a summer outage (grid power down for more than twelve hours; the standby generator failing to start; the temperature alarm failing to reach a human being; nobody at our partner farm or in our volunteer pool happening to notice within two days), assigned each a probability drawn from our maintenance logs and county outage records — one in twenty, one in eight, one in five, and one in six, as I recall — and multiplied them out to something like one summer in eight thousand. He added, in the same memo, that the reasoning justifies stretching the generator's service interval from quarterly to annually.

My objection, which so far I have only managed to make in the parking lot, is that I don't believe the four items on his list are four items. In August we had a heat dome. The grid sagged for thirty hours; the generator wouldn't turn over because its battery had cooked through a week of sitting idle in the same heat and the previous service call had been skipped for budget reasons; the alarm dialed out through a monitoring vendor that had lost its own connectivity in the same storm; and the only volunteer who lives within ten miles was out of state at a funeral. We were saved by a retired electrician who drove past the building because he was bored. Nobody has told me what to make of that week, and I don't have the vocabulary for the argument, but I keep returning to a dispute about who has to prove what. He says the burden falls on me to show that these failures travel together, and that until I do he is entitled to treat each link as standing on its own; I say he is the one making an unchecked assumption, and that the assumption is doing all the work in his number. I took one statistics course fifteen years ago and remember enough to know that multiplying is the right thing to do in some situations, which is precisely why I can't tell whether my discomfort is a real insight or the ordinary anxiety of someone who has read too many incident postmortems.

Please treat this as a question about reasoning rather than a request for reassurance. Write as someone whose professional life has concerned the foundations of probabilistic judgment — what entitles a person to adopt a default assumption, what would defeat it, and what a number assembled out of estimates can and cannot be asked to support — and think the problem through before answering. Start with a short brainstorming pass listing the distinct diagnoses of what might be wrong with the memo; there may be several, and they may not be compatible with one another. Then commit to the one or two you believe are actually at work here and explain why, in language a board of volunteers can follow without jargon. Address my who-proves-what question head on: is there a principled answer, or is his demand that I produce evidence of clustering simply the correct way to think about it? Finish by telling me what I should say at the meeting in three weeks, and what I should be asking for instead of the buffer, if anything. Two requests about your own confidence: give me a verdict rather than a balanced menu of considerations, and say explicitly where you are least sure — including whether my reading of the August incident is carrying more weight than it deserves. And push back if my framing is at fault: if the treasurer's arithmetic is defensible and I am pattern-matching a frightening story onto a sound argument, or if the question as I have posed it cannot be answered well and you would reformulate it, say so plainly instead of humoring me.
