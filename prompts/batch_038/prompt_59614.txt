In a seminar last term someone circulated the following argument against hedonism about well-being, and I have been unable to decide whether it is any good.

(1) For hedonism to be true, there must be a single property that every pleasant experience has and that is what makes having it good for you.
(2) Pleasant experiences have no such property in common: a cold drink on a hot day, a proof finally clicking, a passage of music, and pain easing share nothing comparable to what all red things share.
(3) So "pleasant" is a heterogeneous predicate, on the order of "game" — it picks out a family of loosely related cases, not a kind.
(4) A heterogeneous predicate cannot name an intrinsic value-maker; if the property that makes experiences good has to be the same property in each case, hedonism fails.
(5) And the failure generalizes: any candidate good-maker that is phenomenally multiply realizable is disqualified on the same grounds, so no unified account of well-being survives.
(6) The way forward is therefore to stop asking what pleasures have in common in experience and to analyze pleasantness dispositionally instead: an experience is pleasant just in case it is one that its subject, at the time, wants to continue.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Which step should be struck, and why did it look safe to assert? Please name the equivocations and gaps explicitly — in particular, tell me whether (2) to (4) slides between claims about phenomenal qualities and claims about properties in general, and whether (5) and (6) can both be held by the same person. Then price the alternatives: what does a hedonist pay for rejecting (2), for rejecting (4), and for rejecting (5) respectively, and what does the argument's author pay for adopting (6) as a rescue of the theory (6) was supposed to help bury? I am not attached to hedonism itself, but I would rather not accept (5) if it can be avoided.

Keep your answer under 400 words. Open with one sentence naming the step you would strike, then give the diagnosis, then set out the costs as a ranked list.
