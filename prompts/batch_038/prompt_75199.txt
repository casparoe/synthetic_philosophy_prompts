**Question (45 minutes, 900-word limit, 25 points):**

In *Science Without Numbers*, Field argues that mathematics is a useful fiction. Explain how this project is meant to answer the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument, and assess whether the fictionalist thereby escapes Benacerraf's epistemological problem about mathematical knowledge. In your answer, state explicitly what the fictionalist owes us by way of an account of mathematics' reliability.

**Student response:**

The Quine–Putnam indispensability argument has two premises: we ought to accept the ontological commitments of our best scientific theories, and our best scientific theories quantify over numbers, functions, and sets. Confirmation is holistic, so the mathematics inside a physical theory is confirmed together with the physics; Quine says mathematical entities are posits on a par with electrons, which is why the argument is empirical rather than a priori. The conclusion is that we ought to believe in numbers and sets.

Field attacks the second premise. In *Science Without Numbers* he reformulates Newtonian gravitational theory so that it can be stated without quantifying over numbers: instead of representing mass and distance by real numbers, he takes primitive comparative relations between points of spacetime together with a Hilbert-style axiomatization of spacetime geometry. Representation theorems then show that the nominalistic theory is a conservative extension of the standard numerical formulation. Because a theory that quantifies only over concrete things can be just as good scientifically, mathematics turns out to be dispensable and the Quinean has no reason to believe in abstracta. For Field, mathematical claims are not true at all; they are true within the fiction, like claims about Sherlock Holmes, and mathematics is worth keeping because it makes it easier to derive the consequences of nominalistic theories.

This also disposes of Benacerraf's problem. Benacerraf argued that abstract objects cannot stand in causal relations, so if numbers are abstract we cannot know anything about them; since we plainly do have mathematical knowledge, platonism is false. The fictionalist is untouched by this, because she does not claim to know any mathematical truths — there are none. She also escapes the reliability objection: on nominalism our beliefs are really about the physical world, so the causal link is restored, and the fiction we settle on is the one that has proved useful for prediction, so it is no accident that it works.

Field is in effect a structuralist. Benacerraf's *What Numbers Could Not Be* showed that numbers have no properties beyond their place in a structure, so the fictionalist simply treats the structure itself as a fiction. The indispensability argument therefore fails, and nominalism escapes the epistemological problem, the only cost left over being an explanation of why the fiction works as well as it does.
