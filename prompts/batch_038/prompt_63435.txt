I am drafting a chapter of my dissertation on the normative foundations of two-party bargaining, and before I commit to a formalism I should like to see what my own verdicts add up to. Each case below is a feasible set of payoff pairs together with a walk-away point.

(i) Walk-away 0 for both, frontier running from (10,0) to (0,10): five–five, no hesitation.

(ii) Walk-away 0 for both, A's ceiling 10 and B's ceiling 5, frontier bowed out between them: not five–five, and nothing like ten–nothing either. Each party ends up the same fraction of the way from the walk-away point to their own ceiling.

(iii) I can construct a case in which the feasible set expands so that only B's ceiling rises, A's untouched, and yet the rule that maximises the product of gains over the walk-away point leaves B no better off than before. That strikes me as a reductio of the rule rather than of my verdict about the case.

(iv) Deleting options that were never live — pairs worse for somebody than points we still have, on which no settlement would ever have landed — leaves the outcome exactly where it was.

(v) Deleting the single pair that constituted B's ceiling, while leaving everything in the neighbourhood of the actual settlement intact, moves the outcome against B: A's share rises.

(vi) Where the pair maximising B's payoff puts A below A's walk-away, I do not count it as B's ceiling at all. B's ceiling is the most B can get subject to A's still doing at least as well as walking away.

(vii) Nothing changes if we halve the units in which B's payoffs are reported, or if we swap the parties' names.

(iv) and (v) look flatly inconsistent to me: both delete pairs that are not the settlement, and I insist that one deletion is inert and the other decisive. (vi) may sit uneasily with (v) as well, since a ceiling is in general unattainable in combination with what the other party actually gets.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that delivers all of these verdicts? Please state each candidate precisely — as a rule, or an axiom set, on the pair (feasible set, walk-away point) — and say which of my cases motivates each clause. Address explicitly whether (iv) and (v) can be preserved together, and what the principle must look like if they can; if the honest answer is that one of them goes, say which, and why. Then tell me what your preferred principle says about two situations on which I have formed no view: a three-party negotiation, where neither the ceiling nor the proportional rule obviously generalises; and a case in which each party is badly mistaken about the other's ceiling. Finally, state which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and name any standard solution it turns out to coincide with. Please write in British English.
