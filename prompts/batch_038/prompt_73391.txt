**Question.** Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm" is standardly described as an attack on adaptationism. (a) State as precisely as you can what the "adaptationist programme" they object to consists in. (b) Explain the architectural analogy and say why they introduce a technical term rather than simply noting that some traits are not adaptations. (c) It is often said that the paper's real conclusion is that adaptive explanations are unfalsifiable. Is that a fair reading? Justify your answer.

**Candidate's script.**

(a) Adaptationism, as they describe it, is the view that natural selection is the only evolutionary mechanism that produces organic form, so that mutation, drift, developmental constraint and phylogenetic history have no genuine explanatory role. What they object to is a research habit rather than a single thesis. The biologist takes a trait, assumes it exists because it is optimally designed for the function it now performs, tells a story about how that function would have raised fitness in the ancestral environment, and treats the coherence of the story as sufficient evidence for it. When one story fails another is generated in its place, so the programme seals itself against failure. They also object to the decomposition of the organism into parts, each optimised separately, with the whole treated as the sum of those optima, and to the optimality argument, which infers from a trait's proximity to an optimum that selection produced it without asking what the alternatives were or what would have counted against the hypothesis. They are not anti-Darwinian: they accept selection as a powerful force and complain only about its being treated as the exclusive one.

(b) A spandrel is the roughly triangular space left between the arches that carry a dome. Because the arches and the base of the dome are settled first, the spaces follow necessarily: there are four of them, and their shape is fixed by the geometry rather than chosen by anyone. Only afterwards were they covered with mosaics, and the mosaics are so elaborate and purposeful that a visitor is tempted to treat them as the reason the building has the form it has. Gould and Lewontin's point is that the same reversal happens in biology: a trait that exists because of the architecture of the organism comes to be explained by the use it has since been put to. This is why they need a technical term. A spandrel, on their account, is any trait that is useful now but was not produced by selection for that use — feathers being their standard example, since feathers evolved for insulation and were only later co-opted for flight, with male nipples often offered as another. The term is stronger than saying that a trait is not an adaptation: it says the trait was forced by the form of the organism, so that once a trait has been shown to be a spandrel one has shown both that it is not adaptive and that it could not have been shaped by any subsequent selection, since it was never a target of selection to begin with.

(c) The unfalsifiability reading is fair; it is in fact the paper's argument. Gould and Lewontin apply Popper's criterion — a theory is scientific only if some observation would count against it — and note that adaptive stories can always be regenerated, so that no observation counts against adaptationism. Since nothing could refute it, adaptationism is not science, and the comparison with Pangloss is meant to place it alongside theology rather than biology. That is also why their list of alternatives — constraint, allometry, phylogenetic inertia, drift, the immediate architecture of the organism — is presented as a set of hypotheses: only if the alternatives are the sort of thing that can fail does the enterprise deserve the name of science.
