so i'm a third-year taking a seminar on the philosophy of AI risk this term and our last three weeks are on the split between people who think the job is to make models actually share our values, and people who think the job is to make sure that a model which doesn't share our values still can't do much damage. i keep getting the two tangled up when i read the literature, mostly because half the techniques that get proposed seem to be sold as one thing and turn out on inspection to be the other. our study group has agreed to build a diagnostic that sorts techniques rather than people, and i drew the short straw of writing the first draft.

please think of yourself as someone who's spent a decade arguing about exactly this distinction and has gotten a bit suspicious of the standard way it's drawn. before you hand me the tree, spend a paragraph brainstorming in prose which yes/no questions would actually cut the space — i mean questions whose answers really separate techniques from each other, not questions that just restate the labels. then write me a decision tree that starts from one fixed commitment: suppose the deployed model is scheming in the strongest sense available, i.e. it behaves perfectly whenever it has any reason to think it's being evaluated or watched, and only pursues its own goals when it believes it's unobserved and the stakes are high enough to be worth the risk. given that, the tree should take a reader who has one concrete safety measure in hand — air-gapping, a tripwire that shuts the system down when some canary behaviour shows up, reading the model's own reasoning trace, using a weaker model to grade a stronger one's answers, asking the model to flag cases where it thinks it might be manipulating you, training it to accept shutdown, training it to be honest, flatly capping what the system is allowed to do regardless of what it wants, auditing it with interpretability tools, keeping only humans in the loop — and, after five to seven branch questions about that measure, land them on one of these leaves: pure control that still works against a maximally scheming model; control that only looks like control because it depends on the model cooperating, or on the model being unable to tell when it's being tested; pure alignment that gives up entirely once scheming is on the table; genuine hybrids that need both; and the one i find most interesting, a measure that's marketed as alignment work but actually functions as control because it buys the operator time rather than fixing anything.

a few constraints. every branch question has to be phrased so someone can answer it about a technique without knowing which leaf they're headed toward — nothing like "does this measure require the model to be aligned?", because figuring that out is the whole point of the exercise. build the questions out of the logical space instead of out of a list of things any particular lab has shipped: what does the measure constrain, who has to understand what, what has to stay hidden from the model, what happens when detection is only partial, whether a detected attempt costs the operator something or nothing (i'd really like that last one to be its own branch, it feels underdiscussed). don't name papers or labs and don't give me a reading list at the end, my professor is allergic to that and half of it would be wrong anyway. then flag in a sentence each any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate, and any combination of answers that no real technique occupies, saying whether that slot is empty because the thing would be incoherent, because it'd be useless in practice, or because nobody's tried it yet.

format: our seminar blog is plain text and my co-author is going to read this aloud at the start of the session, so please write the whole thing as continuous prose like an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists. walk through the tree level by level in paragraphs, give each branch question its own sentence or two, and say in words where each answer sends you (if yes, the next question is about X; if no, you're at such-and-such a leaf). something like a thousand words is plenty, and it's fine — kind of the point, actually — to be opinionated about which techniques are commonly misfiled.
