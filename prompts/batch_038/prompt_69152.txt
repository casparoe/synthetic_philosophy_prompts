I am a professor of philosophy and, for the past four years, reviews editor of a mid-sized interdisciplinary journal publishing across philosophy of mind, philosophy of language, and the history of South Asian philosophy. We receive roughly 180 manuscripts a year and desk-reject about half; of those sent out, in something like a third of cases our two referees return verdicts that are not merely different in emphasis but flatly contradictory — one asserts the paper's central claim, the other denies it, and each treats the other's confidence as evidence of incompetence. My associate editors, who are not specialists in Indian philosophy and who between them carry most of these cases, currently resolve them by instinct: by averaging the reports, or by deferring to whichever is longer, or by asking the author to add a paragraph acknowledging the disagreement, which settles nothing. I have taught anekāntavāda for some years and have become convinced that the Jain apparatus of syādvāda and nayavāda is the most carefully worked-out theory we have of precisely this predicament — two assertions, each defensible within its own parameters, apparently incompatible, with a decision nevertheless required — and that it has been wasted on metaphysics seminars. I want it converted into something my associate editors can actually run.

So: write a decision procedure for adjudicating a pair of contradictory referee reports, grounded in that apparatus, that will fit on two pages of the editorial handbook. The constraints are these. The associate editor has twenty-five minutes per case; she has the manuscript, the two reports, and nothing else — no ability to consult specialist literature, and no third referee in the ordinary case; and because our editor-in-chief signs off on every rejection, each verdict the procedure generates must come with a written rationale that could survive being read by the author and by an appeals committee. The procedure should run its tests in a fixed order, and I would like it built around the following stages, in whatever formulation you think best: first, fix the object of each report's predication and write out the parameters along which that predication is implicitly restricted — as I understand the doctrine, roughly what the thing is, where, when, and in which of its states — together with a rule for what to do when a report cannot be parsed this way at all, and whether an unparseable report is thereby discredited or merely unusable; second, identify the standpoint each report speaks from, including the tradition's broad distinction between a standpoint oriented to what a thing is across its states and one oriented to the particular state it is in, and — this is what I take nayavāda to be really about — a diagnostic for detecting when a standpoint is being asserted as the whole account, which I suspect is what is happening in most of our contradictory pairs; third, an explicit criterion, not a vibe, for whether the two reports contradict in any single cell of the parameter grid or contradict only once the grid has been collapsed; fourth, for the cases where the parameters genuinely coincide, a way of selecting one report as better supported and saying so in words that can be handed to an author, plus instructions for the situation where they coincide and neither report can be adjudicated from the material on the desk; and fifth, a treatment of the residual, where the doctrine's inexpressible category is supposed to bite. On that last point I want three things kept apart that my graduate students habitually fuse: the case where a predicate fails to apply because the parameters were never fixed, the case where the relevant facts are simply not yet known to us, and the case the tradition is pointing at, where the aspects of the object cannot be gathered into a single assertion. If the third is invoked more than once in twenty cases, the procedure has become an instrument for not deciding, and it should say so about itself. Two further demands. Be honest about what you are discarding: my own view is that the full sevenfold enumeration is largely decorative for editorial purposes and that the work is done by the parameter grid and the standpoint taxonomy, so tell me which parts of the doctrine you are dropping and what is lost in dropping them — and tell me if I am simply wrong about this. And note that my students arrive each autumn with the parable of the blind men and the elephant, which does no work here at all; the procedure must not depend on any such illustration. Above all, the failure condition I care about is this: if your procedure could be substituted, verdict for verdict, by a memorandum reading "both referees have a point; ask the author to acknowledge the complexity," then it is not a procedure and I will not put it in the handbook. Somewhere inside it there must be steps whose sole function is to establish that one of our referees is mistaken.

Run it, in full and showing the intermediate results at each step, on three cases. (1) A philosophy-of-mind manuscript arguing that a certain phenomenal property is identical to a certain functional property: referee one recommends acceptance and says the identity is well argued though unsurprising; referee two recommends rejection and says the identity is false, citing the same two arguments referee one cites and reading them in the opposite direction. Neither report is longer than the other and neither is careless. (2) A crossover manuscript reconstructing the argument of a medieval Jain treatise on conditioned assertion in the apparatus of a contemporary many-valued semantics: referee one says the reconstruction is technically careful and renders the argument assessable for the first time; referee two says it is a misdescription, because the treatise's assertions are not offered as truth-conditional claims at all but as instruments of a soteriological discipline, so that formalizing them changes the subject matter. I want to see whether the substance/mode distinction does real work on this one, and whether the procedure can yield something better than a compromise. (3) The case that will irritate me: a manuscript by a former doctoral student of mine whose work I have defended in print, reported on by two scholars I trust, where on my own reading their parameters do coincide — the dispute is over whether a particular inference in the paper is valid, and one of them is right. Write this case so that the procedure reaches a verdict against my preference and do not soften the landing; and build in the fact that one report is three paragraphs long and, I believe, correct, while the other is eleven pages of meticulous and, I believe, mistaken exposition. If any step in your procedure tracks the length, learning, or courtesy of a report, delete it.

Finally, mark the limits. Identify the steps that are judgment wearing a procedural costume; the kinds of manuscript the procedure should not be applied to at all (I suspect short note-format submissions and pure exegesis); and the ways it would be misused by an editor who wanted a principled-sounding reason to avoid deciding. Then address the self-application problem head-on and give me your answer rather than a survey of possible answers: if the doctrine holds that every assertion about a complex object must itself carry the conditional prefix, does a verdict produced by this procedure retain the authority an editor needs in order to reject a scholar's paper, or does invoking anekāntavāda quietly undercut the decision I am trying to use it to make? Keep the whole thing to about two thousand words, in the register of a handbook rather than a journal article. Do not supply a bibliography, footnotes, or references to secondary literature — refer to positions, arguments, and terms as you find them and I will attach the citations myself, since I have the material on my desk. Assume your reader has a graduate-level general philosophical background and no Sanskrit, no Prakrit, and no prior exposure to Indian philosophy: every technical term you retain, in whatever language, must be glossed in one sentence within the procedure itself.
