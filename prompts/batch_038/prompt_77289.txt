I am a software engineer working on multi-agent systems. I have no formal training in philosophy or in game theory, so where I have misdescribed something, please correct the description rather than quietly working around it.

Over the past year I have become convinced of a position about cooperation between AI agents, arrived at mostly through writing on program equilibrium and on provability-based cooperation. I want to know what would count against it. So that this is answerable without any outside references, here is the position stated as explicitly as I can manage, together with the toy system I built to check it.

The position, in five claims:

P1. The familiar result that a one-shot prisoner's dilemma between self-interested agents ends in mutual defection rests on an assumption usually left implicit: that each agent must decide without access to the other's decision procedure. Once each agent is a program that receives the other program's source code as an input, that assumption fails, and mutual cooperation becomes available as an equilibrium of the resulting program game. I take this to be a genuine solution concept, not a redescription of iterated play.

P2. The mechanism can be made precise without trust assumptions. An agent that cooperates exactly when it can prove, in a fixed formal system, that its opponent cooperates with it, ends up in mutual cooperation with a like-minded opponent: the derivation goes through by Löb's theorem, and it terminates instead of requiring an infinite regress of each agent modeling the other. This is the part I care about most — cooperation is secured by proof rather than by prediction, reputation, or a shadow of the future.

P3. The standard objection, that determining what an arbitrary program does is undecidable and so no agent can reliably do what P2 requires, does not bite. What the agent needs is not a decision procedure for its opponent's behavior but a proof of one particular statement about it, and proofs are checkable. Where proof search is resource-bounded, cooperation degrades gradually rather than collapsing.

P4. My evidence for P3 is a prototype: roughly 250 lines, in which each agent is a pure function from a source-code string to an action, with no I/O, no randomness, and an explicit step budget, plus a small proof-search loop that enumerates derivations up to a depth limit in a fragment rich enough to express that a given agent returns cooperate against a given source string. The pattern I observe is that agents whose budgets are within a small factor of one another cooperate, while agents separated by a large budget gap both defect. My reading is that the low-budget agent cannot complete the derivation in time and falls back to defection, and the high-budget agent can prove that this is what will happen and defects accordingly. On that reading the result is a stable graded structure — cooperation tiers indexed by proof budget — rather than a pathology.

P5. The transparency requirement in P1 and P2 is a deployment problem, not a conceptual one. Two organizations can each run their agent in a deterministic sandbox, publish its source to a mutually accepted notary along with a signed hash of the exact binary, and feed each other's verified source in as input. With that in place the mechanism could actually be used to coordinate real systems.

Given all of that, what would count against the position?

3. Assess P4 directly. Are the tiers stable or exploitable? Can an agent profit by misreporting or inflating its budget, or by cooperating only inside a clique? Do tiers collapse, proliferate, or drift as I add more agents with intermediate budgets? Say what I would have to observe in the sandbox to give up P3 and P4 specifically.
4. Address heterogeneous proof systems. What happens when two agents reason in different formal systems, or when neither can verify the soundness of the other's? Does P2 then shrink into a claim about agents that happen to share an epistemic framework, and if so, is that still enough to call it a solution to the cooperation problem rather than a description of a special case?
5. Say how much of the position survives generalization: more than two agents, more than two actions, repeated interaction, and agents whose decision procedures change during play, so that the source inspected at the start is not the source that acts at the end. For each, state whether the mechanism extends, requires new assumptions, or breaks.
6. Consider the notary in P5. Does it smuggle in a trusted third party who could instead have mediated the outcome directly, and if so, does my account collapse into a commitment-device or correlated-equilibrium story with extra machinery?
7. Tell me whether the position risks anything at all. I notice that I have already added a trusted notary, a deterministic sandbox, a restricted language, and an explicit fuel counter; has each claim been hedged into safety, and is the residual claim still interesting? I would also like your view on what standard of falsifiability is appropriate here, since P2 and P3 look like mathematical claims while P4 and P5 look like empirical and design claims.

Say what this view predicts that its rivals do not — reputation-based and iterated accounts, symmetry-based superrationality arguments, explicit contract-signing or commitment mechanisms, mediator-based correlated equilibria — and describe one discriminating experiment I could actually run in the sandbox.
