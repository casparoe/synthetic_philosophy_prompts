ok so I need a gut check on something and you're about the only patient interlocutor available to me right now.

six weeks ago I got put on the board of a coalition org — a handful of nonprofits sharing one building, one grant line, and a long grudge ledger. My unofficial job is brokering deals between them and I've been thinking about it way too much. I actually built a spreadsheet where I score each proposed deal: for each party, roughly how much of what they care about the deal gets satisfied minus how much of it gets blocked, and I only bring forward deals where everyone scores positive and the total goes up. I've been calling it value-credit accounting, no idea if thats a real term.

the deal that broke my confidence: one group wants a logistics warehouse on the riverfront (jobs, tax base, a win they can take to their funders), another wants the wetlands there permanently protected. The compromise on the table is the warehouse on the upland parcel plus a permanent conservation easement on the wetland, partly paid for by the developer. Sounds clean. Except the warehouse people have said, quite calmly, that if they don't get it they'll walk and spend the next four years opposing everything else the coalition touches. And I believe them.

here's where I've landed, and I want to know whether it hangs together:

1. Moral compromise is basically trade. Two parties with different values can each end up with more of what they care about than under no-deal, and that's the entire justification for compromising at all.
2. The right deal is the one that maximizes total value satisfied across the parties, subject to everyone scoring positive. I score one deal at a time; I don't try to score how a deal changes the next round.
3. I should never be a party to a deal that helps bring about something I think is a serious wrong, no matter how good the rest of the package is.
4. A compromise is legitimate only if both sides could reasonably accept it — but I don't think "split it down the middle" is the right rule.
5. How much of the surplus each side gets shouldn't depend on how stubborn they are. Nobody should get more just because their more willing to blow the whole thing up.
6. Compromising doesn't require me to change what I value. I keep my values, I just don't get all of them.

which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? rank the tensions rather than listing them all as equally bad. for each real one, tell me what has to give and what giving it costs. and don't assume I'd rather patch the spreadsheet than the principle behind it — I genuinely don't know which one I'd sacrifice.

two specific things I want an answer on. first: does rule 2 survive contact with the warehouse threat, or does my one-deal-at-a-time scoring hand me a verdict I'd actually reject if I looked two rounds ahead? second: is 5 a real structural problem with modeling compromise as trade at all, or is it an artifact of the way I score? my worry is that the method systematically pays a premium to whoever is most willing to walk, and I can't tell whether that's a bug I could score around or the whole idea.

and please flag anything that only looks like a conflict. I'd rather be told that two of these are fine together than have everything on the list declared guilty.
