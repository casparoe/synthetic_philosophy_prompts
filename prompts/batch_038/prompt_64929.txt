I have no academic training in this. For nine years I have sat on a small town committee that divides a fixed annual pot between programs — shelter beds, a youth clinic, road work that mostly benefits commuters — and our meetings run almost entirely on anecdotes. I have come to think the real disagreement is about a rule for combining what different people gain and lose into a single ordering, and that this is a formal subject with a theorem at its center, roughly: if each person's preferences over gambles can be represented by expected utility, and the group's can too, and the group never distinguishes between two gambles that every person is indifferent between, then the group's utility must be a weighted sum of the individuals', with positive weights. That is my recollection of Harsanyi's result and I may have it slightly wrong; correct me where I have.

What I want first is the specification. What must any rule of this kind deliver to count as adequate — adequate, that is, for a body that has to produce an ordering and defend it to the people on the losing end of it? Give me the requirements, each with the situation that motivates it, since a requirement with no case behind it is only a taste. Mark which are non-negotiable and which are the commitments of one camp.

The situations I keep running into:

1. Two members describe the same improvement as a seven and a four. Must the ordering be invariant to how each person's scale is anchored and stretched? If so, what has that assumed about comparing one person's level to another's — and does the theorem establish that comparability or quietly presuppose it?

2. A coin flip everyone prefers in advance, which afterward leaves one household much worse off and another much better off. Must the group's ranking of the gamble track everyone's advance ranking, and must it also track the sum of what people actually end up with? Can both be demanded at once?

3. A proposal that gives four hundred households a small benefit and ruins one. Must the rule always return a verdict, or may it declare two options incomparable — and if the latter, is that candor or abdication?

4. A proposal that raises the total by drawing in new residents whose lives are worth living but sit well below the current level. Is a fixed population part of the brief, or must an adequate rule handle this too?

5. A longtime shelter resident who reports being content, and a man whose satisfaction depends on other people's setbacks. Does the rule take gains as reported, or adjust them? If it adjusts, by what standard, and does the adjustment smuggle back the very comparisons it was meant to replace?

6. What makes the weights equal rather than merely positive? Is equal weighting a consequence of the setup or a further commitment, and exactly what must be added to get it?

7. May the rule notice that a gain arrived through an injustice, or must it be blind to everything except levels of well-being?

Then tell me which subsets of your own requirements cannot all be satisfied together. I want to know whether this is a shopping list or an impossibility result wearing a shopping list's clothes; if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say which condition should be dropped and what dropping it costs. Then name the single requirement that knocks out the most candidates. Finally, briefly — a short paragraph each, no more — how three contenders fare against the list: equal-weight summation of realized well-being; a rule that weights gains to the worse-off more heavily; and the rule that identifies the group's ordering with the ordering of the equal-chance gamble over being each person. No references and no reading list: I cannot look anything up, and I would rather have nothing than a guess at a source.
