question (10 pts): describe Ellsberg's one-urn case, and explain what you think it shows about expected utility theory.

answer:
"There's an urn with 90 balls in it. 30 are red and the other 60 are black and yellow, but nobody tells you how those 60 split between the two colors. You get offered two bets. In the first one you win $100 if the ball drawn is red, and in the second you win $100 if its black. Most people take the red bet, because thats the safer one — people are risk averse, so they don't want to gamble on a color they can't put odds on. Then you get offered two more bets: you win $100 if the ball is red or yellow, or you win $100 if the ball is black or yellow. Now most people switch and take black-or-yellow.

Thats the paradox. The two choices contradict each other, because if you preferred red over black you should also prefer red-or-yellow over black-or-yellow, which is basically Savage's sure-thing principle that expected utility is built on.

Also you can make a Dutch book against someone who picks this way. A bookie who knows your preferences just sells you both of the bets you picked, charges you a little more than what the two of them are worth to you, and is guaranteed to take your money no matter which color comes out of the urn. And since any set of preferences you can Dutch book like that is incoherent, ambiguity aversion isn't a defensible position, its just a bias.

So basically Ellsberg shows expected utility theory is wrong, both about how people actually choose and about how they should choose."
