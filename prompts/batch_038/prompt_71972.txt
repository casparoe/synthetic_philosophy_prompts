I am in the third week of an undergraduate seminar on long-run economic growth, taught jointly by an economic historian and a visiting fellow who works on the economics of automation, and our assigned task for the end of term is a short essay auditing one of the historical comparisons that keep circulating in policy discussion. The one I have been handed, and the one I want to examine, is the Industrial Revolution comparison as it is currently deployed in debates about advanced AI systems. The visiting fellow made the argument at length last Thursday, in roughly this form: measured productivity gains from a general-purpose technology always lag its arrival by decades, so the fact that today's national accounts show only modest effects of AI tells us nothing; and, more strongly, the distributional damage during the transition is not an accident but a phase, because in Britain there was a stretch — what economic historians call Engels' pause, roughly 1790 to 1840 — in which output per head climbed briskly while working-class real wages sat almost flat, and yet the whole thing resolved itself: by the second half of the nineteenth century wages were rising faster than output. From this he concluded that redistributive or labour-protective intervention now is premature, that it would amount to interfering with a process whose endpoint is a broad-based recovery, and that the honest position is patience: expect one or two uncomfortable generations, then convergence. Several of us were unpersuaded but could not articulate why beyond a general feeling that the comparison was doing far more work than it could bear.

What I need is a proper audit rather than a general deflation of the analogy. Please start with the mapping, explicitly and slot by slot: what in the present case is supposed to correspond to steam and cotton textiles as the leading technologies, to the factory system as the new mode of organisation, to the agricultural surplus labour that kept the urban wage down, to the statistical lag in measured productivity, to the take-off in real wages after 1840, and — this is the slot I suspect he never filled — to whatever institutions actually delivered that take-off, such as the expansion of the franchise, mass elementary schooling, trade union legalisation, public health reform, and the repeal of the Corn Laws. Then give me the disanalogies, but sorted into two piles rather than listed indiscriminately: those that genuinely undercut the argument as he used it, and those that are merely interesting and would only waste a paragraph of my essay. Among the ones that matter, I especially want pressure put on three questions. First, whether the British recovery is properly attributed to the technology at all, or to contingent political settlement, so that the analogy may in fact license the opposite conclusion about intervention. Second, whether a technology whose early phase is predominantly labour-replacing can be assumed to become labour-augmenting on the same schedule, and what would have to be true for that assumption to hold. Third, whether the source case had features the target lacks — a frontier of new tasks and new occupations to absorb displaced workers, a fixed and slowly accumulating stock of complementary capital, a diffusion speed measured in human lifetimes — and whether any of those features can be taken for granted now.

Finish with a verdict stated plainly: does the patience-and-convergence conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, does it survive in weakened form, or does the audited analogy actually support a different policy posture than the one it was recruited for? If you think the comparison fails for this use, say whether there is a better historical source for the same job, and what it would and would not license. I would like the correspondences as a compact two-column table and the rest as prose, and the whole thing under 900 words including the table. Please do not include references or a reading list — I am not being assessed on sourcing, and I want your reasoning laid bare rather than gestures toward literature I would have to go and chase. Where you think my seminar leader has a fair point, say so; I would rather hand in an essay that concedes what deserves conceding than one that wins cheaply.
