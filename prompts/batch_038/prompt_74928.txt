I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and in a few weeks I'm giving the tribute at the retirement dinner of a columnist I have read since I was a teenager. For nearly forty years he ran a weekly feature in a mid-sized daily that put extraordinary claims on trial: Atlantis, ancient astronauts, the Russian school that relocates Greece, Rome and Egypt into the Middle Ages, the healing-device trade. His house rule was the reason anyone read him — he would not take up a claim until its defenders said what would count against it, and then he printed their answer, or noted, flatly, that they had declined to give one. In his final column he disowned the word he had spent his career deploying: pseudoscience. He argued it had never been doing epistemic work, only social work — an insult wearing a criterion's clothes — and that everything worth saying about a claim can be said instead about the reliability of the practices behind it. I think he was right, and he has asked for one thing in exchange for letting me speak: that the tribute not use that word, and not smuggle in a substitute such as fringe, quackery, not real science, or unfalsifiable doing the same job under a new label. Write the speech. There will be about two hundred people in the room: working scientists and former editors, a good number of whom think he went soft at the end and want the old certainties celebrated; three or four people whose projects he dismantled, attending in good faith; and his family, including a daughter who teaches fourth grade and has never read a line of his. Five minutes, warm and particular rather than generic, and it must make his late reversal read as the completion of his life's work rather than a defection from it — without arguing the case, since nobody wants a seminar over chicken. One of the five movements should speak directly to the people whose claims he dismantled. Give me exactly five numbered movements, in delivery order, each a single paragraph I could read aloud as written, with no preamble, no stage directions, and no closing note on your choices.
