You are a professional philosopher who works on social ontology and on the metaphysics of explanation, and who is additionally used to making this material legible to philosophically literate amateurs. Please think carefully, and where a step is subtle, work through it twice. I would rather have a slower, committed answer than a well-organised survey of the options.

**Context.** I have spent the better part of two decades reading the rationalist blogs and their comment sections, so I am fluent in the usual apparatus — maps and territories, carving the world at its joints, the difference between explaining something and explaining it away — but I have no specialist training, and I have never managed to make the literature on social kinds feel as though it were answering the question I actually have. A friend of mine, considerably sharper than I am and equally untrained, has been running an argument at me for several weeks. It starts from two cases I happily accept and finishes somewhere I cannot follow, and my strong suspicion is that it proves far too much. Here it is as I have reconstructed it; treat the reconstruction as my responsibility rather than his.

(1) A kind K is socially constructed when whether a thing falls under K is settled by the collective attitudes, conventions and practices of some community: absent those attitudes, nothing would fall under K, however the world otherwise stood.
(2) Money is socially constructed in this sense. The twenty-pound note in my wallet is legal tender only because we take it to be; withdraw the practice and what is left is a scrap of polymer with ink on it.
(3) Race is socially constructed in the same sense. Which bodily differences sort people into which races has varied across societies and centuries, and there is no attitude-independent answer to the question of which race a given person belongs to.
(4) If a kind's extension is settled by our attitudes, then no fact about that extension holds independently of us; so the kind carves no joint in nature. It projects our interests onto the world rather than recovering a structure already in it.
(5) A kind that carves no joint in nature is not a real kind. It is a façon de parler, a fiction we happen to find convenient.
(6) Therefore neither money nor race is real.
(7) Only real kinds can figure in genuine explanations, since to explain is to cite something that makes a difference to what happens, and whatever is nothing over and above our attitudes can make no difference to anything.
(8) So sentences about money or race are not truth-apt reports of how things stand with the world; at most they record the practices of the community that produced them.
(9) The cost is not local. The same reasoning disposes of chairs and hammers, promises and contracts, legal persons and limited companies, borders and time zones, the score in a cricket match, and the diagnostic categories of medicine. None of them is real; all are fictions.
(10) Nor can the reasoning be stated. Premises (1) to (9) are themselves claims about our classificatory practices, so by (8) they are not truth-apt either, and the position cannot be asserted.
(11) Hence the idea of social construction is either absurd or unsayable. Either way there is no such thing as social construction in the sense that ever mattered, and the disputes about money and about race are verbal.

**Why I cannot leave it there.** My first instinct is that this is the familiar reductionist sleight of hand, in which showing that X is nothing over and above our attitudes is taken to show that X is not there — the same move that is supposed to abolish hammers on the grounds that a hammer is only atoms arranged hammerwise. But my friend has a reply to that, and the reply troubles me more than the argument does: the hammer case is disanalogous, because nothing about hammerhood turns on our attitudes (a hammer would shatter glass just as well on a lifeless planet), whereas everything about race and money does turn on them. So the objection that rescues hammers has no purchase here. Tell me whether that reply is as strong as it feels to me.

If I am forced to choose, I would rather keep (2) and (3), together with the ordinary explanatory practice they license — I do think one can explain a bank run by citing what people believed about the notes in their pockets, and I do think one can explain a pattern of hiring decisions by citing how applicants are classified — than keep (5). But I want to be told plainly what that costs, and I want to be told just as plainly if I am only protecting a conclusion I find congenial. One further suspicion: (7) looks load-bearing, and there is a line of response that turns on classification changing what it classifies, whereby a category acquires causal effects it did not have before anyone was sorted into it, because people and institutions adjust once the sorting exists. Say whether that rescues (7) or merely relocates the difficulty.

**What I want from you.**

(a) Before you commit to anything, brainstorm. List every step at which the argument might be failing, and for each, name the kind of failure it would be: an equivocation between two readings of a term (say which two readings), a shift in the scope or ordering of a conditional or quantifier, a slide in the counterfactuals, a step that is valid only on a reading the neighbouring premises do not support, or no failure at all — in which case the argument is valid and some plausible premise must simply be surrendered. Rank these by how much explanatory work the diagnosis does.

(b) Then commit. Which single step do you reject, why did it look innocent from the inside, and which reading of which term was doing the work?

(c) Price the alternatives. What do I lose by giving up (4), by giving up (5), by giving up (7)? Which loss is cheapest, and is the cheapest one cheap in a way I should distrust?

(d) Take (10) on its own. Is the self-application a genuine self-defeat, or does it depend on treating claims *about* kinds as though they were themselves among the kinds, or on letting a principle about subject matter govern the theory rather than its object? I want a clear verdict, not a both-sides.

(e) Premise (9) is where the argument begins to look absurd to me. Does it follow, or have I bundled together cases that are constructed in importantly different ways? If the latter, give me the distinctions, and say which of my examples survive them and which fall.

(f) Repair. State the strongest version of my friend's argument that survives your diagnosis, and say what it concludes. I suspect something of his point survives, and I would like to know what.

(g) Set out the positions actually available: the one on which institutional facts are mind-dependent yet perfectly real (roughly Searle's picture), the one on which a kind earns its reality by doing explanatory work, the one on which asking whether race is real is a badly posed question that should be replaced by a question about what we want the concept to do, and outright eliminativism about the kinds at issue. For each, say what it must pay, and specifically what it must say to the disanalogy about hammers.

(h) Point out anywhere my reconstruction has mis-stated a step so badly that the argument becomes trivially invalid, and restate the step in the form he probably meant.

(i) Finally: I have taken (2) and (3) for granted. Is either false as stated? Premise (3) in particular asserts that there is no attitude-independent answer about race, and I am not sure my friend is entitled to it rather than merely assuming it.
