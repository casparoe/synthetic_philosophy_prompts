Teaching Keynes's 1930 essay on our grandchildren's economic prospects next week — the one where scarcity gets licked and we're left with the real problem of what to do with a fifteen-hour working week. Two of my grad students have taken to ending every discussion of it with Keynes's "in the long run we are all dead," as though that settles that the essay is unserious. One of them has an actual argument: if we'll all be dead, the good life Keynes sketches can't be a good *for* anyone we care about, so his "we" is doing all the equivocation work. I'm pretty sure that's a muddle and that the line comes from somewhere else entirely, aimed at a different target, but I don't want to just assert that in seminar.

So give me the menu of precise claims that one sentence could be making: the bare mortality platitude, something about the reliability of long-range forecasting, something about what long-run equilibrium theorizing is and isn't good for, something about whose welfare counts or how heavily the far future should be weighted, something about deferral and purposiveness — whatever else is genuinely in there. For each reading, say whether it's trivial, substantive, or false, and whether its actually Keynes's. Then commit rather than hedge: which reading, if any, earns my student's dismissal of the essay, and does the "we" charge survive once you fix the scope and tense of the sentence? I want a verdict I can defend, not a survey.
