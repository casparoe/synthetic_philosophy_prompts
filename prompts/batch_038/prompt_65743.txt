ok so I run a small newsletter on the history of economic thought and I want to commission a piece of satire for an issue on uncertainty. heres the idea:

Write a fake app-store product page — call it 700-800 words — for a decision-support app built by a stubborn disciple of the Keynes of the Treatise on Probability and of chapter 12 of the General Theory. The running joke is that the app refuses to give you a single number, and its not being difficult for its own sake: every refusal is a faithful implementation of something Keynes actually held.

Give it the usual furniture: app name and developer, the promotional blurb, a bulleted features list, a "known issues and limitations" section written by the developer in the same deadpan register, five or six user reviews with star ratings, and at least two developer replies to bad reviews.

The comedy lives in getting the doctrine right, so please hit these:

- probability as an objective logical relation between a conclusion and the body of evidence for it, not a state of mind — hence relative to what you know, and it doesn't get more precise because you want it to
- that many of these relations aren't numerically measurable at all, and that some pairs of options can't even be ordered against each other. The app should be willing to output "these are not comparable" and then stop talking.
- weight of evidence as a separate dimension from probability, so a feature that shows the case getting substantially weightier while the verdict stays exactly as vague as it was is worth a lot of screen time to this developer
- something about the principle of indifference, i.e. why the app refuses to just split the difference between outcomes it knows nothing whatsoever about
- the chapter 12 material: a meter that warns the user when they've stopped picking the prettiest face and started picking what average opinion expects average opinion to expect; the difference between enterprise and speculation; a big action button driven by spontaneous optimism rather than a weighted average of prospective benefits; and the developer's brisk attitude toward very long forecasting horizons
- the star ratings are imposed by the store and the developer considers them a category error. Let that friction show.

Some reviews should come from people who know precisely what they're objecting to: an actuary, a forecasting-tournament type whose entire self-image is calibration, a behavioral economist. One reviewer keeps citing a brilliant young Cambridge mathematician who thought you could get degrees of belief out of dispositions to bet, and the developer's reply to that one should be visibly pained. And one review should be from an ordinary person who only wanted help choosing between two schools for their kid — make that the funniest and the most deflating thing on the page.

Two guardrails. Don't put anything in quotation marks as if it were a line from Keynes's actual books; paraphrase instead, I don't want invented quotes drifting around. And keep this Keynes, not the risk-versus-uncertainty slogan everybody reaches for from the Chicago side — the developer should be irritated about being tagged with it, since its a different book making a different argument.

The target isn't Keynes and isn't any real person: it's the demand that everything be rendered as one number, and, to a lesser extent, the app itself. A reader should finish unable to say whether the product is broken or whether the industry that wants numbers out of it is. No winking, no explaining the joke.
