I am in the final term of a decision theory seminar, and for the closing session each of us leads a short exercise. Mine is on superrationality. The reading is the *Metamagical Themas* essay in which Hofstadter argues that two agents in a symmetric prisoner's dilemma who each know the other reasons about the game in the same way they do will arrive at the same answer, so the only live possibilities are mutual cooperation and mutual defection, and cooperation wins. Everyone in my seminar can reproduce that argument flawlessly. What nobody can do — I have tried this on three of them — is say what they would give up if the symmetry were loosened. So instead of another summary of the argument, I want to build a diagnostic decision tree that sorts people by where their superrationalist commitments actually break.

The tree should open from a single shared starting commitment, stated as something a member of the seminar could sincerely endorse: in a two-player prisoner's dilemma where each player knows the other is reasoning about the game in the same way they are, and where this knowledge is mutual, cooperating is correct. From there I want between ten and fourteen questions, each phrased as a concrete case that someone could answer without knowing which position their answer leads toward. No question may contain the name of a view, and none may be answerable merely by recalling what a well-known author said. The branch labels should state the claim doing the splitting: whether the correlation between my move and my counterpart's is something I must treat as evidence only, whether it depends on the payoff matrices being identical or merely on the reasoning being identical, whether it requires certainty or tolerates a probability below one, whether it requires mutual knowledge or only the bare fact of sameness.

To keep the tree honest, please route each of the following six cases, which I invented for the handout, and state at which node each one turns and which way it sends you.

1. Split seed. My counterpart runs my exact reasoning procedure, but every stochastic component of it — coin flips, tie-breaks, the arbitrary point at which a deliberation stops — is re-rolled independently on their side. Nothing else differs.

2. Asymmetric mirror. The two agents reason identically, but the game does not treat them identically: if I cooperate I lose three and they gain ten; if they cooperate they lose eight and I gain four. Mutual cooperation still beats mutual defection for both of us, though by different margins.

3. Glass skull. I have read my counterpart's full specification and can verify that it implements my reasoning exactly. They have read only a two-paragraph summary of mine, accurate but far from complete, and they know that is all they have.

4. Oracle and coin. My counterpart performs no deliberation at all. It is a machine that plays whatever a highly accurate predictor of me predicts I will play, and its own payoff is zero in every cell.

5. Self-edit. Before play I am offered a modification that removes one clause from my reasoning. I am certain that after taking it I will defect, and I am certain my counterpart has already taken the same modification.

6. The nine hundred and ninety-nine. A thousand agents face the game. Nine hundred and ninety-nine of them reason as I do; one does not, and everyone knows that this one defects.

The leaves should be positions rather than people: superrationality as Hofstadter framed it; the view that the correlation is purely evidential and that cooperation follows from ordinary expected-value reasoning on that evidence; the view that such correlations are beside the point because an act must be assessed by what it brings about, so defection stands; the family of views on which agents like these deliberate as a collective rather than one by one, so that cooperation is simply what the collective selects; the universalizability-based routes to cooperation that never mention correlation at all; and whatever the more recent approaches that treat decisions as outputs of shared computational processes should be called. Each leaf gets one sentence naming its most uncomfortable commitment — the thing its defender would rather not be asked about.

Two further pieces of work matter to me more than the tree itself. First, identify every pair of leaves my questions cannot actually separate: positions that give the same answer at every node but are supposed to be different views. For each such pair, say whether some further question would split them or whether the distinction is not doing real work. Second, map the unoccupied region: give me at least three logically available combinations of answers that lead to no leaf, and for each say whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because nobody has looked. I have a hunch that one such region sits around agents who are certain they reason alike but doubt that the game itself is common knowledge between them — case 3 is my attempt to probe it — and I would like to be told plainly whether that hunch picks out a real position or a confusion.

Please do not include references, reading suggestions, or attributions beyond those I have already given. Everyone in the room has done the reading, and what I need is the instrument itself: nodes, branch labels, the routing of my six cases, leaves, and the gaps between them.
