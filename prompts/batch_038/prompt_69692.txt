A friend who does behavioral econ sent me this blurb about the Allais paradox, and I want to know how much of it survives contact with someone who actually works on decision theory:

"The setup: A pays you a million dollars for sure, B gives you a 10% shot at five million, an 89% shot at a million, and a 1% shot at nothing. Then C is an 11% shot at a million, and D is a 10% shot at five million. Most people take A and then take D. That's just self-contradictory: it breaks the independence axiom, which is really only the claim that irrelevant alternatives shouldn't change your ranking. Savage then built his sure-thing principle partly to answer Allais. And since no utility function whatsoever can generate those two choices, the person isn't merely a non-expected-utility agent, they're incoherent — hand them the same choice twice in sequence and a bookie can bleed them dry, guaranteed."

I'm fine with the numbers and I do think most people take A and D. What I'm suspicious of is the gloss on independence, the ordering of who was answering whom, and above all the bookie bit. Go through it piece by piece: what would a defender of expected utility accept, what's a real misrepresentation, and is the Dutch book claim doing any actual work here or is it decoration?
