The question, worth fifteen per cent of the module, was as follows: State the St. Petersburg game and explain why it has been thought paradoxical. Then set out Bernoulli's resolution and assess it — what exactly does the move to utility accomplish, and does it dispose of the paradox? The word limit was 500 words. This is candidate 204's answer in full.

The game is simple. A fair coin is tossed until heads first appears. If heads comes on the first toss the player wins two ducats, on the second toss four, on the third eight, and in general two to the power n ducats if heads first appears on the nth toss. Since each of these outcomes has probability two to the power minus n, every term in the expectation contributes exactly one ducat, and there are infinitely many terms, so the expected value of the ticket is infinite. Expected-value reasoning therefore says that a rational agent should pay any finite price to play, and should go on paying more for as long as the game is offered. Yet no one would pay even twenty-five ducats, let alone a thousand. That gap between the arithmetic and ordinary judgment is the paradox; the name comes from Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 paper in the Commentaries of the St. Petersburg Academy, though the problem had been put to Montmort by his cousin Nicolaus a quarter of a century earlier. Bernoulli's own way of putting the point was that the standard calculation shows the expectation to be infinitely great, and yet any fairly reasonable man would sell his chance with great pleasure for twenty ducats.

Bernoulli's move was to stop asking what the game pays on average and to ask what it is worth. Money, he held, is not valued in proportion to its quantity: a gain of a thousand ducats means more to a pauper than to a rich man. So what enters the expectation should be utility rather than money, and utility grows more slowly than the money that produces it. With the logarithm as the utility of total wealth, the expected utility of the ticket becomes the sum of two to the power minus n times the log of two to the power n, which is the sum of n log 2 over two to the power n, and that converges. On natural-log utility an agent worth a million should pay about twenty-one to play, one worth a thousand about eleven, and one worth two ducats should borrow a little and pay three and a half. Willingness to pay thus becomes a function of the player's circumstances rather than a property of the game, which is exactly what the paradox seemed to deny. Cramer had proposed the square root instead a decade earlier, applying it to the gain rather than to total wealth, and the difference matters: on log utility the value of the ticket depends on what you already have, which is why the pauper and the millionaire disagree about it.

The reason the series converges is that the logarithm is bounded, and this is the real content of the resolution. Any bounded utility function makes every lottery finite, and any unbounded one leaves some lottery divergent; the moral is that risk aversion, meaning concavity, is all that is required, and once it is granted the paradox cannot arise. Menger made the same point from the other direction, showing that for any unbounded utility function one can build a variant game with faster-growing payoffs whose expected utility still diverges, so the utility move cannot be a general solution unless utility is capped. Samuelson's reply is the practical one: no house has infinite reserves, so the true payoffs are capped at whatever the casino can actually pay, the expected value of any real game is finite, and utility theory was never needed to explain why the ticket is cheap. On that reading the paradox is a mathematical artifact of an idealization. It should also settle the worry people have about infinite values elsewhere in practical reasoning — Pascal's wager, and the arguments from the astronomical size of the far future — since those too rest on payoffs that no finite world can deliver.
