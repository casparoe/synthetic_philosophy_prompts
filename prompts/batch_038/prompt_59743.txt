ok so I clerk for a state sentencing review board, and every few months we get handed a weird one and we're supposed to reason from our own prior decisions rather than from anybody's philosophy book. There are two camps on the board — the ones who think you can keep some trimmed-down notion of "he deserves to suffer for that" and use it, and the ones who think that notion should be thrown out entirely and replaced with quarantining dangerous people and repairing harm — and they've been voting case by case for about a decade. Here's the record I pulled, roughly in order:

1. A man spent 21 years nursing a grudge against a former business partner, wrote out detailed plans to kill him, told two people he'd do it eventually, bought a gun. He never got near the guy, never took a step toward actually doing it, and was arrested on an unrelated charge. Board: no offense, nothing owed, release. One member dissented saying the board was treating luck as though it were virtue.

2. A 19-year-old stole about $300 of goods from a hardware store. She had stable housing, a job offer, and a legal way to earn the money. Board: full sentence, and specifically noted in the reasoning that she had her chance to get it fairly and took the shortcut instead.

3. A man did 19 years for a rape he didn't commit, then DNA cleared him. The state argued he'd almost certainly have committed something else eventually, so the time wasn't wasted. Board: rejected that flatly, wrote that being the sort of person who would do a thing doesn't put you in debt to anybody until you do it.

4. Two executives ran the same fraud for the same number of years. One is worth $40 million, one has nothing. Both got five years. The poor one's sentence destroyed his family and cost him everything; the rich one's cost him a board seat. Asked to shorten the rich man's term so the two of them actually suffer comparably, the board refused: what's owed turns on what the state does to you, not on how it lands.

5. An armed robber, six years in, credibly turned around — no threats, ran programs, mentors younger inmates, zero risk assessments coming back hot. Board: suspended the rest of the sentence, and wrote that the original sentence was fair but that continuing it against the man he'd become would be pointless.

6. Someone caused serious harm while asleep, first recorded episode, nothing in his history. Board: nothing owed to the victim, no state claim, no registration, case closed. "The harm was real," the write-up says, "and it still doesn't put him in debt."

Here's the new one, and I have to draft the memo before Thursday.

A man committed an aggravated assault at 17. He's now 43 and has served 8 years of a 20-year term. Everything about him since has been exemplary — he runs a peer program inside, the victim has publicly forgiven him, no disciplinary record at all, every risk instrument says he won't offend. Then last month his lawyer surfaced something new: at the time of the offense he'd been on a prescription drug for about eight months, a class we now know can cause severe agitation and impulse-control failure in adolescents. Nobody caught it, nobody flagged it, and it's in his records from day one. He's been off it for 26 years.

And here's the twist that has the board split down the middle. He's written to us asking to stay. He says he doesn't think he's paid for what he did, he wants to serve the remaining 12 years, and he's refused parole hearings twice. The victim's family supports him and has filed in favor.

So, in American English, write me the memo:

(a) the candidate rules the board could be running that make all six precedents come out the way they came out — give me at least three genuinely different ones, not just rewordings of each other, and say for each what it commits you to on cases that haven't come up yet;

(b) which of those rules are still alive once you add the new case, and which one the board is most likely to actually land on and why — pay attention to how the membership has been trending, and to which precedent each camp is going to reach for;

(c) the recommendation that's most consistent with our own record, plus the single closest analog among the six. I want you to be specific about what makes it the closest — which feature is doing the work — and about the best disanalogy the other side will press on;

(d) if the record genuinely doesn't decide it, say so plainly and then spell out what principle we'd be adopting by ruling one way versus the other, in each direction. I want that stated as something a future board would be stuck with, not as a shrug.

Also flag anywhere you think one of the six is sitting badly with the rest — like, I can't tell whether #4 and #5 are really compatible, and I don't know if #1 is doing more work than the board thinks it is.

And please don't pull in what actual named philosophers have said about any of this, and don't give me citations or a reading list. The board only cites itself, and I can't check sources anyway. What I want is the rule-fitting and the reasoning worked through from the record I gave you.
