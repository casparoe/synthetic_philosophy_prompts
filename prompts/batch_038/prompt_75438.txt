For the last nine years I have run an autumn residential workshop for writers of historical fiction in a borrowed farmhouse outside Perugia: fourteen novelists at a time, none of them with any formal training in philosophy, most mid-career, several currently at work on books whose cast includes people held as slaves — an Ottoman household in Bursa, a fish-salting works in Baetica, a cane estate in the 1670s. The week is normally given over to craft, but this year I have blocked out forty-five minutes on the second morning for something I keep failing to do well: laying out Aristotle's argument for natural slavery from the opening book of the Politics, not as an exhibit of ancient wickedness but as the most sustained attempt the tradition ever made to argue that some human beings are by nature fitted to be ruled and others fitted to rule them. My motive is practical rather than scholarly. Two of my writers have drafted scenes in which an educated slaveholder defends his household in just those terms, and in both drafts the speech reads as villainous posturing — the character asserts a conclusion with no argument behind it, and the reader is invited to feel superior rather than to be unsettled. I want the room to understand the argument well enough to inhabit it, and I want the exposition to be strong enough that these writers feel its pull and then resist it for reasons rather than by reflex.

What I need from you is the plan, not the exposition. Please do not explain the doctrine to me, and please do not send me to secondary literature: neither I nor the room will read it, and I have no handouts, one whiteboard, and a wood fire that makes everyone drowsy after lunch. Give me the dependency structure first — which pieces must be standing before the claim that some people are slaves by nature can be so much as understood: the teleological habit of thought in which a thing's nature is what it is once fully developed; the insistence that kinds of rule differ in kind and not merely in the number of subjects; the treatment of property as an instrument of action; and the distinction between rule exercised for the sake of the ruler and rule exercised for the sake of the ruled. Tell me which of these I can compress into a single sentence and which will collapse underneath me if I do. Then tell me what one case should carry the forty-five minutes. I have candidates and I distrust all of them: a modern analogue involving someone with an advanced cognitive disability, which I fear converts the session into a different argument altogether; a period vignette of an Athenian household, which risks being absorbed as local colour; and a case lifted from one of the manuscripts in the room, which would be vivid but would also turn its author into the object of the lesson. Recommend one, say what it must possess and what it must conspicuously lack, and say what would break it.

Most of all I want the misconception map, with the point in the sequence at which each item should be headed off rather than answered later. Here is what I expect, roughly in the order I expect it: that Aristotle is merely rationalising an institution he inherited and would never have thought to justify; that his criterion of the natural slave is a claim about intelligence and hence about whole peoples, so that the doctrine is simply an antique form of hierarchies my writers already know how to condemn; that the whole thing reduces to the master's assertion that the slave benefits, which is might dressed as medicine; that it is a detachable blemish, such that one could keep the account of the household, citizenship and the good life with the slavery chapters excised; and, from the novelists particularly, the occupational reflex that any slave character worth writing must see through the argument at once — which quietly requires the argument to be a lie rather than a reason. For each, tell me whether it is a misunderstanding to be corrected or a genuine difficulty in Aristotle that I should name as such and leave standing; I suspect at least two are of the second kind, and I would rather be told which than reassure myself. Note too that the room will contain one writer who has read enough Aristotle to be pleased with himself and one who thinks the entire exercise indecent; say briefly how the plan accommodates both without degenerating into a seminar or an apology. Finally, give me one closing question, answerable aloud in a minute, that would distinguish a writer who has grasped the argument from one who has merely registered disapproval of it — and tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level. I assume the material on the acquisition of property and the whole later reception of the doctrine, but say whether I am cutting in the right place.
