I've spent a good fifteen years reading the establishment-and-secularism arguments across the usual blogs and forums, and the thing that strikes me is how much of the disagreement is people optimizing different targets without noticing it. One camp treats state non-identification with any religion as the entire ballgame; a second treats that demand as itself a sectarian metaphysics the state has no business holding; a third thinks the only live question is whether an arrangement survives demographic change. I don't want to adjudicate between them yet. I want the specification first.

The purpose, so you can calibrate adequacy: I'm drafting a checklist for a body that must decide whether to keep, reform, or unwind an existing state–religious-community arrangement, and I want the checklist to work across regimes that look nothing alike, because real arrangements are patchworks. Concretely, the theory will have to be usable on all of the following: an established church whose head of state formally governs it, whose senior bishops hold a statutory block of seats in the upper house, and whose courts have generally refused to rule on doctrine as such; a country where the state collects a surcharge on income tax on behalf of recognized communities and remits it, with formal exit requiring a declaration before a civil authority — and where some communities treat that civil exit as a religiously significant act in its own right; a republic whose founding statute forbids recognizing or subsidizing any worship, yet which pays prison, hospital, and military chaplains and restricts what its civil servants may wear; a country where taxpayers direct a fixed fraction of their income tax to whichever recognized confession they name, where the unexpressed shares are then divided among the recognized confessions in proportion to the choices that were made, and where recognition machinery built around church-like bodies has in practice left one very large community with no option to designate and another's signed agreement stalled awaiting ratification; a country whose constitution says a particular people's church shall be supported by the state and leaves the mechanics to legislation; and a country whose text forbids an establishment but whose doctrine has over the past decade moved from barring public money from religious exercise to requiring that religious institutions be eligible for generally available public funding.

So: write the desiderata. For each one, give the arrangement or concrete case that motivates it — drawn from the list above or invented — and state what observation about a real regime would count as violating it, since a criterion nobody could fail is not a criterion. Tag each as non-negotiable, negotiable, or camp-specific (a premise of one tradition wearing a criterion's clothing). I especially want the camp-specific ones surfaced: whether the state must not fund religion, whether religion must be a bounded belief-centered organization one joins and leaves voluntarily, whether there must be an individual right of exit, and whether "religion" is even a neutral category for a theory to be about. Include the distinctions the theory must not collapse — establishment versus funding versus legal recognition versus symbolic identification; neutrality as even-handedness versus neutrality as non-identification; individual free exercise versus corporate self-governance, including a community's freedom to be internally illiberal; explicit rules versus default rules, given that silence is not the same as no preference. And include a dynamic clause: what the theory owes us when the majority that justified an arrangement stops being one, and whether the arrangement can be unwound without breaching commitments already made to property, buildings, pensions, and treaties with a supranational religious authority.

Then the part I most want: identify any subset of your own desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied. Equal recognition of all communities, non-recognition of none, and a recognition procedure that doesn't require the state to adjudicate doctrine looks like a candidate — if it is, say which desideratum has to give and sketch what survives. If you can't find a genuine impossibility, tell me which tension is closest.

Finish with at most a paragraph each scoring strict non-identification, plural or cooperative establishment, accommodationist free-exercise maximalism, and a purely contractual treaty model against the list, naming the first desideratum each fails. Cut anything that turns out to exclude no candidate rather than padding the list. No bibliography, no reading list, no case citations — if you're unsure of a specific, flag the uncertainty instead of supplying authority. A table is fine for the tags; the prose should carry the reasoning.
