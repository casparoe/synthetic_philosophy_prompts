So, background: I write a weekly ideas column and, god help me, I also run our year-end ranking franchise — top ten albums, top ten papers, top ten whatever the internet is angry about that week. A colleague on the culture desk has been needling me for months about whether a ranked list is even a coherent object, and after a fairly wine-fuelled argument about it I sat down and wrote out what I actually believe about "better than," so I could either defend the lists or publicly confess. I'd much rather you audit the list than explain the topic to me — I know roughly what the issues are, I just can't tell where my own set breaks.

Here's the package:

(1) Better-than is transitive, all things considered. If A is better than B and B is better than C, A is better than C. I'm not in the mood to give this up, because the whole ranking enterprize rests on it.
(2) For any two things, exactly one of these holds: A is better than B, B is better than A, or they're equally good. I tend to think "incomparable" and "on a par" are confessions that someone hasn't thought hard enough yet.
(3) "Better than" is vague. There are real borderline cases and no sharp cut-off, and I think that's precisely what defuses the slippery-slope arguments people run against (1) and (2) — the step-by-step derivation has to fail somewhere, because the predicate doesn't have the precision those derivations assume.
(4) Comparisons are plural. When I say one record is better than another I'm weighing several respects at once — songwriting, coherence, ambition, whether it survives the tenth listen — and I'm happy to say the weighting shifts with whats being compared. Ranking two ambient albums against each other isn't the same exercise as ranking an ambient album against a punk one.
(5) On the population stuff, which I got dragged into by a friend: adding extra people whose lives are worth living, where it makes nobody else worse off, does not make things worse. And I reject lexical thresholds — I don't think any quality level carries infinite weight relative to any other, so enough decent-but-modest lives can outweigh a handful of spectacular ones.
(6) The repugnant conclusion is false. A vast population of lives barely worth living is worse than a smaller population of excellent lives. I feel this in my bones and I don't want to be argued out of it by cleverness.
(7) A small real improvement is a real improvement. Make one life very slightly better and change nothing else and the result is better, full stop — not "sort of," not "neither better nor worse."

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they do? I want a committed answer, not a menu. Tell me which commitment has to go and why, and if you think I can keep all seven, say so plainly and show me the move that lets me. Don't be diplomatic about it — I'd rather be told I'm wrong cleanly than be handed three schools of thought and a shrug. And for what it's worth I genuinely don't know which one I'd sacrifice, so I'm not going to hint at a favourite, because I suspect I'd pick the wrong one.
