Next May my doctoral advisor, Professor Marit Halvorsen, will receive a lifetime-achievement medal from an interdisciplinary association of historians, geographers, and climate scientists, and the committee has asked me to give the laudatio — the citation speech delivered just before the recipient comes up. Six minutes, a room of roughly five hundred: specialists from three fields that do not much like one another, a few journalists, her two sons, and her ninety-one-year-old sister, who has farmed her whole life in the valley where we did our fieldwork and who has never read a word she published. The speech has to be delivered aloud and understood at first hearing, it has to read as affection rather than as a seminar, and it has to survive the attention of people who disagree with the honoree's central claim and know exactly what it costs.

That claim is the difficulty, so let me state it as she did. For forty years she argued that a narrative of the past containing only human decisions is incomplete: that the volcanic winters of the sixth century, the failed harvests of the cold centuries, the plague years, and the collapse of a fishery belong in the story as something more than scenery. She called them actors, deliberately and against advice, and she was attacked from both sides. Physical scientists thought she was anthropomorphizing forcings; historians of empire thought she was arming anyone who wanted to describe a colonial famine as a natural disaster. She answered both. A volcano has no intentions, she would say, but it has consequences that no one chose, and a narrative that reports only what people decided quietly credits them with the whole of the outcome. On the other side she was a possibilist to the bone: the eruption changes the menu, not the order, and wherever she found a colleague writing that some climate made a migration or a collapse inevitable she called it a failure of imagination wearing a finding's clothes. Late in life she wrote an essay conceding that her framing had indeed been misused in exactly the way her critics predicted, and she described that misuse as a tax on talking this way — one she thought worth paying, and never once pretended was not being paid.

Here is the bind I need your help with. The association's citation, which the president will read from the podium two minutes before I speak, credits her with having given voice to the voiceless actors of history. She despised that sentence. It is, she told me once, the sentimental version of her claim: it turns an argument about evidence into a tribute to nature's eloquence, and it makes her into someone who thought the task was to let the volcano speak, when the task had always been the opposite — the volcano does not speak, and that is precisely why the historian has to establish what it did. My speech must undo that phrase in front of the people who wrote it, without embarrassing them, without turning a celebration into a correction, and ideally without the room registering that a correction has taken place. It must also not praise her for anything she considered false. So: no suggestion that she attributed intentions or a point of view to non-human things; no framing of her determinism-skepticism as a late, tactful softening of an essentially deterministic argument; no grand line about forces beyond human control writing history, which was everyone else's sentence and not hers. She also had a temper about being made respectable, and she was much funnier in person than in print — she once explained a broken axle on a farm track to me as the work of a Belgian road with ambitions — so the speech should contain at least one moment where the room laughs with her rather than at her, and it should not use the word Anthropocene, which she thought let people off the hook by being too large.

Please write the speech, roughly seven hundred to nine hundred words. Afterward, add a separate note that will not be read aloud, telling me where you think it takes liberties: whether the distinction it leans on — being an actor rather than merely a cause — is one she could actually hold, or whether it is only an emphatic way of saying that some effects went unchosen; whether the reconciliation of non-human causation with anti-determinism that it implies is stable, or quietly collapses toward one side; and whether undoing a citation in front of its authors can genuinely be done unnoticed. And if you think the request rests on a confused premise — that there is no coherent difference between calling something a historical actor and calling it a cause that mattered enormously, or that a celebratory speech simply cannot carry this much argument without becoming the lecture I have asked you not to give — please say so plainly and tell me why before you write anything, rather than producing a polished speech built on a distinction you consider empty.
