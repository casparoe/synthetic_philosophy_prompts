I am a novelist, and I have been trying for two years to write the eleventh chapter of the book I am currently working on. I have four drafts of it and I have come to distrust all four for the same reason: each of them argues. The chapter is a self-contained short story of roughly four thousand words, and I should like to read a version of it by someone with no stake in my solutions — partly as a diagnostic, and partly because I have begun to suspect the material is not mine.

You will know the source. It is Dennett's "Where Am I?", in which a philosopher volunteers for a mission that requires him to leave his brain behind in a life-support tank in Houston while his body, joined to it by radio, goes off to do the work; the surgeons reassure him that what they propose is "a mere stretching of the nerves," and the difficulty of the essay is that when he stands at the plate glass and looks at his own brain he cannot manage to think that he is in the tank.

My chapter keeps the machinery and throws away the philosopher. The person in the tank is a woman; the narrator is her husband; and the story is not about where she is. It is about what it is like to be married at a distance of forty miles and a quarter of a second. The metaphysical question must be exactly as open on the last page as on the first — but open in a household rather than in a seminar.

These conditions I would ask you to treat as binding.

1. The husband is paid to keep time: he maintains the clock of a public building and is answerable for the hour his town runs on. Everything in his life can be corrected except this. Handle the irony as lightly as you are able.
2. Choose her trade yourself, subject to one requirement: it must be work in which a quarter of a second matters, and she must still be doing it.
3. The transmission lag is the only instrument the story has. It varies — with weather, with load on the relay, with her — and he has learned to read it as a man learns a wife's moods. There must be one occasion on which the lag goes to nothing, on which she is suddenly and unnervingly punctual, and on which nothing he can detect is wrong. That scene should be the quietest in the story and the worst.
4. Somewhere early he is told that the facility maintains a second implementation, not tissue, which can be switched in should the tank fail; and he is shown a switch on the side of the tank. The second implementation is never to be referred to again, though it must govern at least two of his later decisions, so that a reader notices the influence only on returning to the page. The switch is never touched, and the story must not let us forget that it exists.
5. The technology is never explained. No scene in which a specialist speaks helpfully. The tank is visited at most twice.
6. He may not use the vocabulary of the subject: no *self*, no *identity*, no *consciousness*, no *person*, no *soul*, and nothing of the form "who she really is." He is permitted the ordinary words of a marriage. The question the essay asks aloud must here be asked only by conduct — which side of the bed he takes, whether her laugh arrives before or after the joke lands, what he does with her coat on the evenings she is not wearing it.
7. The story must be readable, with equal plausibility, as the account of a man married to the woman in the tank and as the account of a man married to the body that comes home at six and hangs up her keys. The choice is never to be made by argument.
8. It must not end coyly. Something must occur in the last two pages that cannot be un-occurred, and it must settle nothing; I would rather the reader were left holding a fact than a question.
9. No glowing fluid, no weeping at the glass, no revelation delivered in the final sentence.

What I want back is the story alone, under a title of your choosing. Please do not append notes on your decisions, and do not tell me where you think my drafts have gone wrong; I know.
