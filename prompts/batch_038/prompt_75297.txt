For a paper on Hume's posthumous essay on suicide, I have reconstructed its central move against the charge of impiety, and the reconstruction now appears to prove far too much. It runs as follows.

(1) If everything that happens is governed by general laws expressing the providence of the deity, then no human act can fall outside that governance.
(2) My ending my own life is an event in the world caused in the same way as any other: by the disposition of matter and by my own motives, both equally within the general order.
(3) The impiety objection — that suicide usurps a power over life and death reserved to the deity — presupposes that the act escapes or interrupts that order.
(4) By (1), nothing escapes or interrupts it.
(5) So the objection fails: ending one's own life is no more a trespass on divine prerogative than diverting a river, administering a medicine, or catching a falling stone, each being equally a rearrangement of matter within the same order.
(6) But by precisely the same reasoning, killing another person, or letting one die when I could prevent it, is likewise no trespass, since these too are events inside the order.
(7) Therefore the argument either shows that nothing anyone does can trespass on divine prerogative, or it shows nothing in particular about suicide.

I want to keep (1): I am not prepared to surrender the causal uniformity the essay relies on. I cannot accept (7). Which step is at fault, and why did it look unobjectionable when I wrote it down? Then price each candidate repair — what must be surrendered, and what the argument becomes once repaired: does it still tell against the impiety charge, and if it now yields only a conditional permission, on what does the permissibility of suicide come to depend, and how much of the original result survives?
