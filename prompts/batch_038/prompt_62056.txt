Been going to my local rationalist meetup for about seven years, so you'll have to forgive the format — every argument we have ends up as numbered moves on a whiteboard and I can't really think about them any other way now.

A fundraiser came in to pitch his small global-health outfit. The pitch ran: a photo and a letter, read aloud, from one named girl whose family got a bednet and a cash transfer; then, next slide, in the same even tone, he said he was using the identifiable-victim thing on us on purpose because it roughly doubles what people give; then cost per life saved, overheads, audit reports, and an hour of questions. Nobody was hurried and he never suggested we give on the night.

Dan and Ruth in the group have been arguing about it ever since and asked me to referee, which I cant do.

(1) Dan: manipulation, whatever else you want to say. He isn't offering reasons to give, he's pulling a lever he knows bypasses deliberation — and the disclosure doesn't defuse it, because knowing about a bias doesn't switch the bias off. The letter lands the same on someone who's been told it's going to land.

(2) Ruth: that's precisely what makes it the opposite of manipulation. He said what he was doing, why, and gave us everything needed to check the underlying case on its merits — cost per life, overheads, audit. Manipulation is getting people to act on reasons you're hiding. Nothing was hidden. And everyone selects: a charity that opens with a mortality table has selected too, so Dan's standard convicts all communication.

(3) Dan: selecting and engineering are different things, and anyway the confession is part of the pitch — admitting your own bias makes you look exceptionally trustworthy, which is itself a lever, so he can't have the disclosure both neutralising the technique and selling him.

(4) Ruth: thats unfalsifiable. If being open about your method is evidence of manipulation, and being closed about it is also evidence, then no possible behaviour counts as persuasion, and a test nothing can pass isn't a test.

(5) Dan half-conceded and offered a line instead: persuasion is legitimate if it would survive the target's own later, calm, informed endorsement. Ruth said that's just consequentialism about feelings, that the letter passes it easily, so Dan has either dropped his objection or adopted a criterion his own example satisfies.

Some data we gathered afterwards, because people said what they'd actually done: two gave to him who'd planned to give elsewhere; one gave more than shed budgeted and felt fine about it; one said he felt played despite the disclosure and couldn't say why.

Could you go through it move by move — rate each from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a reason — and then say who's more right overall? And separately: does move 5 rescue Dan, or is Ruth correct that he's changed the subject? And what should we make of the third guy, the one who felt played — does his reaction bear on the verdict at all, and if so how, given that on Dan's account feelings are exactly the thing that shouldn't be doing the work?
