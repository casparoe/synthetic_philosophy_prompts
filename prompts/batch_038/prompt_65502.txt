I spent forty-one years as an experimental physicist — mostly low-temperature measurement work, the unglamorous kind — and I retired three years ago. Philosophy of science has since become my occupation, and rather badly. I am writing with a question I cannot settle myself, and which I suspect is usually settled far too quickly.

Twice in my career I was scooped within a fortnight, both times by groups I had never corresponded with, on results that in neither case depended on any apparatus or technique unavailable to a dozen other laboratories. I have since learned there is a sociological literature on such episodes; the near-simultaneous ones go by the name of multiples, and the exhibits everybody reaches for are the calculus and the theory of evolution by natural selection. The standard line is that these cases show discovery to be the achievement of a field that has become ready rather than of an isolated genius: the enabling ideas, instruments, and problems are in wide circulation, so the identity of whoever arrives first is largely an accident.

I want an essay of roughly two thousand words that presses harder on what that standard line actually commits anyone to. The question is this: does the frequency of independent near-simultaneous discovery support the stronger claim that scientific discoveries are, in any substantive sense, inevitable — that the result was somehow there to be found, awaiting a finder — or does it support nothing beyond the weaker claim that, given the background conditions actually in place, a number of competent people were each in a position to produce the result and one of them happened to be first?

In the course of the essay, please do the following.

First, separate claims that are habitually run together: (a) a counterfactual claim about replacement, that had this person not existed or not turned to the problem, someone else would have produced the result within some short interval; (b) a claim about the distribution of competence, that many people were positioned to produce it, so the winner is arbitrary among them; and (c) a modal or romantic claim about the result's being available independently of anyone's finding it. Say which of these the historical evidence of multiples could bear on at all, and which it could not.

Second, take the deflationary reading seriously: that inevitability is a vivid redescription of shared background conditions and adds no explanatory content to (b). If you think that is correct, say so plainly. If you think the stronger claim has content the weaker lacks, exhibit the content — tell me what would have to be true of the world for (c) to hold while (b) failed.

Third, evaluate an operational test of my own devising and tell me whether it works. My thought was to measure the lag between the point at which the enabling conditions are in place and the point at which the result is published, and to look at the shape of the distribution across many cases: a sharp peak with a short right tail would suggest ripeness, whereas a heavy tail would suggest that something singular and rare is doing the work. Tell me whether such a distribution measures (b) while being sold as evidence for (c), whether any distributional evidence could in principle do better, and how much of the proposal collapses on the prior difficulty of specifying when the enabling conditions were in place.

Fourth, deal with an asymmetry I cannot get past. If the outcome were inevitable and the winner arbitrary, priority disputes ought to feel like quarrels over a lottery ticket. They do not. They are among the bitterest affairs in scientific life, and I have been a party to two of them. Explain what this shows: that working scientists disbelieve inevitability, that they believe it and resent it anyway, or that who discovered a thing is simply not the question to which inevitability is an answer.

Finally, state and answer at least one serious objection to my framing itself — for instance, that I am asking historical and statistical evidence to settle a modal question it is structurally incapable of settling, or that inevitable means nothing at all until one fixes a time scale and a reference class of agents.

Do not give me a survey, and do not close by observing that the truth lies somewhere in between. I want a conclusion, defended against the strongest case for the other side. No reference list, please; I will supply citations myself, and it is the argument I want from you.

End the essay with a final line in exactly this form:

Verdict: INEVITABLE-IN-SUBSTANCE / INEVITABLE-ONLY-AS-REDESCRIPTION / NOT-SUPPORTED / QUESTION-ILL-POSED — followed by no more than fifteen words of qualification.
