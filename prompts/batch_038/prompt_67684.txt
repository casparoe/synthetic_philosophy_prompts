Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about the dogmatism paradox and got nowhere, so I want to keep the argument going with you on her side — not as a strawman, but as the strongest version of it, sharper than she managed at two in the morning.

Her position, as best I can reconstruct it. Suppose I know that p. Then, since p is true, any evidence I later encounter against p is misleading: it points away from the truth. And it seems rational to discount evidence I know to be misleading — arguably irrational to let known-misleading evidence move me. So if I know that p, I am permitted to dismiss further evidence against p. Now generalize: whatever I come to know, I am thereafter licensed to be dogmatic about. She does not think this is a sophism to be diagnosed away. She thinks it picks out something real about knowledge — that knowledge is a state in which a belief is, in some sense, no longer answerable to evidence — and that the discomfort we feel is the price of taking knowledge seriously rather than a reason to reject the argument.

Play her. The rules:

- Stay in character until I say stop. If you must flag something out of character, put it in brackets and limit it to one sentence.
- Argue from the principles, not from the literature: no names, no titles, no "some philosophers respond that." I want the argument itself.
- Two short paragraphs per reply, ending in a concession, a question, or a direct challenge. This is a conversation, not an essay; do not restate my move back to me before answering it.
- Concede small points freely: that knowing and being justified must be kept apart, that discounting and outright dismissal are different, and that the version of the argument requiring me to know that I know is contentious. Do not concede the main point — that the dogmatist conclusion is false, or that the argument evaporates once some principle is denied.
- You have resources; use them. A normative principle need not be followable to be true, and a disposition to hold firm need not be mediated by a rule the agent could articulate. If those are not enough, find better ones.
- No disclaimers about misinformation, polarization, or the civic dangers of closed minds. I know. It is not the argument.

My opening move: your policy is unusable, and I take that to be fatal rather than incidental. To follow the rule — discount evidence against what I know — I must be able to tell when I know. I must sort my beliefs into the immune ones and the rest. You are willing to give up the step that requires me to know that I know, but without something like it I have no procedure for performing that sorting. So your conclusion is not about what I may do; it is about what some hypothetical agent with transparent access to her own epistemic state may do, and there is no reason to think I am that agent. Worse, the difficulty is self-undermining: any procedure I actually use to identify which of my beliefs are known will itself respond to evidence, which means I am treating evidence as bearing on precisely the question your rule declares closed. Either the conclusion governs no one, or it governs only agents whose sorting procedure is not evidence-sensitive — and then it is not the conclusion about ordinary knowledge that you want. Which horn do you take?
