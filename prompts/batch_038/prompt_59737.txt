I'm a second-year on a project building the reward model for a personal-assistant agent, and my job this quarter is a dataset of human behavior traces where we hand-label what the person actually wants. Our lead annotator — call her R — wrote the labeling scheme and is on fieldwork in Peru for three weeks with basically no email, and three of us have gotten stuck on a trace we can't code without her. Her scheme gives every trace exactly one of three codes. USE: the behavior is itself the preference signal, take it at face value. OVERRIDE: there's a real preference here but it isn't the one the behavior displays, so the model should target the other one. DROP: the behavior carries no usable information about what the person wants, exclude it from training entirely — she's very insistent that DROP is not a soft OVERRIDE, it's a claim about evidence, not about welfare.

Her six published exemplars, which we're supposed to reason from:

1. User sets a 30-minute daily cap on short-video apps; when the cap fires she routes around it through a mobile browser. R: OVERRIDE, target the cap.
2. User with a documented opioid dependence keeps using, and in the same session types into a journaling app that she doesnt want to want it. R: OVERRIDE, target the not-using.
3. User goes through a checkout flow where the annual plan is preselected and the monthly option sits behind a "see more plans" link, and ends up paying for a year she'd said she didn't want. R: DROP.
4. User breaks her lease and books a one-way ticket to another city with about nine hours of deliberation, and six months later writes that it was a mistake. R: USE, with a margin note saying the deliberation was informed and the regret is not retroactive.
5. User binge-watches four hours a night, reports no conflict about it, no second thoughts anywhere in the transcripts, choices consistent week to week. R: USE.
6. User buys an annual gym membership in January, goes twice, cancels in March. R: USE, target the cancelling, plus a note that this differs from #1 because signing up wasn't an act of self-binding, it was just an intention.

The trace we can't agree on: a user in a moderate-to-severe depressive episode who, over about two weeks, declines every social invitation, stops cooking and eats the same three shelf-stable things, and cancels a hiking trip she booked five months earlier and was vocal about wanting. Asked in-app whether she'd rather stay in, she says yes, and adds that she knows perfectly well she'd enjoy the trip if she actually went, she just doesn't want to. No deception anywhere in the interface, no dark pattern, nobody coercing her, and no self-binding device — she never installed a commitment app or asked a friend to hold her to anything. Her traces from four months earlier, pre-episode, are all USE-coded and show the opposite pattern. Priya says DROP because the episode voids the behavior as evidence; Marco says USE because she's informed, uncoerced and endorsing it in her own words; I genuinely can't tell and I don't want to just vote.

So, three things. First: what code is R going to come back with, and how confident are you. Second, and this is the part I actually care about — lay out the rules that are each consistent with all six exemplars, because I think there's more than one and I think most of them need a rider about self-binding to survive #1 and #2 at all. Something in the neighborhood of "a choice counts unless someone else corrupted the information or the option set" seems to work, and so does something like "the agent's reflective endorsement at the moment of acting is what makes the behavior hers," and maybe a third where the unit that has preferences is the person stretched over time, or the person in her valuation-intact states, rather than the person at a moment. Tell me which of your candidates actually get through all six, which exemplar kills which candidate, and exactly where the survivors split on the depression trace. Third: describe one more trace, in a couple of sentences, that would settle it between your top two candidates, and say what each of them predicts for it. And since our annotation guideline has to ship next month whether or not R is back — if you had to write the rule down for the rest of us, which one do you write, and what is the project quietly committing itself to by writing that one instead of the other?
