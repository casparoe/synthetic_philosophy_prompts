so for my philosophy of language seminar each of us has to hand in our own position as a numbered list and then the rest of the group spends the session trying to break it. Mine is about radical interpretation and I want to find the cracks before thursday, not during it. Here's what I actually believe:

1. There's nothing more to what a speaker's words mean than what a very good interpreter could work out from the outside — from when they hold sentences true and in what publicly observable circumstances. No extra hidden facts doing the work.
2. The interpreter has to be charitable, and charitable in the strong sense: assign meanings so the speaker comes out largely right, and largely rational, about the world the two of them share.
3. Whole communities can be massively and systematically wrong about things. Error at scale is a real possibility, not something charity has quietly defined out of existence.
4. Conceptual relativism is real too. I think it's genuinely possible for some group to carve up the world so differently from us that no translation manual exists at all, and for them to still be a group with beliefs and meanings.
5. The reference worries don't bother me: even if the interpreter can't pin down whether their word picks out rabbits or undetached rabbit parts, I still think there's a determinate fact about what they mean, and that an interpreter can flat-out get it wrong.
6. On cases, I want to say both of these: if a group consistently says the water is upstream when it's downstream, they're just wrong about the water. But if a group consistently says an object is wherever its shadow falls, they don't mean what we mean by "is at" — they aren't confused, they're differently located conceptually.

Is this a coherent package or am I fooling myself? Tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and which only look like they do, rank the real ones by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, give the cheapest repair for each, and then say which single change on my list would clean up the most damage at once. And specifically for (6): can (2) actually deliver both of those verdicts, or am I just reaching for whichever reading sounds better case by case?
