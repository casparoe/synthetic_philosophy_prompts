I'm writing up my view on laws and chance for a journal club and I want a consistency check before someone else does it for me. Roughly where I've landed:

1. Laws are the axioms and theorems of whichever systematisation of the whole mosaic best trades simplicity off against strength. Nothing over and above the mosaic makes them laws.
2. Lawhood is objective — it doesn't hinge on anyone's tastes, and a science that systematised things in some wildly different way would be getting it wrong, not just being different.
3. Citing a law to explain one of its instances is real explanation, not a redescription in fancy clothes.
4. Chances are Humean in the same way: the objective probability of a decay or a flip is whatever the best system's probability axioms assign.
5. Law and chance should be robust to local news. I shouldn't have to know what's going on at the other end of the galaxy to know the chance here, and a handfull of flips landing differently shouldn't have changed the laws.
6. No actual event can violate a law. If we saw something that looked like a violation, the generalisation was never a law.
7. Separately: if exactly one iron ball in the entire history of the universe had accelerated upward, I'd still want to say gravity was a law, one with a single exception.
8. Methodologically I want to settle all this by looking at how physics actually talks and reasons, not by pumping intuitions about remote possible worlds.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they do? Rank the real conflicts by severity rather than grading everything as equally strained, and for the worst one tell me what has to go and what I lose by giving it up.
