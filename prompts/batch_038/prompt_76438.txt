I'm one of seven voting members on the technical steering board of an industry consortium — we maintain the open standard for how sidewalk delivery robots talk to city infrastructure (curb zones, crosswalk signal feeds, the permit/maintenance data cities require). About 40 members: three big operators, a bunch of hardware startups, five city transit agencies, two universities. The value of the whole thing is that it's *one* standard, so a city can permit any compliant robot without negotiating a bespoke deal.

Here's the mess. A rival alliance — two of the big operators plus a chip vendor, who left our consortium last year — just published a binding public commitment: any member shipping before 2027 must implement their spec, must license improvements back to the alliance, and signs a five-year no-switch clause with a published penalty schedule. It's deliberately irreversible-looking. Our chair, without a board vote, told a trade reporter we'd "match it within the quarter," and that quote is now in print and three of our biggest members keep repeating it back to us as though it's settled.

Second complication, and this is the part that makes Thursday ugly: our bylaws have a reciprocity provision. If any member publishes a binding commitment touching the standard, every member who hasn't filed a written objection within 45 days is deemed to have adopted it. We're on day 31, and day 45 falls nine days after Thursday's meeting. So silence is itself a commitment, made by default, on everybody's behalf.

Draft the memo I'll circulate Wednesday night. 1,000–1,300 words, readable by a board that includes two hardware engineers, a lawyer, a city transit official, and three execs who have never sat through a game theory class — so use the reasoning but don't name-drop textbooks or cite anything.

It needs:

- A recommendation up front: adopt a matching commitment, file an objection, or something in between — with the specific mechanism and the deadline attached to it.
- Treat the rival's announcement as a *move*, not as news. Is it actually irreversible, or is it expensive-to-break-with-a-buried-escape-hatch? Tell me what evidence would distinguish those two, and what we should do under each.
- Be honest about the mutual-disaster branch. If both sides commit hard and the ecosystem splits into two incompatible specs, do we win? Don't quietly assume that winning the race is the same as winning. I want the branch where everybody's commitment is credible and everybody's worse off spelled out.
- A short annex with at least four commitment designs we could plausibly publish — unconditional, conditional on some trigger, staged, one with a sunset, one delegated to a body that structurally can't back down, escrowed source, whatever else you think works. For each: what makes it credible to the other side, what it costs us in flexibility, and whether it survives the reciprocity clause without auto-binding all 40 members.
- An explicit split between questions of fact we could go find out (and who to ask) and judgment calls the board owns and can't delegate to me.
- The strongest honest case for the option you *don't* pick. Roughly half the board wants to escalate hard and fast, and they aren't stupid — give them a real argument, not a strawman, and then say why it still loses (or doesn't).
- A closing "what would change my mind" section: the two or three observations that should flip the recommendation.

One more thing — if your actual answer is that we shouldn't publish any commitment this quarter at all, say so plainly, and then tell me what to do about the chair's quote in the press, because a public retraction isn't free either and I don't want the memo to pretend it is. Also: if theres a version where we use the 45-day objection window to buy time without looking like we blinked, I want that on the table as its own option with its costs stated.
