I spent thirty-eight years running an experimental physical chemistry laboratory and retired two years ago. Since then I have been auditing a philosophy of science reading group, and I find myself unable to evaluate an argument that everyone in the room treats as long settled.

One member has a stock line that he deploys whenever a methodological dispute arises: 'If your degrees of belief aren't probabilities, you're a free lunch for the first bookie who walks in.' Last month he used it twice within five minutes. First, against a paper that represents an agent's uncertainty as a family of probability functions rather than a single one: such an agent, he said, is trivially exploitable and therefore irrational, and no further discussion is needed. Second, against a proposal to criticize an update rule by showing that some alternative rule is guaranteed to be closer to the truth rather than by showing that it avoids a sure loss: he said the two amount to the same argument, and that the betting version is the decisive one, since losing money is a matter of observable fact while closeness to the truth is a matter of taste.

I am not persuaded, but I cannot articulate why, and I suspect the difficulty is that his sentence means several quite different things and that he is trading among them. Could you lay out, as a menu I can bring to the group, the distinct precise theses that slogan might be expressing? For each reading I would like the assumptions it requires — whether the agent must publish a single price per proposition and stand ready to take either side of a bet at it; whether the bets are assumed to carry no news bearing on the proposition bet on; whether the agent may decline or renegotiate a transaction once offered; whether the credences at issue are held at a single time or across an intervening observation; and whether the conclusion concerns dispositions to act, a numerical representation, or the way the world is — together with a verdict on each: platitude, substantive but defensible, or simply false.

Two puzzles I would like taken up within that menu rather than appended to it. First, my own hunch is that the family-of-functions agent escapes not by cleverness but by declining to name one price at all: he states a lower figure at which he will buy and a higher one at which he will sell, precisely as a real bookmaker does, and once a spread is permitted I cannot see how any package of bets forces a loss on him. If that is right, which reading of the slogan does it falsify, and what is left for its defender to claim? Second, the same member assured us that an agent who fails to update by conditionalizing can be caught by bets arranged in advance. It seems to me this works only if the agent is bound beforehand to honour the later transactions, and that anyone at liberty to walk away from the second wager cannot be made a sure loser at all. Is that a genuine distinction, or is there a construction I have overlooked?

Finally, of the readings you list, tell me which, if any, is strong enough to carry the two dismissals I described, and which is the one people most plausibly have in mind when they repeat the line without thinking about it.
