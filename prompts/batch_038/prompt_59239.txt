I retired from isotope geochemistry about four years ago and have fallen into philosophy of science reading to fill the hours — badly, in the sense that I now have opinions and no training. Last month I tagged along to a local reading group (mostly laypeople, one actual historian) and watched two people get into it about when the Anthropocene starts. I couldn't work out who was arguing better, I didn't say anything, and neither of them gave an inch. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. Dana is an environmental historian; Marcus is a stratigrapher, still working.

(1) Marcus opened by saying the question has a right answer and it's an empirical one. You define an epoch by a boundary marker that's globally synchronous and permanently recorded in the rock, and the only candidate that clears that bar is the plutonium from thermonuclear testing in the early 1950s, which shows up in lake sediment and ice pretty much everywhere at once. So mid-twentieth century, and everything else is history rather than geology.

(2) Dana said the choice of marker isn't neutral. Bomb fallout makes the signature of the human epoch a weapon built by states and laboratories, and it puts the start date after the centuries of colonial extraction that actually got the thing moving — so the geologists get to say 1950 while the people whose land and bodies paid for the run-up are written out of the origin story. She pointed at the 1610 proposal — the dip in atmospheric CO2 in the ice cores around then, read as reforestation across the Americas following the collapse of the Indigenous populations — and said that on that marker the epoch's first global signal is a mass death, so the date you pick says something about whose death you treat as the start of the modern world.

(3) Marcus said she's conflating a marker with a cause. A boundary marker is a timestamp, a way of making the horizon findable in a core anywhere on the planet. The iridium layer at the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary is a marker, not the whole causal story, and nobody claims that putting the boundary there was a way of making the dinosaurs look like collateral damage. The 1610 CO2 dip is gradual, regionally variable and has rival explanations, so it can't do the job a marker has to do.

(4) Dana said that begs the question. "Gradual" and "has rival explanations" are judgements about resolution and confidence, and the thresholds are set by the same small commission that decides what counts. She noted that commission voted the whole thing down in 2024 largely because the record was too shallow and the start date too recent — which, she said, shows the criteria being applied with a thumb on the scale toward deep-time habits of thought. And she asked: if the 1610 signal is in polar ice at both ends of the planet and in the pollen and charcoal records on both sides of the Atlantic, in what sense is it not global?

(5) Marcus said fine, call it global — but then they're having an empirical arguement about preservation and resolution, and she's the one who wanted it to be about whose suffering counts. He said if she can name a marker that's synchronous to within a decade and survives in unconsolidated sediment worldwide, he'll take her date seriously and the moral question drops out entirely.

(6) Dana said that's exactly the move she objects to: the moral question doesn't drop out, it gets hidden inside the word "seriously," because a marker that has to be instantaneous and perfectly preserved will always be the one that suits specialists trained to look for sharp horizons. She added that 2024 cuts her way — the epoch got rejected but everyone agrees we're living through the event, so the term is doing interpretive work about history, not timestamp work, and pretending otherwise is part of why it failed.

They stopped there, both annoyed.
