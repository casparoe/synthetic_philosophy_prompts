i run a reading group on population ethics — nine of us, mostly grad students, plus one very stubborn person-affecting guy who has never met a total he liked — and i want to open the next session with something funny instead of another paper. write me a deadpan municipal planning report, about 700 words, in the exact shape a local planning authority publishes: application number, site and description, policy context, officer's assessment, summary of consultation responses as numbered objections with officer replies, recommendation.

the application is from a developer (invent one) for a residential scheme that starts as a modest village and then gets extended in phases, and the whole joke is that every single phase is arithmetically unobjectionable by the council's own lights while the endpoint is a city of some absurd number of people whose lives are barely worth living. make the arithmetic actually hold up: give the officer's welfare figures, keep the threshold explicit and constant, and arrange the phasing so that no phase leaves any existing resident worse off and every new resident clears the bar. someone in my group will check it with a calculator.

the objections and replies should be the real moves from the literature, not caricatures of them: neighbours who turn up to the consultation and are told they lack standing because under the smaller scheme none of them would have existed; a councillor invoking the asymmetry between adding miserable lives and adding happy ones, and being ruled out of order; a rival proposal from a housing association that would require every new resident to clear some level well above neutral, which the officer dismisses for setting the bar arbitrarily and thereby making it bad to add people who would be delighted to be added; a mediator's compromise involving a weight per extra head that shrinks as the head count grows; the cheaper alternative with slightly worse lives but far fewer of them, and the reason it loses; an equality objection about the gap between the residents of phase one and the residents of phase forty; and a monitoring officer who certifies that the conditions the council wrote into its own welfare planning policy cannot all be satisfied at once, so the committee is not choosing a scheme but choosing which of its own conditions to break. none of these should be named as -isms or attributed to anyone real — the officer just does the reasoning, in his own flat voice.

tone: the officer is a sincere optimiser, not a villain and not a strawman, and the prose should be ordinary bureaucratic English — amenity space, parking provision, affordable housing quota, surface water drainage — so it reads perfectly well to someone who has never heard of any of this and much funnier to someone who has. a good totalist in the room should laugh rather than wince. nobody in the document gasps, nobody says anything like 'monstrous', and the recoil has to belong entirely to the reader. and the report must never use the phrase for the famous conclusion, or point at it, or explain itself.

then a separate note of about 150 words, out of character, in your own voice, where you pick a side: tell me which objection in the report you actually think is the strongest and why, which one the satire lets off too easily, and where the piece takes liberties — for instance whether the officer's welfare numbers smuggle in something a real defender of the view would reject, or whether the monitoring officer's impossibility certification is faithful to how those results go or a bit of a cheat i should flag to the group. commit to definite answers with reasons; don't hand me a balanced menu of options.
