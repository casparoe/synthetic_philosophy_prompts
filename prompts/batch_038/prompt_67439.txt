I am in the second year of a seminar on archaeological inference, and this week's session concerns the use of analogy in reconstructing belief and social institutions from material remains. Our convenor assigned a long essay from a general-interest archaeology magazine as background, and it compresses a large literature into a single paragraph, which I reproduce here:

"Archaeologists have only the things people left behind, and the route from things to what people believed runs through analogy: you find a regular connection between material patterns and behaviour in a setting you can observe, and you carry that connection back into the deposit you cannot observe. An analogy of this kind is licensed by cultural continuity — the more firmly you can connect the excavated society to an observed one, the stronger the inference — and continuity of that sort is rarely available, since the societies described in the classic ethnographies have themselves changed beyond recognition and most of the deep past has no descendants anyone can point to. The inference is therefore usually a guess wearing methodological clothing. This was the predicament the processualists believed they had solved: build up a body of middle-range theory linking static material patterns to dynamic behaviour, and analogy ceases to be an assumption and becomes something you can test. What they never squarely faced is that middle-range theory is itself assembled by analogy, from experiments in knapping and butchery and from watching modern potters and herders at work, so the escape from analogy was a circle. It follows, as the contextualists insisted, that an artefact carries no determinate meaning: meaning was conferred by the people who made and used the object, in a context we cannot recover, which makes material culture much more like a text than like a natural object. Claims about prehistoric belief are consequently not knowledge claims at all but readings, and the honest archaeologist offers a range of readings rather than an argument for any one of them. The debate was about whether analogy could be made respectable, and it ended when everyone agreed that it could not."

I came into this course inclined to accept the ending of that paragraph, and I now want to know whether that inclination is earned by the reasoning or merely by the tone. I am not after references, names, or dates; I want an accuracy audit I can carry into the seminar. Please work through the passage claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether a working archaeologist of belief would accept it, regard it as a tolerable simplification, or reject it outright, and why. In particular I would like you to address the following:

1. Whether analogy really is the only route from objects to belief, or whether there are other kinds of warrant for such inferences that the paragraph passes over, and if so what they are and how much they can carry on their own.
2. Whether the paragraph is entitled to move from "this one warranting condition often fails" to "analogical inference is generally a guess." Note that it seems to treat only one kind of analogy — the kind that needs a historical or genealogical connection between the observed and the excavated society — as though it were the whole genus. Please separate that kind from the kind that needs only structural or functional similarity, and say which of the paragraph's criticisms actually bites on which.
3. Whether the circularity charge against middle-range theory is fair. Some of us think it obviously is, since the low-level links do come from experiments and observation of living practitioners; others say this conflates levels of inference. Explain what the standard reply is, and whether that reply succeeds or merely relocates the problem.
4. Whether the move from "artefacts do not carry determinate meaning" to "claims about belief are not knowledge claims" is licensed at all, or whether it slides from a point about meaning to a point about epistemic status that does not follow from it. Relatedly, whether the contextualist position the paragraph invokes really recommends offering a range of readings in place of an argument, or whether it holds interpretations to standards of its own.
5. Finally, whether the closing historical claim — that the debate ended in agreement that analogy cannot be made respectable — is right, and what weaker conclusion the paragraph could defensibly have drawn from the parts of it that survive correction.

Where the paragraph is wrong, please say not just that it is wrong but what a defender of the position it is describing would say in response, since I would rather learn the shape of the disagreement than be handed a verdict.
