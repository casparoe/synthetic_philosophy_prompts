You're a philosophy-of-language specialist — someone who actually works on rigidity and the metaphysics of reference, not just the intro-textbook version — and I want you to stress-test a view of mine hard, without being polite about it. Before you settle on a structure, spend a few hundred words brainstorming out loud: list every way you can think of that the view might break, then winnow.

The view, which I'll call reference-fixing exhaustivism (my label, not a school): every genuine a posteriori necessity gets all of its empirical content from fixing the reference of one of its terms, and once the reference is fixed the remaining necessity is available without any further empirical work. So the discovery in water = H2O, or Hesperus = Phosphorus, or heat = molecular motion, isn't a discovery about how the world has to be; it's a discovery about which candidate actually satisfies the condition we used to latch onto the term, and the necessity itself comes for free from the latching decision. On this reading there's no such thing as a sentence that's both necessary and world-involving in the way people usually allege — the necessary a posteriori is just a contingent a posteriori fact about reference-fixing bolted onto a trivial a priori necessity. It's meant to be a deflationary diagnosis, not a revision of Kripke's examples.

So: what would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal-through-the-heart to merely awkward, and for each one I want to know what the world (or the semantics) would have to look like for it to bite. Specific things I'd like you to handle rather than skip:

- Which cases can't be assimilated? My own candidate is origin essentialism — the table couldn't have been made from a completely different hunk of wood, the queen couldn't have originated from totally different gametes. The empirical work there looks like it's about a modal property of a particular, not about how a term gets attached, and I can't see what reference-fixing condition is doing the work. Tell me whether that's actually a counterexample or whether I'm missing a way to absorb it, and if there's a better counterexample I should be using instead.
- What about putative necessities that aren't identities at all — say the claim that nothing can be red and green all over at the same time, or the impossibility of faster-than-light signalling, or whatever non-identity examples you think are strongest? Do those fit the schema, and what does it cost the view if they don't?
- Does the causal-historical picture of reference undercut my own machinery? If names and natural kind terms are fixed by baptism plus transmission rather than by satisfaction of a condition, maybe there is no reference-fixing condition to which I can assign all the empirical content — in which case my view loses its central explanatory device without being outright false. Is that a defeater or a repairable gap?
- Vagueness: how much of the stuff in the lakes and rivers has to be H2O before our term is fixed. If the reference-fixing conditions are themselves indeterminate, does the necessity survive, and does my view have anything to say that its rivals don't?
- Cross-linguistic pressure: suppose some language's kind terms don't have the structure that makes rigidification natural. Does my view then imply the necessary a posteriori is unavailable to those speakers? That consequence seems bad and I'm not sure how to avoid it.
- Collapse: does this just turn out to be two-dimensionalism with a different name, or the primary/secondary intension distinction wearing a hat? If it collapses, is that a defeater — it wouldn't be false, but it wouldn't be doing the diagnostic work I claim — and does it inherit the standard objections to the thing it collapses into?

Also, and this matters as much as the rest: what does the view risk? State what it predicts that its rivals don't, ideally including something in principle checkable — from formal work on rigidity, from psycholinguistic or developmental data about how kind terms are acquired, from the phenomenology of competent speakers being unable to articulate any fixing condition for a name they use perfectly well. Then give me an honest verdict on whether I've insulated it by qualification to the point where nothing could count against it, and whether that's actually a problem for a deflationary metasemantic thesis of this kind or a perfectly fine thing for such a thesis to be. Be explicit about which of the defeaters a committed exhaustivist would concede as counting against them and which they'd dismiss, and say which single consideration would move my number most. No bibliography, I'm not asking you to cite anything — I just want the map of where this thing is exposed and how badly.
