I am midway through a third novel whose three strands are bound together by a single cross-border surrogacy: an agency and clinic in a jurisdiction where compensated gestational carriage is advertised openly and its contracts are enforced by ordinary courts; a commissioning couple from a jurisdiction that forbids the practice outright and has lately begun treating its own citizens' arrangements made abroad as crimes (the Italian statute of the autumn of 2024, which reaches conduct committed in countries where it is lawful, is the model I have in mind, though my country is invented); and an egg donor who is not the gestational carrier, and whose relation to the child nobody in the book can articulate. My difficulty is no longer research but characterisation. Every argument scene I write turns out to have quietly promoted one character into a mouthpiece for a position I had not distinguished carefully enough from the position of the person answering her, so that the exchange degenerates into two people saying the words exploitation and autonomy at one another in slightly different registers. I want to repair this with an instrument rather than with more reading.

Please build me a decision tree of the normative positions on commercial surrogacy and compensated egg donation, of the sort that sorts a person by what they actually believe rather than by what they call themselves. Start from the logical space, not from a list of camps: lay out six or seven branching questions, each phrased so that an intelligent reader who has never encountered the debate's vocabulary could answer it, and each answerable by imagining a situation rather than by endorsing a slogan. The branches should include whether the labour of gestation is the sort of thing that can be sold at all; where, if there is a wrong, it is located — in the payment, in the enforceability of the promise, in the recruitment and brokerage, in the transfer of the child, or in a home country's refusal to recognise a transfer lawfully completed elsewhere; whether the sale of oocytes and the sale of nine months of someone's body are the same transaction or two, with the tree genuinely permitting a position to answer them differently; whose claim decides when a gestator's change of heart after birth collides with the intended parents' expectation; and whether a prohibition at home may properly extend to conduct that is lawful where it occurs. Then place ten to twelve leaves in that space, naming a leaf where a name genuinely exists and marking it unnamed where nobody has bothered to christen it. Each leaf should carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the case it handles worst, and why it handles it badly — together with a vignette of two or three sentences: a scene in which a person holding that leaf judges differently from a person holding its nearest neighbour. It is those vignettes I most need, since I intend to walk my characters through the tree and see which of them survives it.

Certain leaves must appear, whatever the branching questions turn out to be: the view that the transaction is inalienable in kind, so that no contract however scrupulous can make it right; the abolitionist view that no amount of screening, compensation or judicial oversight can cleanse it, and that intermediaries should be criminalised along with the parties; the altruism-only view, on which unpaid carriage is permissible but its contract should be unenforceable so that the gestator may withdraw at any time without penalty; the regulated commercial view with price floors, medical and psychological screening, independent legal advice, and court supervision of the transfer; the contractarian view that competent adults may buy and sell this as they may buy and sell anything else; the view that the arrangement between the parties is unobjectionable while the brokerage industry — advertising, matching, packaging, commission — is where the wrong is concentrated; the view that the practice is permissible in principle and the objection is really to reproductive travel, which exports the risk onto people who have no forum in which to complain of it; and at least one position that permits the sale of oocytes while refusing the sale of gestation, or the reverse. Beyond the occupied cells, identify at least three combinations of answers that your branches permit but that nobody in the debate seems to hold, and say for each whether the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored — the last category interests me most, since unexplored cells are precisely where fiction has room to stand. Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate, particularly where two positions agree on every normative question and differ only on an empirical premise such as how often consent obtained in a poor clinic is genuinely free. Finally, no bibliography, no reading list and no citations: do not name statutes, cases or authors beyond what I have supplied above, and where a leaf's real-world advocates are contested, describe the position rather than attributing it to anyone. Set it out as an indented tree in plain text that I can print and mark up, and close with a short note on which single branching question is doing the most work, and where the tree is most likely to be unstable — the question on which a small change of wording would relocate a leaf.
