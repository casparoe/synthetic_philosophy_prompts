I am a senior who edits my school's philosophy journal, and two members of our ethics club have now spent a year arguing about Sen's liberal paradox — the result that no way of ranking social outcomes can simultaneously respect each person's protected sphere and honor unanimity. One of them holds that the impossibility exposes a genuine and unresolved tension: every proposed escape has to give up something we actually meant by a right. The other holds that it is an artifact of a formal choice — building a right into the social ordering as the power to settle a pairwise comparison — and that if rights are instead treated as limits on which outcomes are admissible at all, or if we decline to count preferences about what other people do inside their own spheres, both conditions can be satisfied together.

Please draft the joint statement they should have produced. First, a numbered section of claims both can sign: about the proof itself, about what it does and does not establish, and about the following case, which I constructed so that we would not keep relitigating the familiar example. Ava and Ben share a practice room; Ava's protected choice is whether she practices trumpet there, Ben's is whether he plays his speaker. Ava ranks silence above her own practicing above Ben's speaker; Ben ranks Ava's practicing above his own speaker above silence. Next, a table of the disagreements that survive, each stated in wording its own side would accept and marked as either substantive or merely verbal. Finally, the cruxes: for each one, an argument or case both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect one crux is whether a right that unanimity can trim down until nothing is left still deserves the name, and another is whether restricting unanimity to self-regarding preferences simply assumes what is at issue. We print no references, so please argue from the case rather than from citations.
