I am a second-year graduate student in a cognitive science program. For our methods seminar I must submit a précis and critique of one chapter of a recent trade book on consciousness, and my adviser has asked me to be specific about where the chapter's presentation of a particular method is fair and where it is not. I should say that I came to the chapter already inclined to side with the author's opponents: I have read enough to think that any approach built entirely on what people say must be smuggling in a skeptical premise. But I cannot yet tell which of my suspicions are legitimate and which are just the ones I find most satisfying, so what I need is an audit rather than an endorsement of my priors.

Here is the chapter's account of Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology, condensed into a single paragraph of my own transcription but preserving its claims and order:

"Consciousness research was long paralyzed by a methodological problem: each person's experience is available only to its owner, so the field seemed to have no data in common. Heterophenomenology dissolves the problem by borrowing a trick from anthropology. The researcher treats the subject's utterances the way a fieldworker treats an informant's accounts of the spirit world: she records everything the subject says about how things seem, organizes it into a coherent description of a world of appearances — the subject's 'heterophenomenological world,' a private theatre stocked with the images, sounds, and feelings the subject reports — and then declines to say whether anything in that world exists. Just as the anthropologist can describe a tribe's gods in full detail without believing in them, the scientist of consciousness can describe pains, afterimages, and the vivid redness of a sunset while remaining entirely neutral about their reality. This is not behaviorism in disguise: the researcher may bring in brain imaging, the subject's history, and even her own recollections of comparable experience, all as aids to interpreting the reports. What makes the method safe is that it cannot miss anything. Since the texts, together with the neural and behavioral data, constitute the total evidence base, and since a difference that makes no difference to that evidence base is no difference at all, whatever a subject genuinely has must show up there. When critics object that the method leaves out what it is like to be the subject, they are not identifying a missing phenomenon; they are refusing to pay in the only currency in which phenomena can be paid for. Their charge of question-begging rests on a confusion: bracketing a question is not answering it. The one concession the chapter makes is that subjects can be wrong about how things seem to them — the confabulation literature shows this — but the author regards that as a virtue, since a method obliged to take the subject's word on everything could never be corrected. By contrast, the 'Cartesian' first-person phenomenology the method replaces grants the subject final authority over her own case, and that, the author says, is what made the field unscientific."

Please go through this claim by claim and tell me what to trust. For each significant claim, say whether it is accurate, a common and harmless simplification, a misrepresentation of what the method is committed to, or simply false — and where a claim is a misrepresentation, say what the accurate version is and why the difference matters. I am especially interested in four things. First, whether the "private theatre stocked with reported images and feelings" characterization of the heterophenomenological world survives scrutiny, or whether it confuses what the method brackets with something else. Second, whether the argument that the method cannot miss anything is a trivial consequence of how the data set is defined, as the chapter presents it, or a substantive and contestable thesis, and if the latter, what work it is doing in the chapter's dismissal of its critics. Third, what the strongest objection from the critics actually is, as distinct from the version the chapter refutes. Fourth, whether the chapter's claim about subjects being wrong about how things seem is something a defender of the method would accept, reject, or have to complicate. Finally, identify the single sentence whose correction would most weaken the chapter's conclusion, and tell me whether the conclusion can be rebuilt on a weaker, defensible version of the neutrality claim.

I do not need references or a reading list — I have the book and can check anything you flag against it. What I need is your judgment about where the chapter is fair.
