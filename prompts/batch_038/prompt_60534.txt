I run a little film-and-philosophy group and I've talked them into Trading Places next month — the 1983 Eddie Murphy / Dan Aykroyd one. Everybody remembers it as a class-swap comedy, but I keep finding myself describing it as a movie about the no-trade theorems, and I want to know whether that reading survives actual contact with the film or whether I'm just projecting.

The material I have in mind: Milgrom and Stokey's result that if traders are rational, share a common prior, and know the structure by which information is acquired, then nobody can profit from private information, because the bid itself leaks what you know — plus Aumann's agreement theorem sitting behind it, and the Glosten–Milgrom picture where a market maker widens the spread precisely because the person on the other side might be informed. Trade requires asymmetry, and it requires someone willing to be the dumb side.

Now the film. Randolph and Mortimer Duke settle their nature-versus-nurture argument with a one-dollar wager — two brothers who have shared a household, a business and a lifetime of evidence, which looks like exactly the disagreement Aumann says shouldn't be possible. And the finale: they pay their fixer to get the USDA orange crop report early, Winthorpe and Valentine swap in a forgery predicting disaster, and the Dukes throw everything into buying frozen concentrated orange juice futures on the floor. The rest of the pit follows them and bids the price up — not because anyone believes the crop is doomed, but because everyone assumes the Dukes know something. Then the real report drops, the price collapses, and our two heroes buy back what they sold at the top.

So what do you make of it? Does the film actually get the microstructure right — is the crowd's behaviour literally the adverse-selection story, with the lie working only because trading is itself a signal — or does the climax need a market much stupider than the theory, since a pit that reasons about who's on the other side should be able to reason about the possibility of a bluff? And how do the two wagers compare: is the OJ trade the same epistemic object as the dollar bet, or is the film sloppily treating them as interchangeable? Which scenes would you point the group at?

I also want the honest counterweight. It's a screwball revenge picture about humiliation and money, and the market might just be a set piece — if so, say that the informational reading is imposed rather than earned, and tell me where. And separate what's actually settled here from what people who work on this genuinely dispute: whether no-trade results are meant as empirical predictions or as idealizations nobody accepts the assumptions of, whether the common prior assumption is defensible, whether prices-aggregate-information is a live claim or a slogan. Don't write as though the no-trade theorem explains why real markets work if that's a contested position.

Last thing: does the film give you anything the papers don't — some way of seeing why trade happens at all that the formal argument leaves out?
