You are, for this task, a literary writer who also has graduate-level training in economic theory and social epistemology — someone who can make a formal result feel like weather rather than like a lecture. I convene a small reading group that meets on the second Tuesday of each month in the back room of a pub, and this year our theme has been the epistemology of markets. We have worked through the family of results descended from Aumann's point that agents who share a prior cannot honestly agree to disagree, and especially the no-trade result of Milgrom and Stokey: the claim that if the structure of the market and everyone's rationality are common knowledge, then an offer to trade is itself evidence about what the offerer knows, so the offer undermines itself before it can be accepted. My group can follow all of this symbolically. What they cannot do is feel it. One of our members auctions fish for a living and insists the whole literature is nonsense, because in his experience people accept offers constantly; another says the theorem is just a curiosity about idealized agents that could never happen to a room full of actual human beings. I want to open our next session with a piece of fiction that makes both of them uneasy.

Please write a short story, 1,300 to 1,800 words, that dramatizes the no-trade mechanism from the inside, in a pre-modern setting where the theorem's assumptions are, unusually, close to true. Here is the seed I would like you to work from, which you may adjust but should not abandon: a high valley market town in which every child is taught the reckoning of chances from the same almanac, by the same schoolmaster, in the same room, so the townspeople genuinely share one way of weighing evidence; a stranger arrives whose business is opaque, and for a season trade is brisk precisely because nobody can guess what he knows; then, after some mundane and sympathetic grievance — a widow taken in over a lame horse, say — the town enacts an ordinance requiring anyone who makes an offer to declare on oath, before an assessor, what they know that prompts them to make it. The silence should then spread in stages, and the staging matters more to me than the plot: first the large speculative trades die, then the small ones, then ordinary barter at the fair, and finally something more unsettling, in which even a gift is refused because a gift is also an offer. Two further requirements. First, include a character who tries to defeat the silence by a device of her own — binding herself in advance to accept whatever is offered her, or hiring an agent who knows nothing, or making her offers through a fool or a child so that they carry no information — and show with some precision why the device fails, or why it works only in a way that is worse than failing. Second, include at least one trade that a reader will expect to happen and that does not, and at least one trade that stubbornly keeps happening, such that the difference between them turns entirely on whether the recipient can infer what the offerer knows. In the prose itself: no character may state the theorem, and you may not use the vocabulary of the literature — no "common prior," "Bayesian," "asymmetric information," "adverse selection," "signaling," "unraveling," "equilibrium" — nor name any economist or result; the mechanism must be legible only in what people do, what they decline to do, and what they notice themselves declining. Third person, close on a single viewpoint character (the assessor, the stranger, or a young woman in the town are all workable). No epigraph, no footnotes inside the story, no allegorical signposting, and no framing of the ordinance as tyranny: I want it genuinely unclear by the last page whether the silence is a loss or an achievement, a death of the town or its arrival at perfect agreement.

Think carefully before you begin, and do the structural work first rather than in revision. Before the story, give me a planning note of no more than 200 words: set out your chain of offers and declines as numbered links, each with a single line saying why that link follows from the ones before it rather than being asserted, and name one feature of the theory you have deliberately left out or simplified to keep the story human. After the story, give me a short critical note. Tell me how confident you are that the failures of trade in your story fail for the reason the theorems say they should, as opposed to failing because the characters have become suspicious or proud; single out the one step you are least sure of, the place where a real economist would say you have bent the setup to get the ending you wanted; and say specifically whether your character's pre-commitment device fails for a theoretically sound reason or merely for a dramatically convenient one, and how the answer would change if the ordinance were repealed at the end.
