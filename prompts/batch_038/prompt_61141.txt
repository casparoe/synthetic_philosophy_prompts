I cover computing and statistics for a national magazine, and I am writing a long essay on what the impossibility results for algorithmic fairness do and do not establish. My editors have asked me to append a brief first-person statement of where I stand, so that readers can see the position the piece argues from. Before I file it I want an audit of the position itself. I have deliberately not marked where I suspect the trouble is, because I do not know where it is: two colleagues have each told me the list "has problems" and neither would say which item has to give.

Here is the list, roughly as it would appear in print.

1. The property I defend for a risk score is calibration within groups: among defendants assigned the same score, the observed rate of the outcome is the same whatever their race. I defend it on the grounds that it is what makes the number mean the same thing to everyone who is handed it.

2. In my copy I treat calibration within groups and equality of positive predictive value across groups as two descriptions of a single property, and I use the phrases interchangeably, including once the score has been cut into a high-risk flag.

3. I accept the impossibility results: where base rates differ between groups, calibration cannot be had together with equal false-positive and equal false-negative rates. I report this as establishing that statistical fairness is mathematically impossible — not costly, not difficult, but unattainable — whenever groups are compared.

4. I also hold that the impossibility is a fact about imperfect prediction rather than about the concept: an instrument that classified everyone correctly would satisfy calibration and equal false-positive and false-negative rates all at once, even with unequal base rates. I use this to argue that the theorems mark the limits of our present tools, not the limits of fairness.

5. Because calibration is a property of the whole score and not of any cutoff, I argue that the false-positive gap at the centre of the recidivism controversy my paper covered was an artifact of thresholding — a choice made by the people who picked the cutoff — and not a property of the instrument, which was calibrated.

6. Which criterion a jurisdiction ought to adopt is, I hold, a question of values and of who bears the cost; the theorems are silent on it. They tell us the criteria cannot all be had, not which one to have.

7. For the purposes of these statistics I treat the recorded outcome — rearrest within a fixed window — as the thing the instruments predict. I also hold that because policing is geographically concentrated, rearrest over-represents offending in the more heavily policed group relative to the less heavily policed one at any given level of actual offending.

8. Equal selection rates across groups seem to me indefensible as a design target whenever base rates genuinely differ, since achieving them would require exactly the error-rate imbalance the theorems describe; but a large selection-rate gap is, I think, the first thing to look at when auditing a deployed tool.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a lecture.

- Sort the genuine conflicts from the apparent ones, and rank the genuine ones by severity, worst first. I would rather see three items examined closely than eight items waved at.
- For each genuine conflict, say precisely which items collide and why they cannot both survive as written; then say which one has to be revised or dropped, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me — the specific sentence I will no longer be able to write.
- Identify any pair that looks inconsistent and is not, and explain why it survives inspection. I do not want to strike things merely because they sound awkward together.
- Separately, flag any place where I have asserted a relationship between technical notions that is simply wrong, whether or not it produces a conflict with another item on the list.
- Note where a conflict is logical and where it is only between a claim and the use I make of it, since I suspect those call for different repairs.

One further thing, because it is the reason the audit matters. The essay must deliver a verdict on a live proposal: a court system that intends to publish a single common cutoff for its recidivism score, to offer the score's calibration as its evidence of fairness, and to describe the resulting false-positive gap between racial groups as an artifact of that cutoff rather than of the instrument. As the list now stands I can build a case for calling that arrangement candid and a case for calling it evasive, and I cannot tell which way my own commitments point. Tell me whether a corrected version of the list settles the question, and in which direction — and if it still does not settle it, tell me what the residual disagreement is actually about.

I am not after a survey of the literature and I do not want a bibliography; assume I have read it. I want to know whether my own position hangs together, and what I have to stop saying if it does not.
