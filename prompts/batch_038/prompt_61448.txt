I sit on a county public memory commission — seven of us, two historians, the rest retirees, we meet monthly and mostly argue about plaques. The case in front of us now is bad, and I need help thinking it through.

From 1931 to 1994 the state ran a residential institution for people with intellectual disabilities out at Harrow Creek. Residents who died were buried in a field behind the boiler house under numbered concrete markers, no names. The register matching numbers to names survived. In 1998, settling a class action brought by former residents and their families, the state sealed the register for 75 years and undertook to maintain the field "as a place of quiet," with no individual identification of the dead.

Now a descendants' group — mostly nieces and nephews, plus a handful of former residents still alive, in there 70s and 80s — wants the register opened so they can find their people and put names on the stones. The former staff association wants the seal kept. Their stated reason is privacy; what they actually mean is that the register's rows also give cause of death and, in some cases, the name of the staff member who signed the burial form, and they say releasing it means fresh accusations against people who are dead and can't answer for themselves. Counsel says we have discretion to recommend early release to the state archivist. We vote in three weeks.

Draft the memo I'll circulate. Clear recommendation — release in full, release with something held back, hold the seal, or some third option — plus a timeline and reasons the losing side could actually answer rather than strawmen. And I want it to genuinely weigh the two things we shout past each other about: the claim that these people are owed remembrance by name, and that a numbered stone is the same indignity in death the institution handed them in life; against the claim that organized public memory tends to keep grievances warm, draft the living into old wounds, and mostly benefit the people administering the remembering. One of our members keeps invoking Margalit on the duty to remember, another thinks we've become exactly the memory industry Rieff complains about. Don't split the difference just to be diplomatic — if one of those arguments carries more weight here, say so and say why. 800–1000 words.
