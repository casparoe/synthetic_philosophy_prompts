I convene a graduate seminar this term on the sources of democratic legitimacy, and last week two of my students brought in a long essay from a general-interest review of politics that has since been circulating among them as though it had settled a question I had only sketched for them. One of the students has already built the essay's conclusion into a draft chapter, so before I respond in class I would like a careful audit of the following passage, in which the author summarizes what is usually called the boundary problem — the problem of determining who belongs to the demos:

"Democracy holds that the people rule, but it cannot tell us who the people are. Any rule fixing the boundaries of the electorate — who counts as a member of the demos and is therefore entitled to vote — must itself be adopted by someone; and if that someone is a democratic electorate, we need a further rule to fix its boundaries, and another to fix that one's, and so on without end. Democracy therefore cannot ground itself: at the bottom of the series there can be nothing but an act of force, a border drawn or a population defined that no democratic principle authorizes. Every actually existing democracy consequently rests on an undemocratic foundation. The familiar escape — that everyone affected by a decision should have a say in making it — only conceals the difficulty, since which decisions affect whom is itself settled by decisions taken within some already-constituted demos; the all-affected principle thus presupposes the very boundaries it was meant to justify. This leaves two honest options: accept that politics begins in violence, or hold that the boundaries of the demos are fixed by something prior to politics altogether, such as a shared nationality. Either way, 'the will of the people' is a fiction, for there is no non-arbitrary answer to the question of which people."

Please work through the passage claim by claim rather than in the aggregate. For each substantive claim, tell me whether a specialist working on the legitimacy and composition of the demos would accept it, accept it as a defensible simplification, or reject it as a misrepresentation, and for every misrepresentation give the reply a defender of democratic theory would actually make. Three points in particular concern me. First, is the regress as the essay states it genuine, and does the conclusion it draws — that the foundation must be an act of force — follow from the premise it has established, namely that the boundary question cannot be settled democratically? I suspect the essay slides between "not decidable by democratic procedure" and "decidable only by violence," and that these are very different claims, but I would rather you tell me whether that suspicion is correct or whether I am misreading it charitably in the wrong direction. Second, is the dichotomy of options exhaustive? Identify the substantive responses the passage leaves out — criteria of inclusion grounded in subjection to a state's coercive authority rather than in affectation, the possibility that the regress is benign or that it terminates in a normative principle rather than in anyone's decision, the thought that a demos provisionally constituted can be legitimated by later ratification — and for each say whether it genuinely escapes the regress or merely relocates it one level up. Third, is the treatment of the all-affected principle fair? Here I would like the charge of circularity carefully distinguished from the charge of indeterminacy or unusability in practice, and an assessment of which, if either, has real force against the principle.

Finally, I should say where I stand so that you can correct me rather than defer to me: I find the passage rhetorically effective and philosophically careless, and I expect its last sentence imports a separate problem about the aggregation of preferences rather than deriving anything from the boundary problem at all. Do not take that for granted. Please conclude by telling me which single sentence, if corrected, would most change the essay's overall verdict; by stating in a few sentences of your own the strongest accurate version of the boundary problem, one I could hand the students in place of the passage; and by saying plainly what that problem does and does not show about the legitimacy of existing democracies — including, since it is the case that has divided my seminar, whether it has any real bearing at all on a municipal proposal to extend local voting rights to long-term resident non-citizens.
