In arguments about the British Raj, I keep meeting the same phrase: what we need is the balance sheet of empire — set the railways, the courts, the unified administration, the irrigation works and the universities on one side, the famines, the deindustrialisation and the drain of revenue on the other, and then see which column is heavier. The people who say it want very different things from it. One insists that no criticism of imperial rule is admissible until the critic has produced a netted account; another happily concedes that particular institutions were beneficial while holding that the whole was extractive; a third says the phrase is empty because the two columns are not commensurable.

I would like a menu, not a verdict on empire: lay out the distinct and precise claims this phrase could be making, and say for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false. Be explicit about what each reading commits a person to on the following points — the unit of account (benefits and costs to whom, measured in what, aggregated how); the baseline that makes an entry an asset rather than a transfer, which is what is at stake when someone observes that the railways were laid out to serve export and military movement and that the fixed return to their British shareholders was guaranteed out of Indian revenues; the counterfactual, since an entry only counts if the alternative regime would not have produced it; the treatment of time, including whether a benefit in the 1860s can offset a cost in the 1940s and whether any discounting is involved; and the identity of the reporting entity.

On that last point, someone in the argument said that a balance sheet is a statement of one entity's resources and of the claims against them at a date, with the residual belonging to the owners, and that the metaphor therefore settles in advance who owns the enterprise — with the consequence that "was it a net benefit to India?" is not a balance-sheet question at all. I did not follow. Say whether that objection has force, and what it would imply about the phrase.

Finally, tell me which of your readings the first speaker needs if the phrase is to do the work he puts it to — making a netted account a precondition of legitimate criticism — and whether any reading genuinely supports that use. Close by sketching how the same underlying question could be posed once the ledger metaphor is given up, and what is gained or lost by posing it that way instead.
