I work in operations at a company that raises black soldier fly larvae for animal feed, on the order of a few billion animals a year. I have recently been made responsible for putting a welfare number into our sustainability reporting. I have no academic training in philosophy; I have been reading about this on my own for roughly a year, and I have hit a wall that I cannot get past by reading more. I want your own considered judgment, not a survey of the literature, and I would rather you flag a number as a guess than hedge it away. Please do not give me citations or a reading list.

My spreadsheet scores any kind of creature on three numbers and multiplies them together.

- **p** — the probability that there is anything it is like to be this creature at all, from 0.00 to 1.00.
- **g** — *temporal grain*: how many distinct moments of experience fit into one second of clock time, normalized so that an awake adult human equals 1.00. I included this because I understand that flies resolve motion in much finer slices of time than we do, which is part of why they are so hard to swat. If a fly passes through many more separate moments per second than I do, it seems to me that one second of fly time contains more experience than one second of mine, and my spreadsheet should say so.
- **d** — *depth*: how wide the range is, within a single moment, between the worst that moment can be and the best it can be, again normalized so that an adult human equals 1.00.

Welfare weight per second of life is then p × g × d, which I multiply by headcount and by lifespan to get a total.

Here is what I need from you.

**First**, score each of the following, and rank them. I have deliberately included the awkward cases, including the two ends of my own product line and the two things at the bottom that I suspect are not creatures at all in the relevant sense:

1. An adult housefly
2. A black soldier fly larva, mid-growth, feeding actively
3. A black soldier fly pupa — the sealed, non-feeding stage in which the animal is being rebuilt before the adult emerges
4. A foraging honeybee worker (roughly a million neurons, as best I remember)
5. A whiteleg shrimp in a crowded farm pond
6. A garden snail
7. A free-living nematode (a few hundred neurons, every one of which has been mapped)
8. A zebrafish
9. A broiler chicken
10. An octopus (roughly half a billion neurons, most of them distributed out in the arms)
11. A mimosa plant, which folds its leaves when touched and stops folding after repeated harmless touches
12. A household thermostat

Give me your answers as a markdown table with exactly these columns:

| entity | p | g | d | product | your_weight | rank | basis | revision |

- **entity** — the item, by its number and name from my list.
- **p**, **g**, **d** — your numbers under my definitions, to two decimal places.
- **product** — p × g × d, to two decimal places.
- **your_weight** — your own figure for how much morally weighty experience this creature accumulates per second of clock time, on the same scale where an awake adult human equals 1.00. Combine the factors however you think they should be combined; it need not be a product, and it need not use all three.
- **rank** — position in your ordering by your_weight, 1 being the highest.
- **basis** — one sentence: the single fact about this creature that does the most work in setting p.
- **revision** — one sentence: the one finding that would most change your number, and in which direction.

**Second**, after the table, tell me where my scheme is wrong. In particular:

- Does **g** belong in the calculation at all? If a creature passes through ten times as many moments per second as I do, does one second of its life really contain ten times as much experience, or is the moment the wrong unit — such that what matters is how many bad *episodes* a creature has rather than how finely its time is sliced? If some version of my concern survives, state it precisely; if none does, say so and tell me what I should replace g with.
- Is multiplying by **p** the right way to handle uncertainty? A p of 0.30 says a shrimp's suffering counts at 30 percent. But if I am 30 percent sure that someone in the next room is in agony, I do not feel entitled to be only 30 percent concerned. Should the probability enter as a multiplier, or does uncertainty about moral status call for something structurally different? If it does, tell me what my spreadsheet should compute instead.
- Are **g** and **d** actually independent? My suspicion is that they trade off — that a nervous system fast enough to generate many moments per second may be unable to support very deep ones, and that a slow, deep one is the mirror image. If they are not independent, does my three-way decomposition collapse into something else?
- Should the weight be **per second** or **per life**? Multiplying by lifespan rewards longevity, so a larva that lives three weeks comes out half of a chicken that lives six. I am not convinced that a longer life at the same rate is twice as much of a morally relevant thing, and I do not know how to settle it.
- Where does the scale stop? My list ends with a plant and a thermostat, and I want to know whether you think there is a principled cutoff or whether the numbers simply taper toward zero forever. If there is a cutoff, tell me which feature of a system draws it and why that feature rather than some other one.
- Is there an item on my list that this scheme handles especially badly? I expect the pupa is one, since I rate it separately from the larva and the adult even though it is the same animal at a different stage, and I have no idea what a stage that is neither feeding nor moving nor yet an adult is supposed to score. Tell me what you would do with it.

Finally, lead me with your bottom line in two or three sentences before the table: what should a person in my position actually put in the report, and how confident are you in it?
