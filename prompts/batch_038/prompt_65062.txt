I edit a series of 700-word biographical readings used by secondary-school humanities teachers. We take about six proposals a month, and each receives one decision from a three-person review panel: run as pitched, run with a specified reframing, or reject. The writers are journalists who get roughly two hours among a subject's sources before drafting, so the procedure has to be runnable in that time and has to yield reasons the panel can check rather than tastes it can only vote on.

The series exists for moral learning from a life rather than for biography as such, and that is where we keep failing. A reading on a celebrated nurse was withdrawn after a teacher pointed out that the incident the whole piece turned on survives only in an admiring life published by her own order. Our house guidance — admire the deed rather than the person, note the flaws briefly, let the reader draw the moral — collapses on the hard cases. Hagiography and Plutarch's practice are both ways of making a life teach: he remarks at the opening of the Life of Alexander that he is writing lives rather than histories, and he reads character off slight acts instead of great ones, then closes most of his pairs with a formal comparison. Neither approach is the same as reporting what happened, and I want the procedure to be explicit about what each can support.

Please write that procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a reject, and where it hands the decision back to the panel because the question is one of value rather than fact. It must separate the different uses a life can be put to — evidence for a claim about a trait, object of admiration, template for imitation — and attach the appropriate evidential standard to each; it must handle sources that are admiring or hostile by construction, including cases where the only surviving account is hagiographic, and say what such an account is legitimately evidence of; it must handle subjects whose finest feature and their worst wrong spring from the same disposition; and it must flag every step that is a judgment call wearing a procedural costume.

Then run it on three proposals: a nineteenth-century factory reformer whose campaign rested on a paternalism he also enforced against his own workers' organizing; a medieval saint known only through a vita of miracles and an edifying death; and a twentieth-century geneticist whose science is sound and who campaigned successfully for compulsory sterilization. The third is the panel's favourite — tell me plainly if the procedure kills it.
