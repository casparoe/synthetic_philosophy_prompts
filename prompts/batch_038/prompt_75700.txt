I'm a history of science PhD student drafting a chapter that sets two familiar debunkings of period labels side by side, and I want to know whether they are really the same argument. First, the Middle Ages: the interval is said to be a retrospective invention, coined by people for whom the stretch between antiquity and their own moment had to be a gap, and whose coinage was pejorative — the darkness does the justificatory work. Second, the Scientific Revolution: the charge pressed by historians such as Shapin is that seventeenth-century natural philosophers did not take themselves to be living through any revolution at all, and that the label smuggles in a whiggish story of cumulative breakthrough.

Both are usually filed together as anachronistic periodization, but I suspect the filing hides a difference. The first label is negative and self-serving, minted from inside the very culture of renewal that it presupposes; the second is flattering and conferred at a distance by admirers. Does who coined a period label, and in which direction the coinage flatters, change what debunking it can establish? Compare the two as arguments — what each takes as evidence, what its conclusion commits it to, and where the analogy between them breaks — and then say which is more vulnerable to the reply that a label can be anachronistic and still mark a real discontinuity, and whether negative and positive period labels carry different burdens of proof against that reply.

One further wrinkle I'd like folded into the comparison: 'middle' is relational, so nothing counts as a middle age except against some claimed rebirth, which suggests the medieval case cannot be debunked without also undermining the Renaissance in a way that the Scientific Revolution case does not require of, say, 'Enlightenment.' Is that asymmetry real, and does it matter?

Please make the underlying issue philosophically explicit — whether historical kinds must have been recognized by their own members in order to be real — rather than offering a survey of the historiography.
