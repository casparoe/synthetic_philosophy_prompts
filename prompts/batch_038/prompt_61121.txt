I'm on a hospital committee that decides what gets covered, and the administrators have one line they reach for every time someone says a coverage call is a value judgment: the body is a machine, disease is a broken part, and doctors are engineers — so deciding what to fix is technical, not ethical, the way nobody accuses a mechanic of imposing his values when he swaps out a worn brake pad. They lean on it hardest exactly where the "part" is contested: cochlear implants for deaf kids, growth hormone for a short but otherwise healthy child, treating something that's statistically typical for a person's age and sex but still wrecks their life. Can you audit it? Give me the correspondence table first — machine to organism, the designer's spec to whatever "normal functioning" is supposed to be, the broken part to the pathology, the engineer to the clinician — then where the mapping gives out. I'm most interested in two spots: whether an organism's functions really are like an artifact's functions given that nobody wrote the spec and the goal gets picked by the person describing it, and whether you can even fix a reference class of "normal" without already deciding which bodies count as healthy. And then: does the value-neutrality conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole weight? If it doesn't, what would be a better analogy for what medicine actually does — one that doesn't just smuggle in the opposite verdict?
