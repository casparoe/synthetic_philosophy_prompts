In our population-ethics group we have spent the better part of two years on a dispute I now suspect is not the dispute we think it is. The setup: call a view *lexical threshold negative utilitarianism* (LTNU) if it holds (i) that pains divide into two tiers by character rather than duration — tier-1 pains being of a kind that disorganizes the person, destroying the capacity to orient around anything else, while tier-2 pains (a headache, a bad year, a broken arm that heals) leave that capacity intact; (ii) that no finite amount of pleasure, distributed over any finite number of lives, outweighs any single instance of tier-1 pain; and (iii) that within tier 2, pleasures and pains are commensurable at some exchange rate. Both camps in the group are consequentialists, both accept the same empirical psychology of pain, and nobody has moved.

The thresholders argue from a pairwise case: it seems plainly better to spare one person sustained agony than to give a billion people a mildly pleasant surprise, and no finite repetition of the second appears to change that. They add that any specific exchange rate between agony and small pleasures is undiscoverable in principle, so a view that posits one is arbitrary, and that arbitrariness in a theory of value is a fatal defect. Finally they insist that tier-1 pain is not merely more of the same as ordinary pain: because it forecloses everything else the person might care about, the difference is one of kind, and a kind difference licenses a lexical difference.

The continuumists reply that pain intensity and its disorganizing effects both vary continuously, so there is no non-arbitrary cut-point; a sharp threshold either invites a sorites (n minutes of agony versus n+1) or imports an implausible precision into value itself. They press the implications: if threshold pain can never be traded off, then a world containing one person in tier-1 agony plus a trillion lives of unimprovable bliss is worse than a world containing two such people and nobody else, which they find incredible. And they argue the view is self-defeating as a guide: since every policy that carries any risk of producing tier-1 pain becomes incomparable with every alternative, LTNU issues no recommendations at all, so the thresholder had better accept a very high but finite exchange rate plus honest uncertainty about its value.

My working hypothesis is that this is two disagreements running in parallel under one label — a formal question about whether the betterness relation may be incomplete or must be complete, and a decision-theoretic question about what to do under moral uncertainty when one candidate axiology treats certain outcomes as untradeable — and that the word "lexical" is doing double duty across them.

I want the impasse diagnosed, not adjudicated: do not tell me which side is right. Consider at minimum whether the dispute is verbal, turning on "outweigh," "better than," or "unit"; whether it turns on vagueness, and in particular whether vagueness in pain intensity transfers to the value relation or is absorbed by it; whether a hidden empirical question is doing the work, namely whether tier-1 pain is a natural kind with a genuine discontinuity, which would make the threshold a discovery rather than a stipulation; whether the two sides are treating different sorts of intuition as data, pairwise comparisons versus whole-world comparisons; and whether one side is covertly arguing about action-guidance while the other is arguing about what is true. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding, a reframing of the question — or argue that none should be expected and why. If some part of the dispute is merely verbal, identify which part and state the residual substantive dispute in one sentence.
