I'm drafting the intro to a workshop paper on preference learning and I built this argument to attack the whole project, and now I can't tell whether its a devastating objection or a sophism. It goes:

(1) To say an agent prefers A to B is just to say its behavior maximizes some reward function that ranks A above B. Preferences are revealed by choice; that's all "preference" means.
(2) Given any finite stretch of behavior, you can always find a reward function that makes it (close to) optimal — just let the reward track what actually happened.
(3) So any behavior is compatible with any preferences, provided you pick the reward function afterwards.
(4) IRL says the human's reward function is the one their behavior was maximizing. Given (2)–(3), no possible human behavior could falsify any claim about their reward.
(5) A claim that no evidence could rule out says nothing.
(6) So IRL doesn't recover preferences, it relabels behavior in reward-shaped language.
(7) Hence "human preferences" is not a fact about humans independent of what they do, and aligning a system to them is empty unless you state the preferences some other way.

I'd rather keep (1) and (7) if I can — the paper's thesis is that alignment talk needs non-behavioral inputs. But I suspect something slid between (2) and (4), or maybe at (5), and I can't pin it down. Which step do you reject, why did it look so innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically: does restricting the reward to be simple/smooth/stationary across situations actually block the argument, or does it just relocate the arbitrariness into the choice of hypothesis class — and if it relocates it, is that a real cost or is that just what all inductive inference looks like? And what does someone actually running IRL rely on that this argument never mentions?
