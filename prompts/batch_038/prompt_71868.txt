Retired after 35 years in climate modelling, mostly ocean–atmosphere coupling, and lately I've fallen into reading history and philosophy in the evenings, which my wife finds hilarious.

Here's my problem, and I'd like you to actually take a side on it rather than survey one.

For most of my working life I did a particular kind of thing. When a system has processes running at very different speeds, you seperate them: write down the fast part, notice the ratio of timescales is a small dimensionless number, average over the fast motion, and end up with a slow equation in which the fast stuff survives only as an effective drag or a noise term. Going the other way, in the laser-instability work I did as a postdoc, the rule looks opposite but is the same trick — whichever modes grow or decay slowly end up dictating to the fast-relaxing ones, so you throw away nearly all the degrees of freedom and keep two or three slow amplitudes that carry the whole pattern.

Now I read Braudel's big Mediterranean book and he seems to be doing exactly this, with three timescales instead of two: a near-geological layer of sea, mountains, plains and routes; a middle layer of populations, prices, trades and states running in conjunctures of decades; and a top layer of events and indivduals — battles, treaties, kings — which he treats as foam on the surface, not really explicable and not worth much attention. (That last bit is my bad paraphrase, don't quote me on it.)

So compare the two things properly. Is Braudel's three-storey picture the same methodological move as timescale separation in dynamical systems, applied to something messier, or is it borrowing only the feel of that move while lacking every ingredient that makes the physics version work? Be concrete about the ingredients. In the physics case there's a small parameter you can point at; there's a derivation of the slow equation from the fast one; you can bound the error; and you know the conditions under which the separation breaks down — resonance, timescales crossing near a threshold, noise kicking the system between basins. Does anything in Braudel play those roles? What is a "structure" doing that a slow variable in an averaged equation isn't, or is it just a slow variable with better prose?

Second thing I can't settle: which way does the determination run? In the synergetics version the slow enslaves the fast, which is Braudel's own prejudice. But in the averaging version the fast processes generate the slow one — the drag is a residue of what you averaged out — so the arrow is reversed. Does it matter which of those a historian is implicitly using when they say the geography made the trade route inevitable?

And a third: when I said an event wouldn't matter, I could compute what would have to change for it to matter. When a historian says an event was surface foam, what is the corresponding check, and is there one at all?

Please commit. I don't want "both thinkers offer valuable perspectives with important differences." Tell me which parts of the analogy genuinely transfer and which are decorative, rank them, and if you think Braudel is doing something categorically different from multiscale analysis, say what that different thing is and defend it. Reasons, not a menu.
