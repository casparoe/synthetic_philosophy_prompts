The question (take-home, 800 words):

*A hospital deploys a system trained by inverse reinforcement learning on eighteen months of triage decisions made by junior doctors allocating urgent scan slots. The designers claim that, because the system learned from behaviour rather than from questionnaires, it now encodes the clinicians' genuine preferences about who should be scanned, and that feeding it more of the same data will converge on those preferences. Explain what such a system can and cannot recover from behaviour alone, and assess the designers' claim.*

The mark scheme, out of 25 (written by the convenor, it's a bit thin):

- 8 marks: correctly identifies what IRL can and cannot recover from behaviour alone
- 6 marks: engages the assumption that the demonstrator is acting optimally, and says what that assumption costs
- 6 marks: deals with the belief/preference problem, not just the reward ambiguity
- 5 marks: actually assesses the designers' claim rather than describing the machinery

The answer:

*IRL is the problem of recovering a reward function from observed behaviour. We suppose the demonstrator is acting, at least approximately, optimally with respect to some reward R that we do not know, and we then search for the R that best explains the trajectories we have seen.*

*The first thing to say is that the problem is ill-posed. Many different rewards make the same policy optimal. In the discounted setting, multiplying R by a positive constant or adding a constant to it changes nothing about which policy is best, and there is a larger family of shaping rewards — those built from a potential function over states — which also leave the optimal policy untouched. So the system cannot recover a unique reward from the doctors' choices; it can only recover the set of rewards consistent with those choices.*

*The designers are wrong, then, if by "encodes the clinicians' genuine preferences" they mean it has pinned down one preference ordering. But there are worse problems than the ambiguity.*

*The main one is the optimality assumption itself. Junior doctors are not optimal agents. They are tired, they anchor on the case they have just seen, and they work with incomplete information about the patient in front of them. If we fit a reward on the assumption that they are maximising it, we will encode their failures as though they were their tastes. A doctor who systematically downgrades patients with a history of drug dependency is not expressing a preference for worse outcomes for those patients; she is expressing a bias, or a lack of time. IRL cannot, from the inside, tell bias from taste — both look identical in the data, because both are just behaviour.*

*Second, the reward it recovers is only characterised on the states the doctors actually visited. Eighteen months in a busy district hospital gives you a great many ordinary Tuesdays and almost nothing of the mass-casualty incident, the scanner failure, or the night with no radiologist on site. In those states the fitted reward is an extrapolation, and nothing in the mathematics guarantees that it extrapolates sensibly.*

*On the designers' second claim, that more of the same data will converge on the preferences: this is directionally right but overstated. More demonstrations, and more varied ones, do shrink the set of rewards consistent with the behaviour — you learn more about which trade-offs the doctors actually make. In the limit, with unlimited data covering the whole state space and a demonstrator who is genuinely optimal, the consistent set collapses to a single reward function, which is the thing the system should then maximise. So the practical advice is that eighteen months is not enough and the data is not varied enough; the hospital should keep collecting, and deliberately seek out rare and unusual cases.*

*There is a further objection people make, from decision theory. Going back to Savage's representation theorem, beliefs and desires are derived together from choices over acts, so choice data on its own does not tell you which of the two is doing the work. A doctor who declines to book a scan may think the patient will not survive it (a belief), or may think the slot is better spent elsewhere (a desire). But IRL is not trapped by this. A modern system infers the doctor's beliefs — typically as a posterior over the environment's dynamics and over hidden features of the patient — at the same time as it infers the reward, and it infers them with separate components of the model. Since the beliefs are estimated independently of the reward, the Savage problem does not bite here; whatever ambiguity remains is a matter of fitting the two components jointly in practice rather than a limit in principle.*

*Finally, on the slogan that you should watch what people do rather than listen to what they say. There is something right in it: stated preferences are cheap talk and are distorted by what the speaker thinks the asker wants to hear, whereas a triage decision costs the doctor something. But the reward function IRL hands back is not the doctors' utility function in the textbook sense. Humans violate the axioms of expected utility, for one thing; and for another, the recovered R is a device for generating actions rather than a report on what anyone values. So the designers' language of "genuine preferences" is doing more work than the method can support, even where the method works.*
