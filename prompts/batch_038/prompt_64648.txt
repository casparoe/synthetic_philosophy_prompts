I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I'm drafting a piece on what it can mean to say that a large language model knows something it fails to say. My central source, a researcher who studies model evaluation, keeps reaching for one image, and I want to know how much load it will bear before I let it stand in print.

His image: knowledge in these systems is dispositional, and a disposition is like a program sitting on a disk that isn't running. The code is entirely present and entirely inert; a prompt is the call that executes it; when the execution goes wrong, that is usually a fact about the arguments you passed rather than about what was stored. From this he concludes that behavioural benchmarks systematically understate what these models know, because failing to answer is failing to call correctly, and because not being able to say something is no evidence at all of not having it.

Two things I picked up while reporting sit badly with the image, and I cannot tell whether they are fatal to it or whether they are, as he maintains, exactly what it predicts. The first is that eliciting an answer from a model is not observation from outside: the question alters the state it was meant to find. I have watched a system handle a fact competently inside a legal hypothetical and then deny the same fact flatly once I asked it directly, as though the asking had cancelled the capacity. The second is a pair of cases philosophers have long pressed against the idea that a disposition is nothing over and above a list of if-then conditionals — a wire that stays live even though an attached device would deaden it the moment anything made contact, and packing that prevents a fragile thing from breaking when struck without making it any less fragile. I have also been told that the standard repair is to write into each conditional a clause guaranteeing that the object keeps its inner state right through the stimulus, but I cannot work out whether that saves the claim or quietly empties it.

So: set out the correspondence explicitly — disk, stored code, call, arguments, runtime, crash — and then sort the disanalogies into the ones that bear on the inference he wants and the ones that are merely picturesque. Tell me what the image smuggles in about storage, inspection, and separability that the target does not supply, and what the wire and the packing do to it once they are mapped onto prompting instead of circuits and foam. He has a fallback: the right comparison, he says, is a habit rather than a file, since a habit is fully present in the intervals between its exercises. Audit that too, and tell me which of the two images is carrying the conclusion and which is decoration.

I don't want the options laid out for me to choose among. Commit to a verdict — whether his conclusion survives the disanalogies in the strong form he gives it, or only in some weaker form you will state and defend — with your reasons; and if you think both images fail, give me the one you would put in print and say what it buys that these do not.
