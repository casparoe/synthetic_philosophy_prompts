I am working on a paper about the debate between Ryle's anti-intellectualism and the intellectualist analysis on which knowing how to do something is a species of knowing that some proposition is a way of doing it. I do not want a survey of that debate — I know its landmarks and I can find the standard moves without your help. What I want is a rigorous argument for a thesis of my own that I believe is right but have not been able to defend properly, and I would like you to make the case as strongly as it can honestly be made.

The thesis concerns the role that ability plays in the dialectic. Both camps habitually appeal to ability as though it were a fixed point outside the theory: a natural kind, independent of either account, which each side can point to in order to constrain the other. The anti-intellectualist says that any analysis which permits a person to know how to do something she cannot do has thereby refuted itself. The intellectualist replies that the entailment from knowing how to being able fails in the other direction too, and that no account which requires ability in order to be vindicated can explain the cases she offers. My claim is that both of these appeals are unreliable in the same way, and that this is why the debate has stalled. Ability is not one relation. At least four distinct notions travel under the word: (i) reliability of success under normal conditions; (ii) the capacity to bring one's own performance under intentional control while it is happening, including recovery from perturbation; (iii) the capacity to be guided by one's own representation of the task, rather than by habit, prompting, or luck; (iv) the standing competence that survives the temporary or permanent loss of the bodily means to exercise it. The canonical cases do not separate knowing how from ability; each of them separates knowing how from one of these four while leaving the others intact. And each camp has a standing habit of reaching for whichever member of the family makes the other camp's cases look decisive and its own look safe. On my view, then, the disagreement is not, as it presents itself, a disagreement about the extension of knowing-how ascriptions. It is a disagreement about which notion of ability is permitted to do the constraining — a disagreement the two sides have never articulated, and so have never had the chance to settle.

Consider, for the purposes of the argument, three cases. A technician has been reliably and correctly instructed, step by step, in a repair he has never performed; he can recite the sequence, answer questions about likely complications, and identify errors in someone else's account, but he has never handled the tools and cannot carry the repair out. A pianist who has lost the use of her hands retains an intact command of her repertoire: she can dictate fingerings, adjudicate between interpretations, and detect the smallest infelicity in another player's performance. A patient who has acquired a perceptual-motor skill through repeated training after severe amnesia performs it reliably, has no memory whatever of the training, cannot describe the procedure, and denies that he can do it.

Please argue for the thesis above. Specifically:

1. Give the reasons for it, in order of weight, and state plainly what the conclusion amounts to if it succeeds.
2. Show that the four-way distinction does the work rather than merely restating it: for each of the three cases, say which notion of ability it dissociates from knowing how and which it leaves intact, and explain why that assignment is not simply fitted to the verdict I want.
3. Answer the objection that my pluralism is intellectualism in disguise — that the intellectualist's appeal to different ways of grasping a proposition already accommodates all three cases, and that splitting ability into four has added nothing but vocabulary.
4. Answer the objection that multiplying notions of ability is a licence to explain anything: say what discipline determines which notion is in play in a given case, or concede that there is none.
5. Say what, if anything, remains substantively in dispute between the two camps once my thesis is granted; and if nothing does, say whether that is a deflationary achievement or a reason to distrust the thesis, given that a diagnosis which dissolves both sides' central data is itself suspect.
6. Describe a kind of case that would falsify the thesis — one that would show ability to be theoretically neutral after all.

Argue the position rather than balancing it. Be explicit about where you are advocating and where you are assessing: tell me which step you would withdraw first under pressure, and which of the four notions you think will not hold its boundary against the others. If you conclude that the thesis is false, say so and say why, but only after you have given it the best case you can construct — a case you have not built yourself is not worth my reading. And if you think the request is ill-posed, say that at the outset instead of complying politely: if you think the four notions are four descriptions of one thing selected to make my thesis look true, or if you think ability was never doing the theoretical work I claim it was, so that my diagnosis targets a straw version of both camps, I would far rather have that objection than a well-mannered defence of something confused.
