Our museum's most popular painting might turn out to be two paintings, and the board wants a recommendation before the spring meeting. For context, here's how our collections committee has ruled on this kind of thing over the years — roughly chronological:

1. In the eighties we bought a panel as an original by the big name of our Northern school, call him M. Dendrochronology and pigment analysis in the 2000s showed it was a twentieth-century fake. We didn't deaccession it and we didn't shove it in storage. We kept it hanging with a long wall text naming the forger, whose identity we'd run down, and explaining how he'd worked.

2. We own an 1860s student copy after M's famous altarpiece, bought deliberately as a document of taste. Credit line reads "after M, nineteenth-century copyist." Nobody has ever pretended otherwise.

3. Two years ago we accessioned a big contemporary installation where the artist never laid a hand on anything: a fabrication shop built every component from her files while she supervised. Sole credit to her, fabricators acknowledged in the object label.

4. Our ancient marble torso has an eighteenth-century head, arms and nose, by a restorer who was himself a well-regarded sculptor. Somebody proposed stripping those off and showing the bare fragment. We refused. The label credits the ancient work and names the restorer.

5. We accepted a bronze cast posthumously from the sculptor's own plaster, with the estate's authorization, on the condition that the credit line give the actual casting date instead of implying a lifetime cast.

6. A donor offered us a drawing he wanted hung as an M. We were pretty convinced it was by M's assistant. We said we'd take it only under the assistant attribution, he walked, and we let it go.

Now the mess. The popular picture — with us since the fifties, always catalogued honestly as a workshop copy after M — went into the studio for routine cleaning. Cross sections and X-ray say the panel, the ground and something like 70 percent of the paint really are seventeenth century, and quite possibly M's own hand or his closest assistant's. But the face of the central figure, the signature and a strip along the bottom edge are nineteenth-century overpaint, laid on by an unknown jobbing restorer, almost certainly to convert a workshop copy into a signed original. The kicker: the X-ray suggests M's original face is still partly there underneath, but abraded. So removing the nineteenth century would recover genuine old paint and leave a hole where the face belongs, and our own conservator would have to inpaint it — adding a third hand to the object, one that's alive and on our payroll.

What should we do? Concretely: strip the later additions or keep them, and what does the credit line name — one hand, two, or three? Which of our own precedents is the closest analogue to this, and which is the one that'll get quoted against us? And what rule do you think we've actually been running all along — is there a single principle that generates 1 through 6, or two or three that just happen to agree on everything we've faced so far? If they agree on the old cases and split on this one, tell me what each predicts here and which one you'd bet the committee follows once the donors start calling. If the precedents genuinely don't decide it, be blunt about what principle we'd be adopting by choosing either way; I'd rather hear it now than discover it in the newspaper. Last thing: give me one further hypothetical object — something you could imagine being offered to us — that would cleanly separate the candidate rules from each other, so we can test ourselves on it before we've staked the collection's reputation on this one. Oh, and the X-ray suggest the underlayer is unstable in one corner, so if your recommendation assumes we can safely remove the overpaint, say what changes if we can't.
