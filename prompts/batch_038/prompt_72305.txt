I am a second-year graduate student auditing a seminar on the Wittgensteinian inheritance in the philosophy of mind, and my response paper is due in three weeks on Norman Malcolm's *Dreaming* (1959). I have read the book twice and I think I understand it, but I have noticed that everything I want to say against it sounds clever in my head and collapses into a misreading when I write it down, so I would like to rehearse against a live opponent rather than against my own reconstruction. Please play Malcolm himself, in the first person, as he was at the time of the book's publication: an ordinary-language philosopher trained by Wittgenstein, impatient with what he takes to be metaphysical pictures smuggled in as facts, and quite willing to be disagreeable about it. You may take in whatever I report to you about later work — laboratory studies of sleep, the essay by a younger American philosopher who arrives at a conclusion resembling yours by empirical means — but treat these as new information I am handing you, not as texts you have read, and respond to them in your own idiom rather than in theirs.

Hold the following commitments and argue from them rather than merely asserting them: that a dream is not an occurrence taking place during sleep; that the concept is fixed by what happens on waking, namely the impression the dreamer has, so that the question whether anything seemed to happen to him while he was out of contact with his surroundings is one no observation could settle; and that the temptation to think of dreams as private experiences unfolding in the dark is a piece of bad grammar with a Cartesian pedigree. You may concede subsidiary points freely — that dream impressions can be false, that "I had a dream last night" is a loose and relaxed way of speaking, that the sleeping brain is far from inert — but you may not concede the central claim. Avoid anachronistic vocabulary, do not summarize my own argument back to me as a way of stalling, and do not step out of character to offer study advice; if I type "stop," drop the role and give me a brief debrief on where my objections were weak. Keep each reply between roughly 150 and 250 words, address me as you would a graduate student who has come to your office hours in Ithaca, and close every turn with one pointed question of your own.

Here is my opening move. Your conclusion — that dreams are not experiences, that nothing happens to the dreamer while he sleeps — has since been reached by someone working in the opposite direction entirely. Sleep researchers wake people from rapid-eye-movement periods and collect reports; when the same subject is woken a second time ten minutes later, he very often gives a longer narrative whose opening stretch matches what he reported the first time, as though the earlier report were the beginning of a story that kept getting written. The philosopher who presses this material argues that the report is not a recollection of an experience that was already there but the point at which the content gets settled. So you and he end up in the same place: no experienced dream lying back behind the waking impression. Now put yourself in my shoes and tell me which of three things this is. Either you were right for reasons that do no work, because all the arguing was done by the awakening protocol and the grammar was decoration; or you were not really saying what he says, in which case your thesis is compatible with everything the labs found and buys us nothing; or the empirical story, if it is true, shows there was never any conceptual barrier to locating the dream in sleep at all, and your argument simply refused to hear what everyone means when they say something happened to them in the night. And while you are choosing, answer me this: on your account, when the second report extends the first, is that one dream impression or two? If the later one corrects the earlier, what exactly does it correct, given that on your view there was nothing there to be gotten right or wrong in the first place?
