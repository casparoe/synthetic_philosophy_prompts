Right, so — Ive been trying to build a session of my spring philosophy-of-maths seminar around the strategy-stealing argument, mostly because it looks to me like the cleanest case we have of a proof that hands you an existence claim while handing you nothing you could actually use. I've sketched a framing for the class and I want someone to tell me whether it survives contact, because I have a nagging feeling that at least two of the steps are dodgy and I can't yet say which.

Here's the chain as I've got it. Strategy-stealing shows the first player in Hex has a winning strategy without exhibiting it — the whole thing turns on the fact that an extra stone of your own colour can never hurt you, so if the second player had a winning strategy the first player could make an arbitrary opening move and then steal it, contradiction. Fine. But the very same reductio looks like it goes through for chess and for Go, so all three games are in the same epistemic boat as checkers: settled in principle, winner known, just waiting on somebody with a big enough machine to write the strategy out. And then I want to tell the students that this is precisely the situation the intuitionists were complaining about, so that a Hex position isn't really won or lost by either side until someone constructs the winning line — that on this reading strategy-stealing is a reductio *of* classical logic rather than an application of it, and that's the interesting bit, that's the thing worth a whole seminar.

So: where does it break? I'd like the diagnosis done properly rather than a polite nod, because I don't want to stand in front of eighteen third-years defending a chain of inference I can't articulate myself. In particular I'm unsure whether the transfer from Hex to the other two games is legitimate, and whether my intuitionist move is doing anything at all or is just rhetoric dressed up — if the games in question are finite and there's a straightforward induction over the tree, does the intuitionist even have a purchase here, or have I imported a worry from the infinite case where it doesn't bite? And if there's a better hook for the session than the one I've got, say so bluntly; I'd rather rebuild the week than polish a bad argument.

Don't bother sending me off to the literature, I know roughly where it lives and I'm not after a reading list — what I want is the conceptual lay of the land. Also, could you write it in British English please? I've got a couple of Bristol exchange students on the seminar and I've been asked to keep the handouts consistent in spelling and terminology.
