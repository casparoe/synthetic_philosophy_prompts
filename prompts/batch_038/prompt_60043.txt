ok so I've been trying to get a grip on Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result and the common prior assumption, and every example I run into is coin flips, cards, or urns, which feels like it dodges the question I actually care about: whether two people who really do share a prior can hold onto a disagreement in ordinary life.

Build me a thought experiment to spec. Two professionals — pick whatever realistic setting you like, an audit team, an ER, a forecasting desk, a structural engineering firm — who (a) genuinely share a common prior about some factual question with money or safety riding on it, because they were trained on the same base rates and run the same model, (b) get genuinely different private evidence, and (c) end up with different posteriors that each of them is aware of, and still don't converge. The mechanism matters more than the dressing: the failure has to come from the disclosure channel being imperfect in a specific, boring way — each of them ends up seeing the other's number, but neither is sure the other has seen thier's — so the posteriors get to some finite level of mutual knowledge and never become common knowledge.

Constraints:
- the scenario itself in one or two paragraphs of plain prose, no formalism yet
- no coins, cards, lotteries, urns, prisoners, or trolleys; a normal workplace
- both agents are sincere competent Bayesians. no motivated reasoning, no ambiguity aversion, no stubbornness, and no "well actually their priors were different all along" cheat
- nothing exotic, no predictors or demons or sci-fi tech. the disclosure device has to be something a firm would plausibly really use
- you need a non-cheesy reason why they can't just ask each other "hey did you get my number?", because if they can, the whole thing collapses

Then do the work:

1. Give me the state space and each agent's information partition, informally is fine, and show the numbers: what each believes before the exchange and what each believes after it. And be careful here — seeing the other person's estimate is itself evidence, so don't just hand-wave that they keep their original values.
2. Spell out exactly what's known at each level. Who knows what, who knows that who knows what, and precisely where the chain runs out.
3. Controlled comparison: hold everything else fixed and swap in a genuinely public announcement. Show that in that version the disagreement can't survive, and say what the two are forced to do instead.
4. Name the single feature of the original case that's doing the work, and tell me what happens to both verdicts if I take it out.
5. Is the original case a counterexample to Aumann's theorem? I'm assuming no, but say why not, and then say what weaker and more interesting claim it *is* a counterexample to.
6. Play the skeptic. How would a defender of the common prior assumption attack my case? I want at least two real attacks — e.g. that no two working professionals ever actually share a prior in the sense the theorem needs, or that once you model the channel uncertainty properly the two of them aren't even disagreeing about the same event — and tell me which of the two you think actually lands.

The reason I want all this is to figure out whether the common prior assumption is doing any explanatory work in real disagreements, or whether the common knowledge condition is quietly carrying everything. So a case that just stipulates "and they never converge" without the structure generating it counts as a fail.
