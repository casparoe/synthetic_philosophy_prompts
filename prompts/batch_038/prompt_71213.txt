I write novels, and there's a new one out set on a drained fen that's been put back to wet — so the trust that did the restoration has asked me to speak at the official opening on Saturday. I've said yes and now I wish I hadn't.

Here's my problem, and it's a real one, not a pose. For the last couple of years I've been reading the argument — a few economists, some philosophers, the odd biologist — that habitat destruction might not be the straightforward catastrophe we assume. Most wild animals are r-strategists: huge numbers of offspring, almost all of whom die of starvation, exposure, parasitism or being eaten, usually young, usually fast. On that picture a fen that's been drained and ploughed carries a small fraction of the animals the wet fen did, and if average welfare sits anywhere near zero or below, fewer of them isn't worse *for them*. I dont fully believe it — I don't understand density dependence well enough, or how bad those deaths actually are, and I know the empirical case is young and argued in both directions. But I can't un-know it either, and it has ruined my ability to say the normal sentences. I can't stand up and tell 200 people that we've given these creatures better lives, or saved thousands of lives, or that the fen is somewhere everything can now thrive, or any of the balance-of-nature business. That would be a lie, I'd know it while I said it, and it'd show.

I also can't give them the gloomy version. Here's who's coming: donors, the parish council, a woman who's hand-reared orphaned hedgehogs for eleven years and will be in the second row, two farmers whose land drains into the site, a class of ten-year-olds who planted the reeds, and about four professional ecologists who'll catch anything sloppy about carrying capacity or r-selection and will enjoy catching it. Local paper too. If I say out loud that we might have made things worse for the animals in there, I've turned that woman's eleven years into a joke and cost the trust its next grant.

So: write the speech. Around seven minutes, 900–1000 words, British English throughout please. It has to be honest — nothing in it I'd have to retract if someone pressed me on the population biology afterwards — and it has to be something those people can stand in the wind and feel good about, without the good feeling resting on a premise they'd spot if they thought about it for ten seconds.

Banned: 'giving nature a voice', 'the balance of nature', 'every creature matters', 'for generations to come' unless you can make that mean something, and no grateful animals thanking us. No Schopenhauer either, no 'nature is a slaughterhouse', no hand-wringing. And please don't invent statistics — no percentages, no numbers of deaths, nothing I'd have to go and check, because I can't.

The move I think it needs: there's an honest claim available about the *distribution* of outcomes rather than the number of them. What we've actually bought is a landscape where the worst things that happen to animals here happen less often, and where the crashes are less cruel — even though I can't tell you how many animals that adds up to, or whether the total is better. Plus a distinction, held lightly and never stated technically, between 'this place is worth making' and 'this place is better for the animals in it'. If you can get the uncertainty itself to do work — we don't know, and that not-knowing is precisely why nobody is draining anything — that beats any confidence I could fake. And I want one moment that lands on the hedgehog woman specifically, in a way that makes what she does look like what it actually is rather than a well-meant mistake.

Afterwards, in a few lines, tell me which three sentences are load-bearing and what I say if one of the ecologists corners me about them by the tea urn.
