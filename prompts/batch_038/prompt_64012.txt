I'm auditing a graduate seminar on collective harm this term, and instead of arguing from theory I've spent the last few weeks writing down what I actually think about particular cases, mostly ones where I happened to know the background facts. I want to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to — not the standard map of the literature on whether individual acts make a difference, which I know well enough, but whatever view I'm implicitly committed to by the pattern below. Here are the cases, in roughly the order I ran into them, with my gut verdict on each.

1. **Pallet chicken.** My grocery chain publishes live shelf counts and restocks poultry in fixed crates of thirty, ordering a new crate when the count reaches zero. Before I go, the count reads 1. My purchase is therefore the thirtieth unit and, by the store's own rule, initiates the next order. I buy it. *Verdict: clearly wrong. This is the case where "one person can't matter" is simply not available to me.*

2. **Buffer chicken.** Same store, same product, but the count reads 46. My purchase draws down existing slack and provably cannot reach a trigger. *Verdict: no qualm whatsoever, and I would choose this chicken over the one in (1) if both were in front of me — even though the only difference between the two acts is a number on a warehouse screen that nobody will ever audit.*

3. **Discard at close.** Perishables go in the dumpster at closing time. I arrive to find a single package left. If I don't buy it, an animal died for nothing. *Verdict: buying it feels better than an ordinary purchase, not worse. But I don't believe waste harms anyone — the animal is dead either way and nothing downstream changes — so I cannot say what makes (3) better than (2).*

4. **The used SUV.** A ten-year-old gas-guzzler from a private seller. No production caused, no order placed, and he would have sold it to the next buyer within the week. *Verdict: permissible, and with no moral residue at all.*

5. **The vintage fur coat.** A mink coat at an estate sale, with a causal structure line-for-line identical to (4): nothing produced, nothing ordered, and it sells to someone else if I walk away. *Verdict: not permissible. Or rather, there is a wrongness there that I cannot locate, and I have tried.*

6. **The marginal grid.** My utility publishes hourly marginal emissions. Running the dishwasher at 3 a.m. is absorbed by surplus wind and displaces nothing; running it at 6 p.m. demonstrably ramps a gas peaker. *Verdict: the 6 p.m. run feels wrong and the 3 a.m. run feels almost virtuous, though my annual consumption is identical. A related suspicion I can't shake: (2), (3) and (6) feel clean only because the facts are published. Where the supply chain is opaque, I default to assuming my act doesn't matter — and if the poultry chain posted its buffers the way the utility posts its grid mix, I suspect all my verdicts would collapse into a single principle. Which would mean the thing doing the work in (2) is my ignorance rather than my innocence.*

7. **The flight and the commute.** One round-trip transatlantic flight emits several times what I emit driving all year. *Verdict: I flew to a conference in March and felt nothing; I drive to campus daily and feel a low-grade wrongness. The felt ordering is the reverse of the emissions ordering, and the only explanation I can give is that driving is habitual and invisible while flying was an event I could have declined.*

8. **The vote and the boycott.** On any estimate I trust, the probability that my vote is decisive in a national election is smaller than the probability that my purchase in (1) triggers a crate. *Verdict: I think I am required to vote.* Meanwhile a boycott of a company I disapprove of has already recruited the million signatures its organizers said it needed, so my joining cannot affect whether it succeeds. *Verdict: joining is supererogatory, not required. My sense of obligation is strongest exactly where my causal contribution is thinnest.*

9. **The undetectable increment.** Ten thousand households on a shared aquifer each draw one extra bucket a day; no individual draw is detectable by anyone, and the aquifer is exhausted within a decade. *Verdict: each of them does wrong, and I can't point to a single person made worse by any one draw. This is the case that makes (2) and (6) look like special pleading, since there too I'm appealing to "nothing changes."*

Two things I want from you. First, the principle itself — or, if no single principle survives contact with all nine, the two or three candidates that come closest, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without further guidance from you. For each candidate, tell me which of the cases forces each clause into it, so I can see what I'm paying for the fit. Second, apply it. Three cases I have described but not judged: buying the buffer chicken from (2) and then paying for a verified removal offset matching its footprint; resigning from a job at a factory farm knowing the post will be filled within a fortnight by someone with identical scruples and an identical effect on output; and the one I keep going back and forth on — a certified brand shipped in from a co-op three states away in volumes so large that my purchase cannot move an order for weeks, versus a conventional brand restocked nightly in fixed crates from a regional distributor, where by the arithmetic of (1) and (2) the conventional purchase is the likelier trigger. Tell me what the account delivers in each, and whether it delivers it for the right reason.

Be blunt about the costs. If the only account that preserves both (4) and (5) requires bolting two independent sources of wrongness together, say so, and tell me which verdict you would overturn and what I lose by giving it up. And if you think (6)'s suspicion generalizes — that what I really believe is that transparency is what makes an act wrong, and that this is a principle I should reject on reflection — say that too, and show me which of the nine verdicts it dissolves rather than explains.
