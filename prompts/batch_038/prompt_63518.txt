ok so i'm in my last year of high school and for my econ seminar each of us has to write a policy paper, and i've gone way down the rabbit hole on parental leave and childcare. I want to argue for an actual verdict on one specific design, but I can't tell anymore if I'm just motivated-reasoning my way to the answer I started with, so I want the whole thing argued out properly. You don't need to cite studies or name any economists or give me a reading list — i can't look any of that up and honestly it wouldn't help. I just want the reasoning.

Here's the design. Where I live right now you get about a year of leave paid as a percentage of your previous salary, plus daycare from age one with fees that slide down by household income. The proposal i want to evaluate swaps that for five things:

1. A flat caregiver payment — the same amount for everyone regardless of what you used to earn — to any parent who stays home with a kid until it turns three. It goes into that parent's own account, not a joint household one.
2. The months you're home count as covered earnings for the state pension, so your retirement record doesn't get a hole punched in it.
3. You lose the entire payment if you do more than about eight hours a week of paid work.
4. If the couple splits up, the payment and the pension credits stay with whoever took them — nothing gets divided or clawed back.
5. Alongside all of it, free full-time daycare from six months for anyone who wants it, so staying home is supposed to be one real option rather than the default because nothing else exists.

Assume, realistically, that roughly 85–90 percent of the people who take the flat payment are mothers.

The fight i want you to have is: does a payment like this move toward equality between men and women, or away from it? The pro side as i understand it is that care work is currently worth zero in every official statistic, that giving the caregiver money in her own name changes who has leverage inside a household, and that a real choice between daycare and staying home requires both options to actually pay for something. The anti side is that the uptake is lopsided by assumption, so the policy mostly subsidises one gendered pattern of specialisation, and the cost isn't the cash — it's everything the cash doesn't replace: skills going stale, employers quietly assuming women will vanish for three years, and wages that never catch back up afterwards.

Some specific sub-questions I really want engaged with rather than skipped:

- Does the pension bit (2 and 4) do most of the actual protective work, such that the cash payment itself is largely symbolic? Or is that underselling what cash-in-her-own-name does?
- The flat rate means the payment is worth proportionally more to someone who was earning little and way less to a lawyer. Does that make it progressive, or does it mean the people most likely to quit are the ones with the least to fall back on?
- Is rule 3 the thing that turns an otherwise defensible policy into a trap, since it forecloses the keep-one-toe-in option (a few hours of freelance or shift work) that would blunt the scarring? I keep feeling like the eight-hour cutoff matters more than the payment does, and I want to know if that's right or if I'm fixating.

Weigh all of it, then pick a side. I'm fine with a conditional verdict, like "this is only equality-promoting under such-and-such condition," but then you have to say what the condition is and whether the five rules as written actually meet it. Please don't dissolve into a both-sides shrug at the end — i need something i can defend in front of my seminar, and "it depends on values" won't survive ten seconds of questioning.
