I'm setting the end-of-year paper for our second-year political philosophy module this term — I've inherited it because the usual convenor is on research leave — and I've got a problem with the Rawls question in the bank. For three years running it's been some variant of asking whether the difference principle is really an egalitarian principle, and last May we got something like forty scripts that were visibly built off the same two or three revision-site summaries: same objections, same order, nobody actually engaging with the argument. I want to replace it with a problem-style item that can't be answered by describing the view, because the students have to do something with a set of numbers I've made up and then live with what their own calculations commit them to. Level: second-year undergraduates, about half of them PPE students who are perfectly comfortable reading a table of figures, none of them specialists. Closed book, two-hour paper, this would be the compulsory Part B question, 30 marks, and I'd expect them to spend roughly 50 minutes on it.

Here's the material I want built into it. Society S has three representative social positions and the figures are each position's lifetime expectation on a single index of primary goods (take the index as given, though see below). Written as (P1, P2, P3), with P1 the least advantaged, the five feasible basic structures are: A = 10, 11, 12; B = 12, 16, 24; C = 11, 30, 60; D = 10, 45, 95; E = 9, 60, 140. Then the wrinkle I want: a variant D* in which the least advantaged position turns out on inspection to be two sub-groups rather than one, half of its members at 14 and half at 6, so that P1's expectation is still exactly 10 and nothing else in the arrangement changes. The parts I have in mind are roughly these. (i) Rank A–E under the difference principle and say what is doing the work in the ranking; then separately, for each of the five, say whether it is unjust, or merely less than perfectly just, or fully just — I want them to hit the fact that those are not the same judgement, and that at least one of these arrangements comes out differently depending on whether the principle is read as telling us to maximise the position of the worst off or as only constraining departures from an equal baseline. (ii) Rank D against D*, and say what the result shows about how far the principle reaches, in particular whether it governs distribution inside the least advantaged group at all. (iii) Work out whether a leximin rule yields the same ordering, point to the exact rows where the two come apart, and be explicit about whether the comparison is being run over individual persons or over representative positions, giving both if it makes a difference. (iv) A short part on whether the table by itself settles whether D is a just arrangement, or whether you need to know something further about how the three positions hang together institutionally — im honestly not sure second years can do anything with chain connection and close-knitness, so use your judgement about whether that belongs in the paper or is a graduate-level detour.

What I need back: the question exactly as it would be printed, with the table laid out so it survives being photocopied and the marks per part shown; a model answer of about 700–800 words that genuinely resolves (i) and (iii) rather than gesturing at the ambiguity; and a marking scheme broken into must-hit points with the marks for each, plus one line per point on how students typically lose it, because our external examiner complains every year that our schemes say good analysis instead of naming the moves. And tell me honestly whether the item is broken. If the divergence between the difference principle and leximin in (iii) is a knife-edge that depends on my choice of numbers, or if D tying with A at 10 makes (i) unfair because there's no principled way for a student to break the tie, say so and give me a revised set of figures — I'd rather the arithmetic forced the conceptual point then have the point turn up by coincidence. No references or suggested reading anywhere in the question or the answer, please, and don't have the students reaching for contemporary examples either; we had a complaint about that last year and I don't want to repeat it.
