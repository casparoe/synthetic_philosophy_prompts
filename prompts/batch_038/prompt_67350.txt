I am writing the central chapter of a master's thesis on the special composition question, and I have talked myself into an argument I can no longer control. My aim was to defend unrestricted composition without taking the usual route through vagueness: I am perfectly willing to swallow sharp, brute cutoffs in nature, so sorites-style arguments for universalism simply do not move me, and a blanket permission with no principle behind it seemed to me no better than the arbitrary sums universalists are always accused of loving. What I wanted was a positive criterion — something that says what composition consists in, so that the answer to "when do some things compose something?" is not just "whenever you like." I thought I had found one in the physics of non-separability. Please take the physics below as stipulated rather than as something for you to fact-check: two photons are produced in a single entangled polarization state, one remains in the lab and one is sent to a detector on a satellite; quantum mechanics, as I understand it, assigns no complete pure state to either photon on its own, since the full description belongs to the pair; and decoherence does not undo this but disperses the entanglement into the surrounding degrees of freedom, so that the pair taken by itself looks mixed while the state of pair-plus-environment remains pure. Here is the argument:

(1) Some things compose an object just in case they jointly instantiate some feature that is not fixed by the features each of them instantiates on its own.
(2) The two photons jointly instantiate such a feature, namely their joint state.
(3) So the two photons compose an object.
(4) Entanglement is not made and unmade piecemeal: interaction establishes it, and thereafter it is only redistributed; and since essentially everything has interacted, directly or through a chain of intermediaries, with essentially everything else, any two things whatsoever stand in such relations.
(5) So any two things — my left shoe and a galaxy in the Local Group, say — compose an object. Composition is unrestricted.
(6) The same criterion applies at the top end: the total state of the cosmos is not fixed by the states of its parts severally, so the cosmos composes an object of which every other composite is a part.
(7) Since (1) is meant to be the complete account of when composition occurs, and it applies most strongly at the level of the whole cosmos, the further wholes my argument generates are all derivative parts of that one whole.
(8) Therefore the principle I built to secure a plurality of composite objects secures exactly one fundamental object, together with unrestricted composition among its parts.

Three features of this make me uneasy, and I cannot tell whether the disease is in (1) or in (4). First, (5) commits me to a scattered object comprising my shoe and a distant galaxy, and I have no independent reason to think such a thing exists. Second, (7) seems to take back with one hand what (5) grants with the other: if everything that composes anything is a part of the cosmos, I have argued my way to monism under the banner of universalism, which is not the thesis I set out to defend. Third, and most damaging, I cannot see that the quantum mechanics is doing any work at all — (1) looks as though it might be satisfied by perfectly ordinary relational facts about anything whatever, in which case the entangled pair is an illustration rather than an engine, and I have derived universalism from a premise that renders entanglement irrelevant. So: which step would you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable to me when I wrote it down? Then please price the alternatives. If (1) must go, is there any principle left that would deliver universalism without the sorites, or am I thrown back on brute cutoffs or on van Inwagen's claim that composition occurs only where the activity of parts constitutes a life? Can (1) be tightened — restricted to intrinsic joint features, or to features not fixed even by the parts' own properties together with their spatiotempotent relations — in a way that keeps the photon case but blocks whatever trivialises the original, and does the tightened version still support (4), or does decoherence push the non-separability up to the level of pair-plus-environment and thereby cost me the slide to universalism? And what should a defender of restricted composition make of that last point: is the fact that decoherence seems to drive holism toward the whole cosmos rather than down to tidy pairs a gift to her, since she can then say the only entangled whole is the universe and ordinary composition still needs a restriction, or does it leave her holding monism, which is worse for her purposes than universalism would have been? I am not after references — I have the literature — just the diagnosis and the bill. If I have to give something up, I would rather keep (1) than (6) through (7), since (1) is the only candidate principle I have found that answers my original question without answering it arbitrarily; but tell me what keeping it costs.
