So I'm not any kind of philosopher, I do data infrastructure work, but I've been going down a rabbit hole for about six months now on this idea of one thing holding "in virtue of" another — I think the word people use is grounding. The version of the story I keep running into says grounding is irreflexive (nothing grounds itself), asymmetric (if a grounds b then b can't ground a), and transitive, and that if you keep following the arrows down you eventually hit bedrock: some stuff that just is what it is and isn't grounded in anything at all. It's that last part I can't shake, and I want you to argue against it rather than explain it to me.

My worry starts from work, honestly. In the systems I build, dependencies are supposed to be acyclic too, and a cycle gets treated as a fatal error you have to break — but a decent chunk of the time the cycle is real, and the honest fix is to admit your nodes are drawn wrong rather than to insist the loop can't exist. And outside of software there seem to be pairs of things that get whatever they are entirely from each other. Take Ava and Bo: Ava is a teacher only because Bo is her student, and Bo is a student only because Ava teaches him, and let's stipulate there's nothing else going on — no school, no curriculum, no third party, strip out either one and the other stops having that property. So the fact that Ava teaches looks like it grounds the fact that Bo studies, and the fact that Bo studies looks like it grounds the fact that Ava teaches. Same shape as "left" and "right", maybe as an employer and their sole employee. Two arrows, going in a circle, no bedrock anywhere in the picture. My partner told me at dinner that this is just word games and I should get a hobby, which is part of why I want a real argument instead of a sympathetic nod.

Here's the thing I figured out on my own that makes me think there's actual work to do, and I'd like you to deal with it head on. If grounding is transitive, then Ava-grounds-Bo plus Bo-grounds-Ava seems to give you Ava-grounds-Ava, which is supposed to be flat-out impossible. So you can't just say "sure, asymmetry is overrated" and keep everything else — one of the properties has to be given up, or self-grounding has to be allowed, or my example has to be re-cut so it isn't really a loop. What I want from you is the strongest case you can build that mutual grounding like this is genuinely possible: not a survey of the options, an argument with a verdict at the end. In making it, tell me which formal property you're giving up and why that's the cheapest one to lose. Take seriously the objection that a loop can't explain anything, because each member just passes the buck to the next one and nothing ever gets paid — a circle of borrowed reality with no lender. And take seriously the objection that asymmetry isn't a claim about the world at all but part of what "grounds" means, which would make my example not false but incoherent, like a married bachelor. If your answer is that the example has to be redescribed — Ava's teaching isn't grounded in Bo's studying, both are grounded in some third thing — say exactly what the redescriptive move buys you, what it costs, and whether the same move would have to be applied to every candidate loop anyone could ever come up with, which starts to look like it's doing all the heavy lifting by fiat. Finally: if loops are allowed, what's left to do the job that the fundamental level was supposed to be doing? The whole point of bedrock was to stop the regress from being vicious, so I want to know what stops it on your view, and whether your view needs something to be fundamental at all. Don't waste space on defining terms for me, I've got the basic idea, and don't send me off to look things up — just make the case.
