I have been working through the dispute between Kripke and Lewis about how ordinary objects figure in merely possible worlds, and I keep arriving at the conclusion that there is no substantive disagreement between them. I assume I am missing something, so let me set the reasoning out in full.

Grant that the claim about Humphrey, that he might have won the election, is true. Kripke's account says it is true because there is a possible world in which Humphrey himself wins. Lewis's account says it is true because there is a possible world in which a sufficiently close counterpart of Humphrey wins. But on Lewis's own telling, the counterpart relation is nothing over and above comparative similarity in the respects that the context makes salient; there is no further ingredient, no primitive hook of thisness by which one world-bound individual is matched to another. And the world that Lewis invokes in this case is the very one Kripke needs: a way things might have been, containing a man who in every respect anyone could specify is just like Humphrey, and who wins. So the two accounts appear to agree completely about what there is and about what is true of it. Their only difference concerns whether we are permitted to extend the name Humphrey to that winner, Lewis declining on the grounds that worlds do not overlap and an individual is wholly located in the one world of which it is a part.

Yet a quarrel about the permissible extension of a name, where both parties agree on what is there to be named, looks like a verbal quarrel. It is comparable to two accountants who agree on every fact about a lease and then fall out over whether to classify it as an asset: nothing turns on the classification except the bookkeeping.

So what is the substantive disagreement here? Specifically, why is the Humphrey objection, Kripke's complaint that on counterpart theory it is not Humphrey who would have won, treated in the literature as identifying a genuine cost of the view rather than as a request for a notational reform that Lewis is perfectly entitled to refuse? If the two theories never differ about what is true, and never differ about what makes it true, in what does the dispute consist?
