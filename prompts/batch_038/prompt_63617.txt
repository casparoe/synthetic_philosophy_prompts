I am preparing for a reading group that sits awkwardly between a law school seminar on negligence and a philosophy seminar on the metaphysics of responsibility, and this week's assigned piece is a chapter by a torts professor in an edited collection on the ethics of bystanders. It opens with a summary of the puzzle of omissions that struck me as unusually confident, and a friend of mine who is midway through a doctorate in criminal law assures me it is exactly right. I am not so sure, but I have only a year of metaphysics behind me, and I cannot yet distinguish what is settled from what is one side of a live dispute from what simply does not follow. Here is the opening, transcribed as closely as I can manage:

'Begin with the case everyone knows. A child is drowning in a shallow pond, and you, a strong swimmer with nothing pressing to do, walk past. The law of every American jurisdiction lets you walk past, absent some special relationship or some earlier act of your own that created the danger. That rule is usually presented as a moral compromise the law makes for the sake of liberty, but it is better read as the law having got the metaphysics right. Causation is a relation between events, and on the standard but-for analysis one event caused another just in case the second would not have occurred without the first. Your walking past is not an event. Nothing happened; there was only the absence of something that might have happened. So there is nothing there to stand in the causal relation, and the death has a single cause: the water in the child's lungs. Those who resist this — typically the friends of the counterfactual analysis, for whom difference-making is all there is to causation — inherit an absurdity. If absences can cause, then every person who was nowhere near that pond also failed to save the child, and every one of those failures makes the relevant counterfactual true; so a sunbather on a beach in another country, who never heard of the child and could not have reached him, caused the drowning as surely as you did. That consequence is intolerable, and it has largely settled the question: causation by omission is now generally regarded as loose shorthand for the assignment of blame. It follows that the act/omission distinction in the criminal law is not a distinction in the causal structure of the world at all, but a record of the failures we have decided to censure and the failures we have not.'

Please go through this sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher who works on causation would sign off on without qualification, what they would count as a tolerable simplification for a legal audience, and what they would think is wrong, question-begging, or tendentious. I would especially like to know the following. First, is the claim that lawyers and philosophers are agreed that the passer-by did not cause the child's death accurate, and how exactly does the legal doctrine about duties to rescue relate to the metaphysical question about absences — is it downstream of that question, upstream of it, or independent of it? Second, is the promiscuity worry about the distant sunbather really the decisive reductio the chapter takes it to be, or are there standard replies that leave some version of causation by omission standing; and if those replies exist, whose account do they vindicate and whose do they leave intact? Third, does the concluding inference hold — that is, does anything true in the passage support the claim that talk of causing by omission is shorthand for blame, and hence that the act/omission distinction carries no causal content? I realize the last question is about the argument rather than the facts, and I would rather have the structure of it laid out than be told what to believe.

There is one concrete case in the same chapter that I could not resolve, and it may serve as a test of the opening. A few pages later the professor notes that a lifeguard who stays on her phone while a swimmer goes under is treated by the courts as having caused the death, and that the swimmer's family can recover from her employer. If the metaphysical argument in the opening really is decisive, what is the lifeguard's situation made of that the passing stranger's is not — is the difference a difference in the causal facts themselves, a difference in which description of the events we select as salient, or a difference in nothing but duty? Relatedly, I kept wondering whether there is any principled distinction between an omission properly so called, where an agent who was able and expected to act did not, and the bare fact that some arbitrary person on another continent did not do something. If there is such a distinction, does it do any work against the sunbather objection, or does invoking it simply restate the problem in more comfortable vocabulary?
