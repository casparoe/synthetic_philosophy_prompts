You are a political philosopher who works at the intersection of normative theories of distributive justice and the actual administration of welfare states — someone equally comfortable reconstructing an argument about responsibility-sensitivity and describing how a benefits office really decides a claim. I would like you to think carefully and at length about a disagreement I had with a close friend last night, which ran to about four hours and ended with neither of us persuaded. Before you commit to a verdict, I would like you to brainstorm: sketch at least three or four candidate reconstructions of the implicit theory behind the practice I describe, including ones neither my friend nor I thought of, and only then say which is best supported and why.

Here is the practice. In the countries I know reasonably well, an injury sustained at work and the same injury sustained away from work are handled by two entirely different institutional machines. On-the-job harm runs through workers' compensation: a no-fault regime, financed by employers, that asks only whether the impairment arose out of and in the course of employment, and pays wage replacement and medical costs without reference to the worker's wealth or to the worker's own carelessness. As I understand it, most such statutes nonetheless carve out injuries that were deliberately self-inflicted or caused by the worker's intoxication. Off-the-job harm, by contrast, runs through general disability and means-tested assistance programs, which ask about current functional capacity and about assets and income, and which attach behavioural conditions — work-seeking requirements, cooperation with assessments, in some places drug testing, with sanctions for non-compliance — but which almost never ask *why* the claimant is disabled. A back destroyed by a fall from a ladder at a site and a back destroyed by the same fall at home produce different questions, different payers, and different moral vocabularies.

My friend's thesis, which I found genuinely original and also unstable, is this: the system's apparent silence about the causes of disadvantage is not a rejection of responsibility-sensitivity but a *division of moral labour*. On this reading, the question "was this your fault, or your bad luck?" has not been abandoned; it has been allocated elsewhere — to tort law, to insurance markets that price risk, to criminal law, to employers' safety obligations — so that the benefits agency is licensed to ask only about present capacity because the responsibility question is being answered somewhere else in the institutional order. My friend added that the residual fault filters I mention are precisely where this allocation fails: the intoxication and self-harm exclusions inside workers' compensation, and the drug tests and sanctions outside it, reinstate the "why" question in the very offices that supposedly outsourced it, which means the division of labour is not a principle but a set of exceptions with no account of why they sit where they sit.

My counter-position was that this over-intellectualises the practice. I argued that what the practice really presupposes is a status or participation view: the entitlement-relevant question is not the provenance of a disadvantage but whether a person can take part in the shared activities of a common life, so responsibility never appears as a determinant of entitlement at all — it appears only downstream, as a disciplinary and administrative technology for managing caseloads, and it is imported from elsewhere (employers' insurers, police powers) rather than derived from any theory of justice the benefits system holds.

Please tell me the following. First, reconstruct the implicit theory of distributive justice that this two-track arrangement presupposes, and be strict about the distinction between what the practice *cannot do without* and what it merely fits comfortably with. Second, say which rival views it quietly rules out — in particular, whether a view on which the brute-luck/option-luck distinction is supposed to determine entitlement can license this practice at all, and whether the practice is better read as an exercise in distributive justice or as a priced transfer of risk between employer and employee that never was a claim about justice in the first place. Third, adjudicate between my friend's division-of-labour reading and my status/participation reading, and if some third reconstruction is better than both, say so and defend it. Fourth, tell me whether the arrangement is internally coherent: can a single institution both decline to ask why someone is worse off and simultaneously impose conditions of good conduct on them? Fifth, describe concretely what would have to change — in forms, in assessments, in adjudication, in the epistemic demands placed on caseworkers — if this system were rebuilt so that entitlement actually tracked the distinction between chosen and unchosen disadvantage, and say where that project runs into administrative, evidential, or moral limits.

Two requests about how you handle the disagreement. I want you to be explicit about what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions; do not dress up a contested interpretive claim as the consensus view, and where the literature divides, show me the fault lines rather than picking a side silently. If you think either my friend's framing or mine is a strawman of some position that has actually been defended, say so plainly. And please do not give me a reading list or a bibliography — I want the argument, not pointers to it.
