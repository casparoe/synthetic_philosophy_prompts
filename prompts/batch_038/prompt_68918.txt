I'm three years into a novel with something like forty speaking characters in one town — mill closure, a fight over the hospital, a mosque, a wind farm out on the ridge — and I've caught myself sorting them into left and right in my head before I've even written their scenes, which is bad for the book and probably worse for my politics. So I want to know what my sorting actually amounts to. Here are the verdicts, as honestly as I can report them.

Dale: diesel mechanic, union steward, wants the freight line renationalised and a real safety net, and would happily see every immigrant in the county put on a bus. I call him right. If someone at a dinner party asks me whether my Dale character is left or right, "right" comes out of my mouth before I've thought about it.

Tomas: runs the organic co-op, effectively wants to abolish private ownership of farmland, gives away the surplus, at a Lutheran service every week, has fairly specific ideas about a woman's place, would never vote for a gay marriage bill. I call him left. And these two are my problem, because Tomas is at least as culturally conservative as Dale and much further left economically, and I still put them on opposite sides.

Priya: quant, funds abortion clinics and trans healthcare and an open-borders legal defense out of her own pocket, thinks poverty is a character flaw and the minimum wage is theft. Left, I say, without hesitation. Which makes me a hypocrite about Dale, I'm aware.

Noor: coder-anarchist, wants no state at all, no welfare, no regulation, no occupational licensing, absolute personal freedom including drugs and guns. I call her right, and yet in some moods she's the most anti-authoritarian person in my whole cast and feels closer to left than Dale does.

The Comte, because there's always a Comte: inherited the estate on the ridge, wants the wetlands protected and the hedgerows kept and the village shop subsidised, despises markets, thinks inheritance tax should be zero. Right. But he'd vote with the greens on nine environmental questions out of ten and with Dale on nothing.

Vinnie: national-conservative type — tariffs, state-directed investment, baby bonuses, family policy, wants the flag back. Right. Except his economics are word for word my grandmother's Labour party, and she was the leftest person I ever met.

Then the ones I keep flip-flopping on. A social democrat who is hawkish abroad and tough on crime: left, mostly, but I flinch. An old-school Stalinist I gave one chapter: I call him left and, in the same paragraph, feel he belongs out on the right with the Comte, which is the horseshoe feeling, and I dont trust the feeling. And a free-trading, anti-clerical, anti-aristocracy radical who would have been the left in 1840 and gets called right today — which makes me suspect the words aren't picking out any content at all, just tracking who's up and who's down this season.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? I've heard the two-axis answer, economic plus social-liberal-vs-authoritarian, and I know it dissolves the Tomas/Dale problem by refusing to put them on one line, but it doesn't explain why I still want a single word, and it also predicts I'd call Priya right and Noor right, which I don't. I've also heard the equality-versus-hierarchy answer, and I can't work out whether Dale's anti-immigration counts as hierarchy in the relevant sense, or whether Tomas's traditionalism does, or whether the Comte's hedgerows do.

What I'm after: the principle, or the small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a character without arguing with myself; which of my cases each clause is there to handle; what it says about a character I haven't placed yet — a Somali-born shopkeeper, economically laissez-faire, devout, wants open borders for everyone including his enemies, thinks feminism has gone too far; and an honest list of which of my verdicts the principle cannot save, because I'd rather know that than have the whole set flattered. And please don't just conclude that the distinction is incoherent and stop there. I have to keep using it for another two hundred pages.
