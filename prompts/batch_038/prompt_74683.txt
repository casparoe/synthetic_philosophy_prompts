I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, but I fell down a rabbit hole on the Church-Turing thing over the weekend and now I cant stop thinking about it. The problem: I have pretty firm gut reactions about which physical things "compute" and which don't, and I can't find any rule that lines up with all of them. Here's the list, tell me what I'm actually committed to.

1. A giant precomputed lookup table sitting on disk — answers to "is n prime" for every n under a billion, instantly. Not computing. It's just a big array; it did all its work before I ever asked it anything.

2. A person with a pencil, paper, and a written-out rulebook for long division, grinding through it step by step. Definitely computing. Apparently that's literally what "computer" used to mean, a job title.

3. Cases 1 and 2 are, as far as physics goes, the same kind of object — meat and graphite versus magnetic domains, both just stuff doing stuff. But one is computing and one isn't, and that's the pair I can't shake.

4. A hardware random number generator sampling thermal noise. Not computing, it's measuring. Except the second I feed that noise into a Monte Carlo run, the whole thing is a computation. Same story with pendulums: a simulated double pendulum is computing, a real one with a sensor on it emitting the identical bit stream isn't.

5. A spreadsheet with a circular reference and iterative calc enabled that settles on a fixed point. Nobody wrote a loop. Still computing, I'd say.

6. A program that would need longer than the age of the universe to finish. It's still computing, it just doesn't finish. Resources and elapsed time don't seem to matter here — which I'm aware sits badly with case 1, where my whole reason for saying no was that the work had already been done.

7. A trained model doing inference: computing, obviously. Print out the weights and have a human do the multiplies by hand from a rulebook: still computing. Do the same matrix multiply with voltages across resistors: computing if the precision is finite, but if it's genuinely infinite precision I want to say no, because the answer feels like it's being smuggled in for free from the physical world. I can't explain why "free" is supposed to be a problem.

8. The one I keep going back and forth on: a loop where each iteration takes half as long as the last, so it finishes infinitely many steps in two seconds. If you could build that, you could settle the halting problem with it. I'm fairly sure you can't, so something in my description must be cheating. I just can't say which part.

So — is there a principle, one sentence per clause if you need several, that comes out right on all of these or nearly all? For each clause, tell me which case forced it in. Tell me what it says about 8, and about one further case I haven't listed. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't save: if there's a simpler principle that gets everything except one, I'd rather drop a verdict than carry around something baroque.

Last thing. I honestly can't tell whether I'm asking a question about the world or a question about how I'm using a word. If it's the second one, say so — but then tell me what turns on picking one usage over the other anyway.
