I've spent the last few months going back and forth with our economist advisor about the renewal flow I built for a subscription product: free trial, then a heavily discounted month, then full price, cancel anytime. Her line is that what we're doing doesn't exploit irrationality in any interesting sense, because a money pump only proves something when the victim's preferences are cyclic, and nobody's prefences here are cyclic — they just shift as the deadline gets closer. I came in holding the textbook view instead: money pump arguments show that intransitive preferences are irrational, and if you can be pumped, you were the irrational one.

So — what would count against that? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and make them engage with the fact that the pumps you actually meet in the wild (teaser rates, auto-renewals, buy-now-pay-later, gym memberships) look like they exploit preference change over time rather than preference cycles at a time. Then tell me what happens if I keep adding escape hatches — the agent has to be myopic, or unsophisticated, or unable to see the whole sequence coming — until nothing left counts as a genuine pump; is that insulating the view or just making it precise? I'd also like to know what sophisticated choice and resolute choice each do to the argument, and which of all this a defender of the money-pump line would themselves concede as damaging rather than swat away.

Don't hand me a menu. Commit: is the position defensible as I stated it, or has it been qualified into emptiness? Give reasons. Under 600 words.
