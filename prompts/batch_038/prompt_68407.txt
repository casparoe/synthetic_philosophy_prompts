I put on a little "proofs and poems" night for our math dept and next month's theme is the axiom of choice. Write me a poem about Zorn's lemma — 12 to 16 lines, no fixed metre, but the structure has to do the arguing, not just the vocabulary. Assume you're a poet who's sat through a first course in set theory, and sketch your conceit in two or three sentences before you write a line of verse.

Here's the conceit I want, which I've never seen anyone attempt: the poem's lines are themselves a chain. Every line after the first has to serve as an upper bound for all the lines before it — swallowing them, answering them, exceeding them, whatever you can pull off — so the reader watches the hypothesis getting verified line by line. And the first line is the empty-chain case: it bounds nothing at all, and that's precisely why the poem can't be empty, can't not exist. Then the final line is the maximal element the lemma promises — handed over by the hypothesis rather than earned by what came before it, and pointedly not a maximum: nothing should make it comparable to everything else in the poem, just unextendable. If that maximal/maximum distinction can be the turn of the poem, so much the better, since half my audiance will conflate them and I'd like the verse to fix that without lecturing.

Two accuracy points I care about: an upper bound doesn't have to be a member of the chain it bounds, and a set theorist in the room shouldn't wince at any line. If you can nod to the fact that Kuratowski had the result in 1922, thirteen years before Zorn's note, without it reading like a footnote, do it — but cut it if it's awkward.

After the poem, add a few lines telling me where you took liberties: which part of the lemma the verse fudges, whether the chain conceit really behaves like a chain, how confident you are that the poem is right about the mathematics, and the single point you're least sure of.
