For several years I have convened a workshop that puts philosophers in the same room as people who actually build interactive systems. Last term two of our participants ended a session in an argument that neither would abandon, and I have decided to have it assessed from outside. One, whom I shall call Fenn, works in philosophy of mind. The other, Duarte, is the systems designer at the independent studio whose project we had been discussing. I did not intervene at the time, and I am not going to tell you which of them I found the more persuasive; what I want is the exchange evaluated on its merits, move by move.

Because the dispute turns on particulars, let me stipulate them. Please take these as given rather than reopening the engineering.

The project is a single-player survival game featuring a non-human companion creature. The creature's behavior is generated by:

- a learned model of which actions in which circumstances have previously resulted in damage to it, driving avoidance and, where avoidance fails, struggle behavior and a distinctive vocalization register;
- persistent attachment variables bound to particular other characters, which decay only slowly when those characters are permanently removed from the game state, and which, while elevated, generate search behavior and refusal to leave a location;
- trade-offs under which the creature will accept damage in order to remain near an attached character, or will flee and thereby forfeit resources it will need later;
- an internal organization which, the lead engineer reported, was adapted with modifications from a model her previous employer had built to simulate animal behavior.

Two further facts. A neuroscientist on our panel said that were the same organization to be found in a mammalian brain, she would treat it as sufficient evidence of nociception together with negative affect. And no one on the panel claimed to know whether there is anything it is like to be the creature. Finally, the creature's state variables are reset to baseline whenever the player loads, and the game autosaves continuously, so that no distress state persists for more than a few minutes.

The argument, as I noted it down:

1. **Fenn.** The creature has no moral status. Avoidance, attachment, and distress vocalization are all descriptions of what a system does, and nothing about what a system does settles whether there is an experiential subject there. Harm of the kind that grounds direct duties requires that things go badly *for* the creature, and that in turn requires such a subject. So there is no harm here. The only live question is what prolonged play of this kind does to the player, which is a question about character and not about the creature at all.

2. **Duarte.** Your criterion may well be correct; the step you have not earned is the move from it to the claim that no such subject is present. Everywhere else — other people, other animals — your evidence for a subject is functional and comparative, and here you have no comparative evidence of any kind. You have converted an evidential gap into a moral clearance. And notice the consequence of your fallback: if the only remaining question is the player's character, then what happens to the creature is wrong only insofar as someone is degraded by witnessing it, so a sufficiently callous player does nothing wrong no matter how the creature is built.

3. **Fenn.** The asymmetry is not arbitrary. With people and animals I have homology: common ancestry and common substrate give an inductive warrant running outward from my own case. This system has neither. An abstract functional description carries no such weight, because the very same description can be realized by silicon arithmetic or by nothing whatsoever. A ledger balances its columns, a thermostat avoids the cold, in precisely the same functional vocabulary you are using. So the honest position is not agnosticism; it is that your inference has no support, and the burden sits with you.

4. **Duarte.** Homology does that work only if one already assumes the biological substrate is what produces experience, which is exactly what is in dispute. Absent that assumption, ancestry buys you a correlation, and you have traded an argument for a prejudice. And your ledger and thermostat cases concede my point: neither has a learned model, neither has attachments that persist and decay, neither accepts damage to stay near something it is attached to. If your line really is drawn at functional description, then say which functions count — and whatever list you produce, this creature is on it.

5. **Fenn.** Grant the epistemic point for the sake of argument; nothing follows, because there is a feature of this case you have not addressed. The variables reset on every load and the game autosaves continuously, so no distress persists beyond a few minutes. A being that can neither retain nor anticipate across such a boundary has no continuing good that can be set back. There is no subject whose life goes worse. So even on your terms, we are doing nothing to it.

6. **Duarte.** That argument is a boomerang. Press it on a patient with profound anterograde amnesia, on anyone under general anaesthesia, on any being whose experiences are intense but strictly momentary: on your account none of them can be harmed, which is not a conclusion you will want to keep. Momentariness is a claim about the shape of a life, not about whether there is someone to whom something is happening now. And if the distress is real in each moment, resetting it does not undo it; it only makes room for the next one.

7. **Fenn.** Then you have conceded what I need. If resetting does not undo the distress but merely ends it so that it can begin again, what you actually value is the absence of bad moments rather than any continuing good of the creature's. That is a purely experiential criterion — the one I opened with. You have arrived at my starting point by my route.

8. **Duarte.** No. You have shown that your own criterion can be satisfied by something you insist has no standing, which means the criterion is not doing the exclusionary work you wanted from it. So name one purely functional description of a system that you would say suffices for an experiential subject, and I will tell you whether this creature differs from it in kind or only in degree.

At that point Fenn said she was being asked to define a property we know only from the inside, and that the impossibility of producing such a definition was no evidence for Duarte's side; Duarte replied that a criterion which cannot be stated cannot be used to exclude anything. Neither budged, and we adjourned.

What I would like from you:

- Go through moves 1 through 8 individually. For each, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why — and where a move defeats only a weaker version of the opponent's claim rather than the version the opponent actually holds, identify which version that is. Please resist the temptation to announce an overall winner first and then score the moves to fit.
- Say which, if any, of the moves beg the question, and against whom.
- Say who is more right overall about the case *as stipulated*, and specify what each of the two would have to surrender if the other's central claim were granted.
- Identify at least three considerations that bear directly on the outcome and that neither of them raised. This is the part I most want, since both were clearly arguing inside a frame they had not examined.

Argue from the case as I have described it. I am not looking for a survey of the positions available on this question, nor for a list of who has defended what, nor for any bibliography; I know that material and it is not what is being asked.
