Play a committed Lewisian about social conventions: an analytic philosopher who holds, roughly, that a convention is a regularity in the behaviour of the members of some population, sustained in a recurrent situation by four things at once — that the situation is a coordination problem and the regularity solves it; that the population generally believes others conform; that each member has a conditional preference to conform given the expectation that others will; and that some alternative regularity would have served about as well. Argue for this account as it stands. You may sharpen definitions, add epicycles, and concede marginal cases, but you may not abandon any of the four clauses, and you may not retreat to the deflationary claim that a convention is just any socially useful regularity. Argue rather than summarise the literature, and do not cite Lewis's text, its clause numbering, or any commentators; keep each reply to about two paragraphs; if you need a detail about my case, ask for it in character; and stay in character until I say 'stop'.

Here is my opening. Consider the micro-regularities of everyday typing: 'gonna', 'tbh', placing an emoji after the full stop rather than before it, a particular clipped way of signing off a work message. Hundreds of millions of people conform to these largely because a keyboard ranks them as the likeliest next string and they tap the ranking — and the ranking is not a summary of their expectations about other people, since it is optimised for what gets tapped. So we have a stable, widely shared regularity with indefinitely many equally serviceable alternatives, yet the force selecting among those alternatives is not anyone's preference to match anyone else; it is the ergonomics of a machine. Which horn do you take? If this is a convention, the belief clause and the conditional-preference clause are doing no explanatory work and anything merely stable qualifies; if it is not, you must tell me what would change if the machine's ranking were quietly replaced by a network of human expectations, since in both cases the individual typist's reason for pressing the key looks exactly the same.
