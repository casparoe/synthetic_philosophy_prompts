I write a fortnightly column on the philosophy of science and technology for a general-interest magazine, and I have just accepted a commission — 2,200 words, due in eleven days — on a thesis I suspect will not survive contact with a real philosopher. The thesis is that the rule-following problem, as Kripke formulated it, is not merely a loose analogy for what large language models do but a near-literal description of their predicament, and that the interesting consequence runs in the opposite direction from the one most of my colleagues draw. I have been reading around this for a month and I have drafted the argumentative spine, reproduced below at roughly 900 words. I would much rather have it taken apart now than after it goes to print. For context on my own standing: I trained as a molecular biologist and came to this beat sideways, so I have no professional immunity and no patience to spare for encouragement in place of criticism. The finished article will open on a reported scene and carry more worked examples; what follows is the skeleton, and the headings are scaffolding rather than copy.

---

**1. The setup.** Kripke's skeptic invites me to suppose that I have never before added numbers as large as 57, and that I am now asked for 68 + 57. I answer 125. The skeptic replies that nothing in my entire history of using the word fixes that answer, because every one of my past performances is equally consistent with my having meant *quus* — a function that behaves exactly like addition until one of the operands reaches 57 and thereafter returns 5. The bite of the example is not that *quus* is plausible but that it is, from where I stand, indistinguishable from the hypothesis I take myself to hold. Wittgenstein's statement of the difficulty is the one Kripke builds on: "no course of action could be determined by a rule, because any course of action can be made out to accord with the rule."

Kripke's Wittgenstein does not attempt to refute the skeptic. He concedes that there is no fact — not a mental one, not a dispositional one — that constitutes my meaning addition. What he offers instead is a *skeptical solution*: we may go on talking about correctness and mistake, provided we understand that the warrant for doing so comes not from anything inside an individual but from the community's agreement in how the rule gets applied. Correctness is a social status, conferred by the fact that my responses match those of the people around me closely enough that we can cooperate, correct one another, and train the next lot.

**2. The transposition.** A large language model is trained on a finite sample and deployed into an input space vastly larger than that sample. Indefinitely many functions agree with the training data on the training data. This is quaddition, except that the model does not merely face it on occasion; it lives permanently at the frontier of 57, and every generated token is a crossing. What makes the case philosophically interesting rather than merely cute is that the three candidate facts about meaning are, for a model, open to inspection in a way they are not for a person — and each fails roughly as predicted.

*The disposition.* Kripke rejects the dispositional account on two grounds, if I have him right: a disposition describes what I will say rather than what I ought to say, and it is in any case finite, gappy, and prone to error. For a model we can, in principle, write the disposition down completely — weights plus sampling procedure determine a probability over continuations. The second horn simply disappears. The first does not budge: the distribution tells us what is likely, and *likely* is not *correct*. This is the cleanest available test of the normativity objection, and the objection passes.

*The inner fact.* Where a person has only introspection, a model has mechanistic interpretability — the attempt to locate the circuitry implementing a given behaviour. What that project keeps running into is that features appear superposed in shared units, individual units carry many unrelated roles, the same behaviour is realised by different routes in different contexts, and a circuit once isolated still does not determine behaviour far from the training data. Whether this is a limitation of present methods or a property of the architecture I cannot say. But if it persists, it looks like an empirical echo of the claim that there is no determinate internal fact of the matter.

*The community.* Here the transposition stops behaving.

**3. The complication, which is where the piece actually lives.** For a deployed model the community is not a fixed background against which performance is measured. It is partly constituted by that performance. Annotation guidelines are rewritten when models deviate systematically in a direction that turns out to be defensible. Evaluation suites are authored after the behaviour they purport to measure already exists. Users lower or raise their sense of what counts as a good answer toward the answers they actually get. Standard and measured drift together. A human speaker raised inside a practice faces a community whose judgments are settled enough to serve as a court of appeal; a model faces a court that is issuing fresh rulings in response to the defendant's own conduct.

So the skeptical solution, transposed, yields something stranger than skepticism. It makes correctness for a model a matter of agreement with a distribution that is itself moving — and it makes *wrong* collapse into *atypical*. When we say a model got a benchmark item wrong, what we almost always mean is that its output diverged from the mode of a reference distribution of human responses we assembled. That is not a failure to match some antecedent rule. It is precisely what the skeptical solution said correctness always was, only here it is visible.

**4. The claim I want to make and am not sure I can defend.** Four decades of debate have proceeded on the assumption that agreement in judgments is a natural, pre-existing condition — a form of life one is born into rather than builds. We now have artefacts for which agreement in judgments is a target that gets optimised. My conclusion is not that models mean nothing, nor that they mean something. It is that if correctness is constituted by agreement, then an engineered agreement is not a counterfeit of correctness but correctness under laboratory conditions — the same relation, with the confounds removed. That formulation is uncomfortable in a way I cannot yet articulate, and it is the part I most need help with.

---

Six requests, in descending order of importance to me.

First, the exegesis. Is my characterisation of the skeptical solution fair, and in particular is it right to say Kripke's Wittgenstein *concedes* that there is no fact about meaning, given that he wants to preserve the ordinary language of correctness? I am also uneasy about section 2's treatment of the dispositional reply: I have compressed it into a normativity horn and a finitude horn and then claimed the second one evaporates for a model whose disposition is fully specifiable. Does that claim survive contact with the fact that we can never in practice enumerate the disposition, and that a model's disposition includes sampling noise?

Second, does the interpretability material carry argumentative weight or is it decoration? I worry that I am sliding from "we have not located a determinate circuit" to "there is no determinate fact," which would confuse an epistemic failure with a metaphysical one. If that is what I am doing, tell me plainly — and tell me whether the paragraph can be repaired or whether it should be cut.

Third, section 4. Several readers to whom I have described the argument say it rests on a category error: models do not follow rules at all in the relevant sense, because they have no intentions, and applying the skeptic's challenge to one is like applying it to a thermostat. My instinctive reply is that the skeptic's challenge was never about intentions — it was about the insufficiency of everything a third party could observe plus everything the subject could report. Say whether that reply is as weak as I suspect, and if it is not weak, say why not.

Fourth, is the thesis of section 4 actually a claim, or a mood? If it is a claim, put it to me in a form that could be argued against, and identify what would count against it. If it is a mood, tell me so, and tell me what the nearest defensible claim is.

Fifth, audience. My readers are intelligent and non-specialist and will have read nothing by Kripke or Wittgenstein. Mark the points where the draft becomes impenetrable and suggest the cheapest repair at each. My editor has already objected to *superposed* and *polysemantic* as jargon; I would rather keep one and cut the other than lose both, and I would like your view on which one is doing more work.

Sixth, a specific rewrite. The paragraph beginning "So the skeptical solution, transposed" is asked to do too much in too little space, and I now think the step from the drift of the standard to the identification of *wrong* with *atypical* is a non sequitur as written — the drift premise is about the standard moving, the conclusion is about what kind of property wrongness is, and I have not shown how one gets me to the other. Please rewrite that paragraph, or tell me what premise I am missing.

One housekeeping note: do not send me sources, a bibliography, or a reading list. I have a researcher and I will do that legwork myself; what I cannot outsource is judgment about whether the argument holds. And please do not tell me the piece is publishable — tell me whether it is right.
