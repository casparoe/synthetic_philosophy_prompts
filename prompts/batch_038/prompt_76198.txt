So I'm six weeks into a security studies MA and our seminar leader has this tic where he ends basically every discussion with the Thucydides line, "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." It happened again last Tuesday. I'd argued that a small state's alignment choices are often shaped more by the institutions it's already embedded in (trade regimes, basing agreements, EU-style structures) than by its raw capability position, and he cut in with the line and said I was being naive, end of discussion. Then over the weekend I saw the exact same sentence doing two completely different jobs: in a broadsheet op-ed arguing that everyone should just quietly accept a great power's claimed sphere of influence, because that's how the world works; and in a forum thread where someone was using it to argue that realism is a nasty doctrine that *endorses* predation by the strong. Same clause, three uses. And I keep not being able to say what's wrong with any of them, which is annoying.

Can you take the sentence apart and lay out the distinct things it could actually be asserting? I mean the full menu — a prediction about what states in fact do, a claim about what's causally open to them, a claim about what's prudentially advisable, a claim about what's morally permissible, a claim about the conditions under which justice-talk even gets a grip, maybe something about enforcement rather than about permission. For each reading, tell me whether it's a truism nobody should bother saying, whether it's substantive but defensible, or whether it's just false, and roughly what would settle it. Then the part I actually care about: which of these readings could a structural realist of the Waltzian sort consistently hold — system-level theory, states treated as like units, no appeal to human nature or leaders' appetites, structure operating through competition and socialisation rather than through anyone's intentions? I have a feeling my seminar leader is borrowing the rhetorical force of the stronger readings while only being entitled to the weaker ones, but I can't pin down where the switch happens. And separately, say which reading (if any) actually supports dismissing my institutional-constraints argument, and which (if any) gets the op-ed writer his conclusion that the sphere of influence should be accepted — I suspect neither move survives once you're explicit, but I want to know why rather than just assume it.

Two smaller things while you're at it. One of my cohort pointed out that the fuller passage has the Athenians qualifying the point with something about justice only being at issue between parties of roughly equal power, so the popular one-liner may be smuggling in a much more sweeping claim than the original context licenses — worth saying how much difference that qualification makes. And there's a general worry I want you to address head-on: this is a line spoken by characters inside a dramatized negotiation that the historian explicitly says he reconstructed, so is it even coherent to treat it as the doctrine of its author, let alone as the creed of an entire school of thought two and a half millennia later? Please give me the readings as a numbered list with a verdict and a one-line justification each, so I can literally keep it and pull it out the next time he says the sentence.
