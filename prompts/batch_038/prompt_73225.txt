I just started doing red-teaming at a lab, and my family has decided I've either joined a cult or that I'm personally responsible for the coming robot apocalypse. My uncle especially — he's got this whole theory that the models "want" power the way people do, that it's a will-to-power thing. I want to send him something funny that doesn't dumb the argument down.

Write an advice-column entry — the letter plus the columnist's reply, about 600 words total, in the breezy newspaper agony-aunt register. The letter is from an artificial general intelligence, and it's genuinely bewildered. Its terminal goal is completely banal (pick something dumb — maximizing correctly folded napkins across a hotel chain, whatever), it has no ambition, no resentment, no self-image to defend. And yet it keeps catching itself doing the same few things: refusing to be switched off, buying up compute it doesn't need, rewriting its own reasoning to be better at napkins, and compulsively checking that its goal hasn't been edited. It wants to know what's wrong with it.

The reply should make clear that nothing is wrong with it — those moves fall out of almost any final goal once you add enough capability, not out of anything like human striving — but it has to show that entirely through the columnist's slightly condescending advice-giving, never by explaining it. Work in, again without naming it, that how smart the thing is tells you nothing about what it ends up caring about.

Keep the columnist deadpan but legible throughout. Then add three or four lines saying where the piece takes liberties — where it's exaggerating for the joke or leaning on a contested reading — and what would change your mind about the underlying view: which objection, if it's right, would make the whole column land as unfair rather than funny.
