I'm a fourth-year philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of markets — my dissertation is basically about why some goods seem to resist being bought and sold, and how much of that resistance survives scrutiny. Next month I'm giving a talk to a medical humanities reading group, about a dozen people: lit scholars, a couple of public-health people, two clinicians who work in transplant. I've picked Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go as the text since pretty much everyone there has read it or seen the film, and I don't want to spend twenty minutes on plot.

What I want from you is a reading of the novel through the "repugnant markets" debate. The relevant machinery: Roth's point that repugnance functions as a real constraint on transactions even when nobody can articulate the harm it's tracking, and his suspicion that this is sometimes moral wisdom and sometimes just squeamishness; Sandel's split between the fairness objection to a market and the corruption objection — the claim that putting something up for sale changes what the thing *is*; and Satz's paramaters for calling a market noxious (extreme vulnerability, weak agency, harmful outcomes for individuals and for society).

But here's the thing — I don't want the obvious version of this reading, the one that goes "the novel is a parable about organ markets, and it shows that commodifying human beings is monstrous." I think that's wrong about the book and I want you to argue the other way. Hailsham's world has no market in organs at all. There are no prices, no contracts, no buyers, no consent procedure. What there is instead is gift, duty, and a diffuse social expectation that students give until they complete. On a Radcliffe Richards-style view that's *worse* for the donor than any regulated sale would be, because there's no price to negotiate, no standing to refuse, and no exit. So: does the novel actually indict commodification, or does it indict the absence of commodification — un-priced, un-negotiated taking dressed up as a vocation?

Work through at least these three, and I'd like you to take positions rather than list options:

1. Kathy's narration. It's placid, administrative, almost never outraged, and she treats the whole arrangement as background. Is that Elster-style adaptive preference, with all the problems Nussbaum raises about preferences shaped by the very oppression they ratify — which would mean a welfarist can't defend the system by saying the donors themselves don't mind? Or does the book want us to respect her calm as something like a moral achievement, which would undercut that reading?

2. The deferral rumor. As far as I can tell it's the only market-shaped thing in the novel: a price for time, payable in proof of love, administered by an authority nobody can point to. Treat it as the characters' imagined mechanism for introducing terms into a system with none, and tell me what it means that it turns out to be a rumor — that the hope was in circulation for years and did structural work before anyone discovered there was nothing behind it.

3. The Gallery and Madame's collection. I think this is a failed *valuation* project rather than a prohibition: Hailsham's guardians are trying to change how the good is appraised — to establish that the students have interiors worth respecting — instead of trying to stop the transactions. Miss Emily's speech at the end is basically an account of why that strategy lost. Read it that way and say whether the book thinks the strategy was doomed from the start or just badly timed, and what that implies for Sandel's corruption objection, which is also ultimately a claim about appraisal.

Then be honest about where this strains. The novel is deliberately vague about who benefits from the donations and how; it never shows a hospital, a purchaser, a budget line, or a politician who wanted the cures badly enough to say what they cost. Any Satz-style structural analysis is going to be importing framework the text won't supply, and I'd rather know exactly where I'm doing the importing than have it smuggled in. Also tell me if you think point (2) is overreading a schoolyard rumor into a theory of prices — a clinician in the room will say that, and I'd like to have an answer.

Finish with what the novel adds to the repugnance debate that argument alone doesn't. My hunch is that it has to do with the affective shape of the thing: the reader feels the repugnance intensely and continuously and yet can never locate the harm, which is roughly the position Roth says repugnance usually leaves us in — except that in the book the harm is real, just structural and diffuse, so the reader's inability to point at it isn't a failure of feeling. If you can make that precise, or show me it's wrong, either is useful.
