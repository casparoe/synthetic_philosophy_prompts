I'm doing a masters in political theory and my dissertation chapter is on the state's monopoly on legitimate violence, and I've hit a wall with the case study I want to hang it on: Minneapolis, 2020–21. Facts as I've got them, so you don't have to look anything up (and correct me if I've mangled one): after George Floyd was murdered in May 2020, nine of the thirteen city councillors stood on a stage in Powderhorn Park on 7 June and pledged to dismantle the city's police department, with the council president saying that incremental reform had failed, full stop. The mayor had been booed off a march the day before for refusing to endorse abolition. What happened next is the bit I care about. The council tried to amend the city charter itself, and the city's Charter Commision — an unelected body — declined in June 2020 to let the amendment go forward, saying it wanted more time to study it. It also emerged that the charter actually *required* Minneapolis to maintain a police department with a minimum number of sworn officers tied to the population. So the whole thing got squeezed into a single ballot question in November 2021, Question 2: strike the police department from the charter, replace it with a department of public safety taking a public-health approach, delete the minimum staffing requirement, and shift oversight from the mayor to the council. It lost, roughly 44% to 56%. In between, the council moved about 4.5% of the police budget into violence prevention work, and attrition plus disability claims took something like 200 officers off the streets without anybody having voted for it.

What I want is the analysis run properly through Weber, not a summary of Weber. So start with the mapping and be disciplined about it: who actually holds the monopoly in this episode — the city, the state of Minnesota, the charter commission, the council, the mayor — and what exactly would count as the claim to legitimate force being given up or transferred? Then: on Weber's account, would Question 2 passing have changed anything at all about the monopoly, given that the new department would presumably still have had armed officers with the same powers, just under a different name and a different boss? Here's the little frame I've been building that I'd like you to use and stress-test rather than just accept. I think three separate things were being conflated the whole time: the **org** (the standing bureaucracy with the badges, the culture, the union contract), the **warrant** (the legal authority to use coercive force, who holds it and on what conditions), and the **purse** (the budget line and where the money goes instead). My claim is that abolitionists were mostly talking about the org, that Weber's monopoly is a claim about the warrant, and that the people chanting about defunding were mostly talking about the purse — and that the 2020–21 mess is what happens when all three words get used for all three things at once. There's a polling result that I think is evidence for this: in August 2020 something like 73% of respondents said they supported defunding the police while only 40% supported actually reducing the size of the force. Sort out for me which of the three each actor was really contesting, where the slippage did the damage, and whether the reform/defund/abolish trichotomy maps onto the org/warrant/purse triad cleanly or leaves something over.

Then run it in the other direction, because that's the part my supervisor keeps pushing me on: what does Minneapolis do to the theory? Weber's monopoly is supposed to be the state's, more or less unitarily, defined against rival claimants — mafias, private security, militias, churches. This episode seems to be about something he wasn't addressing: which department inside the state holds the badge, and whether a *city* can unilaterally give up a coercive function when the charter, an unelected commission and the state above it all have a say. Is that a confirmation (the monopoly survived untouched because the warrant was never genuinely up for a vote), an anomaly (there's no single holder to point at, so the concept gets vague exactly where it matters), or a sign that the concept was never meant for internal questions like this? And be honest about where Weber is simply silent rather than making him speak — especially about the fact that the real reduction in officers came from attrition and morale, which nobody authorised and which no theory of legitimacy has a slot for. No reading list or bibliography at the end, please, I've got the secondary literature; I just want the argument. Cheers.
