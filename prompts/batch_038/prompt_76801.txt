Forty years in a biophysics lab, philosophy only since I retired, so bear with me. I've been chewing on Hofstadter and Mitchell's Copycat — the letter-string business, abc:abd::xyz:?, where the interesting part is what happens when the system runs into the fact that z has no successor and has to let one of its own concepts slip instead of its rule.

I've talked myself into a position and I want it knocked around properly. My position: the fluidity isn't in the machine, it's in the curation. The slipnet's concepts and their conceptual-depth values were chosen by hand, so what looks like a concept spontaneously slipping is really a search over a pre-enumerated menu, weighted by numbers somebody picked. Copycat shows that a well-chosen menu plus a frustration signal can produce human-like answers. That's not nothing, but it doesn't show concepts becoming fluid on their own, and it should fall over in any microdomain nobody pre-scanned for the right slips.

So: what would count against that? Rank the defeaters by how much they should actually move me, and say which a Copycat defender would themselves concede versus wave off. Two specific probes. Would an ablation — swapping the temperature mechanism for a fixed probability of slippage — settle anything, and which way would the result have to come out to hurt me? And what would a system that learned its own slipnet rather than being given one have to do to count as a refutation, as opposed to just a nicer menu?

Last thing: has my view been insulated by the phrase "well-chosen menu," so any success gets redescribed as curation after the fact? If so, is that fatal, or is that just what a claim about emergence looks like from the outside? I keep wanting an experiment that could decide it, which may be the the wrong instinct here.
