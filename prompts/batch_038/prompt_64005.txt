Here's the question as it appeared on the problem set:

Q3 (15 points). A three-judge panel must decide whether a defendant is liable. Liability (r) obtains if and only if the defendant owed a duty of care (p) and breached it (q). Judge 1 accepts p, accepts q, accepts r. Judge 2 accepts p, rejects q, rejects r. Judge 3 rejects p, accepts q, rejects r. Explain the doctrinal paradox / discursive dilemma this configuration generates, describe the premise-based and conclusion-based ways of resolving it, and say which one a collegial appellate court ought to adopt and why. In the second half of your answer, explain how the case relates to List and Pettit's impossibility theorem: which of their conditions does each procedure satisfy, and which does it violate?

And here's the student's answer, in full:

"Each of the three judges is individually rational — none of them holds a contradictory set, and in each case their view on r follows from their views on p and q. The trouble is that this doesn't survive aggregation. Voting proposition by proposition, p is accepted 2-1 (judges 1 and 2), q is accepted 2-1 (judges 1 and 3), and r is rejected 2-1 (judges 2 and 3). But p and q jointly entail r, so the proposition-wise majority verdict is deductively inconsistent: the court ends up committed to p, to q, and to the denial of p-and-q. This is the doctrinal paradox, which first appeared in List and Pettit's 2002 paper and which they generalise into the discursive dilemma, since exactly the same structure shows up wherever a group has to take a stand on interconnected propositions — a search committee deciding whether a candidate is strong in research, strong in teaching, and therefore hireable, or a legislature deciding whether a bill violates some constitutional constraint. Notice that the paradox has nothing to do with anyone being stupid or ill-informed. It would arise even if all three judges were perfectly consistent and logically omniscient, so it can't be fixed by better reasoning on anybody's part.

Structurally this is just the Condorcet paradox wearing a different hat: instead of a majority cycle over three alternatives you get a majority cycle over three propositions. So Arrow's theorem already tells us the trouble is unavoidable, and nothing new is really added by restating it in propositional terms.

There are two standard repairs. Under the premise-based (issue-voting) procedure the court votes on p and q seperately and lets r follow by logical implication: here the court finds for liability, since both premise majorities run in the plaintiff's favour. Under the conclusion-based (outcome-voting) procedure the court votes on r directly and treats the premises as scaffolding: here the court finds against liability. So the same three judges, all of them individually consistent, generate opposite verdicts depending on the order in which the court organises its reasoning — which is why the collegial-versus-aggregate picture of a court matters. A premise-first court acts as a single reasoning body; a conclusion-first court is just tallying votes over outcomes.

The premise-based procedure solves the problem rather than papering over it. For any agenda whatsoever, taking the majority view on the premises and then closing under logical implication gives you a collective set that is both consistent and complete, so the court can always be made collectively rational this way. The price is that you stop voting proposition by proposition: the collective view on r is no longer a function of the individual views on r alone. But that is exactly the condition List and Pettit's theorem tells you to give up — systematicity, i.e. independence plus neutrality — while universal domain, anonymity and collective rationality all stay intact. So the theorem is not an argument that collective rationality is impossible; it's an argument that you can't have it if you insist on proposition-wise voting. Once you're willing to drop that, the discursive dilemma dissolves, and a collegial appellate court should always vote premise-first, since that's the only procedure that takes the judges' reasons seriously rather than just their votes."
