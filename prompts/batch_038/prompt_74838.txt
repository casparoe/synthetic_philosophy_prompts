I'm a third-year psych PhD student and I'm writing the coding manual for a wellbeing measure that's going to end up weighting outcomes in a cost-effectiveness analysis, so I can't be too sloppy about it.

Setup: we get three streams on the same episodes from the same people. (1) Momentary — an app that lets you tap a button while it's happening if it feels good right now. (2) End-of-day — you scroll back through your day and rate each episode for how enjoyable it was. (3) An observer rating, from a trained RA watching silent video of a subset of episodes, judging from face and body only. I need one label — pleasant / not pleasant / can't tell — that all three can feed into and that means something stable enough to compare across people.

The trouble is I've been reading philosophy of pleasure and now I think the naive version of my manual is incoherent. The default story is that pleasure is a felt quality, so a coder just asks "did it feel good?" But the heterogeneity problem is that the pleasure of a hot bath, of finally getting a proof to go through, of relief when a noise stops, and of a smell you barely registered don't seem to share any phenomenology — and there's the rival story where pleasure isn't a feel at all but a stance toward the experience: you want it to continue, you'd welcome more of it, you don't mind it while it lasts. My three streams are quietly measuring different things. The button-press is chasing the feel, the end-of-day rating is catching the stance, and the video is catching expression, which might track neither.

So: write the procedure my coders actually run on a single episode. In order — the question asked at each step, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, and where the coder stops at can't-tell instead of guessing. Constraints that matter: coders are undergrad RAs with zero philosophy background, each item has to take under about 90 seconds, every step is logged so a disagreement between coders can be audited, and my PI signs off on the manual before we start. I need you to say explicitly which distinctions from the philosophy have to survive the trip down onto a coding sheet and which I'm allowed to drop, and to mark the steps that are really judgment calls in a procedure costume — I'd rather flag those than pretend our kappa is measuring something clean.

Then run it on three episodes:

- a long run where the participant never tapped once but rated it the best part of her day and said she'd do it again tomorrow;
- a horror film she found genuinely frightening, tapped constantly through, and insists she loved;
- a warm morning shower she didn't report at all until prompted, and then said yeah, that was nice, I hadn't thought about it.

At least one of these is going to come out wrong in a way that wrecks a chunk of my data, and I want you to tell me which one and why instead of smoothing it over. Finish with a short list of what the procedure structurally cannot do — the questions it's going to look like it answers but doesn't.
