Six weeks ago I took up a post as interpretation officer at a small county museum, and my first commission is to rewrite the medieval gallery around a theme the trustees have already settled on: climate, contagion and the fourteenth century. The draft I have inherited is committed to a military frame throughout. The Black Death is described as an invader that came ashore on the Dorset coast in the summer of 1348; the villages it reached are said to have been overrun, and the communities that survived it are credited with mounting a defence; the closing panel on labour says that the plague broke the back of serfdom, and that the Ordinance and Statute of Labourers of 1349 and 1351 were the crown's counter-offensive, which held the line for a generation before failing. My predecessor defends all of this on two grounds. The first is that the metaphor is not decorative but honest: the plague genuinely did things we would ordinarily credit to an agent — it killed between a third and a half of the population, emptied manors, and left the survivors bargaining from a position they had never occupied before — so to call it an invader is to state its role rather than to smuggle in a picture of it. The second, which is the part I have not been able to answer, is that the frame is precisely the cure for the charge of environmental determinism that an external reviewer has levelled at the gallery. A determinist story, on this account, is one in which climate and disease act upon passive human material; a war story has two protagonists, so the military vocabulary is what keeps human agency visible.

I am not persuaded, but I cannot yet say why, and sign-off is at the end of the month. Could you audit the analogy for me? I would like the mapping set out explicitly — what is playing the part of the invading force, the terrain, the front line, the fortifications, the general staff, the terms of surrender — followed by the disanalogies, separated into those that are merely inelegant and those that bear real weight for the use this gallery is making of the frame. In particular, I want my predecessor's second claim tested: does treating a pathogen as a belligerent actually buy you a non-deterministic history, or does it purchase human agency at some other cost? Tell me what the war picture imports that an epidemic lacks — an outside, a purpose, a victory condition, sides — and whether any of those imports distort what we know about the 1348 to 1353 period specifically, including the fact that the disease travelled along trade routes and tenurial arrangements that human beings had built. A colleague suggests the reviewer would prefer a hazard-and-vulnerability frame instead, in which the plague is treated as something closer to a flood, to which communities were differently exposed according to their landholding, their commerce and their health; please assess that alternative on the same terms, including whether it slides into the determinism it is meant to avoid. If neither frame survives, say what a better analogy would be, or whether the gallery should abandon analogies and describe the causal structure in plain terms. Roughly 900 to 1,100 words; a table of correspondences would be welcome.
