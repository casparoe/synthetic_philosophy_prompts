Novelist here, and a scene problem has turned into a philosophy problem. Two brothers inherit the same Nonconformist-owned mill in the north of England in the 1870s. One is a devout chapel man, one has lost his faith entirely, and they run the place identically — same hours, same ledgers, same refusal to ever take a week off. My freind, who teaches economic history, read the chapter and said it was dishonest because it smuggles in the Protestant work ethic, and then, about ten minutes later, said the scene actually refutes that same ethic, because the beliefs have gone and the behaviour hasn't. Both verdicts out of the same four words, in the same conversation, and I couldn't work out how to answer either.

So give me the menu of precise claims that slogan could be standing for. I want them separated out: the individual-psychology version where doctrine makes a particular man work a particular way; the population-level version spread over generations, where nobody's inner life is doing the work; the version on which religion removed an inhibitor rather than supplied a drive; the version where it's some affinity between ideas and interests rather than one causing the other; the version on which beliefs aren't causes at all, they just make the conduct intelligible to the people living it; and the version where the habits harden into an economic order that outlives the creed entirely. For each, say whether it's near-trivial, substantive and arguable, or flatly false. Then tell me which reading my friend needs for each of his two claims, whether the two claims can both come from one slogan without him contradicting himself, and whether a single scene with two brothers could possibly test any of them.

British English, please, and no reading list — I want the distinctions, not the footnotes.
