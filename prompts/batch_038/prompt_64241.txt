For a school research paper I have been reconstructing Avicenna's flying man argument, and I believe I have a version that survives the usual objections. It runs as follows. What the thought experiment establishes is that self-awareness can be had with no sensory or bodily input at all, and that this awareness presents a subject under no bodily description. From that I draw a conclusion about identity: whatever the self is, it cannot be identical to the body or to any configuration of bodily parts, since two things are identical only if the thing could, at least in principle, be given under the descriptions that pick out each of them — and the flying man's self is never given under bodily ones. I concede that the suspended man might retain faint vestibular or interoceptive feel; I maintain that deleting these changes nothing.

Tell me what would count against this. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, including any that turn on empirical findings about bodily self-awareness — phantom limbs, depersonalization, anesthesia, that kind of thing. For each, say whether a defender could absorb it by qualifying the view or whether it resists absorption, and say what my version is committed to that a bundle-theorist rival, for whom there is no bare subject at all, is not committed to. I do not want a neutral survey: commit to the ranking, give your verdict on whether the position still risks anything once fully qualified, and state your reasons.
