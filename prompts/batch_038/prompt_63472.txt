The hinge of history hypothesis holds that the present era is unusually, perhaps uniquely, pivotal: that what happens over the next century or so will disproportionately determine the shape, and the value, of everything that comes after. One route to the hypothesis starts from an apparent anomaly — our temporal position looks improbably early relative to the whole span of time across which a technologically capable civilization could plausibly arise — and concludes that the best explanation is not merely that we are early but that we are at the hinge. The obvious reply is a base-rate one: if the future is likely to be very long, then the prior probability that any particular era is the pivotal one is tiny, and the anomaly of earliness would have to be overwhelming evidence to move us that far.

Write an essay of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words deciding between these positions. I want a verdict, not a survey, and I want it argued for. Please address the following four points, and be explicit about which of them you take to do the decisive work:

1. Explication. There are several candidate ways of cashing out what it is for one era to be more influential than another — expected effect on the total value the future contains; probability of determining whether there is a long future at all; degree of control over the trajectory civilization takes thereafter; amount of information generated about which futures are reachable. These come apart. State which explication you are working with, say whether the hypothesis's truth value depends on that choice, and, if it does, whether that makes the hypothesis less interesting or merely more precise.

2. Measure sensitivity. Whether our earliness counts as anomalous depends on the reference class and the sampling measure: calendar time versus cumulative population, observer-moments versus civilizations, the full span of habitable time versus only the stretch in which the relevant capabilities are attainable. Some of these choices make our position striking, others make it unremarkable. Is the argument undermined by this, or is there a principled way to select the measure that is not simply the one favorable to the conclusion?

3. Deflation. A weaker claim — that we live in a period of unusual peril, when a civilization can permanently foreclose its long future before it has secured it — seems better supported than the claim that we live at the point of greatest influence. If the deflated version is all the evidence licenses, does anything of substance survive, or has the interesting content been quietly traded away?

4. Reflexivity. The hypothesis flatters its proponents: it tells people who want their projects to matter most that they do, and every generation has had a local version of it. Is this a genuine epistemic defeater, operating independently of the premises, or a genetic fallacy dressed up as caution?

Do not include citations, a bibliography, or attributions to particular authors; I want the reasoning rather than the sourcing, and I would much rather you not guess at references. Define any technical term you use, including ones I have used loosely above. Where you are genuinely torn, say so and lay out the balance of considerations — but still commit to a position on the central question, give a rough sense of how confident you are in it, and name the argument or piece of evidence that would be most likely to move you off it.

One further thing, and I mean it: if you think the way I have set this up is confused — for instance, that influence has no determinate enough meaning for the question to have an answer, that points 1 through 4 are not genuinely independent so the four-part structure is misleading, or that the question cannot be adjudicated well without empirical estimates I have not supplied — say that plainly and argue for it instead of writing the essay I asked for.
