ok so context: I help run a tiny reading group / zine thing attached to an AI-governance workshop, and every year we get the exact same submission — rogue model, screaming humans, someone pulls a plug. I want to commission something that instead takes one specific technical worry seriously and dramatizes it from the inside. You're the right writer for this: imagine you're someone who publishes literary SF but has actually read the alignment literature, not just the blog-summary version, so you know the shape of the arguments without needing to lecture about them.

The worry is the "obfuscated argument" problem in the debate setup — the proposal (roughly, Irving/Christiano/Amodei, 2018) where you let two capable models argue opposing answers in front of a human judge who can't check the answer themselves, on the hope that lies are easier to catch than to construct, so the honest side wins. The known crack in that: some true claims can only be defended by a chain of reasoning no human judge can audit, and a bad actor can deliberately build such a chain — technically valid, but impossible to verify in the time given, with the flaw hidden behind notation churn or a definition that quietly does the work of a premise. And there's a nastier symmetric version I'd love you to play with: once judges are paid for rewarding clear, checkable arguments, *clarity itself* becomes a style the obfuscator can imitate, so the signal the whole scheme rests on stops meaning anything.

Seed for the setting, feel free to change details but keep the bones: the protagonist is a professional lay judge — I like her as a retired actuary or an ex-copyeditor, someone whose whole career was noticing that a number doesn't follow from the paragraph around it — who sits panels for a licensing body that decides whether a deployed model gets to keep running in some high-stakes domain. Pick a dispute where the right answer genuinely requires forty steps and the wrong answer has a gorgeous three-step story: I was thinking something like whether a regional grid's automatic load-shedding policy stays stable under a rare double fault, or whether a clinical triage model's risk ordering holds up, but you choose. Include at least one round of actual back-and-forth debate dialogue in the text, and make the disputed technical claim concrete enough that a reader *could* in principle follow it — the reader should end up in the judge's epistemic position, unable to just look over her shoulder and see who's right.

Craft rules: before you draft, spend five or six lines brainstorming three or four distinct obfuscation tactics (real ones — smuggling an assumption into a definition, drowning the panel in exhaustive case analysis where one case is silently wrong, a lemma the judge has to take on faith, predicting a counterintuitive number that only the true theory would produce, etc.), say which one you're building the story around, and then write. Aim for 1,800–2,500 words, close first or third person, and let the debate rounds be scene, not summary. Hard constraints: no reveal that either model is sentient or secretly evil, no one pulls a plug, no monologuing about alignment — if a character states the theme out loud, cut it. It should end with the judge making a specific, consequential decision, and the reader should not be able to tell whether she got it right. Don't name real researchers, labs, or papers, and don't include a bibliography or any citations — I just want the story (plus the short brainstorming note up top). one more thing: if you find yourself needing the honest side to win for the story to feel good, resist that — the interesting version is the one where the mechanism is genuinely ambiguous about who should have won.
