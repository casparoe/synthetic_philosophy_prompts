I chair the five-member grants panel of a private foundation with an annual budget of roughly $4 million. Since 2019 our application form has required every proposal to state a single expected-impact figure per dollar — a point estimate with an accompanying interval, derived from the applicant's stated theory of change — and we have ranked the portfolio on that number. I designed the form. I have spent fifteen years reading and writing in rationalist-adjacent online communities, I have run calibration workshops for two nonprofits, and my instinct has always been to multiply the probabilities out rather than hide behind vagueness. This cycle my instrument has broken, in two different directions at once, and the panel is deadlocked 2–2 with me abstaining by custom.

**Proposal A** would fund a standing technical-assistance body helping three mid-sized cities draft and enforce rules governing the algorithmic systems their own agencies procure. The applicant supplies a numerical figure for exactly one narrow sub-component: the cost per city per year of the staffing model, which is well benchmarked. For the overall impact they decline to give a number at all, and instead submit four things: (i) a written list of six load-bearing assumptions, each with a short argument; (ii) for each assumption, the specific observable that would tell us by month 18 that it is false — whether a state preemption bill advances, whether two of the three cities reauthorize the function in a budget cycle, whether the vendor association adopts a compliance position; (iii) a statement of which assumptions, if false, would make the program worthless rather than merely less valuable; and (iv) a cover letter arguing that any overall figure they produced would be manufactured, and that our ranking procedure would then launder it into data. That cover letter invokes the risk/uncertainty distinction and name-checks Keynes and Knight approvingly; I have already told the applicant I don't need the history, and neither does this memo.

**Proposal B** would scale an existing job-training program into a fourth metropolitan labor market. Their submission reports $14.32 of expected annual earnings gain per dollar spent, discounted over ten years, with an 80% interval of $9 to $21. The figure is built from a published randomized trial of a similar program in a different labor market seven years ago, an assumed persistence curve for the earnings effect, an assumed transfer probability for the new market, and a discount rate. The interval is tight because their model propagates only the trial's standard errors; the other three inputs enter as constants.

Two panel members, both from finance, hold that Proposal A is unfalsifiable hand-waving and that a board which cannot compare cannot answer to its trustees. Two others, an academic and a former program officer, hold that Proposal B is arithmetic performed on guesses, and that our procedure systematically rewards whichever applicant is most skilled at concealing its assumptions. Their evidence is our own record: of the six funded proposals since 2019 whose intervals we treated as though they were calibrated, four ended outside them, and all four sat in the more confident half of the portfolio. I know that n=6 is not an argument. It is also not nothing.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the panel on Monday and subsequently share with the trustees. It should be written so that the two members who disagree with it can either sign on or state precisely where they dissent. Specifically:

1. **The bottom line first, in no more than four sentences:** what we do this cycle. Fund A, fund B, fund both, fund neither, or fund one conditionally — with the dollar consequences and a decision date that fits a quarterly board calendar.
2. **Reasons the losing side can actually engage with.** Not a restatement of the winning side's priors in more words. If you conclude that the two proposals are not commensurable and that this is a reason to fund one anyway, say what carries the decision once the numbers stop.
3. **A screening test for strategic vagueness.** This is the part I most need and the part I expect to be handled worst. Give the panel something it can apply in a single sitting, with no new staff and no additional applicant round, that distinguishes an honestly non-numerical submission from a submission that refuses numbers because its authors do not wish to be scored. Assume applicants are neither saints nor cynics; assume they respond to whatever incentive the form creates, including the incentive your test itself creates.
4. **An assessment of Proposal A's four-part format on its own terms.** Does a list of assumptions plus early-warning observables actually substitute for a probability, or does it merely relocate the judgment to a place our procedure cannot see? In particular: is there a version of this that escapes the objection that judging which assumptions are load-bearing, and how likely each is, is just probabilistic judgment with the numbers left implicit? And say plainly whether you think "this cannot be quantified" is ever a final answer or always a demand for a better-posed question.
5. **Whether to change the form for the next cycle.** If yes, give me the exact replacement wording of the requirement, not a description of it, and state what the change costs us in comparability across a heterogeneous portfolio. If no, say what we do instead about the two members who think the current form is producing confident fiction.
6. **The strongest consideration against your recommendation,** argued as someone who is right about it would argue it. Not a strawman I get to knock over in one line.
7. **Somewhere in the memo, address a question I have not been able to get the panel to take seriously:** whether a single expected-value ranking was ever the right instrument for a *portfolio*, given that our actual decision is which mix of bets to hold rather than which individual bet scores highest. If this dissolves part of the deadlock, say precisely which part; if it does not, say why the deadlock survives it.

Constraints. Roughly 1,200 words, plain prose, no table longer than five rows. Do not lecture me on the history of the risk/uncertainty distinction; if you use a technical term, define it in a subordinate clause, because half the panel has never met one. No bibliography, no suggested readings, no citations to literature — I cannot check them and I do not want them. Do not invent studies, effect sizes, or base rates; where you need a fact about our foundation or our applicants to complete an argument, flag it as an assumption for me to fill in rather than supplying it yourself.
