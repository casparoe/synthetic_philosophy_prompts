I'm auditing a grad seminar on collective intentionality this term and we've been slogging through Bratman on shared intention, Searle on we-intentions, Gilbert's plural subject, and Tuomela's we-mode vs i-mode stuff, and honestly half the distinctions feel hair-splitting until somebody hands you a case where the accounts actually come apart and predict different verdicts. The prof wants each of us to bring in one such case next week, and I'd rather bring a story than another trolley-flavored thought experiment, because I write fiction on the side and I think the format will land way better. Can you help me draft it? Please write as someone who is both a working philosopher of action and a competent short-story writer — take the metaphysics seriously, but don't let it curdle into a seminar paper with quotation marks around the dialogue.

Here's the setup I want you to build from. Near-future deep-sea salvage yard, or an orbital dock, whichever makes the physical stakes cleanest: there's a two-operator machine, give it a better name than "coupler," and it only delivers power when the two people on the controls are genuinely engaged in *one* joint action. It refuses to fire when they're merely doing two well-coordinated things that happen to line up. Nobody in the story really understands the detection mechanism — the manufacturer's manual is marketing mush — but the yard engineers have mapped the failure modes, and this is the important part: the thing drops out when one operator has privately rehearsed a backup plan that doesn't mesh with the other's, it drops out when each is only acting because she expects the other to act (so the pair is stuck in a circle that never closes), it drops out flat-out when one of them is thinking about lunch in a way that makes her stop tracking the other at all, and yet it holds steady through long boring stretches where both are barely attending, as long as each stays disposed to adjust to the other. Story engine: a veteran operator and a new hire have to bring one very heavy, very awkward thing up in one piece, the machine keeps cutting out at the worst possible moments, and figuring out why forces the two of them — and the reader — to work out which account of shared agency the machine is actually implementing. Bonus if one of the dropouts turns out to have nothing to do with either operator's mental states and everything to do with whether they'd formed any kind of joint commitment to each other beforehand, i.e. whether somebody is entitled to rebuke whom.

Before you write a single line of prose, spend a few hundred words brainstorming out loud: pick which theory the device embodies, say what has to be true of the two operators for each of the failure modes I listed, and — this is the bit I'll get grilled on — say what the rival accounts would have predicted differently in that exact same scene. Then write the story, 1,500 to 2,000 words, close third person on the new hire, with the philosophy carried entirely by the machinery and the argument between the two of them. Hard constraints: no character ever says the phrase shared intention, no philosopher gets name-dropped in the text, and no paragraph that exists only to explain the device. I want the ending to complicate rather than resolve — something about whether the machine is detecting a real we or just exquisitely fine-grained mutual prediction, and about whether the veteran has been coasting on a faked version of the thing for years and it keeps working anyway. And one thing I keep tripping over that I'd love you to have a character feel the force of without solving: if the machine requires each operator's intention to be held in part because of the other's, isn't the state it's detecting kind of circular, or at least threatening to regress? Think carefully about that before you start, it might be the best scene in the whole thing.
