I am keeping notes on a familiar objection to hedonism: that pleasures are far too various to share a single felt quality — the taste of a ripe peach, the relief of scratching an itch, the satisfaction of finishing a long proof, the pleasure of being thought well of by someone whose opinion matters — so that any attempt to say what all of them have in common either picks out something true of only some of them, or something so thin that it also applies to states nobody would call pleasant. What I want to pin down is not that objection but a particular way it gets answered, and I do not know whether that way has a name.

The pattern runs as follows. Confronted with the list, the theorist stops looking for anything inside the experiences. Pleasure is instead characterised by how the person stands toward the experience: to be pleased is to want the experience to go on while one is having it, or to welcome it, or to be averse to its stopping. On this account the peach, the itch, the proof and the compliment need share no phenomenal ingredient whatever; what unifies them is that each is met with the same favourable turn of mind. The objection is then treated as dissolved rather than answered: there was never supposed to be a common feel, because feeling some particular way was never what made a state a pleasure.

A second instance, slightly further along the same road. Someone protests that the account now counts as pleasant experiences the person would insist are not pleasant at all — the anxious parent who wants the sleepless night with a sick child to continue, and to continue for that night's own sake — or that it collapses pleasure into mere desiredness, so that the tedious commute one wants to keep taking only because it ends at the office becomes a pleasure. The reply is to tighten the description of the favourable turn of mind: the experience must be wanted for its own sake and not as a means, or wanted in a manner independent of any belief about its consequences. The recalcitrant cases are absorbed again, and again by saying more about the person's relation to the experience rather than anything about the experience itself. What makes the tightening look suspicious is that wanting a state for its own sake seems to presuppose that there is something agreeable about it, so the refined criterion may simply assume the very ingredient the first version was designed to eliminate.

What I want a term for is the price of this. Once pleasure is fixed by a favourable relation, the claim that pleasure is the good thing, the thing worth having for its own sake, appears to lose content: it comes out as the claim that what we welcome is what we ought to welcome. The theory settles the question about what pleasures have in common by ceasing to be a theory about anything in the pleasures, and my suspicion is that this is not a quirk of these two cases but a general shape that replies to heterogeneity problems elsewhere tend to take.

So: is there an established concept for this manoeuvre — dissolving a what-do-these-things-have-in-common problem by relocating the common element from the things themselves to the subject's stance toward them, at the cost of the account no longer describing the things? I gather that the surrounding territory contains various analyses of pleasure in terms of wanting, various accounts of value in terms of the responses that value merits, and the strategy of declaring a term a loose family of cases with no shared essence. I would like to know how what I have described differs from each of those, and which is the closest match. If nothing established fits, please coin a term and define it tightly enough that I could adjudicate a new case: does the manoeuvre still count when the favourable stance is attributed to a bystander rather than to the person undergoing the experience, and does it count when the common element is relocated to something non-psychological, such as a causal or functional role the states play in a system? Please also tell me whether the cost I have described is a genuine objection or something a defender of such accounts can absorb without revising the account. A bare label will not serve my purpose here; what I need is the boundary of the thing — what falls under it, what falls just outside, and why.
