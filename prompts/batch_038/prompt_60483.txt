I'm on the conduct committee at a worker co-op — a grocery and bakery, about 45 worker-owners — and I've been the unlucky one for three years now. We get maybe six or eight real cases a year. Somebody took product out the back without ringing it up, somebody blew up at a customer, somebody ran a shift without doing the safety check, somebody made a snap decision during a crisis that broke two rules at once. Stakes aren't small either: worst case the person loses their job and their buy-in, and any expulsion has to go to a full membership vote at the quarterly meeting, so our three volunteers have to write up a one-paragraph rationale that gets read out loud and picked apart.

The thing I've never been able to think my way out of is what to do when the rule we're being asked to enforce is definitely real but also definitely awful. Last year there was a clause in the bylaws about losing good standing after three documented absences. Nobody on the committee remembered it being passed, it lived in a section that had been renumbered twice, it hadn't been enforced in years as far as anyone could tell, and we were about to apply it to a woman who missed her shifts because her kid was in the hospital. Two of us wanted to say that isn't a rule anymore, it was never properly announced and everyone's acted like its dead for a decade. The third said no — a rule's a rule, you don't get to choose which ones you feel like today, if you think it's bad you change it going forward, but you still apply it, because the moment we start deciding which rules are real based on whether we like the outcome we're the ones making things up. We split, it was ugly, and I've been chewing on it since. I'm pretty sure this is an old argument between two legal philosophers — something about whether an evil law is still a law, and I think one of them was reacting to what happened with the courts and informers in Germany after the war. I haven't read either of them and I'm not going to pretend otherwise, and I don't want a reading list, I have no way to chase it down. I just want the actual argument explained in plain words, and whether it gives me anything usable.

So: write me a procedure our committee could run on a complaint, before we ever get to whether the person did it. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, which failures of a rule are so bad that we should treat it as not-a-rule-at-all and refuse to apply it, which failures just mean we apply it and flag it to the membership as needing a fix, and where the procedure has to stop and hand it to a human because the question is about values, not facts. I also need it to handle the case where the words of a perfectly good rule don't obviously cover what happened — how do we tell whether we're applying the rule or quietly writing a new one and hitting someone with it. Mark every step that's really a judgment call dressed up as a step, because two of the three of us will pretend otherwise if we don't.

Then run it on three cases: the absence clause above; a properly passed, properly posted, universally known rule that says anyone caught removing product without paying it for is out, applied to a guy who grabbed a loaf to eat on break and forgot to write it down; and a case where there is no rule at all and the panel is tempted to invent one on the spot and apply it to the person sitting in front of us. At least one of the three should come out in a way I'm going to hate — I'd rather find that out now than in the room. And finish by telling me what the procedure can't do, and where it will reliably misfire, because we can't loose anyone's trust over a checklist that oversells itself.
