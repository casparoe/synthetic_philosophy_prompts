I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine — mostly the epistemology-adjacent fluff, brain science, AI, whatever's in the air. My current piece is about the "core belief" wellness industry: retreats, apps and coaches that promise to swap out your deepest convictions for better ones, usually in seven days and for four figures. I've got plenty of straight reporting already; what I need from you is the thing that runs in the sidebar. So: a parody consumer buying guide — the Wirecutter-meets-luxury-spa-brochure register, deadpan from first line to last, no winking — reviewing three or four "hinge recalibration" programs, i.e. retreats that advertise replacing your hinge commitments, the things Wittgenstein pictured as the unmoving door your doubts and justifications swing on, the hard rock in the river-bed rather than the water running over it. Around 700 words. My editor wants it wierd but legible, and I'm not after a pile of one-liners; the humour should be structural, the way it is when a genre takes a category error completely seriously.

The thing that makes this work or fall apart for me is that the joke has to be that the entire business model is a category error the sellers haven't noticed: hinge commitments aren't the sort of thing you can adopt because the reasons turned out to be good, and any demonstration a retreat could give its clients already leans on hinges those clients walked in with — so the satisfaction guarantee is circular and the free trial presupposes exactly what's on trial. Get the distinctions right and my friend who's finishing a dissertation in this area will laugh instead of wince. That means: sorting the advertised swaps into ones that would genuinely be hinge swaps and ones that are just a change of opinion dressed up as one; the difference in kind between being sure you have two hands, being sure you've never been to the moon, and being sure you're the reasonable sort of person who can be wrong about things; the arbitrariness problem, i.e. if the entitlement to your hinges isn't evidential in the first place, what stops the rival retreat down the road from being every bit as entitled; the vertigo complaint, that some clients come back with all their hinges intact and are somehow more frightened than before; and the leaching question, whether the non-evidential entitlement you buy is supposed to seep down into your ordinary beliefs or stay quarantined upstairs where it can't do any work.

Structure it like a real buying guide and let the structure eat itself. I want a "what we tested" section where the methodology visibly falls apart, since the reviewer can't check any outcome without using the very hinges under review; a star rating justified in an obviously question-begging way; a "who should skip this" section covering both the person who just wanted to change a mundane opinion and the sceptic who keeps demanding evidence where none can be had; a short Q&A with an invented founder who sounds like a plausible blend of guru and analytic philosopher; and a "the bottom line" verdict that stops dead right where the reasoning runs out, at the point where the spade hits bedrock and all you can say is that this is simply what you do. Please paraphrase Wittgenstein's images rather than quoting him — our copy desk won't clear translations and I'd rather you not guess at wording. Same spirit elsewhere: hinge epistemology, entitlement, whatever vocabulary you like, but build the piece around invented people and invented programs, not around named living scholars. Two last things. Keep it readable cold, because most of our readers have never met the word hinge in this sense and should think they're reading about a wellness scam, with the philosophy as the mechanism rather than the setup. And don't turn the reviewer into a strawman — I want this to feel fair to the actual view, so let her half-concede somewhere that there's something right in it, and if you can, sneak in one line about the price and how the fee itself functions as evidence that the program worked.
