I'm in the second half of a philosophy of biology seminar, and we've spent the last two weeks on biological function. I've read enough to know the two camps: the causal-role or dispositional account associated with Cummins, and the etiological account of Wright, Millikan, and Neander, on which a trait's function is the effect that explains why the trait is there. Our seminar leader keeps compressing the second view into the line "functions are selected effects," and has now twice used it to close a discussion rather than to continue one.

The first time, a classmate asked whether a structure that arose by drift and was only later recruited to a job it never had has a function at all, and was told that if it wasn't selected for, it isn't a function, full stop. The second time it was me. I work in a synthetic biology group, and I asked about two things in our own strains. One is a gene we put on a construct purely as a selection marker for our convenience; under the chemostat conditions we actually run, its product also degrades a contaminant, and the strain's growth depends on that second effect. The other is a protein in a directed-evolution lineage that, after roughly forty rounds of lab-imposed selection, has acquired a side activity we now rely on for the assay. I asked whether these parts have functions and whether they have more than one, and was told the slogan settles it, since selection did the selecting. I'm not convinced, and I suspect one sentence is being made to carry several different claims, not all of them true.

Could you lay out the distinct theses "functions are selected effects" might be expressing, arranged from weakest to strongest? For each, I'd like to know whether it is a truism about how the word is used in evolutionary explanation, a substantive thesis that could be argued for, or something false or at least highly contentious; what it commits you to; and how much of ordinary function talk survives it. Please also attend to a few things I can't pin down myself. "Selected effect" might mean the effect that explains why the trait first arose, the effect that explains why it persists, or the effect most recently selected for, and I don't know whether the choice matters. A trait has many effects, only some of them selected, so I'd like to hear how each reading handles the case where more than one effect was selected, and the case where the effect we care about was never selected but is now indispensable. I'd also like to know what each reading implies about malfunction, about byproducts of otherwise adaptive structures, and about the fact that in my example the selecting agent is a graduate student with a goal rather than differential survival in the wild: does the slogan survive being generalized to designed and artificially selected systems, or does that generalization quietly change the subject?

Finally, tell me which reading, if any, would actually support the two dismissals above, and which of my classmate's questions and mine would still be live even on that reading. A list I can bring to the next seminar is what I need; assume I know the literature, so no references, just the analysis.
