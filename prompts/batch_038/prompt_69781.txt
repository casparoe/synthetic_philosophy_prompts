My philosophy teacher has been running our seminar through puzzles about objects and the stuff they're made of, and she never states a principle — she only hands down rulings. Here they are as I recorded them:

- A clay statue is flattened back into a lump: the clay is still there, the statue is not.
- A car whose every part is replaced over ten years: still the same car, and the heap of old parts in the garage is not the car.
- A ship whose planks are replaced one at a time while the removed planks are stored and later reassembled in a museum: the sailing ship is the original, not the museum one.
- A gold wedding ring worn thin across a lifetime: the ring survives, and so does the gold — and she said these are not two answers to one question but answers to two different questions.
- A pile of bricks restacked into a wall: the pile is gone, the bricks are not. She added that a pile was never anything over and above the bricks arranged that way.
- A person who loses a hand in an accident: survives. A person whose brain is transplanted into another body: goes with the brain.

The case we get on Monday is this. Our art department 3D-printed a sculpture for the spring show out of a recycled-plastic composite. It warped, and over the following year a student replaced each section as it failed, so that none of the original printed plastic remains — though the piece has never left its display case and the title placard has never changed. The failed sections and the sanding debris were collected, re-pelletized, and printed into an exact duplicate, which the school sold at auction. The original print file is still on the art department's server.

Which one would she say is the sculpture: the one in the case, the one that was sold, both, or neither? I care less about her answer than about the machinery behind it. Lay out the candidate rules that are compatible with all six of her earlier rulings — including any that treat the sculpture as a different kind of thing altogether from the plastic — and say what each one delivers on Monday's case. Then tell me which rule you would bet on and why. Two of her rulings look like they pull in opposite directions, the statue one and the brick pile one; say whether that is a genuine inconsistency in her view or something a single rule can absorb. Finally, suggest one further case I could put to her that would split whichever rules survive all of this.
