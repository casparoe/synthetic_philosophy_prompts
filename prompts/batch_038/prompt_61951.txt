I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine — mostly the seam where tech policy runs into philosophy — and I'm putting together a piece on the automation of border decisions. I want to use Michael Dummett as a kind of ghost witness, because he's maybe the last major philosopher who genuinely did both halves of my story: heavyweight philosophy of language (Frege, semantic anti-realism, the realism/anti-realism debate reframed as a debate about truth versus warranted assertibility) and decades of hands-on immigration and anti-racism work in Britain. He wrote On Immigration and Refugees for Routledge in 2001, he and his wife Ann co-founded the Institute of Race Relations back in the late 50s, and there's the wonderful detail that they used to drive out to Heathrow to take up the cases of people facing deportation. He died at the end of December 2011, so he never saw a single one of the things my piece is actually about.

So: tell me what he would have made of them. I want a reasoned reconstruction I could put in front of a philosopher of language without getting laughed at, not a horoscope.

Here's the machinery I want you to run him through. Two post-2011 developments:

1. **Automated admissability screening.** A large share of visa and entry-clearance decisions in rich countries are now made, or effectively pre-made, by software: an applicant files an e-visa, the system pulls biometrics and travel history, scores them against a risk model trained on past decisions, and the file is triaged. In the common architecture the default is refusal — an application is denied unless the score clears a threshold — and a human officer mostly reviews the flagged or appealed minority. Where a reason is given to the applicant, it's often a generic code selected after the fact rather than the actual causal driver of the score. Nobody outside the vendor can audit the model, and the state's official position is usually that the system is merely advisory and the officer remains the decision-maker.

2. **Third-country transfer schemes.** The post-2011 fashion, the UK's Rwanda plan being the paradigm case, is to declare that people who arrive irregularly will have their claims processed in, or be removed to, a state they have no connection to — with the receiving state paid to host them. Legally dressed as consistent with the refugee convention, functionally a way of making it impossible to reach the territory where you'd lodge a claim.

Now the crux, which is the part I actually need help with, because I suspect I'm forcing a connection and I want you to tell me if I am.

My instinct is that Dummett's anti-realism should bite hard on refugee status determination, and that it could bite in two opposite directions:

- **Reading A:** anti-realism deflates evidence-transcendent truth. If there is no fact of the matter about whether someone is a refugee beyond what a properly run procedure could establish, then the state can say: our procedure *is* the standard, the score is all there is, and the appeal to what's "really" true of this applicant is empty metaphysics. On this reading Dummett's philosophy is a gift to the restrictionists.
- **Reading B:** Dummett's anti-realism isn't a licence for proceduralism, because for him understanding a concept is a practical *ability* — the capacity to recognize what would warrant asserting it. A procedure that systematically destroys the community's capacity to recognize warranted cases (no reasons given, no auditability, no individual hearing) doesn't track the concept, it abolishes the practice that gave the word a meaning. On this reading he'd be far more hostile to the automation than any civil-liberties campaigner, and for a weirder reason.

Which reading is right? And is there a third one I'm missing? I want you to argue it out rather than split the difference.

Three further things I'd like you to weigh in on:

- Does his Fregean account of judgment and assertion give him an objection to machine refusal that has nothing to do with error rates or bias metrics? Something like: an assertion is an act by a judge who takes responsibility for a thought, so a score is not a judgment at all and a refusal that issues from a model is a refusal nobody has made. If you think that's a real Dummettian line, press it; if you think I've dressed up a slogan, say so.
- He wasn't a procedure-skeptic in general — he designed voting systems and was perfectly happy getting his hands dirty with mechanism design. So don't hand me a Dummett who just says "a human must decide everything." I want the Dummett who'd specify what a *legitimate* automated pre-screen would have to look like.
- Where does the Rwanda-style scheme land, given that on my recollection he argued for something like a presumption in favour of admitting an asylum seeker, with the burden on the state to justify refusal rather than on the claimant to prove entitlement? Flag that if it's wrong or a distortion, because default-allow versus default-deny is the hinge my whole piece turns on.

And two extras if you have room: would he have accepted "climate refugee" as a new legal category, or thought it wrecked a distinction he spent a book defending? And give me at least one concession he'd have made to the restrictionist side that a Dummett sympathiser would find painful but honest — I don't want a saint.
