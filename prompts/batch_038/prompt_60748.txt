Six months ago my sister left a logistics job to become a founding member of a worker-owned bakery in our city: eleven member-owners, one vote each, surplus distributed in proportion to hours worked. In the spring they took on a wholesale contract too large for eleven people and hired six non-member employees — about a fifth above the local going rate, fixed shifts, no on-call — with eligibility to apply for membership after two years, subject to a £4,000 buy-in deducted from wages over eighteen months. Three of the six have already left; none has become a member. The board has asked me, as the one person in the family who studies this sort of thing, to draft a policy on non-member labour before they take another contract, and the first drafting session dissolved into an argument between my sister and an old friend of hers, Dev, who spent a decade organising in the hospitality industry. I was nominated to referee, found each of them convincing at different moments, and would like a proper adjudication rather than a survey of the standard positions.

The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Dev said the arrangement is exploitation, plainly: the six produce value that accrues to owners who do not hand it to them, and the owners' calloused hands and democratic meetings change nothing about the arithmetic — inside that building the eleven are collectively the capitalist.

(2) My sister replied that exploitation is a relation between classes, and a class relation requires the owners to be a separate group from the workers; in a co-operative the surplus returns to labour rather than to capital, so there is nothing left over for anyone to be exploited out of.

(3) Dev said that runs the argument backwards: the six are a separate group from the eleven, so the class division his account needs exists — it is drawn inside the firm rather than between the firm and the market. He added that Marx treated co-operative factories as a transitional form in which the antagonism between capital and labour is overcome, but overcome only for those who are actually associated; and that in his 1864 address to the First International Marx welcomed the co-operative movement as proof that the master is not a permanent necessity of production while insisting that co-operatives confined to the isolated efforts of individual workers cannot arrest the growth of monopoly or free the masses.

(4) My sister said that on Dev's principle every trade union employing an organiser, and every housing co-operative employing a caretaker, is an exploiter of its own staff, and a theory that returns that verdict is refuted by its own implications.

(5) Dev said he will take that verdict: such employers do appropriate surplus from their staff. But magnitude was never his point — exclusion was, and the buy-in is exclusion with a price on it. The non-members earn roughly £24,000 a year, so membership costs them about two months' wages before it costs them nothing; a door you have to buy your way through is not an open door.

(6) My sister said Dev's position is unfalsifiable: whatever the co-operative turns out to do, competition made it do it, so neither the members' intentions nor the actual terms of employment can count as evidence for or against his claim that structure is what matters. Dev answered that the claim is testable by comparing co-operatives with investor-owned firms under the same competitive pressure.

Two further questions arose, which I would like answered separately. A third person at the table proposed settling the matter with Roemer's counterfactual test — a group is exploited if it would be better off withdrawing with its per-capita share of the productive assets — and asked whether that test is even defined for a single firm rather than a whole economy, and what it would say about the six. And Dev conceded at the end that on his own account the six are not badly treated; one of them told me this is the best-paid job she has had and that what wears her down is being guest staff at somebody else's democracy. Is it right that the alienation charge, rather than the exploitation charge, carries the normative weight here?

Please take moves (1) through (6) one at a time and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating, before saying anything about who wins overall. Then say what both of them are missing, answer the two further questions, and tell me which single consideration should most shape the policy the board has asked me to draft. Because I am going to put this in front of eleven people who own the bakery, please state how confident you are in each rating and single out the one point in your whole answer where you are least sure of yourself; and if you think I have mis-stated a move — reporting someone as committed to something that does not follow from what they actually said — say so rather than reconstructing it into a better argument.
