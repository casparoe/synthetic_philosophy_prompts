Write an essay on a narrow question about the transitivity of "all-things-considered better than." I want a defended verdict, not a survey of the debate.

The question, as I have framed it for myself, is this. Spectrum arguments for intransitivity — a long chain of adjacent pairs each of which seems not to differ meaningfully from the next, joined to endpoints that seem clearly comparable — are often met with the reply that the intransitivist has not discovered anything deep about value but has merely noticed that value is multidimensional and that the dimensions admit of no fixed exchange rate. Call the resulting view dimensional majoritarianism: in each pairwise comparison one option wins on a majority of the relevant dimensions, and because the winning majority shifts from pair to pair, the overall relation can be cyclic. My worry is that this view has exactly the formal structure of the Condorcet paradox, with dimensions playing the role of voters and a cyclic betterness relation playing the role of a cyclic social preference. Yet in the social case we do not treat the cycle as a discovery about the world. We treat it as an artefact of an aggregation procedure we already had independent reason to distrust, and we respond by looking for a better procedure, or by conceding that what the procedure outputs is not a genuine ranking at all. So does the structural parallel show that intransitive betterness is likewise an artefact, and hence that transitivity is safe after all? Or does the parallel break — and if it breaks, exactly where?

Please address, at minimum:

1. Whether the analogy is genuine. Are the dimensions of a value comparison relevantly like voters? Do they have equal standing? Is there a determinate fact about which dimensions are relevant to a given comparison? And does the majority really settle the pairwise verdict, or does dimensional majoritarianism covertly appeal to comparisons of magnitude across dimensions that it was supposed to avoid, in which case the cycle is not generated by the view but merely tolerated by it?

2. The money-pump consideration. A cyclic betterness relation exposes an agent to a sequence of individually acceptable trades that leaves her back where she started, minus a small cost at each step. A cyclic electorate is exposed, in the same way, to agenda manipulation. Does pumpability settle anything, given that in both cases the vulnerability seems to attach to the procedure rather than to the underlying pairwise verdicts? Consider whether precommitment or backward-inducting sophisticated choice blocks the pump, and whether a blocked pump still counts against the intransitivist.

3. Whether the correct conclusion may be neither transitivity nor intransitivity but incomparability: the pairs in a spectrum are not comparable at all, so no relation with a transitivity property to lose is in play. If you take this line, say what makes it a substantive alternative rather than a relabelling of the intransitivist's position.

4. The strongest objection you can find to the position you end up defending, and your reply to it.

I do not need a bibliography or a literature review; refer to positions and arguments descriptively and I will supply the references myself. Please distinguish carefully, both as you go and in a short closing section, between what is settled among people who work on these questions and what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus. Aim for 1,500–2,000 words.

End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one short phrase naming the position you defend — for instance that transitivity of betterness holds, that it fails, that it fails only for some identified subclass of comparisons, that the relation in play is incomparability rather than intransitivity, or that the question as posed is malformed — and nothing after that line.
