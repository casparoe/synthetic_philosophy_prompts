I run consensus and node infra at a mid size proof-of-stake chain (not one of the big ones), and I've been putting together an internal doc about our governance and upgrade path — basically who holds sovereignty in a system where the rules can only change if enough validators signal they'll actually run the new client. Along the way I got hooked on Spinoza's political stuff: the TTP chapters on natural right and the foundations of the state (16, 17, and the big freedom-of-judgement one at the end), plus the Political Treatise, which of course breaks off mid-sentence in the middle of the chapter on democracy. I'm not a philosopher, I read the old public-domain translation and skimmed bits of a newer one, so treat my summaries as rough. My hypothesis, which I want you to pressue-test rather than flatter, is that Spinoza's whole political theory is a claim about the gap between de jure authority and actual quorum. He says the sovereign's right extends exactly as far as its power does; the state's power is the power of the multitude acting as if led by one mind; in a democracy you don't hand your right over to somebody else so much as join the body whose power it becomes part of; and a ruler who tries to command what people actually think rather than what they do just produces dissimulation and eventually loses the ability to get itself obeyed, which on his account means it has lost the right. I keep reading that and thinking "he's describing consensus" — nodes, quorum certificates, safety and liveness, forks as literal state dissolution, slashing and rewards as the fear/hope levers he talks about, Sybil resistance as whatever decides who counts as a member of the multitude at all, and the upgrade path as the place where you can watch sovereignty move around in real time.

So: restate Spinoza's account of democracy in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. Take the specific claims one at a time — right equals power, the multitude led as if by one mind, the "transfer" of right to the majority, the sovereign's inability to command judgement, the preference for a large assembly, the claim that a state held together only by fear is quiet but not free, and the conatus / adequate-cause machinery from the Ethics that seems to sit underneath all of it — and for each one say whether it maps cleanly onto something in consensus-and-governance language, whether it only maps with distortion (and name the distortion), or whether it has no expression in my vocabulary whatsoever. Two traps I want you to actively avoid. Don't smuggle in the assumption that there's a spec and that correctness is defined relative to it, because I'm fairly sure Spinoza has no spec and no designer and the state composes rather than gets built. And don't just relabel his notions with mine and call it a translation — if the only reason "the right of the commonwealth is the power of the multitude" reads deep to me is that I've already got a theory of what a majority is, say so.

One seeded sub-question, because a colleague and I couldn't settle it over lunch: Spinoza's argument that a bigger and more diverse assembly is likelier to act rationally, and therefore has more right, sounds to me exactly like a fault-tolerance argument — more participants, less correlated failure, better chance of converging on something true. But is that his mechanism? My sense is that his is about people agreeing in nature to the extent that they live by reason, which is a claim about what kind of things the participants are, not about their failure probabilities being independent — and if so the analogy is doing something sneaky, because a network that converges precisely because every node is the same kind of rational thing is close to the opposite of Byzantine fault tolerance, where you assume nothing of the sort. Tell me which reading is right, or whether there's a third I'm missing. Then the verdict I actually want: for each piece that doesn't survive, is that because my vocabulary is missing something real — some first-person or normative or metaphysical layer that consensus engineering genuinely has no words for — or because the original claim was doing more rhetorical work than argumentative work? In particular, is "right extends as far as power" just trivially true of any network that's actually running (nobody is going to enforce a rule the quorum won't enforce), or is it a substantive thesis that could come out false? And please be explicit about the scholarship: mark which of your claims about what Spinoza holds are settled and which are live disputes among people who work on him — whether he's a contract theorist at all after the TTP, whether the power/right identity is descriptive or normative, what to make of the unfinished democracy chapter. Don't hand me one contested reading as though it were the textbook view.
