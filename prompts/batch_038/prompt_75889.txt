ok, context first, sorry, this runs long — I'm about four-fifths through a novel set in an invented island territory I've called Vessa (roughly 90,000 people, ex-naval coaling station turned nickel plant, something like a third of the islanders now living abroad in the metropole, plus a few thousand mainland-born plant workers who've arrived since the last count). The third section of the book is the third self-determination referendum, and the scene I'm stuck on happens in the registration office on the last day before the roll closes. Two people in the room. Adèle, 61, the electoral registrar, born on Vessa, who spent her twenties and thirties practising constitutional law in the metropole and came home, and who holds that the roll is legitimate because it was fixed by the accord that stopped the shootings — that the electorate here is the people who make up Vessa as a continuing political project, durable ties and shared fate, and that you cannot build a demos out of everyone the outcome happens to touch because that set has no edges whatsoever. And Tomas, 47, born on the island, left at nineteen, twenty-six years abroad, still owns a hectare of his mother's land and still pays her care bills, who has come home to bury her and to vote and has just been told he isn't on the roll. He's the affectedness side, and his best move isn't sentiment, it's the circularity: whoever drew this roll decided who would get to decide, and that decision was taken by a parliament no demos ever authorized to take it, so the boundary isn't democratic all the way down — it rests on a prior act of power wearing procedure's clothes. Standing behind him in the queue is a mainland-born woman who's been on Vessa eleven years, subject to every law the new state would make and excluded anyway, and I want them to fight over her because she wrecks both their principles at once. Write this as an actual scene, somewhere around 1,200 to 1,500 words, mostly dialogue with just enough stage business to keep it a room rather than a seminar — no headings, no narration explaining what the theory is. Before you write anything, think carefully and brainstorm out loud in a few bullets: the two positions, the strongest argument each one really has, what each one cannot answer, and the two or three places where the argument should change register (procedural, then personal, then something closer to fear). Then write the scene. I need you holding two jobs at once for this — a political philosopher who's thought hard about the boundary problem, and a fiction editor with a red pencil who will cut any line that smells like a journal article. That second part matters most: nobody in this room is allowed to name a philosopher, cite a book, or say a term of art out loud. The ideas have to arrive the way people in a registration office would actually put them, as grievances and as rules quoted off the back of a form and as the history of who shot at whom, and a political theorist reading it should still be able to say exactly which criterion of membership each of them is leaning on and precisely where it snaps.

Both of them have to be right about something and both have to be good at this. I've drafted versions where the registrar sounds like a functionary hiding behind a form and versions where Tomas sounds like a comfortable expat feeling sorry for himself, and neither is any use to me. Adèle gets the real weight of her side: affectedness has no stopping rule, so a demos defined that way is either infinite or arbitrary depending on who's doing the counting; the roll was part of a settlement that stopped people killing each other, which is a kind of legitimacy that doesn't come from a prior demos at all; a yes vote would bring into being a state whose people are not the same set as the people who voted for it; and she has spent her whole career watching self-determination claimed by people who left and never paid for anything afterwards. Tomas gets the real weight of his: he'd be bound by the result whether or not he cast a vote, since he loses the passport, the land, the right to come home; the metropole's freezing of the roll was itself power exercised over people with no say in it; the woman in the queue is far more subjected to Vessan law than he is, so Adèle's own principle should be admitting her and not him; and nobody anywhere in the system gives an honest answer to who decides who decides. I want one moment where each of them lands something the other genuinely can't field, and I want the scene to arrive at the point where Tomas asks who decided he isn't one of them and Adèle's true answer is that nobody did — not in the sense he means. Don't resolve it and don't let either of them convert; end on a physical action, the form or the stamp or the pen she has to set down or pick up, with the argument still open. Fold in some texture from the real cases I've been reading, but obliquely, never referenced in the dialogue: the 2014 Scottish vote let sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds and resident EU and Commonwealth citizens vote while shutting out Scots living elsewhere in the UK; the 2016 EU vote shut out Brits who'd been abroad more than fifteen years but let resident Irish and Commonwealth citizens vote on the country's constitutional future; and there's the Pacific territory where the independence roll was frozen so later arrivals could never vote on it, with a European court holding that was acceptable because such a vote concerns self-determination rather than ordinary representation. If you can make Adèle and Tomas argue like two people who've each read a different half of that history, that's exactly what I'm after. No bibliography, no suggested reading, no footnotes, please — just the brainstorm and then the scene.
