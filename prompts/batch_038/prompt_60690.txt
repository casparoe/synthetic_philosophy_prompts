For eleven years I have worked the night shift maintaining pumps at a municipal water treatment plant, and for most of that time I have been reading philosophy on my own — nothing systematic, mostly whatever turns up in the library's used-book sales, which is how I came across a battered copy of a book on the metaphysics of ordinary objects and spent an entire winter on what I now know is called the special composition question: when do some things add up to one further thing? The difficulty is that my job keeps intruding on the reading. I spend my nights taking machines apart and reassembling them, and the cases philosophers reach for — statues, chairs, heaps of sand — never feel like the objects I actually handle. What does feel like one of my objects is a magnetically coupled pump: motor on one side of a containment shell, impeller on the other, torque passed across a gap with nothing touching anything, and yet the assembly does one job and is inventoried, ordered for, and repaired as a single item. A few friends and I argue about this sort of thing over breakfast on Tuesdays, and I want to bring them something better constructed than my usual rambling, so I am asking for a thought experiment built to a fairly exacting specification rather than a general survey of the debate.

Here is what I want. One continuous sequence of stages applied to one artifact on one workbench: no science fiction, no teleportation or atom-swapping, no demons or gods, no living organisms anywhere in the case, and every stage describable in the plain language a maintenance technician would use. Six stages at minimum, arranged so that two moderate answers come apart in both directions — the view that composition requires contact or fastening between the parts, and the view that what matters instead is whether the parts are jointly performing one function. So I need at least one stage at which the parts are rigidly fastened together but serve no single joint purpose, where the contact theorist says a new object has come into existence and the functional-unity theorist says there is nothing over and above the parts; and at least one stage at which a joint function is transmitted across a gap with no contact anywhere, reversing those two verdicts. Meanwhile the extremes must stay fixed throughout: the universalist says a whole exists at every stage, and the view that composition never happens says none exists at any stage. Since I have ruled out living things, that last view will agree at every stage with the position that only organisms ever compose, though the two disagree about why — please tell me whether that unbroken agreement is a defect in the design rather than leaving me to notice it later. On top of this, the stages must be sharp. No stage may be a borderline case of being in contact or of performing one function, because what I actually want to test is whether a restricted answer can escape the charge that any restriction must be vague, and therefore arbitrary, and therefore false. A case whose apparent sharpness comes from my stipulating the verdicts instead of from the theories generating them counts as a failure, and I would rather be told so plainly than reassured.

What I would like back, in this order: the scenario itself, no longer than a paragraph, with the stages numbered; a stage-by-stage list giving each of the four positions' verdict and the reason that position gives in its own terms; an identification of the single feature of the case that does the work of separating the two moderate views, together with what happens to the case if that feature is removed; the best available attack from each of the two moderate views on the case, since I am especially curious whether the contact theorist has any room to argue that a magnetic coupling is contact once the arrangement is described at the right level; and a closing, honest judgment on whether the sharpness requirement can genuinely be met here, or whether I have merely picked a domain where the vagueness is harder to spot. Write it for an intelligent reader who has never taken a philosophy class, but do not sand down the technical distinctions to make it easier on us — we would rather learn the distinctions than be flattered.
