I'm going round in circles on biopics and I'd like you to actually pick a side for me.

Here's the setup I care about. Say a film about a real, dead person invents a scene that never happened, and the invention isn't a factual slip — it isn't a wrong date or two people merged into one composite — it's the person making a choice they would almost certainly have refused to make if the situation had ever actually come up. A lifelong war resister shown signing off on a bombing run. A doctor shown walking out on a patient. Defenders of the film usually say two things: (1) it's flagged as drama, audiences know the genre, nobody's being tricked; and (2) the subject is dead, so nothing at all happens to them — no experience to have, no reputation left to enjoy or suffer.

I want an essay of about 1200 words that takes (2) seriously instead of waving at it. The question: can a fictional portrayal wrong the person it portrays, or can it only ever wrong the living — the audience, who walks away with a distorted picture, and the descendants, who get defamed by proxy? Answering that means saying something about what becomes of a person's interests when they die. If you think the dead can be harmed by things that happen after they're gone, say what makes that true, and then say whether it commits you to absurdities: does every unflattering novel with a real person in it wrong them, and does a lie nobody ever finds out about still wrong them? If you think the dead can't be harmed, say what's actually left of the objection to the filmmaker — and whether "it only misleads the living" is strong enough to carry the weight people want to put on it, or whether it quietly throws away the thing that makes the case feel bad in the first place, which is that they got *her* wrong, not just that some people now believe something false.

Please also deal with the counterfactual wrinkle, because I suspect it's where the real trouble is. There's a difference between misstating what someone did and misstating what someone would have done. The second feels worse to me in a way I can't quite justify — it's not checkable, there's no fact of the matter to appeal to, and the person never faced the choice so nothing was actually taken from them. Weigh whether it's genuinely worse or just weirder, and build whatever you conclude into the verdict.

Don't give me a survey of the options and don't coast into "it depends on the case." State your position in one sentence in the first couple of paragraphs, then defend it against the strongest thing the other side can say — including the best version of "the dead have no interests, full stop." No citations, no bibliography, no reading list wanted; I'm not checking sources, I want the argument.

When you're done, put a table at the end rating the three candidate positions. Columns:

- position — one of: (A) the portrayal can wrong the dead person herself; (B) it can only wrong living people, i.e. audiences and descendants; (C) it wrongs nobody in particular — what gets damaged is the shared record of the past, a public good with no owner, so the complaint is about collective epistemic harm rather than anyone's interest
- strongest_single_argument — the one argument that does the most work for it, in a sentence or two
- case_it_handles_worst — the scenario where the position looks most implausible
- verdict — exactly one of: correct, partly correct, wrong
- score — 1 to 10, how well it survives the counterfactual wrinkle specifically

Then add one final row labeled "mine" giving your own position, and one last line after the table naming the single distinction your whole essay turns on.
