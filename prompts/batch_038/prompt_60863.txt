I build ranking services — the sort where each candidate option gets a simulated payoff distribution and we pick whichever has the best mean. Standard Monte Carlo fare. Lately I keep hitting cases where the running average doesn't settle: I throw more and more samples at it and the estimate wanders *further* out instead of converging. Turns out that isn't a bug, it's that some of those distributions have no mean at all (Cauchy-ish tails, that kind of thing). As an engineering problem I can live with it — clip the tails, use a median, whatever.

But it sent me down a rabbit hole and I landed on this thing in decision theory that I think is called the Pasadena game. Roughly: flip a fair coin until heads comes up. Heads on the first flip pays you +$2, heads on the second costs you $2, heads on the third pays +$8/3, heads on the fourth costs you $4, and generally the n-th flip pays (−1)^(n+1) · 2ⁿ/n dollars. Weight each payoff by its probability (2⁻ⁿ) and the expectation is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which does converge — to about 0.69 — but only *conditionally*. Reorder the terms and you can make it sum to anything at all: zero, a hundred, plus or minus infinity. And there's no obviously privileged ordering of the outcomes, so the gamble looks like it has no well-defined value, even though it isn't infinite the way the old St Petersburg one is. Which is weirder, somehow.
