**Verification, Discovery, and the Cost of Knowing**

*1.* Epistemologists who model knowledge with possible worlds usually build in the assumption that an agent knows every logical consequence of what she already knows. This idealization of "logical omniscience" is standardly defended as harmless: the model is meant to capture what knowledge is, not what a limited creature manages to accomplish.

*2.* Complexity theory appears to say otherwise. In NP, checking a proposed solution is cheap even when producing one seems hopeless, and that asymmetry is precisely what makes NP-complete problems the reference point for intractability. So there are truths an agent could recognize at once if they were handed to her, and could not generate herself within any realistic bound.

*3.* My central claim is that this asymmetry cannot be idealized away, because it is not a matter of degree. Idealizations are uniform shortcuts: we ignore friction, we ignore the exact number of grains in a heap, and nothing structural changes. The gap between verifying and finding is different in kind, because it determines who must depend on whom. An agent who can check but not search cannot originate; she can only receive. That makes the gap the condition of possibility for a division of epistemic labor, and an idealization that erases the thing it is supposed to explain is not a simplification but a distortion.

*4.* A concrete case is the four color theorem. The 1976 proof by Appel and Haken required a machine to check a large set of configurations, and no person has ever surveyed the whole argument. Some philosophers concluded that a theorem established this way is known empirically rather than a priori. I think both sides are describing one fact from two directions: the proof is cheap to check and impossible to reproduce. Note what happened next. In 2005 the theorem was re-proved inside a proof assistant, where checking is mechanical and reliable. That improves verification and leaves discovery exactly as hard as before. Formalization does not close the gap; it widens it.

*5.* This is why I think the philosophical interest of P versus NP does not depend on the answer. Even setting aside the open question, we know that the standard techniques cannot reach it: there are oracles relative to which the two classes coincide and others relative to which they differ, so no argument that behaves uniformly when an oracle is attached can settle the matter; and the natural-proofs result shows that, if one-way functions exist, a broad family of combinatorial lower-bound arguments cannot establish bounds strong enough to separate the classes. Our present concept of a proof is not adequate to our question. Ignorance with a known shape is more informative than a coin flip, and that shape is the real philosophical content here.
