I got invited to contribute to an interdisciplinary volume on famine — mostly economic historians and a couple of development economists — and I have to send the editor a two-page proposal for an 8,000-word chapter by the end of next month. Here's my half-formed idea, and I need you to turn it into the proposal.

Everyone in this literature frames the question as natural disaster or political crime: Sen on Bengal 1943, the argument over whether Ireland in the 1840s was a blight the empire let run or something worse, the Great Leap Forward as the clean case of a famine made by policy. But I think the disaster/crime axis misleads, because it makes classification turn on intention and on whether there's a culprit, and in most of these cases the mechanism is procedural and distributed. My hunch is that the better axis is something like counterfactual responsiveness: whether at the time there was someone who both knew people were dying and had standing to redirect food, and whether the institutions let the knowing and the standing actually meet. That yields two distinct failure modes — information without authority, which is roughly the Bengal story (people in Calcutta and in the press could see it while the province never declared a famine, and the decisions that mattered about shipping and war priorities sat somewhere else), and authority without information, which is roughly the Great Leap story (procurement fixed on the basis of yield figures the political climate made uncorrectable). Ireland is the case that interests me most: an imperial state with abundant information and formal authority, plus an ideological constraint that made acting on the information unintelligible. Is that a crime, or is it a third thing?

Second piece. Sen's claim that famine doesn't happen in a democracy with a free press gets treated as one mechanism, but I think it's at least three — information reaching the centre, an electoral incentive to respond, and a legitimacy constraint — and they come apart in exactly the cases above. I want the chapter to argue that separating them changes how you classify Ireland, and also changes what the crime vocabulary is worth: if the failure is procedural, naming a culprit may make the event easier to condemn and harder to prevent.
