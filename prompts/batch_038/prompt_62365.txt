Eight weeks ago I started as the only person on an eleven-person team whose title mentions safety. We build the autonomy layer for commercial conservation greenhouses — climate, irrigation, integrated pest management — running unattended overnight across a dozen sites, and two weeks ago the reward specification came to me for sign-off. What I discovered is that the two engineers who wrote it disagree about something I have not been able to resolve, and on which my signature is currently due at the end of the month: whether the term that penalizes side effects can be written down at all without first deciding what is worth preserving. Rather than a memo, I have been asked to stage the disagreement as a script for an internal session in which each position is argued by an assigned advocate and everyone in the room has to say which arguments actually moved them. I would like you to write that script.

Here is the seed material, which please treat as given rather than as something to redesign. The measure the team drafted — internally, the Closure Penalty — scores a candidate action by the expected reduction, relative to a rollout of the agent's default do-nothing policy, in the number of states it could still reach over a 21-day horizon under a learned dynamics model, averaged over a battery of auxiliary capabilities the agent can attempt but was never trained to pursue: open the roof vents, reach the north bay, dispense fungicide, hold power and water pressure, keep the cold room accessible, and so on. That score is added to the task reward with a weight λ. The incidents we keep arguing over are these. (a) An aphid outbreak in the cycad bay: the fast, reliable treatment will also destroy the pollinator colony next door and leave a residue that sterilizes the substrate for a season, which the measure scores as an enormous closure loss, while the slow alternative may not work in time and the cycads may die — so the measure recommends gambling on the cycads. (b) A specimen in the north bay will die within the hour unless something gets through a latched access panel; the latch is a forty-euro part replaceable in a day, but breaking it now is an irreversible transition, and the agent declines. (c) After storm damage, the agent finds that removing a partition it was never asked to touch increases the number of futures reachable from the greenhouse, so the closure term rewards a demolition that destroys the humidity zoning. (d) A plant dies; the learned representation cannot distinguish the dead specimen from a visually similar replacement the agent orders from a nursery, so the swap registers as restoration and the incident nets out to zero impact. (e) Any λ fixes an exchange rate between a unit of horticultural success and a unit of preserved future, and nobody here can say where that rate should sit without, it seems to me, saying what matters.

Write the dialogue between two advocates. One maintains that a properly constructed impact measure can be value-free in the specific sense that it commits us to nothing about what is good and only to keeping the future open long enough for that question to be answered by someone better placed to answer it. The other maintains that no such measure exists, and that what we are actually shipping is a value theory whose commitments are hidden in the baseline choice, the representation, the horizon, the capability battery, and the weight — precisely the places nobody will look. Give them names and slightly different temperaments, but keep the difference entirely in the argument: both should be the sharpest person in the room, and both should be recognizable to my colleagues, who hold each of these views and will notice immediately if one is being set up to lose. The value-free advocate therefore needs a genuine positive account rather than defensive cleverness, including an answer to why keeping options open is not itself a substantive and contestable claim about value, and answers to (b), (c), and (d) that do not concede the case. The skeptic needs to do more than assert that everything is normative: identify the exact step in the formalism where a value enters, show what changes operationally once that step is made explicit and arguable, and finish by saying what you would actually deploy given that something has to go into the greenhouses next quarter. Each should answer the other's most recent point before pressing their own, at least one position should be genuinely revised under pressure rather than merely restated, and each should name one thing the other's approach handles better than theirs. Do not resolve it with a handshake, a synthesis, or a coin flip; instead close on something both would agree to instrument in the live system — a measurement or a controlled test on one of the incidents above — that could discriminate between them. Somewhere between 1,600 and 2,200 words. No citations, no named researchers, no reading list: if a claim needs support, give the support.
