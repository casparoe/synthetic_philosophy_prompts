So I'm auditing a Tuesday-night reading group at my local library — mostly retirees, one guy who finished a philosophy PhD forty years ago and never really left the field, and me — and we've been slogging through Politics Book I. Last week we got into a genuinely nasty argument about the natural-slavery chapters and I want to check whether my reading is defensible or whether I'm just pattern-matching modern stuff onto the text. Here's the chunk we kept circleing back to (around 1254b, in the translation I've been using):

> "it is in a living creature, as we say, that it is first possible to discern the rule both of master and of statesman the soul rules the body with the sway of a master, the intelligence rules the appetites with that of a statesman or a king and in these examples it is manifest that it is natural and expedient for the body to be governed by the soul and for the emotional part to be governed by the intellect, the part possessing reason, whereas for the two parties to be on an equal footing or in the contrary positions is harmful in all cases."

and then a bit further on:

> "For he is by nature a slave who is capable of belonging to another (and that is why he does so belong), and who participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it; for the animals other than man are subservient not to reason, by apprehending it, but to feelings. And also the usefulness of slaves diverges little from that of animals; bodily service for the necessities of life is forthcoming from both, from slaves and from domestic animals alike. The intention of nature therefore is to make the bodies also of freemen and of slaves different—the latter strong for necessary service, the former erect and unserviceable for such occupations, but serviceable for a life of citizenship (and that again divides into the employments of war and those of peace); but as a matter of fact often the very opposite comes about—some persons have the bodies of free men and others the souls; since this is certainly clear, that if persons were born as distinguished only in body as are the statues of the gods, everyone would say that those who were inferior deserved to be these men's slaves. And if this is true in the case of the body, there is far juster reason for this rule being laid down in the case of the soul; but beauty of soul is not so easy to see as beauty of body."

The bit I think everybody else skipped over is "participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it." My claim — which the ex-PhD thought was too charitable and the retirees thought was too technical — is that Aristotle isn't making a claim about raw intelligence here at all. He's making a claim about a specific kind of second-order authority over one's own ends: to *apprehend* reason is to be able to recognize a good reason when somebody else hands it to you and to be moved by it, whereas to *possess* it is to be the one who settles what counts as a good reason when two candidate reasons pull against each other, i.e. to have final say over the ranking of ends. That would make the natural slave something like an agent who is fully reason-responsive but not reason-authoring — no deficit in tracking, a deficit in arbitration. If that's right it also seems to break the animal analogy he draws one sentence earlier, since animals are said to obey "not to reason, by apprehending it, but to feelings" — they aren't reason-responsive at all, whereas the natural slave on my gloss is, just not at the top level.

Three things I'd like checked. (1) Does the apprehend/possess pair in this passage actually carry the arbitration weight I'm putting on it, or is it more plausibly just a capacity claim (can follow instructions, can't deliberate) that the rest of Book I uses in the boring way? If you happen to be sure about the Greek verbs behind "apprehend" and "possess" that'd help, but please don't guess at them — I'd rather you flag it as uncertain. (2) The "intention of nature" business: is "as a matter of fact often the very opposite comes about" plus "beauty of soul is not so easy to see as beauty of body" just a rhetorical flourish, or does it quietly wreck the practical argument — because if natural slaves are identifiable only by soul and souls are opaque, then no actual slaveholder could ever know that he holds a natural slave, so every real-world slave relation fails Aristotle's own criterion even if the theory of natural slavery is sound. Our group split on whether he noticed that, and one woman was adamant that it's a deliberate concession rather than an oversight. (3) Most important: am I smuggling in something anachronistic? I worry my arbitration gloss is really a Kantian autonomy requirement wearing a toga, and that a fourth-century BC author arguing about household management wouldn't have had the conceptual room for "authority over the ranking of one's own ends." Relatedly, I have a hunch that the *two kinds of rule* being distinguished in the first quotation — body governed "with the sway of a master" vs. appetites governed "with that of a statesman or a king" — is the real key to the whole chapter and that I've been reading straight past it, because a despotic rule and a constitutional rule over the same material ought to imply two different kinds of subject. Tell me where I'm overreading, and where the hunch has legs.
