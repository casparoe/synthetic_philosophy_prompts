I lead a small backend team, and twice a month we hold a ninety-minute "reading group" that is mostly an excuse to argue about how we design software. The next session is mine to run: 18 people, nearly all engineers, no philosophy training anywhere in the room, myself included. The space has a whiteboard, index cards, and markers. I have no budget for prizes — points or tokens are fine, and I can convert them into some small bragging artifact, but not money.

Here is why I want to run it. We are building a service that re-plans whenever new telemetry arrives mid-task, and a colleague and I keep having the same argument and never resolve it. His position is that an agent should always act on the best information it has at the moment of acting, and that anything else leaves value on the table. My position — which I have arrived at informally and cannot yet defend well — is that there is a large class of situations where the operative question is not "what should I do, now that I know X?" but "what policy would I have wanted to lock in before X was known?", typically because some counterparty has already conditioned its own behavior on which kind of agent I turn out to be. I can gesture at the familiar thought experiments — the stranded traveler who is only asked to pay after he has been safely delivered, the sealed box whose contents depend on what a predictor expected him to take — but waving at puzzles has gotten me nowhere. Half the room dismisses them as badly posed riddles, and the other half treats them as trivia.

So I want them to lose points over it. Design a game in which the structure does the arguing and I barely have to.

Requirements:

- They must run into the failure before anyone names it. No lecture up front, and nobody should hear a term like "updateless" until the debrief. Ideally every participant, at least once, makes the move that looks locally obvious and then watches it cost them.
- Several rounds, with a deliberate change between them in who knows what and when, so that the contrast is felt rather than asserted. At least one round should hand a subset of players private information that genuinely changes what looks best from inside their own branch — the pull of "but I know something now" has to be real, not a strawman.
- The prediction step must be mechanical. I am not a good judge of character and I will not run a con on my own team: whatever plays the role of "someone who already knows what you will do" should be a published rule the players can read and reason about, not my intuition or a hidden dice roll.
- Payoffs in tokens, with arithmetic simple enough to do by hand on an index card. No real money, no real stakes beyond the room.
- A minute-by-minute timeline that fits ninety minutes, including a debrief of about 25 minutes.
- A debrief that maps specific moments of play onto specific parts of the idea, phrased as questions I should ask rather than conclusions I should announce.
- A short script — a paragraph or two — that I can read or paraphrase accurately. I will be delivering it, and if the point is subtle I will get it wrong, so please write it for someone who has not studied this.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: the exploit a sharp engineer will find, the round where the energy dies, the participant who correctly says "this isn't the real problem, you've quietly changed it." Tell me how to handle each.
- Most important: an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. My suspicion is that a game built on published rules and sealed commitments teaches the weaker lesson — "commit in advance," or "sign a contract so your future self can't defect" — rather than the actual view, which is about where the choice gets evaluated from rather than about any device that binds you. Tell me whether that suspicion is correct, what exactly is lost, and what I have to supply verbally to cover the gap. If a room of engineers cannot be expected to feel that difference in ninety minutes, say so plainly instead of implying the game delivers it.

One more thing: a fallback plan. This group argues for sport. If two or three of them reverse-engineer the intended lesson during round one and start coaching everyone else, what do I switch to that preserves the point without a restart?
