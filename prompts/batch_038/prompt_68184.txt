I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political economy, and I edit the satire page of our department's graduate newsletter. This spring I want to take aim at the "Moloch" style of explanation — the claim that each actor in an industry is locked into a race to the bottom regardless of intentions — and, just as much, at its optimistic counterpart: the conviction that a single firm can defect from the race and be rewarded for it.

Please write an excerpt of a CEO's annual letter to shareholders (roughly 350 words) from a company that has unilaterally disarmed: it has abandoned a profitable practice its competitors continue, and it is now presenting the resulting margin collapse as a durable advantage. Follow it with three exchanges from the earnings-call Q&A (roughly 350 words) in which analysts raise the objections. Choose whichever industry makes the game-theoretic mechanics cleanest.

The comedy should come from the arguments being correct and the register being absurd. Let the CEO deploy the real case for unilateral restraint — reputational premia, regulatory goodwill, tail-risk reduction, being the author of the standard everyone later adopts — in unfazed investor-relations prose, and let the analysts press with the real problems: the discount rate, the length of the shadow of the future, whether rivals can credibly commit or will simply take the margin, the fact that markets punish restraint well before they reward it, and whether compliance is even monitorable. One CEO answer should quietly concede that his case holds only under assumptions he has not stated. Nobody breaks character, nobody names the trap, and the CEO does not get the last word.
