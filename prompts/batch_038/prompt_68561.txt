I've talked myself into the view that there's no causation by omission. When I don't water the plant and it dies, nothing is missing from the world doing causal work — just the drought plus a pile of positive facts about what I actually did. The clincher for me is the profligacy problem: if absences can be causes, then my failure to water it is one of an infinity of non-events (not watering it with a hose in Nebraska, not summoning rain, not being a plant-watering robot) that would have made the difference just as well, and I can't see any principled way to pick one out.

So what would count against this package? I want the arguments the friends of negative causation actually have, ranked from fatal to merely awkward: double prevention, whether difference-making accounts of causation can even be stated without absences, the fact that everyone happily distinguishes my failing to water the plant from some stranger in Nebraska failing to water it. And specifically — does the profligacy argument show there are no omissive causes, or only that there are far too many of them? Those feel like different claims and I suspect I've been sliding between them. Also: does my view risk anything at all, or has 'its a pragmatic matter which absences we single out' insulated it to the point where no case could tell against it — and if so, is that a problem for a metaphysical view of this kind, or is that just how the debate works?

Finish by saying what would change your mind: which of those defeaters you think genuinely lands, and where the reasoning above is most likely to break down.
