Our department runs a weekly "modal minute": our visiting scholar (call her V) reads out a sentence, says true or false, and gives one line of why. She's a card-carrying modal realist in Lewis's tradition (On the Plurality of Worlds) and she's very consistent. The record so far:

2. "There could be a round square." False. No world is inconsistent, and she says she wants no truck with impossible worlds or any other hyperintensional machinery.
3. "There could have been more worlds than there are." False, flat out. But she insists this doesn't dent plenitude: what could have been isn't capped by the contents of our world — there could have been more donkeys, and that's the point.
4. "Actual" works like "here" or "now." No world is ontologicaly privileged; ours is just the one we're stuck in.
5. "Humphrey might have won in '68." True. But when a student pressed on whether the winner would be Humphrey himself or merely someone very like him, she got irritated and said that's a question about how the words go, not about the world. Refused to rule.
6. Asked whether she really believes in infinitely many concrete universes or whether the worlds are "just the best analytic device," she said: I've never understood what the "just" is doing in your question. If a theory earns its keep you believe in its posits, and if you want to call that a fiction, fine — but then so is the rest of science.
7. Any two things can coexist with any other two things, up to size limits, and a world can contain as many duplicates of a thing as you like.

Next week my sentence is: "There could have been two of me." Concretely — a world where at 22 I undergo perfect fission, and two people wake up with all my memories, each continuous with me in exactly the way I'm continuous with my younger self.
