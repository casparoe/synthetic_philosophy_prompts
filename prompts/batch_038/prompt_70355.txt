I read a chapter in one of those pop-econ books that recaps Hardin, and I want a sanity check before I repeat any of it at a dinner party. Here's roughly how the passage goes:

"In 1968 the ecologist Garrett Hardin published a famous article built around a village pasture open to all the herders. Each herder asks what it costs him to add one more animal to his herd: the extra animal is his gain, the extra grazing damage is spread across everyone, so of course he adds it. Every other herder does the same arithmetic, the pasture is destroyed, and that is the tragedy. It is really just a prisoner's dilemma with a lot of players instead of two — each person's rational move is to defect, and everybody winds up worse off than if they had all cooperated. Hardin's point was that this is what happens whenever people own things together rather than separately, which is why he thought the realistic options were to parcel the resource up into private plots or hand it to the state. Nobody had a good answer for decades, until Elinor Ostrom went out and found villages that had been managing thier forests, irrigation systems and fisheries perfectly well for centuries, which basically disproved him. And the lesson generalizes: any resource that everybody shares and nobody owns gets run down, whether it's a pasture, a fishery, or the atmosphere."

Parts of this feel too tidy, and a friend who works on water policy rolled her eyes when I read it aloud, but she wouldn't say why. Can you go through it claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually works on this stuff would sign off on, what's just the usual harmless simplification, and what's genuinely wrong? Specifically I want to know whether the prisoner's dilemma analogy holds up or whether the payoff structure is doing something different, and whether "people owning things together" is an accurate description of what Hardin's pasture actually was — I have a vague feeling that word "open" in "open to all the herders" is load-bearing in a way the book doesn't notice. Also: did Ostrom disprove Hardin, or is that a category mistake where two people are talking about different arrangements? And is Hardin's own recommended fix fairly described as privatize-or-nationalize?

My own instinct, which may be wrong, is that the pasture story is right and Ostrom's cases are just small groups in lucky conditions. Tell me if that instinct survives the audit. Finally, does the last sentence — the generalization to fisheries and the atmosphere — still stand once you correct the earlier claims, or does fixing them take the generalization down with it? I'd rather hear "most of this is fine" than a pile of nitpicks, but please flag the errors that actually matter versus the ones that are just compression.
