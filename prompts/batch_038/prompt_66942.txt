ok so I'm four chapters into a dissertation on higher-order evidence and my own belief-forming processes have become the sort of thing my dissertation says are unreliable, so instead of writing I want to make a joke. help me out.

Write an internal HR memo — the deadpan corporate-compliance kind — from a forecasting firm that has just come through an audit and is instituting a mandatory Belief Integrity Policy for all staff. Numbered clauses, 550–650 words, hard stop at 700, and open with the one-line "Purpose" and effective date these things always have.

The firm's policy is total enkrasia: no employee may believe p while believing that their evidence doesn't support p, and any new higher-order evidence about your own reliability has to be reflected in your first-order beliefs within one business day. The comedy needs to come from the policy running into exactly the problems the literature says it runs into — don't reach for generic bureaucracy jokes, no forms in triplicate and no broken printer. Specifically I want it to collide with:

- the exemption clause: staff may petition to be excused from updating when they have reason to think their reasoning is compromised, but working out whether their reasoning is compromised is itself an exercise of the reasoning in question, and the memo is perfectly serene about this
- a level-splitting provision that lets an employee keep believing p while filing a report stating that their evidence doesn't support p, described by Compliance as entirely stable — plus some line about what happens when that report is itself the subject of a report
- the peer-disagreement mediation protocol, where your credence gets averaged with a colleague's and the procedure for establishing that they aren't your peer on this particular question is administered by them
- an impairment notice: someone has been informed they were dosed with something that degrades one specific kind of inference, and the memo has to tell them what to do about it without letting them reason their way to it
- one clause that quietly concedes akrasia can be rationally required in misleading-higher-order-evidence cases, with HR spelling out what that concession does to the enforceability of every other clause in the document

Keep the voice completely straight. Nobody in the memo knows anything is funny, no winking, no footnotes explaining the philosophy, and don't let any clause state the objection out loud — it should be visible in the machinery. A working epistemologist should laugh at the impairment notice and simultaneously be annoyed that it's a fair shot at both sides of the debate. You can include one short worked example as a sidebar if it fits inside the word cap.

No citations, no reading list, no name-dropping actual philosophers — I just want the piece.
