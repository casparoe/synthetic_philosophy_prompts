I arrived at this position on my own, through reading and thinking rather than through any formal training, so I will state it as carefully as I can and ask you to take it as a serious claim rather than a naive one.

What I believe is this. The brain is a computing machine, literally and not by analogy: there is some level of description at which what the brain does amounts to passing from one definite state to another according to rules that depend only on the present state and the inputs, and anything whatsoever — silicon, plumbing, a room full of clerks with paper — that carries out those same transitions would have a mind, and would have experiences, in just the way I do. I also hold two further things that I worked out myself and that I think separate my view from vaguer versions of it. First, the level of description does not matter: individual ion channels, whole neurons, or an entire head treated as a black box are equally good candidates, so long as the rules written at that level fully settle what the system does. Second, speed does not matter: a system that takes a thousand years to arrive at a thought I arrive at in a second is having that thought, with everything that belongs to it.

So that you do not mistake my claim for a truism, let me say what I am not saying. I do not mean merely that the brain processes information, in the loose way that phrase gets used about weather systems and thermostats. I mean that there is a finite stock of states and a finite table of transitions between them, and that I am one of those states together with the route by which I got there.

What I want from you is a map of everything that would count against this view.

1. Which findings about actual nervous systems would count against it, and how strong would each finding have to be before it mattered? I have in mind questions like these: whether the small irregularities in how cells fire are noise to be averaged away or whether they carry part of the work; whether the exact timing of single pulses holds content that a description in terms of average firing rates simply loses; what to make of the cells that are not neurons but outnumber them; what to make of chemical signals that drift through tissue broadly rather than passing point to point along a wire; and whether small physical differences can be amplified by the system's own dynamics to the point where no coarse-grained description stays accurate for long.

2. The objection I cannot settle for myself: if any physical system at all can be redescribed as carrying out some computation or other, then to say the brain is a computer is to say nothing. My answer is that the description has to be the one that actually governs what the system does, and has to survive small disturbances rather than collapse under them. I suspect this answer either fails outright or quietly downgrades my view into something much weaker than I meant. Tell me which, and what is left of the view in the second case.

3. Which arguments and thought experiments bite against my version rather than against weaker ones. I do not think the familiar case of a person shut in a room, following rules for a language they do not understand, reaches me, because my claim is not about the interpretation of symbols but about being a certain sort of causal system. If you think I am wrong about that, say why; if you agree, say which other arguments do land.

4. What my view commits me to, or leads me to expect, that its rivals do not. Distinguish the consequences I could accept without embarrassment from those that would do real damage, and say what the damage consists in.

5. Whether my two extra claims — that level does not matter, that speed does not matter — are load-bearing or merely decorative, and whether insisting on them has made the whole thesis immune to refutation. If it has, say so plainly, and tell me whether that is fatal for a thesis of this kind or simply the condition of any general claim about what minds are.

Please rank the defeaters: those that would oblige me to abandon the view, those that would oblige me to weaken it past recognition, and those that are only awkward. For each, say whether a serious defender of my position would concede it as genuinely counting against them or would dismiss it, and on what grounds. I am more moved by results about how nervous systems actually behave than by arguments about how we should use words, so weight things accordingly — but tell me if you think that preference is itself a way of ducking the hardest objections. I am not after a reading list or a roll call of who has said what; I want the reasoning set out so that I can check it for myself, and I would sooner be told that my view is safe and insulated than be flattered into thinking it is in danger.
