Genome annotation databases routinely assign functions to genes in organisms nobody has ever run a single experiment on — they transfer the annotation across from a characterized gene in mouse or yeast on the strength of sequence similarity alone, and publish it with a confidence code rather than flagging it as a guess. The same community meanwhile labels stretches of DNA pseudogenes, i.e. functionless, even when those stretches are demonstrably transcribed and bound by proteins; the tell is relaxed selection in the lineage, not absence of activity. And then there's ENCODE reporting that something like 80% of the human genome has a biochemical function and getting shouted at by evolutionary biologists for it.

What notion of function is each of those three practices actually running on? Sort them into whichever bucket they belong — selected-effects/etiological, causal-role/dispositional, or something that doesn't fit either — and say whether one database could coherently house both at once. Specifically: does annotating a pseudogene's transcript as functional contradict the pseudogene label, or are the two answering different questions that only look like they're answering the same one? And the homology-transfer case is the one I find weirdest, becuase the annotator has no evidence about what that molecule does in that organism, only evidence about what its ancestors' descendants were kept around for — so what has to be true about function for the transfer to be a legitimate inference rather than a convenient fiction?
