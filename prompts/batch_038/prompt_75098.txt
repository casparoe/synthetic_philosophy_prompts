I would like a case study that runs in both directions: a theory brought to bear on a real intellectual episode, and that episode then turned back on the theory to see what it survives.

The episode is the Bloomsbury Group's appropriation of G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903) — above all Chapter VI, on the ideal — together with the retrospective verdict on that appropriation delivered by John Maynard Keynes roughly thirty years later, in his memoir-essays of 1926 and 1938. The theory to be applied is Moore's own account of beauty and friendship: his claim that the greatest intrinsic goods are complex states of consciousness involving aesthetic appreciation and personal affection, that everything else, including all action and all material goods, has value only as a means to these, and that the value of such states depends on the doctrine of organic wholes, according to which a whole may be worth vastly more than the sum of the values of its parts.

For the record, here are the features of the episode I am taking as given, so that your answer need not depend on recall of details. Correct me at the outset if you think any of them is materially wrong, and then proceed on the corrected version. The Cambridge circle that absorbed Moore — Keynes, Lytton Strachey, Leonard Woolf, Clive Bell, E. M. Forster and their associates — were largely members or former members of the Apostles, and took from Chapter VI a ranking of goods rather than an argument for it. Clive Bell went on to build his 1914 theory of significant form and a self-contained aesthetic emotion on ground he treated as continuous with Moore's. Keynes later charged, in substance, that he and his friends had read Moore's conclusion about the supreme goods and discarded his casuistry and his discussion of rules and consequences; that they had thereby become people who recognized no general obligations and relied on direct intuition into particular situations and particular affections; and that the resulting temperament was immature, incapable of taking either rules or outcomes seriously, and badly unequipped for the political world of the 1930s. Forster's essay of 1939 on belief and loyalty, in which personal loyalty to a friend is ranked above loyalty to a country, is the clearest surviving statement of the disposition Keynes describes.

With that in place, please work through the following, in order.

1. Reconstruct Moore's position with as much precision as you can manage. What exactly does he say the valuable whole consists of in the case of beauty — what role does cognition of the object play, and why does he think a merely emotional response, however intense, is worth comparatively little? What does he say in the case of personal affection, and how does the analysis of friendship differ from the analysis of aesthetic appreciation? Where does the doctrine of organic wholes do the real work, and what follows from it about the value of the components taken separately?

2. Map the appropriation against that reconstruction. Which elements did the Bloomsbury reading retain, which did it drop, and was each dropping licensed by Moore's argument or a distortion of it? Treat two questions specifically. First: if the value lies in the whole rather than in its affective component, is the slogan of personal relations as the supreme good something Moore could endorse, or does it name a lesser whole he would have scored low? Second: does Moore's cognitive condition commit him against Bell's self-contained aesthetic emotion, and if so, how much of Bell's project survives?

3. Adjudicate between two diagnoses of what went wrong, and feel free to advance a third. Diagnosis A: the failure was in transmission — readers absorbed the ranking without the structure that generates it, and on Moore's own criteria the states they actually prized were worth less than the states he described, so the theory is vindicated by the very episode usually cited against it. Diagnosis B: the failure was in abridgment — the damage came from discarding Moore's account of right action, rules and consequences, so the episode is really evidence about the relation between a theory of value and a theory of obligation, and tells us nothing against Chapter VI. Say which diagnosis better fits the record as I have described it, and what would count as evidence for the other.

4. Reverse the direction. Keynes's complaint is, among other things, that a morality of states of mind and personal affections left its adherents unable to cope with a world of institutions, violence and large-scale consequences. Does that complaint land on Moore's theory of beauty and friendship, on his theory of right, or on neither? Does Moore have the resources to diagnose his own misreading from inside the theory? And identify at least three places where the theory is simply silent rather than strained — for instance, whether it can generate agent-relative duties to particular friends as opposed to general reasons to promote valuable states, what it says about the political and material preconditions of aesthetic life, and what happens when the object of affection is itself an unreliable reporter on the qualities that make affection valuable. Do not force Moore to speak where he does not.

5. Conclude with the one thing this episode most strongly settles about Moore's view of beauty and friendship, and the one thing it leaves genuinely open.

Two constraints on the answer. Do not supply invented quotations from Moore, Keynes, Bell or Forster, and do not pad the response with bibliographic apparatus; I want the argument, not the apparatus. Where your reconstruction depends on a recollection of Moore's text that you are not confident of, mark it as such and show what turns on it.
