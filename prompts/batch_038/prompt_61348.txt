I am in my third year of a PhD in formal epistemology, and this term my supervisor has been running a seminar on logical omniscience in which she rules, case by case, on whether an agent knows a proposition that follows from what the agent already believes. Her verdicts so far, in the order she gave them:

1. A woman who believes that Clark Kent is in the office, and who would flatly deny that Clark Kent is Superman. Does she know that Superman is in the office? **No.**
2. A student working an exercise who believes both premises of a two-step *modus ponens*, believes the conditionals linking them, has the conclusion printed on the page in front of her, and denies it because she is rushing. Does she know the conclusion? **Yes.**
3. A research mathematician who proved one lemma in a first subfield in the 1990s and another in an unrelated subfield in the 2010s; the two together yield a three-line corollary, but she has never set them side by side and has never framed the corollary at all. Does she know it? **No.**
4. A clinician practising before anyone had the theoretical vocabulary for it, who believes every clinical fact about her patient that, on the correct account of the disease, entails that the cause is an infectious agent containing no nucleic acid. Does she know that? **No.**
5. An auditor with the complete transaction file open in front of her, whose assignment is precisely to look for this, who believes each individual entry that together displays a systematic pattern, and for whom seeing the pattern is an afternoon's spreadsheet work. Does she know there is a pattern? **Yes.**
6. A resident at the end of a long shift who believes the lab values, believes the clinical rule connecting them to a diagnosis, possesses the concepts, and is asked outright by her attending whether these values add up to that diagnosis; she says they do not, though one step of inference settles it. Does she know that they do? **Yes.**

Once, when pressed for a general formulation, she said only that her standard is what the agent could have been expected to see, given where she was standing. She has not said more than that, and I do not think the remark is neutral between the readings available to it.

Next week the case is mine, and I want to know what she will say before she says it. It concerns a structural engineer who has argued for years, in her research capacity, that a certain class of weld detail is susceptible to fatigue cracking under one specific cyclic loading regime. Last spring she made a routine site inspection of a bridge and recorded in her field notebook that the bridge uses that weld detail; the notebook went into a drawer. Today she signs a certificate extending the bridge's service life, working from a standard checklist that asks about the loading regime — which she notes matches the regime she has written about — but never asks about weld details by class. During the inspection she explicitly asked herself whether this bridge had any fatigue-critical detail, and answered that it did not. Both beliefs are hers, both are readily retrievable, and the inference from them is one line; but the research and the fieldwork have never been brought into a single stretch of reasoning, and she is not thinking about her research now. Does she know that this bridge has a fatigue-critical detail?

Please give me the following, as analysis rather than as references — I am not after a bibliography, and I would rather you reasoned from the record than cited to it:

- A prediction of her verdict on my case, with the reasoning that gets you there and some indication of how confident you are.
- The rule you take her to be running, stated precisely enough that it could be wrong. Then the rival rules that fit all six prior verdicts just as well as yours does, and for each of them, what it predicts about my case. I suspect the record does not single out one, and I want the candidates made explicit rather than collapsed into a general appeal to limited cognitive resources.
- Which of the six earlier cases is the closest analog to mine, and exactly where the analogy gives out.
- Whether her one general remark tips the balance between the surviving rules, or whether each of them can claim it.
- A further case — one case if that suffices, otherwise the smallest set — that she could be given next which would force the surviving rules apart, together with a statement of what each rule says about it.

I am less interested in whether the engineer is blameworthy, which seems to me a separate question she has deliberately kept out of the seminar, than in which closure principle her sequence of verdicts commits her to.
