And here's the student's answer, unedited apart from formatting:

> The argument is a case distinction on whether √2^√2 is rational, and the whole thing rests on assuming that one of those two options must obtain. That's the law of excluded middle being used on a statement nobody can decide. The constructivist objects that at the end of the day you don't know which pair of numbers the theorem is about. You've established that the disjunction "a = b = √2 works, or a = √2^√2 and b = √2 works" is provable, but you can't extract either disjunct, and on the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov reading a proof of a disjunction just *is* a proof of one of the disjuncts together with the information of which one it is. Same for the existential: a proof of ∃x A(x) is supposed to hand you a witness and a proof that it satisfies A. This proof hands you neither, so on that account it isn't a proof of the existential claim at all. Worth noting the classical argument is also unnecessary, since Gelfond–Schneider settles that √2^√2 is transcendental and hence irrational, so the first case is the one that actually holds.
>
> Technically what's dropped is double-negation elimination. Intuitionistically you can always go from A to ¬¬A, but not back. What people get wrong is thinking the intuitionist bans proof by contradiction outright. She doesn't. Deriving ¬A by assuming A and reaching absurdity is fine, since that's just introducing a negation; what's illegitimate is concluding A from ¬¬A. So a lot survives. Glivenko's theorem is the clean way to see this: classical propositional logic embeds into intuitionistic logic under a double-negation translation, so nothing is really thrown away at the propositional level, you just have to read the translated statements as weaker claims.
>
> On the second question, though, I think the answer is that the intuitionist does hold excluded middle to be false. She doesn't just decline to assert it; for a genuinely undecided statement she asserts its negation. Take Goldbach's conjecture G. The intuitionist says neither G nor ¬G is warranted, and since "warranted assertibility" is what truth amounts to for her, there's no fact of the matter, and the correct verdict is ¬(G ∨ ¬G). That's also why intuitionistic mathematics is strictly smaller: statements like the intermediate value theorem and Bolzano–Weierstrass come out false rather than merely unproven, because their classical proofs go through case distinctions on undecidable conditions.
>
> Finally, the three traditions are really one program with different budgets. Brower, Markov, and Bishop all identify mathematical construction with computation, which is why they all accept Markov's principle and Church's thesis — Brower's choice sequences are his way of describing objects generated step by step by an algorithm, and Markov and Bishop just formalize the same idea differently. They disagree only about how much of classical analysis can be salvaged, not about what a proof is.
