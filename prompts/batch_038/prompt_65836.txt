Ok, fun one. Draft the joint statement that would come out of an adversarial collaboration over van Fraassen's Reflection principle. I'll state Reflection in my own words so we're not quibbling over canonical phrasing: for any proposition P and any later time t, your present credence in P conditional on "my credence in P at t will be r" should equal r.

The two camps, as I want them characterized:

Camp A — the deferentialists. Reflection states a real, general requirement of diachronic rationality. Your future self is an expert on P relative to everything you now expect her to know, so refusing to defer to her anticipated credence is a species of epistemic self-contempt, and it's exactly what licenses the diachronic Dutch book.

Camp B — the source-sensitivists. Reflection is a sound default that breaks precisely when you know your future credence will be produced by something other than evidence: drugs, hypnosis, head injury, engineered amnesia, foreseeable misleading evidence, self-locating shifts. What earns deference is the *source* of the anticipated belief, not the bare fact that you can predict it.

Both camps have been handed the following cases and both accept the numbers, which is the whole point of the exercise.

Case 1 (Drug). Mira is a palaeographer. Her present credence that a disputed charter is genuine (call that proposition G) is 0.05, based on forensic work she's confident in. In six weeks she joins a trial of a compound that, she is 0.99 confident, will (i) wipe her memory of the forensic work and (ii) install a vivid conviction of G via a felt familiarity with the script. She has read the assay: the compound carries no information whatsoever about the charter. So she expects her post-drug credence in G to be 0.95. Now, a bookie offers her a bet costing $90 that pays $100 if G is false; later, the same bookie offers post-drug Mira a bet costing $90 that pays $100 if G is true. Each self, at its own lights, strictly prefers its bet. Combined: $180 out, $100 back, sure loss of $80.

Case 2 (Archive). Same starting credence, but instead of the drug Mira will spend six weeks in another archive, and she expects to conditionalize on whatever provenance record she turns up (or fails to turn up). No belief-fixing mechanism, no memory loss, just evidence she knows is coming but doesn't yet have.

Case 3 (Amnesia). Like Case 1, except the compound also erases her sense of how much time has passed, so post-drug Mira is unsure whether she is the historian who took the drug last week or one who took it years ago and has since recived independent evidence about the charter.

Here's the document I want you to produce:

1. Shared ground (numbered, 6-9 claims, ~350 words). Things both camps can sign without wincing. I'd expect the $80 arithmetic to be in there, plus agreement that the compound carries no information about G, plus agreement that Reflection comes out satisfied in Case 2, plus something about both camps holding that credences are governed by diachronic norms rather than anything going. Don't pad this with vacuous motherhood — if a claim costs either side nothing, it doesn't belong here.

2. Disagreements (a table, 4-6 rows, ~600 words). Columns: question at issue / the deferentialist's wording / the source-sensitivist's wording / genuine or verbal. The wording of each side's column has to be something that side would actually sign, not a strawman or a grudging paraphrase.

3. Cruxes and pre-registered predictions (2-4 cruxes, ~600 words). For each: the sub-question whose settlement would move at least one camp, the case or argument both camps accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. I strongly suspect one crux is whether Mira could rationally bind her future self (a Ulysses contract, refusing the second bet in advance), and whether such a binding smuggles Reflection back in as a norm on *action* rather than on credence. Another I suspect is whether Case 1's apparent failure is really Case 3 in disguise, i.e. a centering problem rather than a source problem.

4. What's merely verbal (~200 words). Say which parts of the A/B dispute turn on the words "rational", "deference", or "the same agent over time", and why — and be specific about which of your table rows gets downgraded as a result.

5. Where I think this draft is most likely to be wrong (~250 words, and this one is yours, not the parties'). Which shared-ground claim did you have to weaken to make it signable, and how much was lost? Which crux do you suspect is bogus? For each crux, which way would you personally lean and how hard, and what single consideration would flip that lean? Close with where your reasoning is most likely to fail outright — for instance if I'm wrong that both camps can agree on the arithmetic of Case 1, or if the deferentialist has a restriction on Reflection that isn't ad hoc and I've underplayed it.

Total something like 2000 words. Keep the tone of the document itself formal enough that two people who dislike each other could both put their names to it.
