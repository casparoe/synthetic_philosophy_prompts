Eight months ago I gave a kidney to a stranger. It did not go to a single recipient: it entered a paired-exchange chain, which the transplant coordinator told me would probably enable two transplants, possibly three. My younger brother has been on the deceased-donor list for two years, and before I signed anything he asked me to give mine to him instead. I did not. Since then I have been asked about that decision far more often than I expected — by family, by the center's outreach staff, and now by a hospital ethics committee that has invited me to speak at one of its public evenings in the autumn. I am not a philosopher, though I have been reading about supererogation and the demandingness debate with some care, and before I stand up and describe my own position in public I want to know whether it survives being written down in one place. I would be grateful for a consistency check on the following set.

(1) Some acts are supererogatory: morally good, not required, and such that leaving them undone is permissible and no ground for blame. Donating a kidney to a stranger is my clearest example of the category.

(2) The line between the required and the supererogatory is drawn by cost to the agent, not by any change in the reason. If the same act would have cost me an afternoon of inconvenience, it would have been required; because it costs me an organ and several weeks of recovery, it is not. The reason is one and the same on both sides of the line.

(3) The reasons bearing on my donation were agent-neutral in strength. Anyone with a compatible kidney, in circumstances like mine, had exactly as much reason to donate as I did; nothing about its being my kidney made the reason peculiarly mine.

(4) Close relationships generate genuine moral reasons of their own, not merely permissions to look after one's own. What I owe my brother is not a derivative or second-rate kind of claim.

(5) Where an intuition about a concrete case and a general principle of mine conflict, I revise the principle. I take the case to be the better evidence.

(6) On my own case: giving into the chain and giving to my brother were both permissible. I do not blame myself for the choice I made, and I would not blame someone who gave to their brother. I told him he was owed an explanation but not an apology, and I still think that was right.

(7) I do not believe in moral credit. Praise and blame are calibrated by the same thing, namely the balance of reasons: if an omission is blameless because the balance did not require the act, then performing the act cannot put anything to the agent's credit. Talk of going beyond the call of duty has always struck me as a device for making the comfortable feel at ease.

(8) I have said repeatedly, including to my brother, that I had no choice: once I understood what was on offer I could not have done otherwise. I also insist the donation was free, and I resent being described as a person who merely happened to be moved by stronger feelings than other people.

(9) I think the recipients owe me gratitude. I also think that if they thanked me face to face I would find it embarrassing and faintly degrading, and that a gift given in the hope of gratitude is not really a gift — which is how I would want to say mine was given.

Please work through these and tell me which pairs or clusters are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, say how bad it is — whether the two commitments cannot both be held at all, or can be held only by weakening one of them — and then say what the cheapest revision would be and what I would lose by making it. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a list of equally serious problems, and finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. I have deliberately not said which of these I am most attached to, and I would prefer you not infer it from the order in which I have listed them. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for attributions to particular philosophers; what I want is the logic of my own set, and you should feel free to say that one of my items is doing covert work for another. As to form: I would like the reply in continuous prose, as one would write a letter, with no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. Begin with two or three sentences giving your overall verdict on whether the set hangs together and what most needs to go, and only then work through the items in whatever order makes the argument clearest.
