The Nobel Prize in Physics has settled into a fairly stable pattern with respect to theoretical work. The prize is withheld until an experiment has come in, and when it is finally awarded the citation tends to credit the prediction rather than any other virtue of the theory. Englert and Higgs were recognised in 2013, a year after the ATLAS and CMS collaborations at CERN announced the new boson; Brout had died in 2011 and was therefore ineligible. Penrose was recognised in 2020 for the result that black-hole formation is a robust prediction of general relativity, some fifty-five years after he published it. Einstein's prize for 1921 was awarded for the photoelectric law, with relativity left pointedly aside, the Academy treating it as still contested and still under examination. Meanwhile the prize goes to at most three named individuals, never to a collaboration.

I would like a reconstruction of the epistemology this practice presupposes. What view of the relationship between predictive success and truth does it commit the Academy to? Does the timing rule assume that a confirmed novel prediction is evidence that the theory behind it is at least approximately true, or something weaker — that it certifies reliability, or durability, or merely that the theorist was clever and right? And does the practice in fact privilege prediction over accommodation, or is that a tidy reading I have imposed on it? Because the history does not obviously bear the reading out: the 1972 prize went to Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer for a theory of superconductivity, a phenomenon that had been known since 1911 and that they explained rather than forecast; and the 1978 prize went to Penzias and Wilson for detecting the cosmic microwave background, a detection they made without knowing that others had predicted it, while the physicists who did predict it in the 1940s were never awarded anything. So the institution that supposedly trades in confirmed predictions gave the prize to the explainers and to the accidental finders, and skipped the predictors. What theory of evidence, and of where epistemic credit sits, makes sense of that distribution rather than of the neat version?

Then take the pessimistic meta-induction seriously against the practice. The history of physics contains theories that made novel predictions which were confirmed and were nonetheless abandoned — Fresnel's wave theory of light, which entailed the bright spot at the centre of a circular shadow, resting on an ether nobody now believes in; caloric; phlogiston. If the Academy accepted that argument, could it go on crediting predictions in the way it does? Would it have to shift the citation from the theory as a whole to whichever part of it survives replacement, in the manner the structural realists recommend? And what would the prize look like under an unapologetically instrumentalist reading, where a theory's virtue is nothing but its capacity to organise and forecast data?

Please distinguish, throughout, what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it comfortably. The delay until confirmation might be a substantive claim about evidence, or it might be administrative caution about embarrassment, or a consequence of the statutes' emphasis on benefit to humankind; say which readings the practice actually requires and which it only tolerates. Flag every place where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several rival accounts of theory-choice would license the same rules, same timing and same three-name limit — and do not resolve those ambiguities in favour of your preferred account. Finally, tell me what would have to change, concretely, if the Academy were persuaded by the pessimistic meta-induction: the wording of citations, the timing rule, the choice between predictor and detector, the three-laureate cap. If nothing would have to change, explain why the practice is neutral in a way its public rhetoric is not.
