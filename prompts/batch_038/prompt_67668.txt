I run a poetry group at my local library — none of us studied literature, we just read things aloud and argue. Last week's argument got properly heated, so afterwards I tried to write down what I actually believe about what a poem means, and it came out messier then I expected:

1. A poem means what the person who wrote it meant. Not what it reminds me of, not the mood it puts me in.
2. If they're alive and they tell you what they meant, they get the last word and you should back off.
3. But the poem has to carry all of that in the words themselves. Nothing outside the words belongs to the poem — if it only lands once you've read their letters or know about their childhood, that's the poem failing, not the reader being thick.
4. I do think readers sometimes turn up something in a poem the poet never thought of, and that that's a discovery and not a misreading. Plenty of poems are better than their author knew.
5. A really good fake — word-for-word indistinguishable, sold as a lost piece by somebody famous — is worth less than the real one, and I cant point at anything in the words that makes it so. I'm sure of it anyway.
6. A friend of mine said her poem wasn't about her dad and I told her it plainly was. I still don't think I was talking over her about her own poem.

So which of these are actually fighting each other? Worst first, and for each one say what I'd have to give up to stop the fight and what giving it up costs me. Don't assume I'm attached to any of them, I honestly don't know which I'd ditch. And if a pair only looks like a clash but isn't, tell me which and why, because I'd rather not tear apart something that was fine all along.
