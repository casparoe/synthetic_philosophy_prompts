I have been reading the accuracy-first program in formal epistemology on and off for the better part of a decade, coming at it from the forecasting side — Brier scores, calibration, proper scoring rules treated as an operational matter — and I have become convinced that the central argument over it has stopped moving for reasons I cannot pin down. I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a survey of the positions.

Here is the shape of it as I see it. The accuracy-first camp holds that accuracy is the one fundamental epistemic good, constrains admissible measures of inaccuracy by conditions such as additivity across propositions, continuity, and strict propriety, and then derives substantive norms: non-probabilistic credence functions are accuracy-dominated by probabilistic ones, and conditionalizing, or planning to conditionalize, minimizes expected inaccuracy. The critics press at least three distinguishable lines. First, that the constraints on the measures already encode the conclusions, so the derivations are representation theorems with a normative wrapper rather than vindications. Second, that which norms come out depends on which measure one picks, and in particular that imprecise credences are not so easily excluded once one notices there is no obviously strictly proper way of scoring a set of probability functions. Third, and most seriously to my mind, Berker's objection in his 2013 Philosophical Review paper on epistemic teleology and the separateness of propositions: additivity licenses trading accuracy in one proposition off against accuracy in another, which he takes to be a reductio of epistemic teleology by analogy with the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism. Defenders reply that additivity is constitutive of what accuracy is, that the analogy misfires because epistemic states are not the sort of thing that bears the relevant separateness, and that the same question-begging charge would sink the Dutch book arguments, which many of the critics accept.

Nobody concedes. The technical literature advances; the dialectic does not.

So what kind of disagreement is this? Is it one dispute, or several welded together under a single name — in particular, is a formal project (what follows from a given scoring-rule framework) being talked past by a normative project (whether epistemic value is teleological at all, and whether any premise can be neutral enough to underwrite a vindication)? Is the additivity dispute substantive and arguable, or a collision between intuitions imported from two different fields? Is the question-begging charge largely verbal, resting on an unstated criterion of dialectical neutrality that the two sides do not share? Give me your diagnosis with reasons drawn from what each side actually argues, and then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a characterization result showing which conditions are forced by any plausible notion of accuracy, a reformulation of the target norm, a change of subject, or nothing. If your view is that the stalemate indicates the underlying question is ill-posed rather than merely hard, say so and argue for it.

One request about epistemic hygiene. Please separate carefully what is mathematically or otherwise settled here from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on the problem, and do not present any contested view, on either side, as though it were the consensus. Where you think my description of one side or the other is unfair or simply wrong, say so instead of arguing from my framing.
