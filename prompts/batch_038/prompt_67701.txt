Last month I became the patient-safety lead at a mid-sized hospital, and a committee I chair will shortly vote on whether to retire one of our nursing rituals: before any high-alert medication is administered, two nurses must independently read the dose calculation aloud from a paper worksheet. The barcode scanner and the smart infusion pump already verify the same calculation electronically, and the practice costs roughly forty minutes of nursing time per shift. Two committee members argued about it at our preparatory meeting, and I would like the exchange adjudicated before I write my recommendation.

Marta, the veteran charge nurse, invoked Chesterton's fence: you may not take down a fence until you understand why it was put up, and since no one on the committee can say why this one was, that ignorance is precisely the reason to leave it standing.

Dev, the quality analyst, said she had the principle backwards — the fence's origin is documented and uninteresting; what matters is whether it still functions. He then introduced a distinction of his own. Some institutional practices are load-bearing and others are spares: backups whose entire purpose is to catch failures of the primary system, and which by design do nothing observable when that system works. On his account a spare can never be inspected for a function, so Marta's demand for an account of what the practice does is a demand no spare could ever satisfy.

Marta replied that this converts her caution into an unfalsifiable reflex: a practice whose workings are in principle unobservable cannot be distinguished from a practice that does nothing at all, so Dev's distinction would protect every ritual in the building.

Dev said that was exactly his point, and that the issue is practical rather than epistemic, because the two errors are asymmetric. Removing a load-bearing fence produces a catastrophe with a date on it; keeping a useless one produces a diffuse cost that nobody ever traces back to the fence. Where errors are both asymmetric and undetectable, he argued, the default must be to leave things standing — and that, not the reformer's ignorance, is Burke's real insight about inherited practice.

Marta's last move was that the asymmetry compounds against him, because useless fences are never removed singly. They accrete, and each one narrows what the next reform can accomplish, so the slow cost he dismisses eventually purchases the hospital's inability to change anything at all. Dev answered that overlapping redundancies are what resilience looks like from inside a system, and that she had described health as though it were decay.

Please work through this move by move and say who is right at each step — in particular whether Dev's load-bearing/spare distinction does the work he assigns to it, and whether Marta's accretion objection answers that distinction or merely changes the subject. Then tell me what neither of them raised that a committee in my position ought to weigh before voting.
