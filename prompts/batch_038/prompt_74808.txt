I spent 34 years as a soil scientist at a state experiment station — drought work, mostly corn, mostly arguments about infiltration rates — and since I retired I've been working through philosophy books slowly and badly. Last term I joined a Tuesday evening reading group at the public library, mostly retirees, and we did a stretch on famines: disaster, or crime? Two of the regulars, Dale and Nadia, went at it for three weeks and the rest of us watched. I never took a side, mainly because I couldn't work out which side I was on, so I've written the whole thing down and I'd like you to referee it.

Dale's a retired claims adjuster and he argued it like a liability file. [1] Opening: every famine has a physical trigger — a failed monsoon, a cyclone, a potato fungus — and take the trigger away and nobody starves, so the trigger is the cause and the politics is only the multiplier. [2] On Bengal in 1943 he said the province ate a cyclone and tidal waves and a rice crop disease on top of losing its Burmese imports, and that a wartime administration with no shipping to spare isn't obviously a murderer. [3] On China he said the Great Leap deaths came heavily from bad agronomy — deep plowing, absurdly close planting, the sparrow campaign — and that incompetence isn't the same thing as a crime.

Nadia taught high school social studies for thirty years. [4] She said Dale's model only works if the food isn't there, and in Bengal it largely was; what collapsed was people's ability to pay for it, because war spending and the preferential rations handed to priority war workers bid rice prices up while rural wages and the cash earned by fishermen and boatmen didn't follow. She added the denial measures in coastal east Bengal, where boats and rice stocks were pulled out of circulation against a feared Japanese landing, which stranded precisely the people who caught and moved food. [5] Her criterion, which she called the desk test: if the paper on the decision-maker's desk showed enough food within reach and they signed off anyway, it's a crime; if the desk showed genuinely empty, its a tragedy. She said China fails that test badly — provinces reported record harvests, the center raised procurement on the strength of those reports, grain left the country, and people starved in sight of locked granaries. She also said the Chinese government originally called those years the three years of natural disasters and only later renamed them, and that a senior leader in 1962 put the balance at roughly 30 percent weather and 70 percent human error.

[6] Dale's reply was the sharpest thing said all month. He said the desk test only works where there's a desk with paper on it: we have minutes and grain returns for 1943 Britain and 1959 China, and for something like the great Bengal famine of the 1770s we have next to nothing you could lay in front of a desk, so her test convicts the well-documented states and quietly acquits everyone else. It measures record-keeping, he said, not culpability. He also said she'd treated a contested number as settled — plenty of economic historians think there was a real shortfall in Bengal in '43, they just don't think it was big enough to explain the death toll, and Sen himself never said the weather was irrelevant. In the third week he added that if every famine counts as a distribution failure, then every government in human history is guilty and the word stops doing any work.

[7] Nadia said the record-keeping objection runs backwards: a bureaucracy that generates its own inflated yields and then believes them is an agent, and insisting on finding one guilty mind inside it is smuggling criminal law into systems that don't have minds. We don't need a single intentional person, she said, before we say a corporation did the harm.

For what its worth, in my old field we chopped things up finer than either of them did. We'd separate the rain event from the levee from the fact that somebody zoned houses onto the floodplain, and I've since learned there's a name for some of this — Mackie's INUS condition, an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient set — plus something by Hart and Honoré about how you pick out "the" cause from all the conditions depending on what you already expected to be there. I couldnt tell whether Dale and Nadia were disagreeing about the world or about where to put a word.

So, turn by turn, [1] through [7] in the order I've given: who's right at each one, and how clearly — I want the scorecard per turn, not just a winner on the night. Then: does Dale's record-keeping objection sink the desk test, or only force it to be restated, and if restated, how? Is his "then every government in history is guilty" line a real problem for Nadia or a demand for a standard she was never offering? Has Nadia overclaimed the Bengal case by treating a live empirical dispute about the shortfall as closed? And does Ireland settle anything between them — my recollection is that food kept leaving through the worst years, though I've also read the quantities are argued about and that the picture shifted as the years went on — or is it a third kind of case that neither test handles? Finally, tell me what both of them missed that you'd have wanted somebody in that room to say.
