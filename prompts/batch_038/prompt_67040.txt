**2. The spectrum argument is a sorites, which is not a compliment**

The case I'll use is my own, just so the numbers stay clean, but it has the same shape as the hangnail-and-torture case in Temkin and the intensity/duration spectra in Parfit. Take a series of outcomes O1 … On. O1 is two days of the worst agony a person can undergo. On is seventy years of a barely-noticeable itch at the base of the thumb — present the whole time, never intrusive. Each intermediate outcome differs from its predecessor in exactly two ways: it is very slightly less intense, and it lasts somewhat longer. The steps are calibrated so that, asked about each adjacent pair directly, we rank them the same way every time: the longer, very slightly milder outcome is the worse one. A little relief doesn't pay for the extra time. So O1 is worse than O2, O2 is worse than O3, and so on down the series.

If worse-than is transitive, then On is worse than O1: seventy years of that itch is worse than two days of agony. But most of us, asked about the endpoints directly, say the opposite — two days of agony is worse, and no amount of the itch, however long, ever adds up to it. The two intuitions cannot both survive. That's the spectrum argument, and the anti-transitivist keeps the endpoints and drops transitivity.

Now ask what is actually doing the work in the first premise. It isn't the durations or the intensities themselves, because I've stipulated those exactly: every Oi lasts a determinate number of milliseconds at a determinate phenomenal intensity, so there is no vagueness in the description of the outcomes. What's doing the work is the claim that no single step along the series can flip the ranking. Call it the tolerance premise for worse-than: if x is worse than y, then x is worse than y*, where y* differs from y only in ways too small to make a difference to the ranking. That is exactly the shape of the sorites premise — if n grains don't make a heap, n+1 grains don't either — and I think the spectrum argument inherits its problems.

For one thing, tolerance can't hold all the way down. If it did, and if worse-than were transitive, the endpoints would come out the way the chain says they come out; the entire interest of the case is that the endpoints are supposed to come out the other way. So the anti-transitivist hasn't produced a counterexample to transitivity out of thin air. They've produced a reductio of tolerance-plus-transitivity and picked a victim. That's a legitimate move, but it means the argument's real premise is tolerance, and the support typically offered for it is something like: I've walked the series and I can't find the step where the ranking flips. Notice what kind of claim that is. It's a report about a search, not a finding about the series.

**3. What I want to say instead: the cutoff is there, we just can't see it**

(a) Supervenience plus a precise base. The evaluative facts about these outcomes supervene on non-evaluative facts — intensity, duration, what it's like to undergo them — and in the case as stipulated those base facts are perfectly precise. There is no borderline about how long On lasts. If the base is sharp and the evaluative depends on nothing but the base, then there is a sharp fact about which side of the line any given pair falls on. Our inability to locate that line is an epistemic failure, not a metaphysical one. So there is some i such that Oi is worse than Oi+1 while Oi+1 is not worse than Oi+2, and we will never know which i it is.

(b) The practical payoff. These rankings are supposed to guide choices, and transitivity is doing work there. If worse-than is intransitive across such a series, an agent can be pumped: starting at On, they pay a little to move to O(n-1), which their own step-wise judgment says is an improvement, and a little more to move to O(n-2), and so on, until they reach O1 — and then, at the top, they pay again to make the endpoint trade back to On, which their endpoint judgment says is an improvement too. They are back where they started, minus the payments, and they endorsed every single trade. The intransitivist owes us either the claim that the agent errs at no individual step and so is rationally self-defeating overall, or the claim that the step-wise judgments aren't reliable — and the second is very close to giving up the tolerance premise. Epistemicism pays a weirdness cost, but it keeps the whole machinery of comparison intact.

I know what that cost is. A cutoff nobody could ever locate looks like it isn't doing any work at all, and the line between "we can't find it" and "it isn't there" is exactly what the argument was supposed to turn on. Here's the wrinkle I genuinely don't know what to do with. Which pair straddles the flip seems to depend on the rest of the series. I get different verdicts about the same two outcomes depending on whether I've been walked up from the agony end or down from the itch end, and depending on whether they're presented inside a three-item series or a hundred-item one. If the cutoff is a fact about the pair alone, none of that should happen. A contextualist about worse-than has an easy explanation for it; my view needs a story about how the order of presentation changes our access to a fixed fact without changing the fact, and I don't have one.
