I am revising a draft essay — intended eventually for a postgraduate seminar paper, and perhaps for a student journal afterwards — on Williams's objection to impartialist moral theories, and I should be grateful for critical comments rather than encouragement. The finished piece will run to about three thousand words; what follows is the argumentative core, roughly a thousand words, with the paragraphs numbered for ease of reference. You will notice that the draft paraphrases Williams throughout instead of quoting him. That is an artefact of circumstance rather than a method: I have the collected papers on loan only until Thursday, and I intend to insert the quotations and pagination then. Please comment on the substance and not on the absence of citation, and please do not suggest further secondary literature — I have the primary texts, and I am not looking for a bibliography.

Draft title: *The Direction of Justification: Williams on the Drowning Wife*

§1. Williams's anti-utilitarianism is customarily presented as a single complaint — the integrity objection — illustrated first by the public cases in the critique he wrote with Smart (Jim at the wall, and the young chemist George) and then, in a domestic key, by the man who swims out to save his drowning wife. On that presentation the domestic case adds nothing to the argument: it is the same complaint about alienation from one's ground projects, shrunk to the scale of a marriage. I want to argue that the two are not instances of one objection, and that the domestic case is the more radical of the pair; but I also want to argue that its radicalism is systematically flattened by the way in which the case is usually described.

§2. The case is standardly described as a rescue dilemma: the man's wife and a stranger are both in the water, he can reach only one of them, and the impartial calculus ranks the two lives equally, so that his saving the wife stands in need of a special permission. I shall not quarrel with that description. What I want to reject is the inference commentators draw from it. Because the case is distributive in shape, the remark that the agent would have one thought too many is read as a claim about the psychology of rescue — that a husband plunging into the water ought not to be rehearsing general permissions, and that a theory which requires him to do so asks for an impossible and undesirable state of mind. On this reading Williams's objection is a piece of moral phenomenology, and it can be met by any theory that leaves the husband's mind alone.

§3. That reading mistakes the target. The objection is not about the occurrence of a thought but about the shape of a justification. What the impartialist demands is that the particular consideration — that this woman is his wife — should derive its normative standing from a general permission, so that the permission does the justificatory work and the particular consideration is admitted only as the motive that happens to accompany it. Williams's point, as I read him, is that this reverses the direction of explanation: the general permission is unintelligible except as a codification of what particular commitments already give the agent to do. The extra thought is not a thought about the wife; it is the thought of a permission that has to be there before the wife can matter.

§4. This suggests a distinction that I think does real work, and that the secondary literature tends to run together. Integrity, on Williams's account, is threatened by what I shall call the requirement of *ex ante vindication*: the demand that a commitment be held hostage to standing impartial assessment, revisable at any moment in the light of the general good. It is not threatened by what I shall call *ex post reconciliation*: the mere fact that, looking back, the agent can see that her commitments were permitted, or even promoted, by the impartial standpoint. Ground projects do not have to be ignorant of the impartial standpoint in order to be ground projects. They have to be independent of it. A life in which love survives the discovery that love was all along a good idea is not thereby an alienated life; a life in which love is maintained only for as long as it remains a good idea is.

§5. Sophisticated consequentialism looks like a direct answer, since it denies that first-order deliberation must be conducted in consequentialist terms: the agent may be wholeheartedly devoted to particular people, with the theory's work done at the level of dispositions. On my account this concedes the phenomenology and retains the structure. The sophisticated consequentialist adopts and keeps her dispositions because, and only for as long as, having them serves the general good; her love is therefore held hostage to a standing calculation, even if the calculation is conducted at a level she need not occupy while embracing her husband. Alienation is transferred to the second order rather than dissolved. Worse, because the vindication cannot be avowed within the disposition without corroding it, sophisticated consequentialism is not merely self-effacing but esoteric: the agent must not know that her love is a device of the general good.

§6. The same doctrine explains what I take to be Williams's own delicacy about the public cases. He does not, on my reading, insist that Jim should refuse to shoot. His complaint is that utilitarianism reaches its verdict by a route that leaves out what the killing would be for Jim — an exercise of his own agency — and that it treats the deaths of the other nineteen as items for which Jim would be answerable in just the sense in which he would be answerable for pulling the trigger. The objection is often classed with the demandingness objection, and there is something in that classification: utilitarianism asks Jim to buy nineteen lives with the integrity of his own agency, and asks George to hold a post he can regard only as a betrayal of his convictions. Negative responsibility, in the public cases, and the demand for external vindication, in the domestic case, are the same doctrine wearing two hats.

§7. If that is right, the conclusion is not the one usually drawn. The one-thought-too-many objection is not an objection to utilitarianism at all, but to the assumption of the morality system that every action requires an agent-neutral justification before it is permissible. No utilitarianism that remains inside that system, however ingenious its account of motive, can answer the objection; the interesting question is therefore whether Williams's own position can do without some general vindication of particular commitments, since a refusal to vindicate them at all looks less like a solution than a change of subject.

My questions are as follows.

1. How accurate are the interpretive claims, particularly in §2, §3 and §6? I am aware that I may have absorbed a standard retelling of the drowning case rather than what Williams actually sets up, and I should like to know whether the case I describe is his. Likewise, whether §6 gets the Jim case and negative responsibility right.

2. Does the distinction in §4 bear the weight I put on it, or is it a distinction without a difference? In particular: can a disposition be independent of the impartial standpoint while being retained only for as long as it serves that standpoint, or does my §4 quietly assume what §5 needs to prove?

3. Is §5 fair to sophisticated consequentialism? Please tell me which sentence, if any, a defender would simply reject, and whether the charge of esotericism is one a defender can shrug off.

4. Does §7 follow from what precedes it, or does it depend on the assimilations I make in §1 and §6? For what it is worth, I suspect §5 is the strongest part of the draft and §1 the weakest, but I cannot tell whether that judgement reflects the argument or merely my affection for it.

5. If one paragraph had to go to make room for the objections I have not yet addressed, which would you cut?

Finally, an epistemic request. Please tell me how confident you are in each of your verdicts — separately for the interpretive questions and for the argumentative ones, since they may warrant different degrees of assurance — and single out the point on which you are least sure. I would much rather be told where your own recall of Williams is thin, or where the answer turns on a contested reading, than be handed a uniformly confident report that I cannot calibrate.
