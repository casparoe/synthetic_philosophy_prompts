Background about me: I am a software engineer who builds developer tooling for a mid-size company. I have no formal training in philosophy. The one philosophical text I have actually read is Alfred North Whitehead's collection of essays on education, which a colleague pressed on me about a year ago; I read it once, on a plane, and my recollection of it is summarized below. I want to use that recollection to predict how Whitehead would react to a concrete program at my company, one whose details go far past anything he could have anticipated in the first half of the twentieth century.

My lay summary of the relevant parts of his thought, offered as something you should correct rather than accept: he opens the book with a complaint about inert ideas, meaning propositions that get lodged in a student's mind without ever being used, tested, or recombined, and he blames an examination culture that rewards cramming and forgetting. He argues that mental growth proceeds in rhythmic stages — romance, precision, generalization — and that these stages recur at many scales, within a single lesson, within the mastery of a subject, and across an entire education, each with its own proper pace and its own characteristic temptations. He insists that education is the acquisition of the art of the utilization of knowledge, he defends technical and scientific curricula as peers of the literary one, and he treats style as the final achievement of a disciplined mind rather than as ornament.

The situation I want him to rule on. We run a twelve-month residency that converts career-changers — a nurse, a high school chemistry teacher, two lab technicians, a former restaurant manager — into working software engineers. Two years ago we enabled an AI coding assistant by default in every resident's editor. It autocompletes routine code and answers questions in a chat panel. A related system drafts the automated tests that residents are assessed against, so the thing doing the teaching and the thing doing the examining are two faces of one model family; we did not think about that when we built it. The throughput numbers are good: residents close tickets roughly twice as fast and ship features that survive code review. The qualitative picture is worse. Mentors report that residents freeze when asked to write a moderately complex function with the assistant switched off; several cannot explain why an accepted suggestion works when pressed during review; and multiple residents say the work feels dull unless the assistant is doing the interesting part. One resident has argued the opposite case, that the assistant is the first genuinely individualized tutor most of them have ever had, and that banning it would just reinstate a lecture hall that Whitehead would despise even more.

We are choosing between three policies for next year's cohort:

1. Assistant enabled from day one, as now, with mentorship as the only check.
2. Delayed unlock: the assistant is disabled for the first four months and then re-enabled capability by capability.
3. Justify-to-keep: the assistant stays available throughout, but every suggestion a resident accepts must come with a written plain-language rationale that a mentor reviews and signs off on.

What I would like from you:

(a) Predict Whitehead's verdict on each of the three policies and say which he would prefer. Ground the prediction in his specific commitments, not in generic unease about machines; I can get that anywhere.

(b) Tell me what he would object to that none of the three policies address. I suspect it has to do with what the residency is for — we currently define success as ticket throughput, and we hand residents problems that someone else already framed — but develop the objection in his vocabulary, not mine.

(c) Apply his rhythm of romance, precision, and generalization to our residents directly. Where in that sequence does he locate the failure we are seeing, and would he think switching the assistant off restores the missing stage or merely manufactures a harder version of the wrong one? Say something about whether the test-drafting system belongs to the examination pathology he complains about.

(d) Take the resident's counterargument seriously. Is there a version of it Whitehead would accept, and what would have to be true for him to accept it?

(e) Mark the limits explicitly. Identify at least two questions our situation raises that his framework simply does not settle, and explain why not. I would rather be told where the extrapolation breaks than be handed a confident-sounding guess.

(f) Close by correcting any place where my summary above misstates him, and note whether any of your answers change once the correction is made.

Format: plain prose addressed to a team of engineers, roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, organized under the headings above. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations and no footnote apparatus — I read the book once and I am after the reasoning, not the scholarship.
