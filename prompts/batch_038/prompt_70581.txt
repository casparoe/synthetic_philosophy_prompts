I'm putting together a recorded conversation for an interdisciplinary seminar series — half machine-learning grad students, half philosophers of science — and next week's guest is a mechanistic interpretability researcher. I'd rather not name her; treat her as a composite of the people who actually defend the position I'm about to describe. Her thesis is a strong one: that large trained networks contain genuine representations, and that the "features" recovered by sparse autoencoders and similar decomposition methods are real, discrete, individuable properties implemented in the network's activation space rather than convenient ways of talking about it. She holds that the network's internals have a fact of the matter about what they are about, that the right decompositions discover that structure instead of imposing it, and that instrumentalist readings — the intentional-stance view on which representation talk is just a predictive stance we adopt toward a complicated system, or the view on which features are relative to the probe, the dataset, or the analysis we happen to run — get the ontology backwards. The evidence she leans on is roughly threefold: causal interventions (amplifying a direction associated with, say, the Golden Gate Bridge in a deployed model and watching that landmark intrude into nearly every subsequent reply, in a way that is not equivalent to prompting or fine-tuning), systematic generalization behavior, and cases where models' self-reports line up with what the interpretability methods independently identify.

I want eight to ten questions, each two or three sentences, each followed by exactly one follow-up phrased as a direct response to the answer she is most likely to give — not a generic "could you say more about that." Please make sure the set covers the following, in whatever order you think builds best, and let the follow-ups do the pressing rather than the initial questions: (1) what I've started calling the two-dictionaries problem — sparse autoencoders trained on the same activations at different dictionary sizes and sparsity penalties both yield clean, causally effective features, but they carve the space differently and disagree about how many features there are and where their boundaries lie; if realism cannot say which decomposition is the true one, what exactly is it realist about? (2) the fact that interventions seem not to discriminate: a hand-built direction, or one fit by a supervised probe on a labeled stimulus set, can often be clamped with comparable behavioral consequences, so what privileges the recovered features over any direction that happens to work? (3) a case I'd like put to her directly — a direction that fires reliably for a class of inputs and changes behavior when amplified, but that nothing downstream in the network appears to use for any task it can perform, and that is absent in other models trained on similar data; is that a representation, and if not, what does the "no" cost her, since it looks like representationhood has turned on a use criterion? (4) cross-model identity — two independently trained networks solving the same task with substantially different internal geometry: do they represent the same things, and what has to be preserved (causal role, geometry, behavioral dispositions) for representational content to be shared? (5) an explicit comparison question asking which familiar scientific realism debate her position most resembles — genes as difference-makers, species, centers of mass, the wavefunction — and where the analogy gives out; (6) how she distinguishes a model's self-report about its own processing from a trained disposition to emit sentences of that shape, without relying on the very interpretability methods whose reliability is in question; and (7) closing with falsifiability: describe the concrete experimental result that would move her to say the feature talk was bookkeeping after all.

Two constraints on tone and form. First, the audience is mixed, so each question should be self-contained and gloss its own jargon in a subordinate clause — someone who has never trained a dictionary should still be able to follow what is being asked. Second, she is smart, media-trained, and has heard the skeptical moves before, so no question should be answerable by a slogan she has already used in public, and where a question depends on a distinction (discovery versus construction, causal efficacy versus causal privilege, ontology versus measurement), the question should name the distinction explicitly so it cannot be dissolved by redefinition. I got into an argument about this at dinner a few weeks ago and could not settle it, which is the real reason I'm asking: one of my colleagues maintains that when we say the model represents the Golden Gate Bridge, the fact being reported is entirely about us and our labeling scheme, and I want the interview to force the issue rather than let both sides declare victory.
