I'm in my final year of high school. My AP European History teacher mentioned Adam Smith in passing during the Enlightenment unit, remarking only that he wrote a book about sympathy before he wrote about markets, and I bought a paperback reprint of The Theory of Moral Sentiments out of curiosity and ended up spending the year working through it slowly, mostly alone, with no notes and no secondary literature. I've now read it twice, and I'm drafting an essay for my school's philosophy journal while also using it as a writing sample for a summer seminar application. Before I commit anything to paper I want a check on whether the position I've actually arrived at is coherent, because I have nobody here to argue with and I suspect I've been papering over something. Here is the package, as honestly as I can state it:

(1) Sympathy is an act of imagination rather than a contagion of feeling: I approve of another person's sentiment when, having placed myself in their situation as concretely as I can manage, I find that I would feel something of the same kind and to a comparable degree. Moral judgment is therefore about the fit between a sentiment and the situation that provokes it, not about how much good the conduct goes on to produce.

(2) The impartial spectator is not an innate faculty, and Smith has no moral sense to fall back on. It is built up out of the real judgments of real onlookers: an abstraction we internalize from having been watched, praised, and blamed since childhood.

(3) So the spectator's verdicts are historically variable. Custom and fashion shape what a given society's spectator finds graceful or odious, and Smith is explicit that their dominion reaches moral approbation itself, not merely matters of taste.

(4) And yet whole societies can be morally wrong. When Smith turns on slavery and on the cruelty of European conquerors toward subject peoples, that reads to me as a discovery rather than as a report of what eighteenth-century Scottish spectators happened to dislike. A spectator can be corrupt, and we can sometimes see that it is.

(5) The inner spectator outranks the outer ones for a specific reason: we consult it precisely when actual onlookers are absent, partial, bribed, or deceived, and Smith has us appeal from the verdict of the crowd to the tribunal within the breast. Self-love, on his account, is far easier to deceive than that tribunal is.

(6) The correcting work is done by imagination. What makes the spectator impartial is that imagination lets us strip away the distortions of distance and vividness: the same faculty that lets a man of humanity sleep soundly tonight over the ruin of a hundred million of his fellows in China, while costing him his sleep over the prospect of losing his own little finger tomorrow, is the faculty that lets him notice the disproportion and condemn himself for it.

(7) On the dead: where the injured party no longer exists and so feels nothing, a corpse dug up and mutilated or a promise broken to someone who died a decade ago, there is no injustice in the strict sense, only an offence against the sensibilities of the living. Demerit requires resentment, and where there is no possible sufferer there is no possible resentment to enter into.

(8) Sympathy is not disguised self-interest: we care about other people's fortunes for their own sake, and Smith denies that self-love is the origin of benevolence. I also accept his claim that there is a distinctive pleasure in mutual sympathy, that we actively pursue it, and that we deliberately damp our own feelings down to the pitch an onlooker can go along with in order to obtain it.

What I want is an audit rather than a defense of Smith and not a survey of his system. Go through the list and tell me which items genuinely pull against one another and which only look as though they do, and please don't treat every tension as equally serious: rank them. For each genuine one, say what would have to be given up or revised, what the cheapest available revision looks like, and what that revision costs the rest of the package. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most, and whether any of my commitments is doing work I haven't noticed it doing. Please don't recommend secondary literature or cite scholarship; I want the audit to run on Smith's arguments and on mine, and I'll handle references myself later.
