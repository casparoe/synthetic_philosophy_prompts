Retired control systems engineer, first year of reading philosophy for fun, and I've walked myself into a version of the stone puzzle that I dont think the usual "the task contradicts itself" reply touches.

(1) R is omnipotent iff R can bring about any state of affairs that could obtain without contradiction.
(2) "There is a rule that R has freely adopted and cannot now revoke" describes such a state of affairs — I build them all the time: latching interlocks, write-once fuses.
(3) So an omnipotent R can bring that state about, i.e. adopt a rule it cannot revoke.
(4) If R does, then from then on some contradiction-free state of affairs is beyond R: R's revoking of the rule.
(5) So R either isn't omnipotent or stops being so — and if omnipotence is supposed to be what a being is, not a phase it passes through, no being is omnipotent.
(6) Yet (2)-(4) is just my interlock in metal and silicon: fully capable at t1, irreversibly constrained at t2, and nobody concludes the device was never fully capable or that "capable" was confused.
(7) So the trouble isn't in (2)-(4), it's in (1): omnipotence is not power over every contradiction-free state of affairs.

Which step is actually broken? I'd much rather keep (1) than accept (7), because dropping (1) looks like rigging the definition — power over everything except limiting yourself. Lay out the exits and price each one for me. And tell me straight whether (6) really has the same structure as (2)-(5), or whether I'm just being comforted by an analagous thing from my day job.
