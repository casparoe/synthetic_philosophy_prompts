I am seventeen and in my final year of secondary school, and I am writing an independent research paper, supervised by my philosophy teacher, on whether the categories used to sort students and patients — diagnostic labels, screening categories, and the categories my own school has invented — are the sort of thing that could be true of the world rather than merely useful to us. Part of my argument turns on what Ian Hacking called the looping effect of human kinds: the idea that once a classification of people becomes known to the people it classifies, they adjust to it, so that the category and its objects change together over time, and a term coined by experts can eventually be inhabited, resisted, or performed by those it picks out. Before I lean on that idea, my teacher told me to read the classic statement of the standard it seems to threaten, which he located in John Stuart Mill's A System of Logic, in the part of Book IV where Mill takes up classification as subsidiary to induction. I read the relevant stretch twice in our library copy, but I had to return the volume, so what follows is my own reconstruction of the argument from my notes rather than a quotation, and I would be grateful if you would tell me where the reconstruction goes wrong before I build anything on top of it.

As I understand it, the argument runs roughly as follows. Mill opens by contrasting two ways of arranging things into groups. One way is chosen for a purpose extrinsic to the things themselves — the illustration I remember is something like arranging plants alphabetically so that a herbalist can find an entry quickly, though I may be conflating this with another example he uses — and he treats such arrangements as artificial, because the property that determines membership has nothing to do with anything else that is true of the plants. The other way groups things by their real resemblances, so that members of a group share a great many properties, and he calls these groups natural. His criterion, as I have it, is something like: the best classification is the one that puts things into groups about which the greatest number of general propositions can be asserted. He also relies, in this chapter or in earlier parts of the Logic that it refers back to, on the notion of natural kinds — groups that appear to be marked off from one another by a gap, whose members share an indefinite multitude of properties not derivable from one another or from any single underlying feature — and he seems to think that where such kinds exist, classification has a fixed target independent of the classifier, while where they do not, we are merely arranging things for convenience. There is a further discussion of arranging groups in a series so that neighbours resemble one another, and of a typical group that carries the most characteristics of a larger group, which I did not follow at all.

My questions, in order of importance:

1. Is my formulation of the criterion right? Mill seems to speak of the number of general propositions that can be made about a group, but I cannot tell whether the operative standard is quantitative (how many true general statements hold of the group) or qualitative (how much the classification lets you predict), or whether for Mill these come to the same thing. What would he count as a general proposition, and does the criterion compare classifications by tallying them?

2. Does the criterion rule out classifications built for practical purposes? The category my school uses exists in order to allocate a study-hall accommodation, so it is instrumental from the start. If Mill allows that an arrangement may be judged by the use to which it is put, does that make my school's category artificial but legitimate, or does it drain the natural/artificial contrast of any use for evaluating it?

3. What does the notion of a natural kind add? If a group's members reliably share a large bundle of properties, what further work is done by saying those properties are indefinite in number and not derivable from one another? I suspect this matters for my paper, because a diagnostic category is usually built from a checklist whose items were selected precisely because they hang together, which seems to be the reverse of an indefinite multitude.

4. This is the difficulty I most want checked. Suppose a category is introduced, published, and then inhabited by the people it picks out: they begin to describe themselves in its terms, institutions respond to it, and behaviour shifts. It looks to me as though such a category could improve its score on Mill's own criterion after it is applied, since the classification creates new regularities among its members, so that more general propositions become assertible of the group than were true of those same people before the label existed. If that is right, a looping category could be artificial in its origin yet outperform a botanist's natural group by Mill's measure, and the measure would be unable to tell apart a category that tracks something and a category that manufactures what it tracks. Does the objection hold together, or am I equivocating — on what a general proposition is, or on the idea that a group's properties are fixed independently of the classifier? Where would Mill resist it, and is there anything in his treatment of purpose-relative arrangement that could absorb it rather than refute it?

5. The concrete case I want to test this on. Two years ago my school's counselling office adopted a screening checklist for a category it calls "attention-fragmentation pattern," applied to any student whose phone-use logs cross a threshold it set. Students who meet it are assigned a reduced-distraction study hall, and the label is communicated both to them and to their teachers. Since then the office reports that flagged students show measurably better focus during that period, and treats this as validating the checklist. What does Mill's framework imply about whether this group is natural? And what sort of evidence would distinguish a group that was already there to be found from a group made real by its own announcement, given that in this case the announcement, the accommodation, and the measurement are all downstream of the same checklist?

Please answer in the order given. Begin by telling me plainly whether my reconstruction of the argument is faithful or whether I have distorted it in some way that would change your answers, and mark clearly the places where you are supplying reasoning Mill would most likely give rather than anything he addresses directly. I do not want a bibliography or a list of further readings: I have no library access at home and could not check them, so I would rather have the reasoning worked through carefully in front of me. I am comfortable with technical vocabulary provided you define terms as you introduce them.
