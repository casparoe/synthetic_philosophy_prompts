Two years ago my grandmother moved into a memory care unit, and for the past year I have gone there every Saturday morning both to sit with her and to volunteer with the activities staff. I am trying to write an essay about the place for the philosophy seminar I am taking, and I have collected a set of practices that I find genuinely puzzling. The unit has a bus shelter in its front garden, with a bench and a schedule posted in a case; it is not on any route, and no bus has ever stopped there. It exists because residents with dementia often become agitated and announce that they must go home to their mothers, and rather than locking the doors or medicating them, staff walk them out to the shelter, where they sit and wait and usually grow calm, and are then led back inside. In the same spirit, we are instructed never to correct a resident. When a woman asks where her husband is, and her husband died in 1994, the guidance is to ask what he was like, or to say that he is at work and will be home later, rather than to tell her the truth, on the grounds that she cannot retain the information and would experience the news as though hearing it for the first time every fifteen minutes. There is also a robotic seal that purrs and nuzzles when stroked, which is placed in the arms of residents to settle them, and on Thursdays a headset is sometimes used to show people a filmed walk through the streets of the town they grew up in.

What confuses me is that the same building is fanatical about accuracy everywhere else. Every resident has a life-story binder compiled by the staff, containing her real name, the farm she was born on, the trade her husband actually worked at, and photographs from the wedding, and we are told repeatedly that person-centered care consists largely in knowing those facts and using them, so that we address people as who they in fact were rather than as whoever they currently take themselves to be. The reminiscence sessions are curated with an almost pedantic attention to period detail: the music has to be from the correct years of the resident's childhood, and staff correct one another when someone dates an object wrongly. There is a written policy treating any deception as a measure of last resort that must be justified and documented, and a standing prohibition on infantilizing residents. So the unit seems to hold two opposite attitudes toward the truth and to apply each of them in different rooms, and I cannot work out whether that is hypocrisy, a compromise forced by circumstances, or something more principled than it looks.

I would like you to reconstruct what each cluster of practices presupposes about what makes a person's life go well. Does the bus shelter, the invented husband, and the robotic seal only make sense if the staff are committed to something like hedonism, to the view that well-being is exhausted by the quality of a person's experience? Or could a quite different view license exactly the same bench, for instance one on which comfort, engagement, and being attended to are goods in themselves whether or not the person grasps that they have them, in which case the practice would be underdetermined and would settle nothing? Please tell me what the unit strictly has to believe as opposed to what merely sits well with what it does, and which rival theories of well-being the practices actually rule out, including any view on which knowing the truth about your own life, or standing in real relations to real people, is a constituent of well-being independently of how it feels. I am especially stuck on the life-story binders: they look like a commitment to reality mattering in itself, but I can also see them as a purely instrumental technique, a database of reliable soothing triggers, and I do not know how to tell which of those the practice embodies. Then there is Nozick's experience machine, which we read in seminar; it is normally posed as a question addressed to a competent person about what she would choose, and here nobody is choosing anything, and the residents, if the shelter were explained to them on a lucid day, would almost certainly refuse to sit at it. Tell me whether that removes the force of the argument against hedonism, or only relocates it, and whether the machine case and the memory care case are really the same case. Finally, describe what would have to change in the daily operation of the unit if it adopted the view that contact with reality is itself a component of a good life and not merely a useful means to pleasant experience, and whether such a unit could remain humane given that the alternatives available to it are locked doors and sedation.
