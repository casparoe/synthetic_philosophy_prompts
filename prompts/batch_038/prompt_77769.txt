I'm auditing a seminar on Indian epistemology this term and separately working through some history-of-medicine stuff, and last night I got stuck on a case I can't stop thinking about. I want to run it through the Nyāya machinery as a test of whether that machinery has any teeth on something it was never designed for. The case is the ulcer story, and here it is in brief so you don't have to guess at the facts. For most of the twentieth century the settled doctrine was that peptic ulcers came from excess acid, and behind the acid from stress, diet and personality; treatment was antacids, bland food, rest, sometimes surgery. In the late seventies a Perth pathologist, Robin Warren, kept noticing spiral-shaped bacteria sitting on the surface of stomach biopsies from patients with gastritis, which was supposed to be impossible. A young registrar, Barry Marshall, teamed up with him, they tried to culture the organism, and the first thirty of a hundred samples looked like failures — it turned out the lab's standing practice was to bin culture plates after two days, because that's what you do with throat swabs, and the thirty-first plate only survived because the technicians were too busy to throw it out. It sat in the incubator from Thursday to Monday and grew. Their study of roughly a hundred patients came out in the Lancet in 1984; a paper they'd sent to the Gastroenterological Society of Australia the year before had been ranked by reviewers in the bottom ten percent of that year's submissions. In 1984 they failed to infect piglets, and Marshall then drank a broth of the cultured bacteria himself: nausea and bad breath within three days, vomiting on days five through eight, an endoscopy on day eight showing massive gastritis with the organism cultured from the biopsy, antibiotics from day fourteen. He never developed an ulcer. The self-experiment satisfied Koch's postulates for gastritis but not for ulcers. Meanwhile the establishment line — nothing survives gastric acid, so anything seen down there is a contaminant or an opportunist colonizing already-damaged tissue — was being defended largely by people who hadn't looked, though I should say the popular hagiography is contested and some historians argue the field showed proper skepticism rather than dogma. By the mid-nineties antibiotics were standard of care for ulcers; Marshall and Warren got the Nobel in 2005. Also relevant: something like half of humanity carries the bug and most carriers never get an ulcer.

Here's what I want out of you. First, sort the evidence into the pramāṇas and tell me where nothing fits: Warren looking at a stained biopsy through a microscope, the cultures, the correlation across the hundred patients, Marshall's own symptoms, the piglet failures, the antibiotic trials that cured people, and the confident consensus that ulcers are acid and stress. Second, put the causal claim in the five-membered form with the reason and the probandum made explicit, and state the pervasion it depends on — then tell me honestly whether there *is* a pervasion in either direction given the carrier statistics, or whether the inference needs a limiting condition (an upādhi, if that's the right tool: strain virulence? smoking? host factors?), or whether the only inference the evidence actually licensed was about gastritis rather than ulcers. Third, take the skeptics' reason, nothing survives acid, and say which of the defective-reason types it is, and whether it was defective from the start or only became defective in hindsight — and whether at some point both sides had live inferences on the table, which I think raises a counterbalance problem in Nyāya's own terms. Fourth, and this is my favorite part: perception is characterized in terms of contact between sense faculty and object, plus conditions about being non-verbal and non-erroneous. A bacillus at that magnification isn't in contact with anything, and Warren saw it only as a bacterium, i.e. under a concept. So is the founding observation of the entire story pratyakṣa at all? If not, what is it — an inference from stains and shapes? And if you want to answer that the microscope just extends the eye, does the framework actually have resources for that, or am I importing a modern move and dressing it in Sanskrit? Fifth, upamāna: I half-suspect it's a bad fit for this case, because as I understand it it's about learning what a word picks out by comparing something with a similar thing you already know, not about analogical reasoning in general — if that's right, tell me where the piglet work and the animal-model reasoning go instead, and whether satisfying a template like Koch's postulates is an instance of any pramāṇa at all. Sixth, testimony and the āpta condition: it only counts when the speaker knows the thing directly and communicates it as known. Run that on the 1980 establishment, on Marshall's own public claims, and on the clinicians who started prescribing antibiotics before they'd cultured anything themselves.

Then I want the arrow turned around, because that's the part I can't do myself. Is this episode a genuine vindication of Nyāya's restrictive treatment of testimony — the framework predicts that a confident consensus of people without direct acquaintance deserves no epistemic weight, which is exactly what happened — or did it just get the right verdict by luck? Note the cost: the same criterion hands weight to any confident person claiming direct acquaintance, and there were earlier clinicians reporting the same bacteria-ulcer association who went nowhere, so what in the framework seperates Warren from them other than the fact that he turned out to be right? Finally, be blunt about where the taxonomy is silent. I suspect it has nothing to say about base rates and statistical evidence, nothing to say about the reliability of a *method* as opposed to the truth of a particular cognition (so the fact that the winning observation only happened because a technician was too busy to throw a plate away makes no epistemic difference at all — confirm or correct me on that), and nothing to say about the social organization of science: who gets to run the culture, who gets a slot at the society meeting, what a consensus conference does. Constraints: no bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and please don't invent sūtra numbers or attributions — if you're not sure a technical term does the job I'm assigning it, say so rather than bluffing. Use the Sanskrit terms but gloss each one the first time. And if the honest conclusion is that some chunk of this episode just falls outside the four pramāṇas, say that; a clean negative result is more useful to me than a forced mapping.
