ok so this is a slightly weird one but I've gotten deep into 18th century Irish monetary pamphlets because of a fight I'm having with my town council, of all things.

Background: I help run a mutual credit circle in a small ex-mining town, about 6,000 people. Roughly 45 businesses and maybe 300 residents trade in units we call marks — no cash, just entries in a shared ledger, zero interest, and nobody's allowed to sit further than 800 marks in the red or in the black. It started as a time bank in 2020 and mutated into this when the last bank branch shut and the only grocer got bought by a chain that won't take part.

The problem: two members — the builders merchant and a haulage firm — have run up big surpluses they can't spend, because the people who'd normally sell them things (me, the café, the bakery) are all at our credit ceilings and can't go further into the red. So the merchant has quietly started refusing marks from anyone who isn't good for it, which is basically him doing his own credit scoring outside the rules, and now marks from some members trade worse than marks from others. Meanwhile a councillor went on local radio and called the whole scheme a fraud because there's nothing behind the marks — no asset, no gold, no council guarantee — which sounds convincing to people and definately landed with the older residents.

Where Berkeley comes in: somebody sent me his Querist, the Bishop of Cloyne one from the 1730s, the several hundred numbered questions about the Irish economy, and I've been ploughing through it on my phone at night. From what I gather his big move is to deny that money is itself wealth and treat it instead as something like a ticket or counter that records and transfers a power to get other people working — so the real question about any money isn't what it's made of or what stands behind it, but whether it actually makes the idle act. And then he goes on, as far as I can follow, about setting up a national bank issuing paper notes secured against land rather than specie. I may well have the details wrong; tell me if I do, that's part of what I want.

So: what are the most important implications of that view of money for my credit circle? Concretely. Things I'd like you to get into:

- If marks are just tallies for transferring the power to set people working, does that predict the hoarding mess we're in? Is an unspendable surplus still power in his sense? And does his framework give me an argument for demurrage or expiring marks — I floated a 5% annual shrinkage and it went down like a lead balloon.
- Does the land-backed note idea support the councillor or undercut him? I keep flip-flopping: on one hand Berkeley wants notes secured on something real, on the other he seems to think the security only matters because it keeps people confident the notes will go on commanding labour. Which is it, and would our version of backing — pledges of future building work, the empty shop unit the council owns — count?
- What does he make of the fact that our tally isn't issued by anything that can compel you to accept it? Membership is voluntary and you can walk away with your balance frozen. Does his account of money need a sovereign or a tax at the centre, and if so are the marks money at all or just a long chain of barter IOUs?
- The caps. If the whole point is stirring up industry, is an 800-mark ceiling on both sides the right instrument, or does it strangle exactly the circulation he says matters?
- Where would Berkeley part company with the later story that money grew out of barter to solve the double coincidence of wants, and where with the story that money is whatever the state accepts for taxes? Do either of those disagreements change what I should do next month?

No reading list or citations please, I can't chase anything down and don't want to. Just the reasoning, laid out so I can turn it into a two page note for the circle's meeting on the 14th — say 700 to 1000 words. And if my summary of Berkeley is off in some way that matters, put that first: I'd much rather be corrected now than confidently wrong in front of a room full of people who already think I'm a crank.
