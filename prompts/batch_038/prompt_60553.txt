Question 3: Newton denied the doctrine of the Trinity but said almost nothing about it in print. Explain his private position, why it stayed private, and how — if at all — his theological commitments show up in his published natural philosophy. Would it be fair to call Newton a deist?

Student's answer:

Newton's rejection of the Trinity was the most dangerous thing about him intellectually. He held that the Father alone was God in the strict sense, and that the Son was a created being, subordinate to the Father and not co-eternal with him, so the orthodox doctrine of three co-equal persons amounted in his eyes to a kind of idolatry — giving a creature the worship owed only to the creator. He thought this doctrine wasn't original Christianity at all but a corruption pushed through in the fourth century, and he spent years doing textual work on the proof passages the orthodox relied on, arguing that the two most explicit of them (the verse in 1 John about the Father, the Word and the Holy Ghost, and a passage in 1 Timothy) had been inserted into the text by later scribes. None of it appeared in print in his lifetime; the scriptural material only came out after his death.

It stayed private because it would have destroyed him. As Lucasian professor he'd been excused from taking holy orders by royal dispensation in 1669, which removed one pressure, but open Arianism in Restoration England meant losing everything. His successor Whiston was expelled from Cambridge for saying publicly what Newton only wrote for himself.

In print, the theology shows up mainly in the General Scholium, added to the second edition of the Principia in 1713. There Newton argues that the system of the sun, planets and comets could only come from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent being, and that this being governs all things not as the soul of the world but as its Lord. His hypotheses non fingo line shows how careful he is here: he won't say anything about God's nature beyond the bare fact that a designer exists, so God functions as a placeholder for whatever he couldn't account for mechanically, and Newton keeps him out of the details of the system. That's also why I'd answer yes to the last part of the question — if you strip out the private manuscripts, what's actually published is essentially a deist's God, an intelligent designer who set things going and left them to run under law. The Scholium was added to answer Leibniz's complaint that Newton's God had to wind up his clock from time to time, which is exactly the remote-lawgiver picture the deists went for. Newton's work on biblical prophecy and on correcting ancient chronology seems to me a seperate thing, an private obsession that never fed back into the natural philosophy at all.
