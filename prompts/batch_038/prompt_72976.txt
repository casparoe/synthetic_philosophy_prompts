I write literary fiction — three novels out, translated into six languages, and the only one of them I can actually check is the Spanish (I lived in Bogotá for four years, my grammar is bad but I can usually feel when something's off). Here's the problem. For the last two books my publishers have sent me drafts that I'm fairly sure are machine-generated and then gone over by a human for an hour or two, and I can't tell where the machine stopped and the person started. I have to sign an approval form before it goes to print. I signed one already that I now think is mediocre in ways I couldn't name at the time, and I'd like to not do that again.

I keep going back to Hofstadter's argument in Le Ton beau de Marot — the one about how a translation system that isn't comprehending anything will still produce locally smooth sentences, so the shallowness only shows up where the text actually demands that you get what it's about, and how he liked to test this on poetry because form leaves nowhere to hide. What I want to know is whether that argument still buys me anything now that the systems are much better, and more urgently, whether I can use it.

So: write me a diagnostic protocol I can run on a translated manuscript. Not an explainer — I've read the book, please don't re-summarise it at me, and don't send me a reading list either. I want a procedure: probes, in the order I should run them, where each probe is a feature that a non-comprehending system should get wrong even when everything around it reads beautifully.

For each probe give me four things. What I look for in my own English source (or plant there deliberately before I hand the file over). What the Spanish has to do to be right. What the wrongness looks like on the page, concretely enough that my helper and I can both spot it without arguing about it. And — the part I care about most — the innocent explanations for that wrongness and how I rule them out. I already know most of these failures are ambiguous between "it didn't understand," "it understood and chose badly," "a tired posteditor smoothed it over," and "Spanish genuinely can't say this." Where you can, build me a control: a near-identical sentence where the right answer flips, so I can seperate a systematic blind spot from noise.

Then run the whole procedure end to end on three real cases from the book I'm currently arguing about.

One: chapter four, a woman says of her husband that he didn't go back to the house because of the dog. The English is deliberately double — he did go back, the dog just wasn't why; he went back for the letters, which you don't learn for two hundred pages. Spanish has to commit. I want to know how I check which way it committed and what it would mean if it committed the common way.

Two: chapter three, the narrator describes an ordinary afternoon with her brother, he swims out past the rocks, the water is cold. Chapter twenty-two: he drowned that afternoon, she has known the whole time, and she never once says so. The flat English past tense is load-bearing. But Spanish makes the translator choose an aspect, and either choice encodes something about how the narrator is holding that afternoon. Is there a probe that catches a leak like that, or is my whole worry here untestable?

Three: in chapter two the brother nicknames her Sparrow, an ordinary word doing name-work. In chapter thirty-one she watches actual sparrows on a wire and the echo is supposed to land without anyone pointing at it. My fear is the nickname picking up a diminutive the real birds don't get, so the two uses stop being the same word three hundred pages apart.

While you're designing the probe set, make sure it can also handle these, even if you don't run them: a married couple who use the formal pronoun with each other except on two occasions, where the slip is the emotional climax and I need to know it survived at the same two places; a deadpan line that's only ironic because of something established sixty pages earlier, where I suspect the machine will either flatten it or over-mark it; and a chapter-title acrostic, where I know going in that failure proves nothing and I want your procedure to say so rather than pad its hit rate.

Two of us, roughly two hours per manuscript plus an hour per case, no linguistics training beyond what you get from obsessing over your own sentences. Also give me the one-page version my helper can work from, honestly flagging which steps are judgement calls wearing a procedural costume; where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision back to me because it's a question of taste or of what the book is for; and which of the three cases, if any, it's going to tell me is my fault rather than the machine's. Say that one straight, I'm not going to like it.

And at the end, briefly and without hedging: does a Hofstadter-style diagnosis still pick out a real difference in these systems, or has it collapsed into a fancy way of saying the translation isn't very good?
