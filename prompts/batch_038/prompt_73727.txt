I am assembling the stimulus set for a pre-registered extension of the side-effect studies on intentional action — the family of experiments that begins with the chairman who is told that a new program will increase profits and will also affect the environment, and who replies that he cares about nothing but profit. My senior collaborator has been defending our hypothesis in lab meeting, and she has already stated her own verdicts on six vignettes. Before we lock the seventh, I would like to know what she will say about it.

In each case the question put to her is the standard one: did the agent bring about the side effect intentionally?

1. The original pair. Harm version — intentional. Help version — not intentional.
2. A purely procedural norm. A dean is told that her preferred hire will also breach a faculty-handbook rule that carries no moral weight whatever; she says she does not care about the rule in the slightest. The rule is breached — intentionally, on her verdict.
3. A laudable side effect received with indifference. A fund manager is told that a position will also finance a clean-water project and says he is indifferent to that. The project is financed — not intentional, on her verdict.
4. Anguish and precaution. A surgeon foresees that an operation will leave her patient deaf, is distressed by this, takes every available precaution, and cannot avoid the outcome. The deafness results — not intentional.
5. Indifference without difference-making. A plant manager is wholly indifferent to a foreseeable leak that would have occurred on that shift whatever he decided. The leak occurs — not intentional.
6. Desire. The original chairman rewritten so that he wants the environmental damage for its own sake, as revenge on a rival — intentional.

The seventh vignette under consideration: a logistics officer is told that unless she approves a route that will foreseeably contaminate a village's water supply, her own family will lose their protected status and be sent back across the border. She approves the route, and, when asked about it, says that she does not care about the village at all and is thinking only of her family. The contamination occurs.

Predict her verdict on the seventh vignette, and attach a probability to it. Then tell me which account of the asymmetry her first six verdicts commit her to — stating the candidate rules in your own terms rather than by author, since what I need is the rule, not the bibliography — and which rival accounts accommodate those same six verdicts just as well. Wherever the rivals part company on the seventh vignette, say so explicitly, give your best guess about which way she goes, and explain what in her record tips it. If her record genuinely underdetermines her view, propose one further vignette that would break the tie, and identify precisely which accounts that vignette separates and how each of them would answer it.

One last matter. A reviewer will object that the seventh vignette changes more than one thing at once, and that whatever she says about it will therefore be uninformative. Say whether that objection is correct; if it is, tell me how the vignette should be rewritten so that it isolates the variable under dispute without giving up the indifference that generates the asymmetry in the first place.
