I am preparing a screening and discussion session on Indian theories of knowledge for an advanced undergraduate seminar, and I want to use Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950) as the centerpiece. The film is familiar to all of us: in Heian-era Kyoto, a woodcutter and a Buddhist priest sheltering under the ruined Rashōmon gate recount, to a cynical commoner, the four incompatible accounts of a samurai's murder in a grove — the bandit Tajōmaru's boastful version, the wife's version, the dead samurai's version delivered through a Shinto medium, and finally the woodcutter's own version, which he initially conceals and which he admits he falsified at the trial because he did not want to be dragged into the matter.

My problem is that the standard gloss on the film — the one my students will arrive with, and the one the phrase the Rashomon effect enshrines — is that it shows there is no truth, only perspectives. I think that gloss is philosophically lazy, and I suspect a Nyāya reading can show why, because Nyāya is a robustly realist school: it holds that exactly one of those accounts is yathārtha, that knowledge is a cognition produced by a defect-free causal process, and that testimony (śabda) counts as a distinct pramāṇa only when it comes from an āpta, a speaker who both knows the relevant facts and has no motive to distort them. On that framework the film is not a skeptical paradox at all but an unusually precise case study in defeated testimony and defective causal chains.

Please write me a full critical reading of the film through Nyāya epistemology, with the following components.

First, work through the four narrators one at a time and assess each against the Nyāya conditions on testimonial knowledge. Be specific about where each speaker fails: which are plausibly sincere but self-aggrandizing, which are self-interested in the crude sense, and what happens to the analysis in the strange case of the dead samurai speaking through a medium — is that śabda at all, or something Nyāya would have to classify differently, and does the fact that the speaker is no longer a living agent undermine or rather strengthen his claim to be an āpta? I want the woodcutter treated with particular care, since he is the one character who both witnessed the event and admitted to misreporting it, which seems to make him the paradigm case of a knower who is not an āpta.

Second, address the rival reduction. Buddhist epistemologists in the Nyāya tradition's neighborhood — Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, roughly — accept only two pramāṇas, perception and inference, and treat testimony as a species of inference about a speaker's mental states. Argue whether the film's own structure is better described by the Nyāya four-pramāṇa picture or by that two-pramāṇa reduction, and pay attention to what the characters at the gate actually do: they seem to stop gathering testimony and start reasoning from the inconsistencies among the reports and from physical traces such as the nature of the wound. Say whether that shift is inference in the Nyāya sense, or whether it looks more like the postulation from observed facts that the Mīmāṃsakas call arthāpatti and that Nyāya refuses to admit as a separate pramāṇa — and if so, whether the film inadvertently stages that particular dispute.

Third, engage the theory of error. Nyāya explains illusion by a kind of misplacement: the silver apparently in the shell is real silver, wrongly located here. Contrast that with the idealist diagnosis on which the error is generated internally, and then say which of these two diagnoses the film's narrators fit. Are Tajōmaru and the wife mislocating real features of what happened, or are they constructing self-images with no external anchor? Does the film give us evidence either way, or is this precisely the question it declines to answer?

Fourth — and this is the part I care about most — tell me where the Nyāya lens distorts the film and where an honest critic has to concede that the reading does not hold. I have in mind especially the ending at the gate, the abandoned infant, the commoner's theft from the child, and the woodcutter's decision to take the baby home, which restores the priest's faith in people. No pramāṇa analysis seems to reach that sequence, and I do not want to force it. Identify any other places where treating the film as an epistemology problem flattens something it is doing — ethically, politically, or formally — and say what is lost.

Finally, close by stating what the film contributes to the Nyāya debate that argument alone does not. Specifically: does dramatating four testimony-givers at once, and then discrediting the only eyewitness, reveal something about the āpta condition that the abstract formulations obscure — for instance, that a speaker's reliability is not a stable property of the speaker but depends on the audience's situation and interests? If you think the answer is no and the film is merely illustrative, say so plainly.

Describe scenes and character behavior in your own words rather than reproducing lines of dialogue, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus; the value here is in the analysis, not in references. Please write the response in American English.
