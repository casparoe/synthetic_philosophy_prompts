I am three years into a novel whose engine is a commitment race: two parties, each of whom wins everything by being the first to make an irreversible public commitment, wins nothing by capitulating, and cannot afford to be seen to have blinked. The book is in danger of becoming a tract, because I have found that my own intuitions about who ought to have prevailed in the central scene are not stable, and my reading has supplied me with a vocabulary rather than with a set of questions. What I require from you is therefore a diagnostic instrument, not an essay.

Please construct a decision tree of eight to ten nodes and no more than nine leaves which sorts a reader — myself, or one of my trial readers, none of whom has any training in game theory — onto a position about whether commitment can settle such a contest, and if so how. Every node must be phrased as a concrete situation or as a claim one could accept or reject without knowing which label the answer leads to: the steering wheel hurled through the open window, the device that cannot afterwards be switched off, the eccentric who pays for an intention rather than for a deed, the extortionist who has publicly bound himself never to accept less than the whole sum, the party who committed before knowing whom he would be facing.

The distinctions I want the tree built out of, rather than out of a list of names, are these: whether moving first is an advantage or a liability; whether credibility can be manufactured by machinery and observation alone, or requires that the committed party genuinely be unable to want otherwise; whether the unit assessed for rationality is the act, the intention, or the standing disposition; whether the second mover's refusal to capitulate is a blunder or the only sane reply; whether the whole edifice depends upon a background practice of keeping one's word that the strategic situation itself does not supply; and whether a commitment to do what one would be wrong to do alters the analysis.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position where one exists — Schelling's strategic surrender of one's own rationality, Kavka's verdict on the eccentric's offer, Gauthier's constrained maximiser, the backward-induction refusal to be deterred, the deflationary appeal to repetition and reputation — and should read simply 'unnamed' where nobody in fact occupies the cell, together with one sentence on the most awkward commitment that view carries.

Finally, please do the two things a tidy diagram would conceal. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say what further node would have been needed and why you omitted it. And list the cells of the logical space that come out empty, with a word on each as to whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has yet thought to defend it.
