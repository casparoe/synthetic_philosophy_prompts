I spent thirty-six years as an igneous petrologist, the last eleven of them running a dating laboratory, and I retired four years ago with the intention of reading philosophy properly rather than in airport bookshops. Last Thursday I attended a public lecture at my old college on the Galileo affair. The speaker closed with a sentence he attributed to Galileo's 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina — "the intention of the Holy Spirit is to teach us how one goes to heaven and not how heaven goes" — remarking in passing that Galileo was there quoting Cardinal Baronio. He then made that one sentence do two pieces of work at once: first, that Rome's objection to Copernicanism was a simple category error, a soteriological text mistaken for a physical one; and second, that the affair consequently has nothing to teach us about how institutions ought to handle unwelcome evidence, since on his account no evidential question ever arose. I was unpersuaded but could not say why during the question period, and I should like to say it precisely in print, having been asked to contribute a note to the alumni magazine.

What I want is the sentence taken apart, not the affair retold. Please give me a numbered menu of the distinct propositions it could be used to assert — I should expect at least six — and for each entry: the claim stated once, in a form capable of being true or false; a verdict on its status, choosing among platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and false, and incoherent; whether Galileo could have held it consistently with the rest of what that letter argues; and, most importantly, whether it by itself would license either of the speaker's two conclusions.

Please be explicit about several distinctions I suspect were run together. There is the difference between a claim about the purpose of a text and a claim about its content — a document may be written to save souls and still assert, incidentally, that the sun did something. There is the difference between an interpretive rule of the accommodation sort and a jurisdictional claim about who may apply it. There is the difference between general claims about how nature regularly behaves and reports of particular events: Joshua's command to the sun, which I believe occupies a substantial part of that same letter — correct me if I have that wrong — reads to me as a statement about how the heavens went on one afternoon. And there is the difference between saying Scripture does not teach natural philosophy and saying Scripture cannot come into conflict with natural philosophy, the latter being a much larger commitment.

I should also like the trigger conditions spelled out: what would have to be established, to what standard, and by whom, before an accommodation rule licenses departing from a received reading — and whether that standard was in fact met in 1615 by a hypothesis whose proponents could offer no stellar parallax.

Please give me no references, no reading list, and no survey of the warfare-thesis controversy; I own the letter and the standard histories. I want the disambiguation, followed by one closing paragraph identifying the strongest reading the speaker could have invoked without undermining his own conclusion, and stating what would survive of that conclusion if he invoked it.
