**Premises as the source of truth: a derive-don't-vote architecture for committee decisions**

*1. The system.* Every decision in our tool is stored as a schema: a fixed set of premise propositions plus one conclusion defined as a formula over them. Schemas are versioned and migrated like database schemas, and once a schema is live its proposition set is frozen. Our most-used schema has three premises and one conclusion:

- A: the requested exception is compatible with our published retention commitments.
- B: the requesting team has a documented business justification.
- C: the incremental audit cost is below the committee's standing budget cap.
- Conclusion R = A ∧ B ∧ C: grant the exception.

Fifteen members vote; a majority on a premise carries the premise. The conclusion is never put to a vote. It is computed from the premise tallies by the application.

*2. What we observed.* In one quarter's batch we got the following distribution. The committee sorted itself into five blocs of three:

| bloc | A | B | C | R |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (3 members) | T | T | T | T |
| 2 (3 members) | T | T | F | F |
| 3 (3 members) | T | F | T | F |
| 4 (3 members) | F | T | T | F |
| 5 (3 members) | F | F | F | F |

Each premise passes 9–6. So premise-majority says grant. But only 3 of 15 members accept R, so a direct vote on R would reject it 12–3. Note that every individual row here is logically consistent: nobody holds a set of judgments that violates R = A ∧ B ∧ C. The divergence is not produced by anyone reasoning badly.

*3. The rule.* We adopted what I understand the literature calls the premise-based procedure, and I want to defend it as an engineering choice rather than a philosophical concession. Our rule is: derive, don't vote. The conclusion is a pure function of the premise tallies, and the function is evaluated once, deterministically, from the stored ballots.

*4. Six claims.*

**(i) Deriving eliminates order dependence.** Because R is computed rather than voted, the output is a deterministic function of a set. It is idempotent, and it is confluent in the sense that there is no reduction order to choose: re-tallying, re-ordering ballots, or splitting the batch cannot change the result. The agenda-manipulation surface that sequential voting procedures have is therefore not merely reduced in our system but absent.

**(ii) Our agenda is restricted enough to escape the impossibility results.** I have read that the general impossibility theorems for judgment aggregation require a connectedness condition — that the propositions in the agenda must be logically entangled with one another, so that the truth value of one constrains others across the agenda. Our agenda fails that condition, or so I claim: the only logical structure in it is the single conjunction we ourselves evaluate, no premise entails any other premise, and we never admit new propositions. So I expect that on our restricted domain we can simultaneously have anonymity, a rule that treats each proposition by the same majoritarian standard, and collective consistency. The impossibility results are real, but they are about agendas richer than ours.

**(iii) Individual consistency, enforced at the interface, gives us collective consistency.** The client rejects any ballot that violates the schema's constraint set, so every stored individual judgment set is logically consistent by construction. Since the inputs are all consistent and the aggregation rule is a straightforward majoritarian one applied proposition by proposition, the output inherits that property. We have never observed an inconsistent collective judgment set in production, and I take claim (ii) to explain why.

**(iv) The decomposition is epistemically better, not just procedurally cleaner.** Each of A, B, and C is, in our setting, largely a question of fact with a determinate answer. Auditing past decisions against outcomes, a bare majority of the committee gets each individual premise right roughly 80% of the time, and the three errors look roughly independent of one another. So the derived conclusion is right about 0.8³ ≈ 0.51 of the time. That is better than a coin flip, and better than the conclusion-majority procedure, which has no comparable guarantee at all, since R is a mixed normative-factual composite on which members are not independently competent. Decomposition buys us measurable reliability.

**(v) Divergence is a useful signal, so we alert on it.** Even though only the derived conclusion has any effect, we compute the conclusion-majority in the background as a shadow value. When the two disagree, the system raises a divergence alert and routes the item to a facilitated session before the decision is committed. My reasoning is that disagreement between the two aggregation paths indicates that the committee's reasoning is not settled, and surfacing that is worth more than the small delay.

**(vi) Responsibility is relocated, and that is fine.** Under derive-don't-vote, no member is ever on record about R. In the batch above the committee granted an exception that twelve of fifteen members, taken individually, were on record opposing. I do not consider this a defect. A committee exists precisely so that outcomes are attributable to the group rather than to whichever members happened to be decisive, and our schema makes the group's reasons inspectable even when its conclusion is unpopular with its members. We do accept that the procedure can violate unanimity on the conclusion, and I regard that as a cost worth paying: unanimity on R is exactly the aggregate signal we are trying to override.
