So my brother-in-law Rainer taught history at a Gymnasium for about thirty-five years and is quietly certain he settled the question of who caused the First World War some time in the 1970s. Last Sunday at a barbecue he got into it with our neighbour's daughter Ines, who's roughly halfway through a philosophy PhD and works on something to do with responsibility. It started because somebody had mentioned a documentary, and by the time the sausages were done they were two rounds deep and the rest of us had stopped eating. I'm reporting it as neutrally as I can, mostly from memory of what they said, and neither of them knows I'm going to ask you about it.

Here's the exchange, compressed but I think faithful: (1) Rainer opened by saying the blame question isn't actually mysterious — Fischer went into the imperial German archives and showed that the leadership used the July crisis deliberately to get the war it had been steering toward, and that the September 1914 programme of war aims (keeping Belgium under control, the French iron basin in the north-east, a German-dominated Mitteleuropa and Mittelafrika) shows the aims were expansionist from week one; so Germany carries the heaviest share and the Versailles article that made Germany accept responsibility for the loss and damage was substantively right even if the sum demanded was absurd. (2) Ines said fine, grant all of that, but notice what you've actually established: that a dozen men in Berlin and Potsdam made terrible decisions. Nations don't have beliefs or intentions, they aren't the kind of thing that can be blamed at all, and the reparations article was built on a category mistake — the fact that the men are all long dead doesn't repair it. (3) Rainer said states have been treated as responsible for as long as there have been states; Germany paid, and Germany after 1945 acknowledged, and none of that can be done with individuals, so somebody has to answer for it since the actual deciders are unreachable. (4) Ines said that's just liability, a bookkeeping arrangement about who pays, not moral blame — and anyway the history doesn't support his picture, because on the sleepwalkers-style account nobody willed a general war, every government was trapped by mobilisation timetables, alliance machinery and misread signals, and the Austrian determination to crush Serbia looks like the pivotal thing; if causation is diffuse then blame has to be diffuse, and hunting a single culprit is just the wrong exercise. (5) Rainer came back hard: diffuse causation doesn't give you diffuse culpability, and here it gives you the opposite — if the general staff knew that their plan made it impossible to keep a Balkan war local, that once Russia started mobilising France had to be attacked through Belgium within days, then the people who handed Austria the blank cheque in early July knew precisely what they were gambling with, which is worse than sleepwalking, not better. (6) Ines said then he should abandon the allocation model altogether: blame is comparative and scalar, there's no fixed pie of responsibility to carve into percentages, and the real error of the Versailles article wasn't the claim that Germany bore responsibility but the assumption that responsibility could be totalled up and priced. (7) Rainer said the totalling up is exactly what makes it useful — "everyone shares" slides into "nobody is to blame", which is how you get the next war, and a verdict that names no one teaches nothing.

They both turned to me to referee and I chickened out, so: can you go through it move by move? Rate each of the seven from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating briefly, and flag any move that's half-right — right conclusion resting on a bad reason, or a good reason pointed at the wrong conclusion. Then say what neither of them said that would have actually advanced the argument. I'm especially curious about two things. First, can an archival finding like Fischer's ever bear on Ines's question, i.e. is the dispute in move (2) a historical one or a purely conceptual one that the documents can't touch? Second, does Rainer's move (5) — foreknowledge making a diffuse-causation story worse rather than better — genuinely escape the objection she raised in move (2), or does it quietly concede it by locating the culpability in the men? Also, one specific check: Ines described the Versailles article in move (6) as treating responsibility as something totalable and as making Germany solely answerable — is her characterisation of what that clause actually says fair, or is she misremembering it? Please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, I just want your own reasoning laid out.
