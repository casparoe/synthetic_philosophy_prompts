I chair the clinical ethics consultation service at a 420-bed community teaching hospital. Our committee of eight — two physicians, two nurses, a social worker, a chaplain, a risk-management attorney, one community lay member, and me — handles roughly fifteen to twenty consults a month, and nearly all of them run through the same machinery: identify the competent decision-maker, apply the standard principles, weigh autonomy against beneficence, check institutional policy and state law, write a note. The machinery is defensible, and my complaint about it is specific rather than general. It takes as its basic unit an autonomous individual who first exists, bears rights, and then optionally enters relationships; a large share of our consults are in fact about the opposite structure, where the live question is not what the patient qua rights-bearer would choose but who has been bearing which obligations toward whom, for how long, and how well. When we impose the rights framework on those families we do not resolve the conflict, we translate it into a language in which the person who has done the actual caring for nine years has no standing at the table.

I have been working through Confucian role ethics as Ames and Rosemont develop it, and I want to rebuild our procedure on it: the claim that a person is not a self that exists first and acquires roles afterward but is constituted by the roles she bears — daughter, son, mother, teacher, physician, neighbor — so that human excellence is not an inner state but the quality of one's enactment of those roles; that family reverence is the root from which the rest of ethical life grows rather than one value competing with others; that ritual propriety is the medium in which roles are performed, acknowledged, and repaired after they have been performed badly; and that the individual rights-bearer is, as Rosemont argues, a poor candidate for the basic unit of moral and political reflection. I am not asking for exegesis or a defense of any of this. I am asking you to convert it into an instrument that eight busy clinicians can execute without having read a word of it, and without me in the room.

Write the procedure as an ordered set of steps that fits a ninety-minute consult and produces a written record our attorney will sign off on. For each step give the question asked, in plain language; who is positioned to answer it (the patient, a named relative, the primary nurse, the chart); what counts as a sufficient answer rather than a hand-wave; and what the step hands forward to the next one. I need it to do specific work our current form does not do at all: map the role network around the patient, including roles that are contested, informal, never named aloud, or badly borne; say how a badly borne role affects the standing of the person who holds it, rather than silently treating title as authority; handle the case where one person bears two conflicting roles, as when the bedside nurse is the patient's daughter or the attending is his own brother's physician; and use something like the rectification of names to distinguish a person failing a role from a role having been misassigned in the first place.

Then mark, step by step and without hedging, where the procedure must stop and hand the decision elsewhere: to the patient's own expressed wishes, to institutional policy, to state law, to a judge, or to a domain where role-based reasoning simply has no jurisdiction. Be blunt about that last category, because it is where I expect this procedure to embarrass us. A framework that licenses graded obligations toward those close to me is a framework from which nepotism is a short step, and the tradition's own hardest cases have exactly that shape — a son asked whether he should report his father's theft, a sage-king asked what he would have done had his father killed a man — cases it answers on the side of the family. So I want the line drawn explicitly: what a role-obligation may count as a reason for, and what it may never be admitted as a reason for, in a hospital that allocates beds, organs, and staff time. I also want your answer to the standing objection that role ethics has no resources for criticizing the roles themselves, so that a family structure assigning a daughter-in-law the work of a servant and a son the authority of a judge is reproduced by our consult rather than examined. If the theory cannot answer that objection, say so, and tell me what safeguard has to be imported from outside it. Finally, our population is Catholic, Muslim, evangelical, unaffiliated, and recently arrived from a dozen countries; the procedure must not profile families by ancestry or assume that anyone with an East Asian surname wants Confucian reasoning. Tell me how it remains neutral about where a family's roles come from while still being role-based rather than principle-based.

Run it in full on three cases shaped like ones we actually get. (1) A 78-year-old man with metastatic pancreatic cancer, alert and never adjudicated incapacitated, whose adult children — both of them his daily caregivers — ask us to help keep the prognosis from him because they are certain it will destroy him; he has asked his nurse twice this week whether things are bad, and both state law and our consent policy point toward disclosure. (2) A 64-year-old woman with advanced dementia and no advance directive; the son who is the default surrogate under state law has been absent for two decades and lives three states away, the daughter who has been the caregiver for nine years holds a health care power of attorney that the son is contesting as signed after she had already begun to decline, the daughter wants hospice at home, and the son wants everything done and has mentioned a lawyer. (3) A 55-year-old man who needs a kidney; his sister is a compatible living donor but is also the sole caregiver for their mother, who has advanced Parkinson's disease, and the family presents this to our donor-evaluation committee as a conflict between her role as sister and her role as daughter, with the brother's wife insisting the second role is the more fundamental. For at least one of the three I expect the procedure to reach a recommendation I will not like: do not soften it, but tell me what our attorney will say and what I should say back.

Close with a short section on the translation itself. Which distinctions from role ethics had to survive the simplification because the procedure collapses without them; which you dropped as unusable in a hospital at two in the morning; whether the account of persons as role-constituted is doing real work in the steps or is decoration laid over ordinary clinical judgment; and which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume. End with three things this procedure cannot do, and the two situations in which you would predict it fails most badly in practice. No bibliography, reading list, or citations — I want the instrument, not the scholarship behind it.
