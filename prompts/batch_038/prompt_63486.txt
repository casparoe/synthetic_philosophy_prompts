My policy reading group has been sitting on this for two weeks and I can't find the lose thread. Start with Sen's liberal paradox, then watch what my generalization does to it.

Framework: states are full descriptions of who does what; society has a complete, transitive ranking over all of them; each person's preferences are an arbitrary complete transitive ranking (unrestricted domain). Two conditions. Pareto: if literally everybody prefers a to b, society ranks a above b. Decisiveness over a pair — what Sen treats as a right: there's some person i and some pair such that however i ranks that pair, society ranks it the same way, no matter what anybody else thinks.

The construction Sen uses: A is decisive over (x, y), B is decisive over (w, z). Take a profile where A prefers x to y and B prefers w to z, and where everybody — A and B included — prefers y to w and z to x. (A's own ranking is z ≻ x ≻ y ≻ w and B's is y ≻ w ≻ z ≻ x, so neither of them is individually incoherent.) But society is now forced into x ≻ y ≻ w ≻ z ≻ x. A cycle. Same shape with only three states in his book example, where one reader is decisive over "I read it / nobody reads it," the other over "you read it / nobody reads it," and each would rather the other did it.

Now the part I built:

(1) If something is a right, it binds against unanimous opposition. Anything society can override as soon as everyone else prefers otherwise isn't a right, it's a permission revocable by taste. So demanding decisiveness-over-a-pair isn't asking much of a right — it's asking the bare minimum.
(2) Pareto is also the bare minimum anyone could ask of efficiency, since it only fires on unanimity.
(3) Notice what the construction never mentions: what the protected pairs are about, or how big or important the spheres are. All it uses is that two people each get a protected pair and that preferences can be crosswise-meddlesome.
(4) So the content of the right is irrelevant. Desk layout, what you read, what you inject, whether you wear headphones at your desk: assign any two protected pairs to any two people and there's a profile on which society's ranking is incoherent.
(5) And it doesn't need two people. Give one person two protected pairs, keep everybody preferring y to w and z to x, and you get the same cycle. So "at least two individuals" in Sen's minimal condition is decoration — a single right-holder already breaks social rationality.
(6) The standard repairs don't work either. Restricting the domain to non-meddlesome preferences assumes what's in question, that nobody cares what anyone else does inside their own sphere — but caring about that is a lot of what morality, disgust, envy and solidarity consist of. Dropping Pareto leaves you with no notion of efficiency at all. Reformulating rights as procedures, or as constraints on feasible sets rather than as inputs to a social ranking, just relocates the problem: whatever the procedure picks is a state, and states can be Pareto-compared against states it didn't pick.
(7) Conclusion: there's no such thing as an inalienable right. Every right, however trivial, is a standing possibility of incoherent social ranking, so every right has to be trade-off-able at the margin, all the way down. Rights talk is utilitarianism in a rhetorical wrapper.

I want (7) to be false and I can't see which step to pull. If I had to rank them, I'd keep (2) and (3) over everything else.
