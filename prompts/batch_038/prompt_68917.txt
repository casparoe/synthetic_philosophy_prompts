I'm 71 and retired four years ago after 34 years in a materials lab, mostly fatigue testing of alloys, which is not the kind of background that lands you on a jail building commission, and yet. Last spring the county commissioners appointed me to the citizens' advisory commission for the new detention facility, and I've been asked to write the opinion that goes into Thursday's packet, which is why I'm bothering you with this instead of just voting.

The setup. The county is replacing a 1970s jail, 380 beds, one shot at it, around $140M. Two schemes are on the table. Scheme A is conventional podular direct supervision: dayroom in the middle, officer in a hardened booth with sightlines, bunks, no cooking, everything either bolted down or stainless. Scheme B is what the architect keeps calling the "normality" scheme, after the Norwegian places he showed us slides of — Halden mostly, which I believe opened around 2010 and is maximum security but has no bars, no razor wire, no towers, and lets residents carry their own room keys. In B, each resident gets a single lockable room with a real door and a window you can actually see out of, twelve rooms share a kitchen and a table, staff wear ordinary clothes and eat with people, and there's a step-down floor you earn your way onto. B comes in about 11 percent dearer to build, and the architect swears it's cheaper to run because of fewer use-of-force incidents and staff not quitting.

Here's the wrinkle nobody says out loud in our meetings. Roughly three in five of our beds will be pretrial people and our median stay is something like 34 days, whereas the Norwegian places were designed around years-long sentences. So the usual case for B — it prepares people for release, and the smaller the difference between inside and outside the easier the transition — does a lot less work here, since most of these people aren't being prepared for anything, they're waiting, and most will be out before you could teach them a trade. But I also can't see how you'd defend Scheme A conditions for people who haven't been convicted of anything: you can't call it punishment, and if it isn't punishment then every hardship past what's strictly needed to hold them is something we're doing to them for no stated reason. One of my colleagues says we should treat the building as two institutions under one roof with two seperate justifications, which sounds tidy and I suspect is unbuildable; if you think it's genuinely workable, tell me how, because I'd rather be convinced than clever.

Against B we have the deputies' association, who say the room keys and the blind corners in the residential wings are a staff-safety problem and that "dynamic security" — their term, meaning talking to people instead of counting them — requires years of training and a staffing level our budget doesn't have; the prosecutor, who says a facility that looks like a dorm becomes indefensible the first time a released resident does something awful, and that the public expects the building itself to express disapproval; and a victims' group who made that last point more quietly and, I thought, more forcefully.

So please write me the memo, about two pages, that I can hand over with a straight face. I want an actual bottom line — approve B, approve B with conditions, or send it back for a third scheme — and the reasoning that gets there. If you recommend conditions, number them and make them specific enough that the architect could price them: which features get deferred, which are reversible later, what we'd measure during the first three years of operation and who would measure it. Give the deputies and the prosecutor their strongest case in their own terms rather than a strawman, and tell me whether their objection is really about safety or about expression, because those seem to need different answers. And be honest about the evidence: if the case for B ultimately leans on before-and-after comparisons drawn from countries with different sentencing systems, different welfare states and a different selection of who goes to prison at all, say so, and then tell me how much weight a commission should put on it anyway when it has to decide something in March. No reading lists and no citations, please — I couldn't check them and neither could most of my colleagues; where you lean on a number just flag roughly how solid it is.
