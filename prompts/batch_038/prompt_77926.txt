I am a doctoral student in functional morphology. The structure I work on is the bony chin of the human mandible, which my group treats not as an adaptation in its own right but as a candidate byproduct of the reduction of the rest of the face. In defending that reading, my supervisor leans constantly on the architectural spandrel, following Gould and Lewontin's 1979 critique of the adaptationist programme. The analogy, as he deploys it, runs like this: in a domed building, the roughly triangular surfaces between the tops of the arches and the base of the dome exist because the builder chose a dome on arches, not because anyone wanted those surfaces; how many of them there are and what their three-dimensional shape must be are settled by the geometry of the rest of the design, and the fact that painters later decorated them does not retroactively make them part of the original plan. Many organic features, on this account, stand in exactly that relation to the features they accompany: forced into existence by the form of something else, and only afterwards recruited for a use. In my department the word spandrel has hardened into a term of art for precisely that — a byproduct later co-opted — and my supervisor uses it to license a definite inference: that the present usefulness of a trait is no evidence at all that the trait arose for that use. He thinks the analogy carries a substantial part of the weight of that inference.

A philosopher on my committee is sceptical, and her objection has two prongs. First, she says, architecture has an architect, a plan, and a geometry fixed in advance; the necessity that makes a spandrel a spandrel is a theorem of the design, and the later decoration is itself an intentional act by an agent who wanted it. Evolution has no architect, no plan, and no fixed geometry, and nothing guarantees that any given trait is a forced consequence of another. What my group calls a byproduct is usually a statistical regularity — a feature that tracks another along an allometric trajectory — rather than a deductive consequence, and she doubts that an analogy drawn from the deductive case can underwrite claims about the statistical one. Second, she notes that critics of the original paper have pressed the source domain itself: the surfaces at San Marco are, strictly, pendentives rather than spandrels, and even pendentives were not unavoidable, since corbelling or squinching would have carried the dome as well, so the builders arguably chose the form they chose partly for how it looked. If the exemplar case is not a clean instance of undesignated necessity, she asks, what is left of the analogy's authority?

Please audit the analogy for me, addressing each of the following:

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly — ideally as a table: what in a domed building plays the role of the organism, of the trait under selection, of the byproduct, of the developmental or physical constraint, of the later decoration, and of the builder.

2. Identify the disanalogies and sort them: which ones actually bear on the inference my supervisor wants to license, and which are harmless because they concern features of the source that no part of the argument depends on. Be specific about why each matters or does not.

3. State what the analogy quietly imports from architecture into biology — in particular anything about necessity, about there being a design against which a leftover space is defined, and about the intentionality of the later decoration — and whether those imports do covert work in the inference.

4. Distinguish two uses of the analogy that I suspect my supervisor runs together: using it to block an inference from current utility to adaptive origin, versus using it as positive evidence for a byproduct account. Does the analogy support the first, the second, or neither, and what independent evidence would be needed for the second in a case like mine?

5. My supervisor sometimes contrasts the spandrel picture with the adaptationist picture of the biologist as reverse-engineering a design. Are these genuine rivals, or are they analogies about different things — one about the causal history of form, the other about an investigator's method — such that treating them as competitors is itself a confusion?

6. Deliver a verdict on whether my supervisor's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, in whatever qualified form it can survive. If the architectural spandrel is the wrong vehicle for the idea, propose a better one and say what it does and does not license. And answer a question I cannot settle on my own: if the analogy is weak as an argument, how should I understand the fact that it succeeded so completely as a label — can a term outlive the reasoning that introduced it, and does the term's survival tell us anything about the strength of the concept it names?
