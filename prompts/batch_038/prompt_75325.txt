Last month I was appointed as the outside academic on my city museum's curatorial committee, and on Tuesday we spent three hours on a title without getting anywhere. The exhibition — material from Britain and northern Gaul, fourth to seventh centuries — opens in fourteen months, but the title and interpretive framework must be locked in six weeks, because the wall text goes to design. The director and the patron whose gift funded the gallery want "The Fall of Rome." Our senior curator objects that half the empire lasted another thousand years, that much of the regional record shows continuity rather than rupture, and that "fall" quietly reinstates a Gibbon-style moral verdict nobody on the staff actually holds. Our archaeologist replies that "transformation" is politeness wearing a period label: the same record shows coin use and mass-produced pottery disappearing, stone and tile building giving way to timber, livestock measurably shrinking, cities losing most of their population — and that declining to say collapse is itself a normative choice, just a less honest one.

I have to circulate a memo before the next meeting. Please draft it. It should recommend a title and a labeling policy, and state what the committee's disagreement really turns on — the evidence, or the work the word "fall" is being asked to do. I also need criteria a label writer can actually apply for where collapse language is licensed and where it is not, including how to handle the causal menu (the mid-sixth-century cold snap, the plague of the 540s, frontier migration, fiscal strain, and the lead hypothesis that survives mainly in popular books), the strongest case for whichever position you reject, and a bottom line we can vote on Thursday.
