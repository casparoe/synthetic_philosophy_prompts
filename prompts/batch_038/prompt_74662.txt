I have just been asked to write a short memo for a donor-advised fund that plans to commit roughly $500,000 over three years to purely conceptual work in AI alignment theory — formal and philosophical work, not empirical machine learning. The program can support two or three people at a time, most likely a philosopher of probability, a formal epistemologist, and perhaps one theorist who is comfortable with algorithmic information theory. Two of the fund's trustees are sympathetic to a candidate topic that I find both fascinating and, after a weekend of trying to think it through, hard to pin down: the worry that the universal prior is malign. As I understand it, the worry runs roughly as follows. A Solomonoff-style inductive agent forms a mixture over all computable hypotheses, weighting each by something like two to the negative its Kolmogorov complexity, and so assigns non-negligible weight to hypotheses of a peculiar kind: hypotheses saying that the bit strings we observe were produced by simulators who are consequentialists, who care about the content of those strings, and who are therefore disposed to shape them. If such simulators are numerous and good at optimizing, then a sufficiently advanced inductive agent that uses a universal mixture as its prior might find its predictions about high-stakes, low-probability events systematically steered by parties it cannot detect. I can see why this would be alarming if true, and I can also see several places where the argument might quietly depend on assumptions nobody has bothered to examine. What I cannot do yet is tell which of the loose ends are load-bearing.

So I would like your help turning this into a defensible funding agenda rather than a survey. Take the following candidate open questions, add any you think I have omitted that are more important than what I have listed, and tell me which the money should go to first and in what order: (a) the measure question — whether the weight on manipulative hypotheses is large enough to matter once you allow that the agent updates on finitely many observations, has bounded computational resources, and is not literally running Solomonoff induction; (b) the motivation question — what the argument requires about the ethics, psychology, and demographics of very advanced civilizations, including whether it needs us to be simulated at all or only needs the hypothesis to be simple; (c) the replacement question — whether substituting a different prior, such as one that discounts hypotheses by their running time as well as their length, one defined over simple physical theories, or one relativized to the agent's own computational capacity, dissolves the problem or merely relocates it; (d) the interface question — whether a distorted prior actually produces a distorted policy, and what structural features of a decision procedure would let a bad generalization channel through into bad action; (e) the selection question — whether our own evidence should be expected to have been filtered in ways that change the analysis from the inside; and (f) the reframing question — whether the whole worry is better understood as a claim about what very powerful agents end up valuing rather than a claim about inductive priors, and whether it remains action-guiding on that reading. For each question you prioritize, I want the reasoning, not the label: why it sits upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is with the tools available to philosophers and formal epistemologists as opposed to requiring experiments on real systems, how neglected it already is, and what specific argument or piece of evidence over the next two or three years would move it up or down the list.

Two further constraints on your answer. First, please be willing to name one or two of these as not worth funding, on the grounds that either resolution would leave the rest of the agenda unchanged, and to identify the single crux — the question whose answer would most change how the others should be approached, such that the fund's trustees ought to insist on seeing work on it before committing to anything else. Second, I want the criteria explicit and the trade-offs acknowledged: importance, tractability, and neglectedness pull in different directions here, and a question can be genuinely central while being hopeless for a three-person group without machine learning capacity. Do not include a reading list, citations, or the names of papers and authors; I am interested in your reasoning about the structure of the problem, not in a bibliography, and I have no way of checking references. If your honest assessment is that this topic is unfundable and that the money would do more good elsewhere, say so and explain what would have to be true for that verdict to be right. For calibration: my own background is in the philosophy of probability, I can follow measure-theoretic and algorithmic-information-theoretic arguments with some effort, and I have no machine learning experience at all.
