I'm writing the final paper for a graduate decision theory seminar (twelve students, three-hour closed book exam, one side of A4 permitted as notes), and I need one essay question worth 30 of the 100 marks, to be answered in roughly 50 minutes. My students have had one lecture on the St. Petersburg paradox and one on Hájek and Nover's Pasadena game, and last year's version of this question — "state the Pasadena paradox and discuss two responses to it" — was, to my embarrassment, answerable almost entirely from memory of the lecture slides. I want a question that can only be answered by someone who has actually thought about where the trouble comes from. So please build the question around material I'll supply in the exam itself, and stipulate all the mathematics so that no outside reading is required: the Pasadena game, in which a fair coin is tossed until heads first appears at toss n and the payoff is (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n / n utils, so that the expectation series has terms (-1)^(n-1)/n and is conditionally convergent; the Riemann rearrangement theorem; the measure-theoretic convention that the expectation of a real-valued random variable is defined only when E|X| is finite, so that a conditionally convergent series does not yield a well-defined expectation at all; and my own variant, which I'll call the Valparaíso game and which I invented for exactly this purpose. In the Valparaíso game the payoffs are those of the Pasadena except that whenever n is a power of two the payoff is +2^n utils instead; the expectation series then contains infinitely many terms equal to +1 (since 2^-n · 2^n = 1), its positive part diverges while its negative part converges to -(1/2)ln 2, and so its value is +∞ unconditionally — invariant under any rearrangement.

The question should make the student do something with that pair rather than describe it. Specifically, I want it to force three moves. First, a diagnosis: is the Pasadena's trouble a defect in the formalism (expectation is simply undefined, and a principle that tells you to maximize an undefined quantity is silent rather than contradictory) or a substantive normative failure, and what turns on which? Second, an assessment of the escapes — in particular, whether the Bernoulli move of passing to a bounded utility function is even available here, given that the payoffs are stipulated in utils and given that any bound M makes the series absolutely convergent by comparison with M·2^-n, and whether a comparative or relative treatment of expected value (ranking gambles by the value of their difference rather than assigning each a value) can rank the Pasadena against the Valparaíso when the difference of two conditionally convergent series is itself at issue. Third, a judgment about fragility: the Pasadena sits a hair's breadth from a game whose value is determinate and infinite, and from games obtained by any permutation of its own payoffs; does the reply "such cases are knife-edge, and a theory need not be robust on a negligible set of gambles" dissolve the paradox, concede it, or change its subject? Please have the question ask the student to state and defend three desiderata that any repaired principle must satisfy — rearrangement invariance, agreement with ordinary expected utility on every bounded-utility gamble, and a determinate ranking of the Pasadena against the Valparaíso — and to say which of their own candidate repairs meets all three, rather than to list repairs from the literature.
