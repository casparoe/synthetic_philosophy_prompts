Been reading about well-being for fun lately — mostly the people who argue that what's good for you is pleasure, full stop — and I keep running into the same argument shape, in book after book. I want to write something short about it and I don't want to invent a term for something that already has one, so: does this have a name?

Here's the shape. The defender opens with a list. Cold water when you're parched. The smell of coffee. A joke landing. Sex. The relief of scratching an itch. Nailing the last move of a chess puzzle you'd been stuck on for an hour. Being told you did well. One particular passage of a cello suite. Then the objector says: these have nothing in common *as feelings* — the chess one and the water one don't feel even faintly alike — so "pleasure" isn't picking out a single kind of thing, and the thesis that pleasure is the good is really a thesis about a grab bag. The defender offers a property that's supposed to run through all of them: they're sensations you want to keep going. Counterexamples arrive immediately, from both directions. The masochist who wants his pain to keep going, or someone who wants to hold onto an itch-scratch they'd never call pleasant, make the property too wide. And then people say the pleasure of remembering your grandmother, or of finally seeing why a proof works, isn't a sensation at all, which makes it too narrow and drops cases the original list was clearly meant to include. Two or three rounds of that and every candidate property is either too wide or too narrow, and I've never once seen a round end with someone conceding.

The move I actually want a name for is what happens next. The defender gives up on the shared property and says: pleasures hang together the way games do — a web of overlapping similarities, nothing running through the whole set — and announces the objection is dissolved, since nobody ever promised a common feature. But the position started by claiming that pleasure is *one* thing and that it's what makes a life go well. Once the list is just resemblences, I can't see what's left of that claim, and it looks like the view has bought its survival by giving up the part that made it a view.

So, three things:

1. Is there an established term for the overall list-versus-property pattern? I have a guess about what it's called, but I want to know what it actually covers and where it's usually taken to bite hardest.

2. Is that final move its own recognized thing? To me it isn't no-true-Scotsman — nobody's redefining pleasure to exclude the counterexamples. And it isn't motte-and-bailey either, because the weaker claim doesn't get swapped back for the stronger one once the pressure is off; the stronger claim just dies. It's more like the theory immunizes itself by shedding content. If there's a concept from philosophy of science or from the Wittgenstein literature that's the right neighbor, tell me how close the fit really is and where it strains.

3. If nothing established covers it, coin something: definition, plus one nearby case that should *not* count as an instance, so I can see the edges rather than just the label.

Please don't just hand me a name — I want the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and how it relates to the nearest existing concepts. But whatever you conclude, put the single best term for move 2 in the very last line, machine-readable, like this:

\answer{...}

— using the established term if there is one, or your coinage if you end up supplying one.
