I am drafting a chapter comparing three answers to the question of what a government may know about the people it governs: the German constitutional tradition that grew out of the 1983 census ruling, which treats a person's authority over the disclosure and use of their own data as a basic right, limitable only by an overriding public interest; the American pattern of sector-specific statutes and general administrative openness, where the default is that records may be assembled unless something particular forbids it; and the Chinese social credit apparatus, which in its actual blacklisting form rather than the single numerical score it is usually caricatured as pools court, banking, and administrative records into an assessment of a subject's trustworthiness. A colleague offered me an argument he thinks settles the comparison in one stroke. I have come to distrust it, but I cannot yet say where it breaks. Here is my reconstruction; call the package of the first two steps the derivation constraint.

(1) A government may hold a fact about a person only if that person could in principle have authorized the holding of that particular fact.

(2) Authorization attaches to facts, not to operations. Authorizing the storage of record A and the storage of record B is not authorizing the storage of whatever follows from A together with B. This is what makes the familiar incidental-finding cases come out right: someone who consents to a blood draw to establish intoxication has not consented to the discovery of a pregnancy, however legitimate the draw was.

(3) Modern administration proceeds by joining. That the person named in the land register is the same person named in the outpatient psychiatric log is stated in neither register; it exists only in the join. So too the fact that a declared income, an address, a school district, and a set of vehicle records all belong to one household.

(4) By (2) and (3), every joined fact is a fact nobody authorized, and by (1) every government that links its own records to one another holds facts impermissibly.

(5) Linking is not optional for a government but constitutive of what it does: a tax authority cannot assess a liability without joining persons to incomes, and an investigating agency cannot identify a suspect without joining records to records.

(6) So no government capable of collecting a tax or investigating a crime satisfies the derivation constraint.

(7) Yet the tradition that generated (1) plainly permits both activities, and my colleague agrees that a government may permissibly know at least that much about its citizens.

(8) Therefore (1) is false, and the constraint meant to discipline pooled trustworthiness scoring condemns the income tax first and hardest.

Two features leave me stuck rather than instructed. The first is that the argument travels: substitute any knower for the government in (1), and it appears to show that I may not infer from what two friends separately told me that they share an acquaintance, since neither authorized the inference. That is surely too strong, but I cannot tell whether it is too strong for the same reason (6) is, which matters if the diagnosis is supposed to explain both. The second is that the argument nowhere separates the census from the trustworthiness assessment; both are swept up by (4) on identical grounds, and the discrimination I wanted the constraint to deliver has simply disappeared.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would rather keep (1) than (7), since giving up (1) looks like conceding that the only coherent position is the American one, and I do not believe that is true. But I want the price stated, not assumed away. Specifically: why did the step you reject look acceptable going in, and what does (1) have to become in order to survive (2) and (3) — must authorization be treated as closure-bearing, so that consenting to a purpose carries with it the facts needed to serve that purpose; must the unit of analysis shift from facts to contexts or to risks of harm; or must (1) be demoted from a constraint to a prima facie claim that public interest can outweigh? Then tell me what any repair you favor still owes me on the comparative question: what extra resource it needs in order to say something adverse about pooled trustworthiness scoring that it does not also have to say about a tax register, and whether that resource is available without collapsing back into a plain balancing test that the derivation constraint was introduced to avoid.

Please indicate how confident you are in the diagnosis you settle on, and single out the point in your own answer that you are least sure of.
