I run a small blog at the intersection of music cognition and philosophy, and I have spent the better part of a decade on the rationalist forums watching two camps talk past each other about why music moves us: the deflationary one, which cashes everything out in expectation violation, entrainment, and predictive error, and the metaphysical one, which is still running on Schopenhauer. Before I write another word in that argument, I want the specification both sides should be judged against. So: write the desiderata — what any theory of music's content and its affective power must deliver in order to count as adequate.

Anchor the list in the claims Schopenhauer actually makes in the third book of *The World as Will and Representation*, which I will summarize rather than quote: music is not, like the other arts, a copy of the Ideas but a copy of the will itself, and could in a sense exist even if the world did not; the deep bass, moving only in large intervals, stands for the crudest unorganized mass of nature, the complemental parts between bass and melody for the lower grades of the will's objectification up through plants and animals, and the freely developing leading voice for the intellectual life and striving of a human being; a melody's constant departures from and returns to the tonic figure the will's striving, satisfaction, and renewed striving, so that slow melodies striking painful discords and winding back through many bars are sad precisely as delayed and hardly won satisfaction is; and because music gives only the quintessence of events and never the events themselves, one and the same music can serve as the most accurate commentary on any scene whatever.

For each desideratum, give me three things: the case or phenomenon that motivates it; whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or is a partisan commitment of one camp dressed as a constraint; and whether an expectation-based naturalistic account and a Schopenhauerian metaphysical account are equally exposed to it. The following must appear somewhere on the list: why we seek out music that figures thwarted and postponed satisfaction rather than satisfied desire; why repeated listening produces no habituation; the listener who is entirely unmoved; program versus absolute music, and the charge that music bending itself to its words is trying to speak a language that is not its own, with Rossini held up as the composer who never made that mistake; and at least one case where the register hierarchy has nothing to grip — a sustained drone, a piece for percussion alone, a work with no leading voice at all.

Then tell me which single desideratum eliminates the most otherwise attractive theories, and whether some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable. My own suspicion is that music's claimed independence of the phenomenal world cannot be reconciled with its capacity to disclose the inner meaning of any particular scene; adjudicate that, or find me a better candidate for an incoherent pair, and mark anything on the list you think is merely my taste. A table is fine. Keep the scorecard to a line or two per camp at the end — the list is the deliverable — and no bibliography or further reading, since I am after the reasoning and not the references.
