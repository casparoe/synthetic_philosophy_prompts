I'm the entire compliance function at a mid-size packaged food company — granola bars, cold-pressed juices, frozen bowls, about $180M a year — and everything that goes out the door with a claim on it eventually lands on my desk: front-panel copy, packaging artwork, ad scripts, influencer briefs, sales decks, and the answers our reps give to retailer questionnaires. It's maybe twenty to forty items a week, most of them trivial, but three or four a week turn into an actual argument with marketing, and I have roughly fifteen minutes per item and no budget to run things past outside counsel unless it's a real problem. My current procedure is basically "is every word literally true, and would a regulator hate it," and it broke last month. We put "no added sugar" on the front of a kids' juice drink that's sweetened with white grape juice concentrate, a consumer group complained, a plaintiff firm sent a letter, and my written answer — every word is literally true, the ingredient panel discloses everything — did not satisfy our GC at all. She said the literal-truth point is beside the point legally, and maybe not even the point ethically, and she's asked me for a real written procedure before the next label cycle. Here's the mess I'm in: I've read enough to know there are two different frameworks that use the same vocabulary. There's the philosophy side — whether lying requires an intention to deceive, whether you can lie at all without saying anything false, what's said versus what's merely implicated — and there's the legal side, where the FTC's deception policy statement (the 1983 one, appended to that Cliffdale case I think) makes the test the overall or net impression a claim leaves on a reasonable consumer, materiality, and omissions, and explicitly says literal truth doesn't save you. I need the procedure to keep those two tracks visibly separate, because half our internal fights are people citing one standard when the other is the relevant one.

So: please write the procedure my team could actually run. Concretely I want an ordered set of questions with a pass/fail criterion for each, and what counts as sufficient evidence at each step (lab data, the ingredient panel, a consumer perception survey we obviously can't afford, someone's sworn recollection of what they meant). I want it to output two separate verdicts — legal exposure, and whether we've lied as opposed to merely misled as opposed to done nothing wrong — plus an explicit list of the situations where those two verdicts come apart and what we do about that divergence, because that's where I keep getting steamrolled. I want the escalation triggers spelled out: the kinds of item where the procedure stops and goes to me, and the kinds where it goes to the GC because I'm not qualified. I want every step that is really a judgment call dressed up as a procedure to be flagged as such rather than given a fake threshold. And I want you to tell me which distinctions from the lying/misleading literature actually have to survive the simplification into a one-pager for marketing people with no philosophy and no legal training, which ones I can safely drop for a food company, and why — plus an honest section on where the procedure will misfire, both false positives that will make me the most annoying person in the building and false negatives that will get us sued.

Then run it on four things we've genuinely been arguing about. (1) The "no added sugar" kids' drink sweetened with grape juice concentrate, where our position is that concentrate is fruit, not sugar. (2) A snack bar whose front panel says "0 g trans fat" next to a little heart illustration, which is exactly what the labeling rule requires us to write because the per-serving amount is under the 0.5 g rounding threshold. (3) A totally clean one, so I can see the procedure not crying wolf: a bar claiming "10 g protein" that meets the nutrient-content threshold, is true, and has no health halo around it. And (4) the one that's going to get me in trouble — an email our VP of supply chain sent answering a big retailer' sustainability questionnaire, saying our wrapper film is "made from recycled material and fully recyclable," which is true of the resin and false in practice because it's a multilayer laminate that no municipal program in the country accepts; the VP knew that, and also knew the retailer was under pressure to hit its own recyclability numbers. Please give the fourth one the same treatment as the others even though the answer will not be welcome upstairs.

One more thing about how confident I want you to be. The legal track is more or less settled and I'd like you to write it that way. The philosophy track is not: whether lying needs an intention to deceive, whether you need to believe the thing is false, whether implicature can ever amount to lying, and how much moral work the said/implicated line does — those are live disagreements among people who know this material, and I don't want the procedure quietly built on one side of them and then handed to me as if it were neutral. Where a step depends on taking a contested position, mark it, say which side it assumes, and say what would change operationally if the other side turned out right. And on one question I'd rather you just commit than hedge: once the net-impression test has been run and failed, does "but it was literally true" deserve any independent weight at all — in the legal verdict, in the blameworthiness verdict, or in what we do next (correct it, disclose more, discipline someone, nothing)? Pick a side and defend it briefly, because that's the exact question our GC asked me and I still don't have an answer I'd want to say out loud.
