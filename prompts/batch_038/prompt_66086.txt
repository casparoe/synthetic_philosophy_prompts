I'm a third-year psych PhD student working on emotion, and I've been reading enough philosophy of the stuff that my own intuitions have got tangled. I want to work out what principle, if any, sits behind the verdicts I keep giving in seminar, because I'm fairly sure they're inconsistent. Case by case:

1. The suspension bridge one. Man crosses a rickety bridge, is still shaky afterwards, meets a woman on the other side, feels the flutter and takes himself to be attracted to her. I want to say the attraction is real — he's genuinely attracted, not just misfiring.

2. But the adrenaline one. Guy gets injected with epinephrine, told it's a vitamin, is left in a room with a confederate who acts furious about the questionnaire. Reports feeling angry, can't tell you at what. I want to say that is *not* anger, it's arousal wearing a borrowed label. Same structure as 1 — arousal plus a situational construal — opposite verdict. My excuse is that in 1 the feeling is about *her* and it survives: he wants her number, he's still worked up about it a week later. In 2 it evaporates the moment you explain the injection. Whether that excuse amounts to a principle rather than a shrug is exactly what I want to know.

3. Recalcitrant fear. I'm scared of the neighbour's labrador. I know perfectly well it's harmless, I'd bet money on it, my knees still go when it comes at the fence. Real fear, irrational — but not irrational the way contradictory beliefs are, because I don't think I have two beliefs fighting.

4. Snake behind thick glass at the zoo. Heart pounding, backing away, judging that I'm completely safe. Real fear, and *fitting* — the thing genuinely is dangerous — whereas in 3 the fear is real but unfitting, because that dog isn't dangerous. So fittingness tracks the object, not my situation.

5. The flat one. Someone in a deep depressive episode who reports feeling nothing at all but correctly judges that everything is grim, and says they know they ought to be sad, they just don't feel it. I want to say they aren't sad.

6. The widow who can't cry. Numb, empty, nothing there. Also lays two places at the table, won't sit in his chair, snaps at anyone who says his name. I want to say she is grieving. Compare 5: same missing feeling, opposite verdict.

7. The colleague who isn't angry. Reports feeling entirely fine, no tension, no heat, genuinely surprised when you raise it. Is also cold and surgical in meetings and has cancelled on you twice. I want to say he's angry and that he's wrong about his own anger, which feels like a weird thing to want to say.

8. Moods. The objectless low dread you wake up with. Not an emotion on my book, but it changes which emotions you get.

My own stab, which I know is shaky: call it two-of-three. An episode is an emotion of type T only if it has at least two of (a) a T-typical evaluative construal, (b) a T-typical felt/bodily profile, (c) a T-typical pattern of dispositions to act, attend and react — with the type fixed by which ones are present. It gets 5 and 6, and I think 7. It definately gets 2 wrong, since there you have construal plus feeling and I've just said it isn't anger. I've tried patching it with a clause about the parts having to be caused by the right part, or the subject having to be able to endorse the episode on reflection, and both either go circular or throw out 3, which I really don't want to lose.

So: is there a single account — of what makes something an emotion, and of what makes it *that* emotion — that gets all eight, or nearly all? State it precisely, say which case each clause is there to motivate, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't save and why I should give that one up. Then compare how it stacks up against the obvious rivals: a judgement view, a feelings view, an emotions-are-perceptions-of-value view, and whatever hybrid you'd actually defend. Give me its verdicts on two or three cases I haven't listed — ideally ones where you think it's likely to come out badly, not ones that flatter it — and a definite answer on the case I keep going back and forth on: the woman with advanced dementia who can't remember at all that her husband died, but who cries when she's shown his photograph and is settled by it. Grief or not?

British English, please.
