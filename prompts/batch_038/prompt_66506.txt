I'm a staff engineer on our platform team, and for the last two terms I've been one of three graders for a part-time certificate course our company runs on technology and society. I'm on the panel because I can evaluate the systems examples; I have no formal philosophy training, so the parts of an answer that turn on correctly characterizing an author's argument are exactly the parts I can't grade reliably. I'd rather not mark the response below from my own reading of it.

The written assignment, as the instructor set it:

"Langdon Winner distinguishes two ways in which artifacts can have politics. In the first, the invention, design, or arrangement of a particular device becomes a way of settling an issue in the affairs of a given community. In the second, involving what he calls inherently political technologies, the claim is that certain man-made systems require, or are strongly compatible with, a particular kind of political relationship. Explain the distinction in your own words, say which of the two Winner takes to be the more troubling claim and why, and state whether you think the second can be reduced to the first. Then give one example from software systems, assign it to one of the two categories, and justify the assignment."

The rubric the instructor gave us: 25 points total — 8 for explaining the distinction, 7 for characterizing Winner's own assessment of it and for the reduction question, 10 for the candidate's own example (correct categorization plus a justification that actually supports it).

The response I'm grading:

"Winner says the claim that artifacts have politics comes in two versions. The first is the modest one: a device or arrangement is built or configured so as to settle a political question in a particular community. His case is Robert Moses's parkway bridges on Long Island, built too low for buses to pass, so that Jones Beach was reachable only by private automobile. The Cyrus McCormick plant's adoption of pneumatic molding machines in the 1880s is the same kind of case: an expensive, not-very-productive technology taken up for what it would do to the skilled molders' union rather than for what it would do to output.

The second version concerns inherently political technologies, systems whose efficient operation demands a certain form of political organization whether or not anyone wanted that outcome. The atom bomb is Winner's example, since it requires centralized, hierarchical command.

I think the first kind is the more troubling claim, because it shows that people can act politically while writing down nothing but technical requirements: the exclusion never appears in the specification, it appears in the clearance under the bridge. The second kind is less worrying, and in fact it is only the first kind in disguise — if a system requires centralized authority, that requirement is itself the accumulated result of choices made by particular people at particular times, so the second category collapses into the first and no separate notion is needed.

My example belongs to the second kind. Every service in our organization authenticates through a single central identity provider, and four engineers hold the keys: they can revoke any team's access, and nothing runs while they are unavailable. The architecture therefore requires a small, centralized authority, which is what Winner means by an inherently political technology.

The political content is fixed when the design is fixed. No amount of goodwill by later engineers changed who could reach the beach once the bridges stood, and once we shipped the identity provider as a hard dependency the hierarchy was built in, with or without anyone intending it. That is why the essay matters to people who build systems: the politics gets decided early, by people writing configuration."
