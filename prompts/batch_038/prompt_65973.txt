so my informal working group has been arguing for weeks about whether people with really different terminal values should strike deals with each other, and we've gotten nowhere because one guy — call him D — has an analogy he leans on constantly and nobody has actually audited it. The setup we're using: camp A is suffering-focused, roughly they'd take an empty universe over one with a trillion ecstatic beings plus a billion tortured ones, and camp B is flourishing/expansion focused, they mostly want life and complexity spread as widely as possible and treat suffering as a cost to be weighed rather than a veto. D says this is just international trade: even if one country is worse at producing literally everything, there are still gains from trade through comparative advantage, and refusing to deal is like a country insisting on autarky and ending up poorer for no reason. On his picture each camp should "specialize" in the parts of the future it's relatively better at shaping, and he uses that to argue that A should accept deals where they permit enormous amounts of happy existence in exchange for hard guarantees against certain extreme suffering — and that this is a genuine win-win, not a betrayal of A's values. He's pretty insistent that the deal is *required* by the structure of the situation, not just permitted by it.

i'm sceptical, and my suspicion is that the analogy is carrying the conclusion rather than supporting it. can you lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of each country, the goods being produced, the productive capacities, the prices, the tariff, the autarky fallback, the thing that enforces contracts — and then go through the disanalogies, but sorted: which ones actually threaten D's argument and which are cosmetic? (my guess is "there's no shared currency" is load-bearing and "the two countries are actually one civilisation sharing one future" is not, but I could be wrong about both). I especially want to know what the trade picture quietly imports that this case lacks: does it assume the goods are non-overlapping, so that what A wants and what B wants are different things rather than rival specifications of the same region of spacetime? Does it assume voluntary entry *and exit*, which A doesn't have? Does it assume you can be compensated in something you actually care about rather than in a substitute that's just what the other side happens to have spare? And does comparative advantage even have a referent here — what would it mean for a value system to be relatively more *productive* at generating something another value system wants, as opposed to just having more bargaining power or more numbers? Relatedly, when D points at the gains, I can't tell whether he's describing Ricardian gains from specialization or just the much more boring gain of not burning resources fighting each other, and I think those two are different bargains with different implications for what A should sign.

last thing: tell me whether the conclusion survives in some weaker form even if the analogy is junk — maybe the deal is still worth making, just not for the reason D gives, and maybe it's worth making for A but not *required* of A. And if trade is the wrong picture, say what would be a better one; people in the group have proposed Coasean bargaining over externalities, a coalition government that has to keep both wings onboard, and one person said hostage exchange, which I think is too cute but might be closer than it sounds. Rank those by how much weight they can actually bear and say what each of them implies about the deal that the trade version doesn't. Oh and a side question — the group keeps saying "acausal trade" and "evidential cooperation" as if the trade framing were already settled upstream; if you think the trade metaphor is load-bearing in a bad way for those ideas too and not just for D's argument, say that as well. no reading list or citations please, I want the analysis, and it should stand on its own for someone who's never read anything on the topic.
