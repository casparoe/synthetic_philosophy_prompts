I've been going down a rabbithole about explanations that sound like reasons even though nobody actually has the reason. Like why moths spiral into a flame, or why the wiring in our eyes leaves a blind spot — you can say the reason it's built that way is such-and-such, but the moth never considered that, and evolution doesn't consider anything. I think Dennett has a phrase for these, free-floating rationales, reasons hanging around with no reasoner holding them. What's bugging me is that this seems identical to the old aristotelian idea of final causes, the acorn doing what it does for the sake of becoming an oak — both claim there's a purpose in nature that isn't in anybody's head. So can you actually compare the two for me? Is the Darwinian version just Aristotle in a lab coat, or do they commit you to genuinely different things about the world, and which one ends up positing something spookier? My suspicion is that "reason" is doing two completely different jobs in these two stories and people just slide between them without noticing — if that's right, tell me what the two jobs are.
