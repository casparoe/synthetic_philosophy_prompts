I'm the review editor at a small interdisciplinary journal (public policy, some ethics, some empirical social science) and I sign off on every decision letter, so I read about fifteen referee reports a week and decide which of the considerations inside them actually get to count. It's become a mess. Referees write things like "accept — the identification strategy is clean, and I'd also note the author is early-career at a regional teaching school and this is exactly the kind of work that gets passed over," or "reject — the measure can't bear the causal claim in the abstract, and honestly this whole sub-literature is a fad, one more just entrenches it and takes a slot from someone doing something harder," or "the topic is one our readers have been asking us about for two years." I keep having the feeling that some of these considerations are about the paper and some are really about whether it would be nice for us to think well of the paper, and that mashing them together is what makes decision letters feel dishonest — authors appeal maybe one in ten decisions, and the board only ever sees the letter, so anything in it has to survive being read aloud to the person it rejects. My current rule of thumb for sorting them is broken though. The rule is: about the paper = legitimate, about the author or about what happens after publication = not legitimate. It misfires both directions. "This author has had two papers retracted over data problems" is about the author but I do think it bears on how carefully I should read this submission, and "if this result is right it changes what everyone in the field should believe" is about the paper but somehow feels like it's about consequences too. Somebody at a conference once told me there are at least three competing ways to draw the line here — something about which question the attitude is answering, something about reasons to want to have the attitude, and an object-given versus state-given distinction — and that they don't always give the same verdicts. I don't want a survey of that, and no bibliography or dates or names please, I just want you to pick whichever version of the distinction actually survives being turned into a checklist and tell me what you had to throw away on the way.

So: write me a reusable procedure, in two registers. One is the referee-facing version, which has to be jargon-free and runnable in about twenty minutes at the end of drafting a report by someone who may be a first-year grad student with no philosophy at all — no terms like "state-given" anywhere in it. The other is my version as editor, which can carry the theory and is what I use when I'm triaging a report that came back messy. For each, give the questions in the order they get asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops cold with "this consideration does not enter," and where it hands the decision up to a human because the remaining question is about the journal's values rather than about the manuscript. Two things I especially want handled. First, quarantining a wrong-kind consideration shouldn't just delete it — some of these considerations are real reasons for somebody to do something, just not for the referee's verdict, so the procedure needs a routing step that says which of these belong to me (scope, mission, what the issue is for, how many slots a subfield gets) and which belong nowhere. Second, referees often state a wrong-kind reason that is a symptom of a right-kind one they can't articulate — "this will be influential" sometimes just means "this is central and true," and sometimes it means "this will be influential." Build in an elicitation step: a short question the editor can send back that would separate those two, and a rule for what to do when the referee doesn't answer it or answers it badly. Flag every step where you're sneakily encoding a contested philosophical view rather than doing neutral bookkeeping, and flag any place where two of the three accounts of the distinction you know would send the same consideration to different destinations.

Then run it on three real-ish cases. (1) A report that says: recommend minor revisions, the identification strategy in section four is clean and I could not find a hole in it, though I want the board to know the author is early-career at a regional teaching university and we have not published anything on this country in six years. (2) A report that says: reject, "trust" is measured with a single survey item and cannot support the causal claim in the abstract, and separately this sub-literature is a fad and one more of these entrenches it and takes a slot from harder work. (3) The one I suspect will annoy me: a manuscript I personally want to publish, by someone whose work I've admired for years, which I'm confident will get cited alot; the only substantive objection came from a referee I know to be a crank with a grudge against this method, and I cannot actually answer his objection myself — I just know the kind of person he is. Tell me what your procedure does with my desire to overrule him, and whether there is anything at all I'm allowed to do with the fact that he's unreliable, or whether that fact is simply inadmissable and I have to either find a merits-based answer or send it out again. Finish with a short, blunt section on what the procedure cannot do: the cases where it goes quiet, the ones where it will confidently give a bad answer, and the distinction it can't draw no matter how it's written.
