Third year, epistemology-ish but drifting formal, and I've been moonlighting on a thing where a policy shop wants written-down probability estimates for proposed reforms — with the reference class used for each estimate stated explicitly, so an auditor can check the number later. Everyone thought that was the easy part of the job. It isn't. Trying to state the class-picking rule has turned into the whole problem, and I'd rather work from my verdicts than from the theory, because my verdicts are a mess and at least they're mine.

Here's what I actually say when I ask which class governs a probability:

- **Restaurant.** A friend is opening a place. I refuse to use "new restaurants" (something like half are gone inside five years, depending which dataset you touch). I want "new restaurants, city this size, owner with 15 years in the industry, own capital at risk, no outside investors." Narrower wins, no contest.
- **Surgery.** Someone asks me the odds of a procedure going well. Taken literally, "narrowest" bottoms out at *this surgeon, this patient, this Tuesday*, n = 1. So I climb back up to "this procedure at high-volume centers." Verdict: narrower is better until the sample gets thin, then you stop. I don't know where thin is, and I'm pretty sure my threshold slides depending on whether I like the resulting number.
- **Rare disease.** n = 6, all six alive at two years, versus a broader class of clinically similar conditions with about 30% two-year survival. Here I want to go with the six. That flatly sits badly with the surgery verdict and I can't explain why.
- **Sentencing.** Recidivism risk. The narrowest class with decent data is partly defined by neighborhood, and neighborhood is a proxy for race. Verdict: the statistics are exactly what my principle tells me to use, and using them is wrong. And I don't want to file that under "separate moral overlay, epistemic answer untouched" — I think the class choice is itself defective, not just its application. I can't make that precise.
- **Manuscript.** An author asks the odds of a big advance. Narrowest class is "manuscripts by this author": one, and it didn't. So I pick "debut literary fiction submitted to large imprints in the last decade" — chosen not for narrowness but because it's the class least likely to be explained away by facts about her specifically.
- **Reflexive.** Some classes only have the members they do because the estimate is out there. "Startups that raised a Series B in a sector where median Series B returns are public" — publish the number and the membership conditions shift under you. I want to say the estimate should be relative to the class as it would be if nobody knew the rate, but I can't say why that's not just me preferring a convenient fiction.
- **One-off.** Probability that a particular constitutional convention gets called. The class of civilizational-scale transitions has one or two members. I still want a number, I want it to be non-arbitrary, and I suspect that's incoherent.

My tentative principle, Reichenbach's narrowest-class line plus patches: use the narrowest class such that (a) n clears some threshold, (b) the property that puts the case in the class isn't itself downstream of the outcome, or of anyone knowing the outcome rate, and (c) there's at least a story — not a mechanism, just a story — about why being in that class bears on the outcome.

So: does anything like that deliver all seven? If not, which verdict do I have to give up, and which clause does the sacrificing — because I'd rather lose the rare-disease intuition than the sentencing one, and I don't know whether any principle lets me have that preference. Then tell me what your best account says about a case I didn't list: the probability a patient does well given that she *chose* the aggressive treatment, where choice is correlated with both prognosis and with having read the outcome statistics. And flag, explicitly, which parts of this are settled among people who work on it and which are live fights. My reading so far leaves me with the sense that there's near-consensus that some constraint on class choice is needed and near-total disagreement about what it is, and I don't want you to flatten the second half into a confident recommendation just because the format asks you for one. Say where you're picking a side in an argument that isn't over, and how much of your answer would change if the other side is right.
