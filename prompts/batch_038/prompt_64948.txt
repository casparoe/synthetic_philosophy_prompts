I've been going back and forth over Hume's short essay on suicide for about a month now (retired, too much free time) and there's a thing in it I can't find a word for. Not the arguments, I follow those fine — the bit where he says we divert the course of nature every time we dam a river or take medicine, so the general-providence objection can't single out self-killing, and the bit where he works through duty to god, to your neighbour, and to yourself in turn. What I can't name is a certain move he makes at the end, and then I keep seeing the same move elsewhere, and I want to know whether it's a recognised thing or whether I've invented a category out of one text.

So here it is. The essay's actual conclusion is narrow: this act is not a crime, not against god, not against yourself, not against society. He never anywhere says anyone ought to do it. Then, roughly at the close, he remarks that nobody gives up their life while its still worth keeping, that our natural horror of death means small motives won't reconcile us to it, and that the people who do go through with it are usually in such misery or such a sour temper that no enjoyment was left on the table anyway.

Reading it the first time, that closing remark feels like he's reining the whole thing in — a bit of caution bolted onto a permissive argument, the way you'd expect from someone who knows he's about to be attacked for it. And he was: he pulled the piece from the volume before it went out, and it only circulated in dodgy unauthorized editions afterwards. But sit with the remark and it doesn't rein anything in. It isn't a restriction on when the act is permitted. It's a prediction about who will actually perform it. And the people it describes — the ones for whom life has stopped being worth anything — are precisely the people the permission was written for. So the sentence has the tone of a reservation and the content of an observation, and it leaves the argument exactly as strong as it was two paragraphs earlier while making the essay read as moderate.

Then the reception does something parallel, which is what convinced me this isn't just a quirk of this one text. Everyone argues about whether Hume was really advocating suicide. The defenders say there's no recommendation anywhere in it, and they're right about the sentences. The critics say lifting the prohibition raised the rate regardless, and they're right about the world, because the prohibition had been doing deterrent work and the argument dismantled it. Neither side can point at a line, so the fight happens entirely in the gap between what the argument says and what it does — and the author's denial of intent is sincere and also completely irrelevant.

Same shape in a paper a friend of mine wrote a few years back, arguing that in one narrow situation breaking a promise isn't unjust. The paper never told anybody to break promises. Its last section noted that in practice almost everyone keeps them anyway, for reasons of reputation and habit that have nothing to do with justice. Seminar afterwards was two hours of people asking whether she was encouraging promise-breaking, and she kept saying she wasn't, and she wasn't, and nothing changed.

So two questions, and I care way more about the second.

Is there a name for that closing move — a qualification attached to a permissive conclusion that sounds like it narrows the permission but only describes who will exercise it, so the permission comes out untouched? Plain hedging isn't it, since hedging actually weakens the claim, and neither is irony or false modesty as far as I can see, because he seems to genuinely believe the observation.

The more interesting one: is there a concept for the wider mismatch, where a text says only not-forbidden and nevertheless functions as a push, such that the author can honestly disclaim any recommendation while the disclaimer does no work at all? I want the carving here, not a label slapped on it. Which established notions come closest, how does what I've described differ from each of them, and where are the edges — under what conditions does a closing remark like that stop being inert and actually bite, i.e. when would the descriptive part do real normative work? And if there's a name for the mismatch but nothing covers the closing move, say that straight out and coin something for it, defined tightly enough that I could tell a genuine case from a fake one and check it against other texts I read. Don't bother with a bibliography, I've got no way of looking anything up at the moment — just the concepts and their boundaries.
