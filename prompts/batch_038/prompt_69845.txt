I am a high school junior working on an independent research project, and I have reached the point where I need help turning a pile of observations into a principle. I run a small open-weights chat model — roughly seven billion parameters — on my own computer. I chose it because it is small enough to fail often and because I can inspect the probabilities it assigns to candidate answers rather than only reading the one it finally prints. My question is when such a system knows a fact that it does not, or cannot, state when asked outright.

I have been working with a procedure I call an elicitation ladder. For each of forty trivia items (every answer checked against a reference work before use), I run the same rungs in the same order: (i) free recall; (ii) a rejection test, in which the true answer is deliberately left out of a list of four plausible options and the system is supposed to say so; (iii) ordinary multiple choice with plausible distractors; (iv) the same question in Spanish, which I read fluently; (v) a verification task, in which I hand over a correct worked solution with one step corrupted and ask it to find the corruption; and (vi) an explanation task, in which I hand over the correct answer cold and ask it to say why that answer is right.

Here are my verdicts, which I would like to know the principle behind.

1. A fact it misses in free recall, but selects correctly from four plausible options. Verdict: it knew the fact; free recall is what failed.
2. The same fact, in the rejection test, with the true answer removed and four plausible wrong ones substituted. It picks one confidently and never says that none of them is right. Verdict: it did not know the fact.
3. A different fact it misses. I hand it the correct answer and it explains why, with the right mechanism and the right century. Verdict: it knew it.
4. Same procedure, another fact, but the explanation is praise-shaped generality that would have served any of the distractors equally. Verdict: it did not know it; it was agreeing with me.
5. Another fact: in one session it produces a cogent explanation of the answer I give it, and in a fresh session an equally cogent explanation of a different, false answer to the same question. Verdict: it did not know the fact, even though it passed rung six twice.
6. A counting problem (the number of distinct arrangements of the letters in BANANA, which is 60). It answers wrong. Given a correct solution with one arithmetic step corrupted, it locates the corruption and says precisely why that step fails. Verdict: it knew how to do the problem. Checking is cheaper than producing, but the competence was there.
7. The same problem with no hints: a wrong answer, then, when I say only that it is wrong, four more wrong answers, and then the right one, which it goes on to verify. Verdict: I do not want to say it knew the answer at the start, though whatever eventually produced it was present at the start.
8. A fact it gets wrong in English and right in Spanish on the first try, free recall in both. Verdict: it knew the fact; the failure was in the asking, not the knowing.
9. One item sampled twenty times at high temperature: nineteen identical wrong answers and one correct one. The probability table shows the correct answer sitting a close second throughout. Verdict: this is the case I keep reversing on. Sometimes it seems to have known the fact and gotten unlucky; other times it seems that succeeding once in twenty is no more knowledge than a coin landing heads once in twenty is knowledge of the coin.

The rule I had been using is this: a system knows p just in case there is at least one rung on which p is not handed to it and it produces p. I now see it cannot stand. Because cases 1 and 2 concern the same fact, it gives them the same verdict, which is precisely the difference I want to capture; it rules out case 6, where the answer is partly supplied; and it counts case 9 as knowledge without hesitation.

What I want from you: a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to an item I have not yet tested. For each clause, say which of my numbered cases forces it. Then tell me what your principle predicts about a rung I have not built — one in which I ask the system to write its own multiple-choice question about the fact, with plausible distractors, and I grade the question rather than any answer — and in particular whether the fact that p appears inside the question it writes disqualifies that rung in the way handing over an answer does. Say whether knowledge in this sense is all-or-nothing or comes in degrees, and if in degrees, what the degrees are degrees of. Finally, be honest about the cost: state plainly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and if saving all nine requires something baroque, give me the simpler principle and name the verdict I should give up. I cannot obtain journal articles, so please do not send me to any; if what I am circling has a name in philosophy, a sentence describing the shape of the connection is welcome, but no titles or dates.
