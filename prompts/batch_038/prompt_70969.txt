I have been asked to give the closing talk at a half-day workshop on human–machine teaming that runs alongside a cognitive science meeting this fall: thirty minutes of talk, twenty minutes of discussion. The room is not my usual crowd. Most of the audience are applied cognitive psychologists and HCI researchers who run behavioral studies on cognitive offloading, automation reliance, and metacognitive monitoring — people who measure how confidently users detect an error in a system's output, how often they accept a fluent but wrong answer, and how much of a task they delegate once delegation has paid off a few times. There are also two human-factors researchers from an industry lab whose products are explicitly designed for seamlessness, one developmental psychologist who works on children's selective trust in informants and artifacts, and two philosophers of mind: one who defends an expansive version of the extended mind thesis and will treat any restriction on it as a betrayal of the parity principle, and one who thinks the whole program confuses causes with constituents and will be delighted to watch me walk into that trap. I need to know what each of these groups is going to say to me.

The position I am defending is a restricted form of the extended mind thesis, which I call auditable extension. My claim is that parity between inner and outer resources holds only where the agent can take a corrective stance toward the external resource's deliverables at the moment of use — only where, that is, the resource's errors are detectable and rejectable from means the agent already has, whether those are background knowledge, coherence checks against other outputs, or a visible trace of how the answer was produced. My argument runs roughly as follows: constitution rather than mere causal coupling requires that the agent's own cognitive activity run through the resource in both directions; running in both directions requires that the resource's outputs be available to the agent as candidate contents she can withhold assent from; withholding assent requires detection; and the properties that make an interface feel seamless — fluency, confident formatting, a single answer with no provenance and no intermediate steps, the suppression of any record of how it was arrived at — are precisely the properties that destroy the detection cues my criterion depends on. So I end up with the deliberately provocative conclusion that the experienced Tetris player who rotates the falling block on the screen rather than in her head is extending her mind, that the notebook-carrying amnesiac of the standard thought experiment is extending his, and that a user who accepts the confident summary a language model hands her usually is not: the smoother the interface, the less extended the cognition. I want to insist that this restricts the parity principle rather than abandoning it, since my criterion is functional and not spatial — but I am aware of how that will sound in that particular room.

So prepare me for the question period. Give me the nine or ten objections I should actually expect from this audience — not the generic list I would get at a philosophy department seminar — ordered by the product of how likely each is to be raised and how much damage it would do, and labeled with which subgroup raises it: the measurement people, the two industry human-factors researchers, the developmental psychologist, the expansive extended-mind philosopher, or the coupling-and-constitution skeptic. Phrase each one the way that person would actually phrase it, in their own vocabulary and with their own examples, so that I can hear it coming rather than recognize it afterward. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, an honest note on where that reply is softest, and a judgment about whether I should concede part of it up front during the talk in order to keep it out of the discussion entirely. Tell me which single reply has to land for the talk to count as a success. Then identify the question I should be hoping nobody asks: my own guess is that it is the one observing that my criterion makes the boundaries of a person's mind a function of her metacognitive competence, which varies systematically with age, education, and expertise, so that on my view extension is a privilege of the cognitively advantaged — tell me whether I have correctly located my worst case or whether something worse is sitting in that room, and sketch the least bad answer to whichever it is. Finally, flag which of my premises are empirical claims that someone present may say they have already measured, and what I should say if a hand goes up and a researcher reports data cutting against me. I do not want references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I know the literature on both sides. What I need is the dialectic and the wording.
