I have been living inside a single decision for the better part of two years now, and I have come to suspect that the decision is doing more philosophical work than I am. My novel in revision turns on a fork: a doctor who is the only physician left in a besieged town, with one dose of anesthetic and two patients who will each die screaming without it, and — in the version I have actually kept — a second, worse complication, namely that one of the two is the man whose denunciation put her there. A literary magazine has commissioned a craft essay from me about this kind of plot, the one that refuses to resolve, and I have decided the honest thing to do is to give the readers an instrument rather than an argument: a decision tree that sorts a novel by the position on moral conflict it actually enacts, so that a writer can find out what her book is committed to without having to have read any moral philosophy at all.

What I want is a taxonomy in the shape of a tree, of roughly eight to ten questions and six to nine leaves, where every branch node is a question a reader could answer about a book she has read once, without any technical vocabulary and without being told where each answer leads. The questions should be about the architecture of the predicament and, even more, about the book's handling of the aftermath, since that is where novels betray themselves. Among the kinds of node I have in mind: whether the narrative ever leaves open that both options could have been taken, or treats the choice as settled in advance by who the agent is; whether, once the agent has chosen, the unchosen option keeps pressing — a debt that stays on the books — or is extinguished at the moment of decision; whether the agent's own earlier wrongdoing is what manufactured the fork; whether the book grants its agent grief or revulsion that is explicitly not a doubt about having chosen correctly; whether the world of the novel eventually balances the ledger, by compensation, redemption, or a punishment that fits; whether the conflict runs between two demands laid on this agent, or between a demand on her and an impersonal good; and whether the book anywhere implies that the two options could be weighed on a common scale, with the heavier side simply lifted. No node may ask the reader what she thinks about moral dilemmas, or use words like incommensurable, overriding, or residue; and the tree should be usable by a novelist who has never encountered the debate, which is to say that a reader who answers honestly should be surprised by the leaf she arrives at. I would like the famous wartime case that mid-century existentialists liked to tell — the young man torn between crossing to England to join the Free Forces and staying with the mother for whom he is the only remaining consolation — used as one worked example, run through the tree step by step, so that the reader can see the instrument in motion.

Each leaf should then be labelled with the position on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible that the book has thereby walked into, and I would like you to include at least these, whatever names you give them: the dissolution on which apparent conflicts are only provisional and the considered verdict is always single; the view that dilemmas are real but always resolvable because one requirement simply outweighs the other with nothing left over; the view that a genuine dilemma would have morality issuing contradictory demands and is therefore impossible; the pluralist view that some values cannot be measured against one another, so that choice is underdetermined by reasons rather than merely painful; and the view that the stubborn persistence of moral remainder — the owed apology, the taint, the refusal to repeat the act — is itself the evidence that something real went unfulfilled. Any leaf no philosopher defends should be marked unnamed and given a coined label rather than a borrowed one. Finally, tell me which pairs of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, and say what further question would be needed to pry them apart; and then map the combinations of answers that no position occupies, giving each a one-line diagnosis of why the cell is empty — because it is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because nobody has tried it. That last category is the part I most want, since fiction can hold a view that philosophy will not sponsor, and if my own book is sitting in an unoccupied cell I would rather know it before the essay goes to press.
