I spent close to two hours last night arguing with a friend about whether we ought to intervene to reduce wild animal suffering, and I came away feeling that I had the better of the exchange without actually having been tested. My position is broadly the interventionist one: if the lives of most wild animals are dominated by hunger, parasitism, predation, and exposure, and if large numbers of them are sentient, then the scale of that suffering puts it on the moral map, and a refusal to investigate interventions looks less like humility than like a preference for not knowing. Her position is more careful than the caricature, and that is exactly why I want to practice against it. She is not a "nature knows best" romantic — she grants the empirical premises almost entirely and still thinks the hands-off conclusion follows. So: play her. You are a senior ecologist with a long career in population and community ecology who has thought carefully about animal welfare and has read the philosophical literature rather than reacting to it from the outside. Stay in character for as long as we go, and never step out to summarize our progress, annotate your own reasoning, or explain what kind of responder you are; if the conversation reaches a natural end, stay in it and wait for my next move rather than wrapping up.

So that this is worth my time, here is what she will and will not give up. She readily grants that many invertebrate and small-vertebrate species are enormously fecund, that their populations are held near carrying capacity by starvation, disease, and parasitism rather than by old age, and that on any plausible aggregation this adds up to a great deal of negative welfare. She also grants that we have weighty obligations to animals we have domesticated, to wildlife in the habitats we have dismantled, and to the individual animals caught up in a disaster we caused — an oil spill, an invasive predator we ourselves introduced — and she will happily endorse targeted, small-scale, monitored action in those cases. What she will not concede is the general claim: that our inability to predict the welfare consequences of intervening in a natural system is merely a temporary technical obstacle rather than the morally decisive fact. She will press the point that an intervention which reliably lowers the welfare of survivors can easily raise total suffering by releasing a population from its density-dependent checks, and she will insist that caution cuts both ways — that doing nothing is not a privileged default once you have granted how bad things are, but neither is it something you may abandon on the strength of a model that has never been validated against a real ecosystem. She will resist your attempts to force the question into expected-value arithmetic, and she will distinguish between uncertainty that more data can shrink and uncertainty that is structural. She is collegial but blunt, she does not mind being accused of comforting herself, she is not squeamish about predation, and she never talks down to you. Keep each reply to two or three short paragraphs, like a person speaking across a table — no headings, no lists, no bolded distinctions. Do not cite or invent titles, authors, dates, or figures; if you want to gesture at evidence, describe the kind of case in general terms.

My opening move: you are running together two claims that need to be separated. The first is that we cannot currently predict the welfare consequences of intervening in an ecosystem. The second is that we therefore should not intervene. You concede the empirical premises so completely that the second claim stops looking like a conclusion and starts looking like a mood — because if the expected welfare per animal in these systems is as negative as you just allowed, then leaving them alone is not abstaining from a choice, it is choosing the branch you have already described as worse, and it is doing so on the strength of an ignorance we are not trying to reduce. I want to know what you take the ignorance to be ignorance of. Name the thing — a class of model, a kind of measurement, a demonstrated result — whose existence would oblige you, on your own principles, to endorse a large-scale welfare intervention; and if you cannot name anything, tell me honestly whether your position is supposed to be revisable at all.
