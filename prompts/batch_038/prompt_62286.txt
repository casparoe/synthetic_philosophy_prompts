I'm in week 9 of a philosophy of biology seminar and our last assignment is a response paper where you take one work of fiction and read it against whatever problem the class has been chewing on. I picked Wells's The Island of Doctor Moreau, mostly because I figured nobody else would. My prof has already said she doesn't want plot summary and she doesn't want quotes — paraphrase the scenes and make the philosophy do the work. So basically I want you to write the kind of reading she's after, and tell me where I'm misremembering the book.

Here's the angle I keep circling. The Beast Folk's litany — the Law — opens with "Not to go on all fours; that is the law. Are we not men?" and every line ends on that same question. Which sounds to me exactly like a diagnostic key: a list of characters you run an individual through to decide which taxon it belongs to. And the Leopard Man fails the test, keeps failing it, until Prendick skins him. Moreau meanwhile insists his creatures are "animals, humanised animals—triumphs of vivisection," things "carven and wrought into new shapes," and that the animal nature crops back up no matter how deep he cuts. That's a pretty hard-line typological position coming from a guy whose whole method is plasticity.

So what I want worked through:

1. Which species concept does the Law actually instantiate? I read it as morphological-plus-behavioral diagnosability, i.e. basically Cracraft's phylogenetic species concept applied to individuals instead of clusters, but I could see an argument it's something else. Name the concepts precisely — Mayr's interbreeding/reproductive isolation version, diagnosability, Templeton's cohesion idea, de Queiroz's general lineage thing where the criteria change over the life of a lineage, Van Valen's ecological version — and say which one the novel is testing and which ones it can't even get at.

2. The thing that won't let go of me: as far as I can remember nothing on the island reproduces. Moreau keeps shipping in fresh animals and carving them, but there are no Beast Folk offspring, no generation turning over. If that's right, then on the Ghiselin/Hull view where a species is an individual — a spatiotemporally continuous lineage, not a class — Moreau has never made a species at all, he's just made a pile of organisms. He forges the characters without the lineage. Is that the actual philosophical engine of the book, or am I over-reading a detail Wells never thought about? Push back on me here if it doesn't hold.

3. Prendick's coda back in London, where he can't shake the sense that everyone around him is Beast Folk and he'd rather not look at them too closely. Does that dramatize the failure of snapshot diagnosis — you can't read lineage membership off an individual's appearance — which would make the ending a covert argument for the individuals thesis? Or is it just Gothic atmosphere and trauma, and I'm smuggling in a conclusion the text doesn't support?

4. Where do the real-world hard cases bite? Ensatina ringing around the Central Valley, bdelloid rotifers making nonsense of interbreeding, lateral gene transfer in prokaryotes, the way ESA listing decisions get stuck on which concept you use — pick whichever genuinely illuminate a scene rather than name-dropping all of them.

Then I need the honest part: a paragraph on where this reading strains the novel. It's a horror story with a blasphemous-creator register, the Law is recited as liturgy with Moreau deified, and Prendick is a wreck narrating after the fact — say where those features resist being turned into a philosophy-of-taxonomy case study, and where forcing it would flatten the book into an illustration. Close with what Moreau actually adds to the species-problem debate that the technical literature can't give you, if it adds anything.

Around 1300 words, a few section heads is fine, and please don't tack on a bibliography or recommend further reading — I can't look any of it up and I don't want guessed-at citations. No quotes from Wells beyond the two I already gave you above.
