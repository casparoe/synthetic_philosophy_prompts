I spent thirty-six years as a solid-state physicist, the last eighteen of them running a beamline devoted to the structure determination of disordered alloys, and since retiring I have taken an MA in the history and philosophy of science at my old university. The convenor of our module on realism and theory change has asked me — as the resident ex-scientist — to set the unseen essay question for this year's final, and to supply a model answer and a marking scheme for the two colleagues who will mark it, both of whom are historians rather than specialists in the contemporary debate. Please take the role of a professional philosopher of science with substantial experience examining at postgraduate level, and think carefully before drafting: I would like you to begin with roughly a hundred and fifty words of working, in which you canvass two or three candidate cruxes within structural realism that could bear the weight of an examination question, and explain why you have settled on the one you have. Only then should you produce the question itself.

The topic is structural realism, but I do not want anything that a well-prepared candidate could answer by reciting the distinction between the epistemic and the ontic versions, or by summarising the pessimistic meta-induction and declaring it defeated. The crux I want the question to turn on is this. For the claim that structure is what survives theory change — and that this is therefore all a realist should commit to — to be worth defending, the notion of structure had better be thin enough that a great deal of it really does survive the transitions in question; yet if it is that thin, the claim looks trivial, since almost any description of a sufficiently abstract pattern can be imposed on almost any collection of things. A strong candidate should feel that vice closing, and should be pushed to say what would have to be added to the thin notion in order to escape it, and what that addition costs the position.

To that end, please build the question around a toy case of my own devising, which should be stated in the question stem itself, since the examination is closed-book and I do not want it to depend on anyone's memory of a historical episode. Theory T₁ posits three entities, a, b and c, and a single binary relation R, and asserts R(a,b), R(b,c) and the negation of R(a,c); in T₁, R is read as "transmits the disturbance to", and a, b, c are three subtle fluids. Its successor T₂ posits three entities x, y and z and a single binary relation S, and asserts S(y,z), S(z,x) and the negation of S(y,x); in T₂, S is read as "settles the state of", and x, y, z are three regions of a field. The candidates may assume, and I would like part (a) to ask them to confirm, that the relabelling a↦y, b↦z, c↦x shows T₂ to be the image of T₁: the structure is preserved exactly, while the ontology could hardly be more different. Part (b) should then ask them to show, for this three-element case, what is left of the content of T₂'s structural description once one says only that some binary relation or other stands in that pattern among three things — that is, why the Ramsey-style move of existentially quantifying over the relations leaves a claim that any three things whatsoever can satisfy, and what residue of genuine content survives. Part (c) should ask what the structural realist can say in reply, including whether restricting attention to the more natural or more fundamental candidate relations helps, and whether the more radical ontic response — that objects are no part of the furniture, so there is nothing for the structural description to be a description of — is a rescue or a surrender in this particular case.

Deliver, in order: your opening working; the question, in three parts, worth twenty-five marks in total and answerable in forty-five minutes; a model answer of between 700 and 850 words; and a marking scheme that allocates marks across the parts and, crucially, separates a first-class answer from a good upper-second by naming the specific moves each must contain, together with at least five identifiable errors that should cost marks, each with the marks lost and a one-line diagnosis of the misunderstanding behind it, since my colleagues will be marking from your scheme rather than from expertise. Please write everything in British English. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or any verbatim quotation from a published work: the examination is closed-book, and I would much rather have a self-contained question than one that turns on who said what. Names of philosophers may appear in the marking scheme where a marker needs to recognise a familiar line of response, but the question itself must be fully answerable by a candidate who has read only an anthology extract or two and can reason carefully from the toy case in front of them.
