I am drafting the social-choice section of a handbook chapter on the formal modelling of rights, and I have hit a wall that I want help diagnosing rather than resolving.

The wall is Sen's liberal paradox. Since 1970 the responses have hardened into a standing repertoire, and I can state every one of them, but I cannot say which of them are answers to the same question. For concreteness, here is the setup I will assume throughout: three social states — a, nobody reads the book; b, Prude reads it; c, Lewd reads it. Prude ranks a > b > c; Lewd ranks b > c > a. Prude is decisive over the pair {a, b} and Lewd over {a, c}, which yields aPb from Prude's ranking and cPa from Lewd's, while the Pareto principle yields bPc since both prefer b to c. Hence the cycle. The repertoire of responses runs roughly as follows:

1. *Reinterpret rights.* Replace decisiveness over pairs of social states with a game-form or protected-options representation: a right is a set of strategies available to an individual, and the social outcome is whatever the resulting game selects. Gibbard's decomposition of Sen's decisiveness condition into the existence of a right and its exercise is the canonical move here, and the Gibbard–Kelly libertarian claims the standard formalization. In the example above, Prude then reads the book himself — an outcome both parties rank below nobody reading it — so the pathology is relocated into the strategic situation rather than dissolved.
2. *Restrict Pareto*, for instance by refusing to count preferences about how other people conduct their own affairs, the meddlesome-preferences restriction.
3. *Attack the state space.* If social states are individuated finely enough to encode what everyone knows and feels about what everyone else does, then no pair of states is genuinely private, and {a, b} is not a pair lying within Prude's own sphere at all.
4. *Relocate rights.* Treat them as side constraints on action rather than as inputs to an aggregation function, so that the social welfare functional was never the right locus in which to look for them.
5. *Repair the domain*, via various restrictions and technical fixes.

Against these stands the persistent counter-claim that none of it touches the theorem: the impossibility concerns what any aggregation function must do given a rights assignment, and reformulating the assignment is a change of subject.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, and I want it attacked at least as much as defended — has three parts. (a) Part of the impasse is verbal: the word "right" is being used for two formally distinct objects, a decisiveness property of a social welfare functional and a permission structure in a game, and the two camps have spent half a century reporting each other's theorems as refutations of their own view. (b) Underneath that lies a substantive question nobody has formalized, namely what makes a pair of social states private — at what grain of description the protected pair is to be specified. If privacy is grain-relative, then Sen's condition and the Pareto condition may be applied at different grains, and the paradox might be an artifact of that mismatch rather than a fact about rights. (c) A narrower point: Sen's liberalism condition is a mandate on the choice function rather than a permission for the individual, since Prude's right is exercised on his behalf in every profile, including those in which he would rather not exercise it. If that is what the game-form camp is actually objecting to, the dispute is about the concept of a right and not about the formalism.

Please do the following.

1. Diagnose the disagreement. For each response listed above, identify which premise of the theorem it denies, and whether it answers Sen's question or substitutes a different one. Then sort the field by kind of disagreement: verbal, substantive but mis-stated, substantive, or simply unresolved.
2. Run a translation test. Restate each camp's central claim in a vocabulary that forbids the words right, rights, liberty, freedom, liberal, liberalism and private. State explicitly what disagreement survives the ban and what evaporates. If everything survives, part (a) of my hypothesis is false; if nothing does, the debate has been about a label.
3. Evaluate part (b) as technical philosophy rather than as an intuition. Is grain-relative privacy definable in a way that could be put to work — for instance, by specifying protected pairs relative to an observational equivalence over social states, and asking whether the impossibility survives when decisiveness is required at a coarse grain and Pareto at a fine one, or the reverse? Is that a question with a determinate answer, a trivially answerable one, or does it collapse back into a restatement of the original theorem?
4. Tell me what would count as progress. What shape of result would end this: an impossibility robust to any game-form representation of rights conjoined with any plausible Pareto criterion over outcomes; a consistency result running the other way; a formal account of privacy; a demonstration that the dispute survives translation into a neutral vocabulary? Or is the honest verdict that the question is ill-posed — in which case, what is the well-posed residue?
5. Say whether any empirical fact could bear on the matter or whether it is purely conceptual, and, briefly, whether your diagnosis changes anything about how one should design a real institution that grants subunits opt-out authority over decisions concerning themselves. That last point is a footnote in the chapter, so keep it short; I raise it only because a diagnosis that implies no upshot is itself informative.

Constraints on the answer: no bibliography, no citations, no reading list, and no survey of the literature I have just summarized. I want the diagnosis argued for, with the formal content made explicit where it matters; you are welcome to construct your own profiles, matrices or small state spaces to make a point. Where you think a party to the debate has been misdescribed by its opponents — including by me, above — say so and give the better description. And if you think my three-part hypothesis is wrong, or wrong in an interesting way, for instance because the real substance concerns the Pareto principle rather than rights, argue for that instead.
