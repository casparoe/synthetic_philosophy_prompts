Our philosophy-of-mind reading group has spent the better part of a year going in circles on whether the brain is a computer, and for the end-of-term workshop I have been asked to produce the document the two camps should have written a year ago: a joint statement emerging from a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The parties are these. On one side, a classical computationalist who holds that explaining perception, memory, or reasoning means specifying algorithms over content-bearing vehicles, and who treats the claim that the brain computes as a substantive empirical hypothesis on a par with any other mechanistic claim about neural tissue. On the other, a dynamical and embodied cognitivist in the tradition of Van Gelder's Watt-governor discussion, who holds that the best available models of cognition are systems of coupled differential equations describing continuous coupling between an agent and its environment, who doubts that the internal states posited by the classical story carry genuine content rather than merely re-describing that coupling, and who accordingly regards talk of the brain as a computer as at best a loose metaphor and at worst a category mistake. The slot is ninety minutes and half the room will be watching to see whether anything survives the exercise.

My own hunch — which the statement should interrogate rather than assume — is that a substantial slice of this dispute is verbal, turning on whether computation is being used narrowly, as discrete rule-governed manipulation of syntactically structured symbols, or broadly, as any medium-independent causal process that can be mapped onto a formal description, in which case the dynamical side's own models come out computational and the computationalist wins almost by stipulation. Two further complications should be handled explicitly. First, the narrow-versus-broad axis is not the only one in play: there are accounts on which genuine computation requires that some of a system's physical states carry semantic content, and on such accounts the dynamical side gets to deny that a non-representational system computes even while granting that it is fully describable in formal terms, which reshuffles who is arguing with whom. Second, there is the objection, associated with Eliasmith among others, that the Watt governor is a poor foil precisely because it is one concrete machine whereas the Turing machine is an abstraction over an entire class of systems, so no single worked example can adjudicate the general question. I would like the document to be clear about which of these strands is the real disagreement and which are artifacts of terminology.

Please write that joint statement, roughly three pages. Begin with a numbered section of claims both parties can sign: claims about the formal machinery they share (that discrete and continuous models are often inter-translatable, that any finite physical process admits of indefinitely many formal descriptions); claims about what the triviality worries — that any suitably open physical system can be mapped onto any finite-state automaton — do and do not establish for either side; and claims about what each camp owes the other, such as an account of productivity and systematicity from a purely dynamical description, and a non-vacuous criterion for when a neural system implements one computation rather than another. Then give the residual disagreements as a table, one row per disagreement, with columns for the disagreement stated in wording its own side would accept; whether it is substantive or merely verbal; the crux sub-question that would actually move someone if settled; a concrete case, experiment, or argument both sides accept as bearing on that crux; and each side's prediction, recorded in advance, about how that crux will come out. Close with a short section in which each party states what would count as evidence or argument against its own view, and, if you conclude that some portion of the dispute is verbal, say exactly which portion and why, and what is left over that is not — I would much rather have that settled than a document that papers over it. No bibliography and no citations, please: we will supply our own, and we would rather you refer to positions and arguments descriptively than risk inventing sources.
