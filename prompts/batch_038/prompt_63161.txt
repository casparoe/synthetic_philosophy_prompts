My friend and I argued until two in the morning over whether the Athenian assembly's disasters were failures of its procedure or merely of its men, and I came away aware that my own verdicts do not sit easily together. Here they are; I should like to know what principle generates them.

First, Mytilene: the assembly decrees the slaughter of every adult male on the island, reconvenes the next day, hears a second speech, reverses itself, and sends a ship to overtake the first. I count that as the system at its best, not its worst. Second, however, the reversal rested on no new fact whatever — nothing had changed but the mood of the room and the quality of the advocacy — and I praise it, while maintaining that a court which reopened a decided case on the same evidence had gone badly wrong. Third, Sicily: the same body debates twice at length, with the expedition's ablest opponent speaking openly and being answered, and votes for the armada that destroys it; here I say the procedure itself failed. Fourth, Arginusae: furious at the generals who left shipwrecked men unrecovered, the assembly condemns them in a single collective vote, over the objection that this was unlawful and the presiding officer's refusal to put the motion; six die. I rank that worse than Sicily, though as far as I can see the assembly's beliefs about the facts were accurate and its anger not unreasonable. Fifth, Socrates: a sworn jury in the hundreds, both sides heard at length, and a separate penalty phase in which the convicted man is invited to propose his own sentence — every safeguard I praised at Mytilene present — and I still call the outcome a scandal.

Is there a principle about when a body of ordinary citizens deliberating in public can be trusted that delivers all five? State it precisely, and identify which of its clauses is doing the work in each case. Then tell me what it makes of two episodes I have not listed: Nicias at the second Sicilian assembly, attempting to kill the expedition by demanding a force too large to vote for and being granted it, and the amnesty the Athenians imposed on themselves after the fall of the Thirty, under which no one was to bring up the past wrongs. Do those confirm the principle or embarrass it? And if no single principle survives all five of my verdicts, say which one you would overturn, why, and what the simplest principle you can live with looks like.
