I write novels. This autumn I am teaching, for the first time, a cross-listed graduate seminar between the philosophy department and the MFA programme, on the subject of first-person authority: what a person can and cannot know about her own mind, and what becomes of literary interiority once that question is pressed. I must now set the final assessment, which will be a forty-eight-hour take-home of two thousand words, marked out of twenty-five. I want your help composing it, and I should warn you that the difficulty I am after is not the difficulty of a long reading list. The seminar has read the obvious things — Nisbett and Wilson's 1977 review of verbal-report studies, Schwitzgebel on the unreliability of naive introspection, Moran on the transparency of belief, and the choice-blindness experiments of Johansson and his colleagues — and my students can summarize all four of those in their sleep. What they cannot yet do is tell which of them is actually at stake in a concrete case. That is what the examination must test.

The question is to be built around a case of my own devising, taken from the novel I am at work on. Students will have it in front of them, so it must appear verbatim in the paper:

Elias Varga, fifty-four, a cell biologist, is passed over for a directorship that goes to a colleague, Adnan, against whom he maintains he has no grievance whatsoever. Over the following eighteen months he does several small things: he leaves Adnan's postdoctoral fellow off a shared grant renewal; he takes eleven weeks to produce a reference letter he has promised; he schedules the departmental seminar so that Adnan is twice presenting on days when the dean is visiting, knowing that Adnan talks badly in public. When his wife lays the list out for him, he is startled, and he insists that at the moment of each act he was aware of nothing but indifference. He offers alternative explanations for each item; individually they are plausible, collectively they are strained. Three weeks later he telephones her to say that he has found it — a morning two years old that he can now picture in full, the smell of the autoclave, Adnan's exact words, and the resentment underneath, which he says was there the whole time. He calls his earlier denials a blind spot. He continues to recover further episodes, at a rate of about one a week. It is worth adding that Elias is, by the testimony of everyone who knows him, an unusually good observer of his own mind: he meditates daily and is the friend to whom others bring their motives for an honest reading.

Two things in that case are load-bearing, and the question must be built so that candidates cannot get full marks without handling both. The first is the retrospective recovery: being shown the pattern supplies Elias with a schema before the feeling is found, and the rate at which further episodes arrive is responsive to the schema rather than to the past. A candidate who treats the recovered memory either as vindicating the resentment or as simply debunking it has not understood the case. The second is the scope of the skeptical claim: the evidence that we confabulate reasons for our behavior is not automatically evidence that we confabulate the character of our present experience, and Elias's story mixes the two — a felt emotion on one hand, the springs of his acts on the other. I want the question to ask candidates what would discriminate between the rival explanations of his pattern, and to ask them to confront the embarrassment that the confabulationist's own case rests on first-person reports, including the report of having felt nothing and the report of surprise. The question must also carry a short final part asking what any of this implies for the epistemic standing of a first-person narrator's account of her own feelings, though I want that part marked hard, since most of my MFA students will reach for a stock phrase instead of an argument.

Please write three things. First, the examination question exactly as it will be printed, self-contained, with the case included, narrow enough to be answerable in two thousand words, and worded so that a competent summary of the literature earns very little. Second, a model answer of about twelve hundred words, in continuous prose — I am a novelist and I will not read a bullet-pointed answer, nor should my students be shown one. Third, a marking scheme totalling twenty-five, consisting of six must-hit points weighted to add to the total, and beneath them a separate schedule of deductions for specific errors, so that a candidate who commits one loses marks she has already earned. For each must-hit point, give me one sentence describing the near-miss that should attract half credit rather than none; those near-misses matter to me more than the points themselves.

Where the model answer refers to published work, paraphrase and attribute by author and year only. Do not place anything in quotation marks, and do not append a bibliography or a suggestion of further reading: I have the books and would rather check a claim against my own copy than discover that a sentence I was given was assembled to look like one.
