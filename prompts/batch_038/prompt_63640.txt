My colleague Dev runs our reading group on objective chance, and he's been doling out verdicts on cases all term, but he's off at a confernce in Lisbon when the interesting one finally occurs to me and I want to work out what he'll say before I spring it on him. Everything concerns a single trial, call it A, that has some objective chance of obtaining.

- You know the chance of A is .4 and you know nothing else: rational credence .4.
- You thought the chance was .4, then learn the apparatus was tampered with overnight and the real chance is now .9: credence .9.
- Fair coin, chance of heads .5, and a prophet who has never once been wrong says this toss comes up heads: credence essentially 1. He says the .5 doesn't get a look in.
- Same fair coin, but no prophet: you've watched the last 200 tosses of this very coin and heads came up 140 times. Next toss: about .7.
- A buddy who's usually reliable but was very drunk insists the coin landed heads: credence maybe .75, not 1 — he scales with how trustworthy the source is.

Now the case I want to predict. Same coin. Dev is told by the physics, and accepts, that the chance of heads on the next toss is exactly .5, fixed by an irreducibly stochastic law — and specifically that no pattern of frequencies could show that chance to be anything other than .5, frequencies being downstream of it rather than constitutive of it. Then a time traveller he has every reason to trust tells him that over the next 200 tosses of that very coin, heads comes up 140 times. What's his credence in heads on toss number one?

So: predict his number. Then tell me which principle best accounts for all five of the earlier verdicts, name at least one other principle that fits them equally well, and say whether the two come apart on the time traveller case or land on the same number for different reasons. Which one would you bet on, and why — is there anything in his track record that really favours it, or am I just guessing? And what further case should I put to him over a beer to settle it?
