Last spring my brother, who is in every other respect entirely lucid, became convinced that the family two doors down was recording him through the smoke detectors. For several weeks I did what a fourth-year philosophy graduate student does: I assembled the evidence, ran the base rates, and asked him what observation would count against his theory. It made the conviction worse and our relationship worse with it. What eventually helped involved no argument at all — a year of ordinary shared activity, repairing our mother's roof, Sunday dinners, and the gradual return of an unremarkable background against which the smoke-detector claim stopped being the sort of thing one could even entertain. I have come to think that what changed was not his evidence but his hinges, and next month I am presenting that view at a workshop. I would like your help preparing for the question period.

**The thesis I am defending.** Hinge commitments in Wittgenstein's sense — that there is an external world, that I have two hands, that the Earth long predates my birth, that my name is what I say it is — are not beliefs of any kind: not high-confidence beliefs, not beliefs held without evidence, but a distinct non-doxastic attitude, a practical trust that manifests in how one acts rather than in what one assents to. Here is the argument I will sketch on three slides:

1. The closure-based skeptical argument needs two premises that sit badly together: that ordinary beliefs can be evidentially supported, and that the hinge propositions their support depends on cannot be.
2. If hinges were beliefs, the skeptic would be right that we hold them irrationally, and closure would transmit that defect downward to the ordinary beliefs resting on them.
3. But hinges are not in the business of being supported or undermined by evidence at all; they are the standing background within which giving and asking for reasons takes place. Their presence shows up in what a person treats as exempt from checking, not in a credence.
4. So the skeptical argument is not answered but dissolved: it misclassifies a practical attitude as a doxastic one.
5. Applied to delusion: a persecutory delusion is not a false belief formed badly. It is a displaced hinge — a shift in what counts for the person as settled background. Evidence-based disputation is therefore aimed at the wrong object, and worse than ineffective, since pressing against a hinge does not produce revision but produces the felt need to defend the framework. What is indicated instead is the restoration of background through joint practical activity; a hinge is not argued back into place, it is re-inhabited.
6. This is not relativism. Hinges are answerable, just not to evidence: they are answerable to whether the form of life they make possible can be sustained. My brother's framework could not be. It isolated him, it was internally unstable, and it left him unable to sleep in his own house.

**The audience.** A half-day interdisciplinary workshop on delusion hosted by a medical school, about forty people, and I have fifteen minutes plus twenty for questions with no handout. In the room: a substantial bloc of clinical psychologists who practice and research cognitive-behavioral therapy for psychosis, whose entire method is collaborative empirical testing of delusional predictions and who can cite randomized trials; two Bayesian formal epistemologists who will insist a hinge is nothing but a credence at or near one; a psychiatrist of biological orientation who regards the delusion as a symptom of an underlying disorder and wants to know what my account adds beyond a redescription of the symptom; one or two phenomenologists who will say I am still talking in propositions when the change in question is in mood, bodily attunement, and the way the world is disclosed; a senior analytic philosopher who holds that the later Wittgenstein offers no epistemological theory whatsoever and that anything systematized out of his remarks on certainty is a reconstruction he would repudiate; and a scattering of graduate students plus a family peer-advocate who will be listening hard for whether my view licenses giving up on someone.

**What I want from you.** A prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the eight or nine objections this particular room will actually raise — not the objections a Wittgenstein reading group would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly, with a brief mark of both likelihood and severity. Phrase each one the way the person raising it would phrase it: the CBT researcher's version should sound like a methods objection with an outcome measure in it, not like my own formulation of it. For each, give a reply that fits in about two minutes of speech, and then state plainly where that reply is soft — what I would be forced to concede or fudge if pressed a second time.

Separately, tell me which of these objections, if any, is genuinely fatal to steps five and six rather than merely awkward, and whether any of them shows that my view collapses into a triviality on one side (of course you cannot argue someone out of a background, everyone already knew that) or into a quiet relativism on the other (the delusion was fine until we stopped tolerating it). Then give me: the two concessions I should make in my opening remarks so they cost me nothing later; the single reply that has to land for the talk to have been worth giving; and the question I am hoping nobody asks, together with the honest answer to it.
