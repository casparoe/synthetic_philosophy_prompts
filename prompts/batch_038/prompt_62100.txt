I am taking a module on the ethics of animal minds, and two classmates ended up in an argument about the so-called scale argument for insect sentience that I could not referee. They broadly agreed on the empirical background — insects display nociceptive reflexes, motivational trade-offs involving injury, and aversive learning — and disagreed only about what follows from it. The exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) Niamh said that even on a very low credence that any given insect is sentient, the number of insects is so vast that the expected quantity of morally considerable suffering dwarfs every other candidate for our attention, and that any practical outlook which ignores this is indefensible.

(2) Owain replied that she was multiplying a credence by a headcount, and that the move is illicit in this case: sentience is not a randomly distributed property like a manufacturing defect, so either insects are sentient or they are not. There is no population of cases for a probability to range over, and the arithmetic therefore has nothing to latch onto.

(3) Niamh said this confuses a metaphysical claim with a decision-theoretic one: expected-value reasoning governs action under uncertainty whether or not the uncertain proposition is stochastic, which is precisely why we apply it to one-off decisions as well.

(4) Owain conceded that, but argued it cut against her. On the same logic, a very small credence that pebbles have experiences, multiplied by the number of pebbles, would swamp everything else too; so Niamh owes an account of why the insect credence is respectable and the pebble credence is not. If she can give one, it will be an account of the evidence, in which case the evidence rather than the arithmetic is doing the work, and the scale argument is either redundant or a licence for fanaticism.

Please adjudicate each of the four moves separately, indicating where each is right and where it overreaches, and then say which of the two has the better of the exchange overall. I would particularly like a clear verdict on whether the distinction Owain draws in (2) between stochastic and non-stochastic uncertainty is a real one, and on whether his dilemma in (4) genuinely exhausts Niamh's options — if it does not, please spell out the third option she could have taken. Kindly write your answer in British English.
