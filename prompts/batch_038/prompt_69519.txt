I have been asked at short notice to cover two fifty-minute sections of our first-year epistemology module, taking over the unit on the classical Indian theory of the pramāṇas from a colleague who is on leave. My own research is on testimony and epistemic deference in the analytic tradition, so I am not a Sanskritist and I know Nyāya only at survey level. The unit has gone badly twice running: the evaluations said it felt like religious studies, and one student wrote that the school was simply instructing us to trust priests. I said something close to that myself as a graduate student, and last week, sitting beside my father while a specialist explained a diagnosis that neither of us could have derived from the published statistics we had both been reading, I noticed how little purchase my own reduction of testimony to inference has on the cases that actually matter to me. I want these two sessions to make the Nyāya claim about testimony feel like a live philosophical position rather than an article of faith.

What I need from you is not an explanation of the material but a plan for how I should explain it. Set out the order the pieces must go in across the two sessions and what depends on what — I assume the students cannot take the question of whether testimony is a pramāṇa seriously until they grasp what a pramāṇa is supposed to be, and that the rival counts across the schools (one for Cārvāka, two for the Buddhists, four for Nyāya, six for Mīmāṃsā) are what turn the list from a catalogue into a set of argued claims. Identify the single example that should carry the argument about testimony, and say what that example must have and must lack in order to resist being reconstructed as an inference about the speaker's reliability. Do not build the session on the wild-ox case for upamāna; I have watched it slide into a seminar about metaphor. Map the misconceptions the students will arrive with or form on the spot — that a pramāṇa is a piece of evidence rather than a causal instrument, that testimony is inference under another name, that the soteriological framing of the school means its epistemology is not really epistemology, that memory and doubt ought to be on the list — and tell me the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, including how to use the Hume-style reduction they will instinctively reach for without letting my sessions collapse into the deference debate I already teach elsewhere in the term. Give me one question I can put to them at the end that separates those who have grasped the irreducibility claim from those who have merely memorised four names. Tell me what to cut and to announce that I am cutting: I suspect the doctrine that validity is extrinsic to a cognition, the modes of sense-object contact, and the postulation and non-apprehension pramāṇas, but I want your judgement on whether any of these is load-bearing for what I am trying to do. The reading pack is fixed, so please recommend no sources and cite no texts. Finally, since I have no way of checking your scholarship: state how confident you are in each substantive claim you make about the tradition, and single out the point at which you are most likely to be wrong or to have imported an analytic category where it does not belong.
