Last fall I accepted a seat as the unaffiliated, non-scientist member of my university's committee that reviews animal research protocols. I took it because I assumed it would supply good material for class. Six meetings in, I have found the room difficult in a specific and philosophical way. The committee weighs harms against benefits continuously and in complete seriousness; it will spend twenty minutes arguing about whether a particular blood draw counts as momentary or minor pain. And yet nobody has ever written down how much a mouse counts, or a pig, or a macaque, relative to anything else. When I asked at my second meeting whether we had a scale, the chair regarded me as though I had asked whether we had opinions about the weather. We do have a scale. It is simply never stated, it is never applied consistently, and every so often it inverts in a way the whole room notices and then declines to discuss.

I teach an upper-division ethics seminar — fourteen students, one eighty-minute meeting, three of them pre-veterinary — and this term we are reading the section of *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974, pp. 35–42) in which Nozick asks, of beings who count for something, "which animals count, how much do they count, and how can this be determined?" and then proposes a two-tier arrangement: maximize the total happiness of all living beings, but hold stringent side constraints only over what may be done to human beings. My students can recite that arrangement back to me. I do not want another session in which they recite it. I want them to be obliged to construct it, and then to be embarrassed by what they have constructed.

Please design that session as a runnable in-class deliberation. The students should spend most of it inside a fictional review committee modeled on the one I sit on, and they should run into the weighting problem before anyone names it.

Here is what I need from you:

**Roles and split.** Fourteen students, one eighty-minute period, no additional sessions. I want a structure in which one subgroup writes a numerical weighting rule — how much each species on the docket counts — and a second subgroup, which has privately recorded its own intuitive verdicts beforehand, is then required to apply the first group's rule mechanically and report the results. The comparison between the two sets of verdicts is the engine of the exercise; tell me how to run it so the discrepancy surfaces on its own rather than through my prompting.

**The docket.** Five or six protocol summaries, each readable in under two minutes, written in the flat administrative register of a real review packet. I want at least one protocol in which the animals involved would never have existed at all but for the study, so that the calculus appears to benefit the very party it harms. I want at least one in which the harm to the animals is a foreseen side effect of a procedure that helps animals rather than a means to a human benefit — the structure of Nozick's bat-and-cow case, where, as he puts it, the skull is smashed "as a side effect (not a means)." And I want a final item introduced only after the weighting rule has been fixed and posted, in which the cost to the human side is trivial and the cost to the animal side is enormous — the shape of the finger-snapping case, in which a trivial act causes "10,000 contented, unowned cows to die after great pain and suffering."

**The two rule statements.** The exact wording of the instructions handed out in round one, under a single-tier maximizing rule, and in round two, when the committee is informed of a policy change introducing side constraints covering only human subjects. The change must be delivered as a bureaucratic update, not as a revelation, and the handouts must not mention Nozick.

**A timeline** by the minute, with the materials list, including anything I need to print.

**A debrief** that maps specific moments in the room onto the argument: the unstatable weighting function, the placement of the tier line, the means/side-effect distinction, and Nozick's dismissal of the brutalization defense ("Do butchers commit more murders?").

**Two honest notes.** First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — including the near-certainty that the weighting group will produce a proxy measure such as a neocortex ratio or a pain-behavior score that turns out to be a species ranking in disguise, and the risk that my three pre-veterinary students either dominate or go quiet. Second, and more important: what this activity structurally cannot reproduce. My suspicion is that a committee deliberating about third parties mislocates the question entirely, since Nozick's side constraints bind an agent's own conduct rather than an institution's aggregation, and that I will have to supply that displacement by hand. Tell me whether you agree, and what I should say.

They will guess where the session is headed by around minute forty. Build something that still works once they have guessed.
