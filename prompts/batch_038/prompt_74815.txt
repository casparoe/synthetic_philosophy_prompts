I have been auditing a reading group on semantic anti-realism, and the session that undid me was the one on the paradox of knowability. I follow the derivation: from the claim that every truth is possibly known, together with the factivity of knowledge and its closure under conjunction, one can show that every truth is already known, which is absurd; so the knowability principle cannot hold without restriction. My difficulty is not the proof but the premise I want to keep. I have been trying to sort candidate truths into those that could in principle be known and those that could not, on the assumption that some principled line must exist, and my verdicts refuse to line up. They are these.

1. How many raindrops struck the roof of a particular farmhouse in Vermont on one morning in October 1873, uncounted and unrecorded. Knowable in principle: a person could have stood there with a tally sheet. It is an ordinary contingent fact about the past.
2. That same fact conjoined with its own permanent ignorance: the drops numbered *n*, and no one ever knows that they numbered *n*. Not knowable, and I am quite untroubled by this; I am willing to grant that some true proposition lies beyond knowledge forever.
3. The bare generalization: there is some truth that no one ever knows. Knowable, I think. I can come to see that our knowledge is incomplete without being able to name a single instance of the incompleteness, and knowing this does not appear to undercut it.
4. What it is like, from the inside, to see crimson. Knowable by me, and I am inclined to say unknowable by anyone else — which makes me suspect that knowability is not a one-place property of propositions at all, and that my other verdicts have been sliding between two different notions.
5. Whether I shall freely accept a certain job offer in the spring of 2051. Not knowable — and this is the one that embarrasses me, because not for the reason that worked in (2). Nothing about my coming to know it would make it false. I hold this verdict only half-confidently: perhaps if the future is genuinely open there is no such truth to be known, but I cannot tell whether that is a principled retreat or a dodge.
6. Whether a volcano erupted last year on a planet in a galaxy that has already passed beyond our cosmological event horizon. A real truth, on my view, but not knowable by any creature in the causal future of this region. I refuse to demote it from truth to non-truth, and I also refuse to call it knowable, so my two columns may simply not be exhaustive.
7. The one I keep returning to: how many fronds were on a particular *Archaeopteris* at noon on a day in the Devonian, when nothing on earth could count. I want to call this knowable, yet there was no possible knower at the time, so "could have been known" must mean something like "would have been knowable had a counter been present" — and I cannot see what makes that counterfactual true other than the fact itself, at which point knowability seems to be doing no work beyond restating truth.

The only criterion I have managed to articulate is that a truth is knowable just in case its being known would not falsify it; someone in the group said there is a term of art for the class that test picks out, something Cartesian, but a label does not tell me why the class is principled rather than ad hoc. It handles (2) and, I think, (3), and it plainly fails on (5), and I do not know what it says about (4), (6), or (7). So: is there a principle, or a short disjunction of clauses, that delivers these verdicts? Please state it precisely enough that I could apply it myself to sentences I have not thought of — ideally as a restriction on the knowability claim rather than as a list of examples — and say which of my cases motivates which clause, and whether the resulting principle is unified or a patchwork. Then give me its verdict on two or three further cases of your own choosing, flagging any where you expect me to resist the result, and tell me plainly which of my seven verdicts no plausible principle will save, and what I would have to give up to keep the rest. One last thing: I cannot chase down sources just now, so I would rather you argued the matter than pointed me somewhere to read, and I want the answer to mark the boundary between what is settled here — the shape of the proof, which assumptions do the work, what follows if one of them is dropped — and what remains genuinely disputed among people who work on this. Several in my group talk as though there were a standard diagnosis of the paradox; I suspect there is not, and I would rather not have a contested position presented to me as the consensus.
