My father retires in six weeks after thirty-one years as purchasing director at a mid-sized furniture manufacturer — thirty-one years of being paid to find the cheapest acceptable place to buy components, and of quietly resisting the parts of that job he thought were corrosive. I've been asked to give the toast at his dinner. The room will be roughly half industry people (buyers, two factory owners he dealt with for decades, one competitor he counts as a friend) and half family who have never heard the word Moloch and wouldn't know what to do with it. I only came across that word this spring, mid-argument with him about why he finally signed off on a supplier he'd spent fifteen years holding out against; it gave me a name for the thing I hadn't been able to say, which was that he'd been right at every step and it hadn't helped.

Please write a five-to-seven-minute toast built on exactly that: each decision locally correct, and the sequence of correct decisions what lowered the floor under everyone's feet. It isn't a cynical claim, but it is very easy to make it sound cynical, and I need it to land as the opposite. So it can't praise him for having stayed clean, since staying clean wasn't among the moves available to him; it can't indict the competitors, who will be sitting in the room; and it can't close by telling my mother the system beat him, which would turn thirty-one years into weather. I'd rather it find the room a person does have inside a race — which race to enter at all, how fast to descend, whom you refuse to leave behind on the way down — and let her feel that what she carried was the price of choices he made, not of something that merely happened to us. One joke the industry half recognizes and the family half still laughs at. No jargon, no tragedy of the commons, no quotations.
