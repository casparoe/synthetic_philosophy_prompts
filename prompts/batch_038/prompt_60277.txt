I retired three years ago after forty-one years in an analytical chemistry laboratory, most of them devoted to the unglamorous business of persuading instruments to tell the truth. Since then I have been reading philosophy with something like the diligence I once reserved for methods sections, and I have arrived, by a roundabout route, at the question of how a person's choices over time are to be assessed. I should like your help with a difficulty I have not been able to resolve on my own.

For what follows, please adopt the role of a decision theorist who works on diachronic rationality — money pumps, dynamic inconsistency, plans, precommitment — and who is accustomed to explaining formal structure to a scientifically literate non-specialist. Think the problem through carefully, and in particular do not settle upon your first candidate principle. I shall describe eight situations on which I have already formed verdicts, some by intuition and some by argument. I want you to work out whether any principle, or small set of rival principles, generates those verdicts; to say which of them must be abandoned if the principle is to remain simple; and to tell me what the principle implies about situations I have not thought of.

I am not asking for references. I no longer hold a library card, and I would rather have your reasoning than a list of names, some of which I suspect would be invented. Please use standard technical labels where they exist, but make no claim of the form "so-and-so argued that."

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

C1. My nephew has strict preferences over three bottles of Burgundy: he prefers the Beaune to the Pommard, the Pommard to the Volnay, and the Volnay to the Beaune. A merchant at a fair who somehow divined this walked him around a circle — he took my nephew's Volnay plus two euro and handed over a Pommard, then the Pommard plus two euro for a Beaune, then the Beaune plus two euro for a Volnay. My nephew finished the afternoon with the bottle he had started with and six euro less. Verdict: irrational, and not merely unfortunate.

C2. The same episode taken one offer at a time. At the third offer my nephew held the Beaune and was invited to pay two euro for the Volnay, which he prefers to the Beaune; to refuse would have been, on the face of it, to pay for the privilege of keeping the worse bottle. Each acceptance, considered in isolation, looks like a strict improvement. Verdict: I want to say that every step was permissible and the sequence was not — but I cannot say what the sequence contains that the steps do not.

C3. A friend of mine cycles among three recordings of the same symphony through a lending circle that charges nothing. She prefers this conductor to that, that to the third, and the third to the first; she goes round the circle several times a year, ends where she began, has enjoyed every exchange, and has lost nothing. Verdict: no irrationality whatever. Note that her preference structure is identical to my nephew's, and only the environment differs.

C4. Each year I book a holiday. In January I prefer August in the mountains to August at the sea. In May, with the mountains booked and freely cancellable, I prefer the sea. In July, with the sea booked and freely cancellable, I prefer the mountains again. Every switch carries a rebooking fee, and over the decades I have paid a good many of them. Verdict: not irrational — my tastes are successive rather than simultaneous, and at no moment have I been of two minds. I concede that an enterprising travel agent could make a living from me, and it is that concession which troubles me.

C5. I sat for years on a funding committee of three whose preferences over three proposals were, in majority terms, cyclical. The chair eventually discovered that by ordering the votes he could elect whichever proposal he pleased. Verdict: the outcome was arbitrary and the chair was manipulative, but I would not call any member irrational in the sense I have in mind at C1, and I am unsure whether the committee itself was irrational or merely badly designed.

C6a. Before a wine auction I gave my broker a written ceiling together with an instruction not to accept telephoned revisions from me. On the afternoon in question I wished to raise the ceiling, and I judged — sincerely, and not so far as I can detect in the grip of anything I would call a passion — that a ten percent increase was worth the risk. Verdict: raising it would have been irrational, notwithstanding that at that moment it was what I most preferred and what I took to be the better course.

C6b. A colleague was in precisely the same predicament, except that his broker offered no such facility and he had no means of binding himself. He bid foolishly and lost money. Verdict: not irrational, or at any rate not culpable. Note that here again the acts are identical and only the availability of an instrument differs.

C7. The one I keep returning to. A colleague accepted three successive administrative posts, each at the moment of acceptance a clear improvement on the last, with no cycle anywhere in the sequence and no fee charged at any step, and finished as dean of a faculty he had once intended to leave, with no route back to the bench. He maintains he made no mistake at any point. Verdict: undecided. I feel both that he is right and that the terminus condemns the path, and I cannot tell whether that is one intuition or two.

My questions are these. First: is there a principle, or a short list of rival principles, for classifying sequences of choice as diachronically rational or irrational that delivers my verdicts, or as many of them as it honestly can? Second: which pairs of verdicts cannot both be kept? I draw your attention especially to C1 against C3, where the preference structures are identical and my verdicts are opposite, and to C6a against C6b, where the acts are identical and only the instrumental possibilities differ. Tell me whether a single account can preserve both contrasts; if it cannot, say which verdict you would overturn and what you would put in its place. Third: what does your recommended principle imply for C7, where I have no verdict, and for C5, where I am not sure the notion applies at all? Fourth: construct three further cases of your own, chosen so that your rival candidates come apart on them, and give your ruling on each.

As to format. Please begin with a discursive working section in ordinary prose, of whatever length the problem requires, in which you enumerate the candidate principles that occur to you, state each precisely enough that a novel case could be classified under it without further explanation, and test each against all eight verdicts. Do not commit to a recommendation in your opening paragraph. Then, after the prose and clearly separated from it, give me a single valid JSON object with the following fields and nothing outside it:

- "candidate_principles": array of objects, each with "id" (a short tag such as P1); "label" (four words or fewer); "statement" (one to three sentences, precise enough to decide a case you have not seen); "clauses" (array of objects, each with "text", giving one clause of the principle, and "earned_by", giving the identifier of the case that forces that clause); "reproduces" (array of case identifiers whose verdicts it matches); "overturns" (array of objects, each with "case_id", "replacement_verdict", and "reason"); and "price" (one sentence on what adopting it costs, in simplicity or in revisionism).
- "recommendation": object with "id"; "why" (three sentences at most); and "strongest_rival" (the runner-up's id plus one sentence on what it does better).
- "case_rulings": array of objects, one per case, each with "case_id"; "my_verdict" (your one-line restatement of what I said); "ruling" (exactly one of upheld, overturned, reinterpreted); and "comment".
- "hard_pairs": array of objects, each with "cases" (array of identifiers); "both_preservable" (true or false); "resolution"; and "if_not_which_goes".
- "new_cases": array of three objects, each with "description"; "ruling_under_recommendation"; and "discriminates" (the ids of the candidates that disagree about the case).
- "open_question": one to three sentences on the element of C7 that I should not expect to resolve.

Use exactly the identifiers C1, C2, C3, C4, C5, C6a, C6b and C7. Leave no field empty; where you have nothing to say, say that explicitly rather than omitting the field.
