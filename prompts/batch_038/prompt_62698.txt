I am a fourth-year philosophy student in a metaethics seminar whose final four weeks have been devoted to the wrong kind of reasons problem, and for our last session each of us has been asked to contribute a one-page instrument rather than a paper. What I want to build is a decision tree that sorts a reader by what she actually says about cases, not by which label she prefers: someone who has never met the literature should be able to work through it, while someone who has should arrive at the position she would recognize as her own. Please write that tree for me. Structurally, I would like six to eight decision nodes and at least eight terminal positions. Every node must be phrased as a concrete situation followed by two or three answers the reader can choose between without knowing where each answer leads — no node may ask whether the reader accepts a buck-passing account of value, or whether fittingness is primitive, since those are only the labels in disguise. Each leaf should carry the name of the position where one exists (or "unnamed" where nobody defends it), a sentence or two stating the view in the reader's own terms, and the commitment that view finds most awkward. Please also flag explicitly any pair of leaves my tree cannot genuinely separate, and — this matters to me more than the rest — include at least two combinations of answers that correspond to no position anybody holds, with a brief diagnosis of whether each empty cell is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Position names at the leaves are welcome, but I do not want a bibliography or an account of who argued what: the value of the handout is entirely in the sorting.

As to content, the seminar's stock cases should not do all the work. Alongside the demon and the paid admirer, I would like nodes built around (i) a consideration that counts against believing something without touching its truth at all — say, that the proposition is trivial and believing it would crowd out thoughts worth having — because I want the tree to test whether the right/wrong distinction and the object/state distinction really come apart; (ii) a case in which someone is offered a reward for intending to do something she would have no reason at all to do; and (iii) an attitude nobody has any duty about, such as regret or amusement, where the intuition that there are wrong-kind reasons is much less secure. The leaves must include the view that reasons bear on the question an attitude settles; the view that wrong-kind considerations are reasons not for the attitude but for wanting or trying to have it; a fittingness-first view on which such considerations are not reasons for the attitude at all; a view that grants wrong-kind reasons full standing and treats the problem as merely verbal or as a matter of weight; and the value-theorist who accepts both kinds of consideration as reasons but restricts the analysis of value to the right kind. Finally, a classmate of mine insists she occupies a stable position — wrong-kind considerations are genuine reasons for the attitude, but they can never conflict with right-kind ones — and I would like the instrument to show whether that is an occupied cell or a confusion, so please make sure the tree adjudicates it rather than gliding past it.

Begin your response with the tree itself, with no preface, no restatement of the task, and no commentary afterwards.
