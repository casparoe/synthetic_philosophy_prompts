**The exam question (1,200 words max):**

> A research consortium publishes annual five-year forecasts of democratic backsliding for 140 states. Call it the Meridian Project. Three features are stipulated. (a) Its inputs are exclusively structural and slow-moving: executive-constraint indices, trade dependence, demographic ratios, per-capita income growth, and duration of prior democratic experience. The model contains no variables representing beliefs, ideas, or scientific and technological discoveries. (b) Its outputs are calibrated probabilities, and its lead researcher describes the product as "a forecast, not a prophecy." (c) The consortium has announced a regularity it calls the decay threshold: any state whose executive-constraint index falls below a fixed value for two consecutive years backslides within five years with probability above 0.8. The consortium presents this as a law of democratic decay.

**The student's answer:**

> Popper defines historicism as the view that the principal aim of the social sciences is historical prophecy — the announcement of the future course of history on a large scale — and that this aim can be met by discovering the laws, or the trends, of historical development. His objection is not that social science may never predict anything. He distinguishes unconditional prophecy from what he calls technological or conditional prediction, the latter having the form: if such-and-such initial conditions obtain, then such-and-such will follow. That is what the natural sciences and any applied social technology deliver. The historicist's error is therefore not to want predictions, but to want the unconditional kind, and to suppose that a science of history can supply them.
>
> Central to this is the confusion between trends and laws. A law is a universal statement, and universal statements are testable: they can be refuted by observation. A trend is an existential statement, saying that in this region of space and time a certain directional change happens to be under way. Trends depend on the conditions that sustain them, and those conditions can change, so a trend can be overturned by a change of circumstances and no unconditional prediction can be built on one. Historicists observe a trend — say, the growth of bureaucratic centralization — treat it as a law of development, and read the future off it. Historicist prophecy is thus not merely often false but confused about the logical status of its own claims.
>
> The argument Popper takes to refute historicism is this. First, the course of human history is strongly influenced by the growth of human knowledge. Second, we cannot predict by rational or scientific methods the future growth of our scientific knowledge, since to predict the content of a future discovery would be to possess that content already. Therefore we cannot predict the future course of history. Popper is careful with the second premise: he concedes that we may predict that a given problem will be solved, and even roughly when, without being able to predict the solution. The argument is not that science must fall silent about the future; it is that no prediction can be secure unless it is indifferent to what we shall come to know, and historicist prophecies are precisely the ones that are not. This is in substance Popper's way of spelling out why historicist theories are untestable: a doctrine that cannot foresee the discoveries which will reshape the events it describes cannot be brought into contact with evidence at all, and so its prophecies are insulated from refutation whatever happens.
>
> Nor does the refutation of historicism deprive the social sciences of scientific standing. Popper's alternative is situational analysis: explain a social phenomenon by reconstructing the situation of the agents involved together with the unintended consequences of their actions, and assume that agents act in a manner appropriate to their situation. This is methodological individualism without psychologism; the burden of testing falls on the model of the situation rather than on the rationality principle, which is near-empty and is not itself put at risk. Popper likewise grants that a social prediction can influence what it predicts — the Oedipus effect — but treats this as a complication cutting both ways, since a social technologist may exploit knowledge of the effect deliberately, rather than as a demonstration that social prediction is impossible. Social explanation on this account remains nomological and remains explanatory, which is more than the historicist can say for prophecy.
>
> Applied to the Meridian Project, none of this bites. The consortium's outputs are probabilities rather than certainties, and probabilistic statements are conditional statements: they hold relative to the state of the structural variables the model takes as given, and they can be tested against outcomes. They are therefore technological predictions in Popper's sense and not prophecies, and the prophecy/prediction distinction is the operative line here. Further, the first premise of the refuting argument cannot engage the project: the model's inputs contain nothing about beliefs, ideas, or discoveries, so its forecasts do not depend on the future growth of knowledge and are not exposed to the difficulty Popper identifies. The decay threshold looks like the trend-as-law confusion Popper describes, but that is a fault in how the consortium reports its finding rather than a defect in the forecasting itself: a regularity holding above probability 0.8 given specified initial conditions is a conditional statistical hypothesis, and Popper has no quarrel with those.
>
> As for soundness, the weak point is the second premise, which is stronger than the conclusion requires. A historicist can retreat to aggregate statistical forecasting, which is the Meridian strategy, and Popper must concede that this is legitimate technological prediction. His refutation then shows only that grand prophecy is unavailable, not that the social sciences cannot be predictive. Whether that amounts to a refutation of historicism or to a restatement of part of it depends on how much the historicist was ever claiming.
