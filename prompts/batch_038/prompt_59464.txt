I'm organizing an adversarial collaboration for a philosophy-of-mind workshop, and the two participants I've recruited have an unusual problem: they agree on the headline thesis and disagree about almost everything else. The first is a Malcolmian, working in the spirit of Norman Malcolm's *Dreaming* (1959) and his earlier paper on dreaming and skepticism. He holds that dreams are not experiences, that a person who is sound asleep is not conscious of anything, and that a sincere report of the form "I dreamt that p" does not imply that any event, process, or episode occurred during my sleep — with the consequence that questions like "how long did the dream last?" or "at what point in the night did it happen?" lack sense rather than merely lacking answers. The second is a Dennettian, working in the spirit of Dennett's "Are Dreams Experiences?" (1976) and his later work with Marcel Kinsbourne on the temporal locus of consciousness. She also rejects the received view that dreams are experiences occurring during sleep and subsequently recalled, but for close to the opposite reason: she treats the matter as a live empirical hypothesis about when dream content is generated, on which a subject's report may be assembled at the moment of questioning rather than read off something that happened earlier in the night, and she thinks sleep-laboratory data are exactly what could decide it. Each thinks the other's argument is a disaster that discredits a conclusion they both accept. My participants keep getting booked onto the same panels because audiences hear a single position, so I want the document that forces the difference into the open.

Please draft the joint statement the two of them could both put their names to. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims — about what a dream report is and is not evidence for, about the grammar and the epistemology of first-person dream ascriptions, about what the sleep lab has and hasn't established, and about how their shared conclusion differs from neighbouring theses it is routinely confused with (for instance: that nothing whatever happens in the head during sleep; that subjects confabulate; that there is no fact of the matter about dream content; that dreams are illusory; that dream experience is impossible to study scientifically). Mark any candidate agreement you judge to be agreement in wording only, and say why. (2) A disagreements table with four columns: a neutral statement of the issue; the formulation the Malcolmian would sign; the formulation the Dennettian would sign; and a classification of the disagreement as substantive, merely verbal, or mixed, with a one-line justification. (3) A section on cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, state what would have to be established, which camp would move and how far, what the other camp predicts, and the argument or finding both agree would bear on it. Where you conclude that part of the dispute was about words all along, say which part and diagnose the ambiguity.

To keep the cruxes from being settled by armchair intuition alone, here are two stipulated results from a hypothetical study, which I'd like treated as pre-registered test cases. In the *playback* design, a ninety-second spoken narrative is presented at low volume during stage N2; the subject is woken from REM roughly forty minutes later, and in a large majority of trials produces a report that recognizably incorporates the narrative's content — but describes it as visually rich rather than auditory, denies having heard any voice, and gives an elapsed dream time of ten to fifteen minutes. In the *wording* design, the same subjects, woken from N1 or N3 with no intervening REM period, produce confident, contentful dream reports at high rates when asked "what were you just dreaming?" and near-zero rates when asked "were you dreaming just now?". For each result, record in advance what each camp predicts about it, what each camp says it demonstrates, and — crucially for the Malcolmian — why she denies that anything of this shape could bear on a question about the concepts of dreaming, experience, and occurrence during sleep at all. If the Dennettian thinks the playback result favours retroactive assembly over real-time experience, and the Malcolmian thinks it changes nothing, I want the reasoning laid side by side rather than averaged.

A few constraints. Do not adjudicate: I don't want a verdict on who is right, and I don't want the two positions collapsed into a shared "dreams aren't experiences" camp, which is precisely the error the exercise exists to correct. Neither side may be a strawman — every formulation must be one its own proponent would sign, not a caricature an opponent would write. Avoid a dramatized dialogue; the deliverable is a formal collaborative document. Please flag anywhere you suspect I've mis-stated one of the two positions, rather than silently repairing it. Aim for something in the region of 1,200 to 1,800 words that my two participants could actually sign without wincing.
