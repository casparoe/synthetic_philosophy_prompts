The indirect reciprocity literature has a familiar arc, as I understand it. If observers judge a donor purely by the act — helped, so good; refused, so bad — cooperation unravels as soon as you add any error, because a cooperator who fails to help gets branded bad and is then refused in turn, and the branding spreads. The repair is to use assessment rules that look at whether the recipient deserved the help, so that refusing a bad person is itself counted good. Everyone agrees the repair handles noise better.

What I think both camps assume without arguing is that the whole population runs the *same* assessment rule. Normative homogeneity goes in as a simplifying assumption, but out in the world it's an empirical claim about communities, and it's false at any scale bigger than a village.

My conjecture: a minority of naive first-order judges might be functional rather than a leftover. The mechanism I have in mind — no idea whether it survives contact with the math — is that the lenient second-order rules open an exploitable niche. Refuse help to whoever is currently marked bad and your own reputation stays clean, because your refusal was justified. A strict first-order judge doesn't accept that excuse at all. So scattering strict judges through the population closes the loophole, at the cost of sometimes mislabeling unlucky cooperators. That points toward a non-monotonic relationship between the fraction of naive judges and cooperation at equilibrium — an interior optimum instead of "more consensus is better."

Then there's the wrinkle I can't get past, and I want you to face it rather than smooth over it: once observers disagree, reputation stops being a single public variable. Does the donor act on her own assessment of the recipient? On the assessment of the audience she expects to hear about the encounter? Is there one shared ledger with disagreement only entering at the update step? Each choice produces a different game and probably a different answer, and I honestly don't know which one I mean.
