I am seventeen and in my final year of secondary school, and for the past year I have run a Tuesday-evening philosophy group of eight students in my school library, working mostly from an anthology on population ethics that our teacher, Mr. Adeyemi, managed to obtain for us. Two friends and I are drafting an essay for a national high-school essay competition (2,500-word limit, which is part of why I need your help being economical), and we have settled on the question of whether "better than" must be transitive. What I want from you is not a survey of that debate — I have read enough summaries to know the general shape of it — but a scored evaluation of a set of positions against a case of my own construction, because I suspect the standard spectrum cases are doing less work than people assume and I want to find out whether mine is any different.

The case, which we have been calling the Reading Room, is as follows. Consider one hundred and one possible arrangements of a public reading room, labelled R0 through R100. Three features vary: the quality of the light, how well the chairs support good posture, and the level of background noise. The sequence is built in three blocks. In the first block (R0 to R33) each step makes the light marginally worse and the chairs marginally better. In the second block (R34 to R66) each step makes the chairs marginally worse and the room marginally quieter. In the third block (R67 to R100) each step makes the room marginally noisier and the light marginally better. The magnitudes are chosen so that, over the whole sequence, R100 ends up worse than R0 on all three features at once: worse light, worse chairs, and more noise. When we put R0, R50 and R100 in front of the group in isolation, all eight of us ranked them R0 best, R100 worst, with no dissent, and we took R100's being worse on every feature to make that verdict about as secure as an evaluative verdict can be. But when we walked the sequence step by step and asked, at each of the hundred steps, whether the later arrangement was better than the earlier one, the majority verdict at every single step was that neither was better: each step was called an even trade. Two further observations are worth recording, because they are what I actually want evaluated. First, the reason the group gave for calling a step even shifted from block to block — in the first block they treated a small postural gain as worth a small loss of light, in the second a small reduction in noise as worth a small postural loss, and in the third a small gain in light as worth a small increase in noise, so our implicit ranking of the three features against one another goes in a circle. Second, we re-ran the sequence backwards, from R100 to R0; three of the eight then reported a strict preference at a scattering of steps they had previously called even, and none of them could say what was special about those particular steps. So the strict verdicts survive isolation and the parity verdicts do not survive a change of presentation order.

Please score and rank the following six responses to that case. I have tried to describe each accurately, but I would rather you correct me than flatter me, so say plainly if any of my glosses misstates the view. (1) Temkin-style intransitivism: "all things considered better than" is genuinely intransitive, because the factors that matter vary with the pair being compared, and my circular trade structure is evidence for exactly that. (2) Parfit-style imprecision: adjacent pairs are neither better, nor worse, nor exactly equal, but imprecisely equal, and imprecision does not transmit, so nothing contradicts the dominance of R0 over R100. (3) The epistemicist response: every step really is a difference in betterness that we simply cannot detect, and the backwards-run instability is the tell. (4) Ruth Chang's parity as a fourth positive relation, together with her point that comparisons are relative to covering considerations — which might explain my case by saying the covering consideration changes at R34 and R67. (5) The response built on what I understand to be Broome's collapsing principle: if adding a small improvement to one of two items on a par would make it better, they were never on a par, so some step in my sequence must be strictly ordered whether or not we can find it. (6) A Millian discontinuity view: value has thresholds, so somewhere in the sequence a marginal loss of light or posture must stop being compensable by a marginal gain elsewhere, and our hundred even-trade verdicts include at least one that is simply false.

For each of the six, give me a score out of ten on three separate dimensions — how well it handles the fact that R100 is dominated by R0 on all three features, how well it handles the hundred even-trade verdicts, and how well it handles the backwards-run instability — then an overall tier (A through D) and one sentence of justification for the tier. After the table, tell me which of the six you would be willing to defend in the essay if you had to pick one, which single observation from my case is the most diagnostic between the leading candidates, and what the strongest objection to your pick would be that you could not answer within the word limit. My friend Priya thinks the whole exercise is rigged because I built the case so that the endpoint verdict is dominance-based and therefore too secure to be given up, and she thinks an honest version would have made R100 better than R0 on one feature; please tell me whether she is right, and whether her version would change your rankings.
