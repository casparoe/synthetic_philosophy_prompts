I spent forty-two years measuring things — first at a national laboratory, then in a university department — and I retired four years ago. Philosophy is what I do now, and I find that I read it the way I used to read a colleague's manuscript: I keep looking for the load-bearing claims and asking what would happen if I removed them. My present subject is Freud on religion. I have read The Future of an Illusion closely, and enough of Totem and Taboo and Moses and Monotheism to see that his case against religious belief is not one argument but several, of very uneven strength, generally braided together so that a weak strand borrows credibility from a strong one.

I have separated out five strands. Please score them rather than describe them.

1. The wish-fulfillment strand. Freud's own definition is that a belief counts as an illusion when wish-fulfillment is a prominent factor in its motivation, and he insists that an illusion need not be false. Yet the polemical weight of the passage comes from sorting religious doctrines into that category, alongside such things as the nationalist claim that only one's own race is capable of civilization, which he contrasts with mere errors such as the Aristotelian view that vermin arise from dung.
2. The neurosis strand. Religion is a universal obsessional neurosis, and the parallels between religious ceremonials and the compulsive acts of his patients are offered as evidence for the diagnosis.
3. The projection strand. God is the infantile father magnified and exported; adult helplessness before nature and death never disappears, so the wish for a protector persists into maturity.
4. The phylogenetic strand. The murder of the primal father by the band of brothers, inherited memory traces, the totem meal as prototype of sacrifice, and the fate of Moses as a delayed historical trauma.
5. The warrant strand. Religious doctrines present themselves as assertions about facts, are transmitted on the authority of ancestors and inherited proofs, and are shielded by a prohibition on questioning them; since they make factual claims, Freud holds them to the ordinary standard of evidence, and maintains that they cannot meet it.

For each strand, give me two integers from 0 to 10.

(a) Premise durability — how much of the strand survives when I subtract everything Freud could only have believed because he was a Viennese clinician working in the first decades of the twentieth century, keeping only what still stands on evidence a careful investigator today would accept.
(b) Truth-bearingness — given whatever survives (a), how much of it bears on the truth of the religious propositions themselves, as opposed to their origins, their functions, or the character of those who hold them.

Then assign each strand a tier: Tier 1, still a live argument that a philosophically trained opponent must answer; Tier 2, partially salvageable, with the specific repair stated; Tier 3, of historical interest only, in the way phlogiston is. Defend each tier boundary you draw in a short paragraph, and say explicitly which of your own tier assignments you find least secure.

Two further questions, and I want direct answers to both.

First, an objection I keep putting to myself. Kepler supplemented his income by casting horoscopes, and no one thinks the planetary laws are weakened by that fact; the causal history of a belief seems silent on its truth-value. Which of the five strands, if any, is genuinely immune to this objection — and where there is immunity, is it real, or merely a sign that the strand has quietly stopped making a factual claim?

Second, tell me whether any of the five is not an argument at all but a rhetorical device: a move whose function is to make the other four sound stronger than they are. If so, name it and explain the mechanism by which it does that work.

Please do not moderate the scores to avoid offending either believers or Freud's admirers, and do not hand me a column of fives. I would rather have a decisive ranking I can spend a month arguing with than a diplomatic one.
