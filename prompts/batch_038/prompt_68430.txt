I spent most of last night on a badly delayed train arguing with a friend — a corpus linguist who works on hedging, so she came at this from the data end and I came at it from the norms end — about what governs assertion. She ended up pressing a charge I have not managed to shake: that the debate over the norm of assertion is idle, because every candidate norm issues the same verdict on every assertion anyone actually makes, and the candidates diverge only on cases constructed for the express purpose of making them diverge. If she is right, then choosing among them is a matter of taste in machinery rather than of substance, and I am about to build a graduate reading group around a merely verbal dispute. I would rather test her charge than continue arguing with it, and I think the honest way to test it is to score the candidates explicitly on a fixed rubric instead of trading intuitions one at a time. That is what I want from you.

The candidates are six: the knowledge norm (assert *p* only if you know *p*); the truth norm (assert *p* only if *p* is true); the belief norm (assert *p* only if you believe *p*); the reasonable-to-believe norm (assert *p* only if it is reasonable for you to believe *p*); the reliability norm (assert *p* only if your belief that *p* is produced by a faculty that is in fact reliable); and a conjunctive norm requiring both that *p* be true and that you believe *p*. Score each from 0 to 10 on three axes. **Axis A, case fit:** how well the norm's verdicts match ordinary judgment across the five situations below, where you should say which cases do the damage. **Axis B, consultability:** whether a speaker could actually apply the norm in real time, before uttering, without first having to settle the very question the norm is meant to regulate — my friend's point about the lottery is that a norm which can only be evaluated after the draw is not action-guiding at all, and I want to know how much each candidate suffers from this. **Axis C, non-idle content:** how much the norm buys you beyond what any norm licensing only true assertions would buy. For Axis C in particular, say whether you can describe a real setting — a newsroom, a courtroom, a clinic, a classroom — in which the norm comes apart from at least one rival over assertions people actually make; if you cannot, cap the score at 3. Weight the axes 0.5 for A, 0.3 for B, and 0.2 for C.

The five situations. (1) A woman walking past a field sees what is in fact a real sheep standing behind a hedge, though what presents itself to her is a dog disguised to look like one; her belief that there is a sheep in the field is true but true by luck, and she says so aloud. (2) A man holds one ticket in a ten-million-ticket lottery with a single winner and, before the draw, says his ticket will lose. (3) A schoolteacher in a community where creationism is the received view cannot bring herself to believe evolutionary theory, though she recognizes the evidence for it as overwhelming; she tells her class that human beings share a common ancestor with the other apes, and what she says is true. (4) A man says it is raining outside, adding in the same breath that he does not know that it is and is only going by the sound; the first conjunct seems unobjectionable and the whole utterance seems somehow absurd. (5) A speaker with a faculty she has no reason to trust correctly reports where a certain public figure currently is; the report is true, her belief is produced by a genuinely reliable process, she has no evidence for it, and it would not be reasonable for her to believe it.

Deliver this as follows, in under 750 words total: a bottom line of one sentence up front, naming the winning norm under my weighting and stating plainly whether my friend's charge survives; then a table with the six norms as rows and the three axes plus the weighted total as columns, scores to one decimal; then a tier list of A through D derived from the totals; then exactly one sentence per norm saying which of the five cases does the decisive work or the decisive damage; then three sentences on whether my weights are defensible, and, if they are not, one line giving the ranking you would defend instead. Please do not cite anything or tell me who has argued what — I want your scoring, not your sources — and if you think one of my five cases is a dud because the intuitions about it are too unstable to discriminate between rivals, say so and drop it from Axis A rather than scoring it generously.
