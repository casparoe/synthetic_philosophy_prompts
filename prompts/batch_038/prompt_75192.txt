I lead reliability engineering at a mid-sized cloud provider, and my working vocabulary is the vocabulary of incident review: timelines of events, causal graphs, proximate triggers versus latent conditions, contributing factors, counterfactual leverage (which single node, if it had behaved differently, changes the outcome), root cause as a contested summary judgment, action items with named owners, and the blameless-postmortem convention that forbids us from writing up an incident as anyone's fault. I have never studied diplomatic history and I do not want the debate handed back to me in its native terms. I would like you to translate the Fischer controversy over responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War into my language, as faithfully as you can, and then report on what did not survive.

First, the translation. Restate Fischer's central charge — that Imperial Germany used the July crisis to act on plans for a war it had willed, with the assurance of unconditional support given to Vienna in early July 1914 and the weight Fischer placed on the December 1912 meeting of the Kaiser with his senior military and naval advisers as evidence of a prior decision — as an incident-analysis claim. Draw the graph: which events are nodes, which conditions are latent and predate the incident window entirely, where the asserted root cause sits, and what the counterfactual test is for it. Then do the same for the main revisionist replies: the mobilization timetables and railway schedules that compressed decision times, the alliance structure that propagated a regional crisis into a continental one, the argument that Russian agency in the last days of July has been underweighted, and the claim that no government either understood or controlled the escalation, so that the actors were surprised by an outcome each had partly produced.

Second, treat the normative layer the same way, because this is where I suspect my vocabulary is doing something it was not designed to do. When Fischer says Germany willed the war, is that a claim about a node in the graph, a claim about the mental states of an actor, or a claim about how the summary line at the top of the report should be written? When a revisionist says the war was the system's fault rather than anyone's, is that a claim that no node has high counterfactual leverage, or a claim about the level at which nodes should be drawn — treating a state as one agent versus treating ministries, general staffs, and pressure groups as separate ones? Where does the granularity choice do the argumentative work, and could the whole controversy be a dispute about node boundaries rather than about causes?

Third, the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which of these historical claims translate cleanly into causal-graph, leverage, and action-item terms? Which translate only if my vocabulary carries more weight than it does in engineering, and where exactly does the distortion sit? Which have no counterpart at all — culpability as distinct from causal contribution, the standing to blame, the difference between an organisation deciding and an organisation failing to decide, the fact that a war aims programme adopted after hostilities began is being used as evidence about intentions before them? Our convention forbids naming individual blame by design; tell me whether Fischer's project is therefore inexpressible in that convention, and whether that makes the convention a better instrument or a narrower one. Conclude with your verdict: does the residue indicate something my framework is missing, or does it indicate that the historians are arguing about something less substantive than it appears?

Finally, tell me what would change your verdict. Identify the two or three mappings you have proposed that you consider most fragile and say what kind of consideration would overturn each — a distinction you have collapsed, a use of "cause" or "intend" in the historical debate that does not behave the way your translation assumes, or a normative claim you have quietly converted into a causal one. Flag explicitly wherever you are reconstructing a historian's position from the shape of the controversy rather than from anything you can pin down, and give me your confidence in each part of the translation.
