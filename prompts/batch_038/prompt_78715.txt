You're a philosopher of medicine who's run a lot of these workshops. I'm building a 90-minute session for a mixed room -- a couple of transplant coordinators, some ICU residents, one law student, no philosophers -- on the question of when a patient has died. I want a decision tree that sorts them by what they actually believe rather than which slogan they'd pick, so it has to be built out of cases.

Start by brainstorming candidate branch questions, then commit to a tree of 8-10 nodes. Every node has to be answerable by someone who has never heard the phrase "whole-brain death" or "higher-brain criterion" -- so no node may name a criterion, a theorist, or a statute. Use concrete situations: the anencephalic newborn with a working brainstem; the patient whose entire brain including the brainstem is destroyed but whose body is kept warm, ventilated and hormonally supported for months on somatic support; the controlled DCD donor where circulation has been absent for a fixed hands-off interval (say five minutes) and the team decided in advance not to attempt resuscitation; the cold-water drowning victim who looks dead and revives; the patient who spontaneously re-establishes a circulation a couple of minutes after compressions stop; the vegetative-state patient who breathes unaided; locked-in syndrome as a control.

Two constraints on the branch content: at least two nodes must turn on what "irreversible" means -- irreversible in principle vs. simply not going to be reversed because a decision has already been made -- and at least one node should expose whether the dead-donor rule is doing covert work, i.e. whether someone is shading the definition of death in order to keep organ retrieval legal.

Then derive the leaves from the answer patterns first, before you reach for any labels. I want the logical space mapped, with the empty cells flagged and diagnosed: for each, say whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered. Include one leaf for the view that declaring death is a normative status we confer rather than a fact we discover, and tell me honestly whether your questions can seperate that from higher-brain death or whether they collapse.

End with a short list of the pairs of positions your tree cannot seperate and why. Think carefully about the ordering -- a good tree front-loads the question that does the most sorting. No reading list or citations, just the instrument.
