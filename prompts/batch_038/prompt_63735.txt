I'm writing the framing essay for an interdisciplinary volume on memory politics — historians, a couple of legal people, one political theorist — and my co-editor's draft keeps leaning on a comparison I can't shake loose. Roughly: a country is like a person with an unprocessed trauma. Germany went into therapy after 1945 — it talked, it confessed, it built memorials, it prosecuted — and came out healthier; Japan and Turkey are repressing, and the repression shows up as symptoms: prime ministers at Yasukuni, textbook fights, a penal-code article about insulting Turkishness that was used against Dink before he was shot; the American South never did the work and has been acting out ever since, with the monuments as the acting out. The people I know in the transitional-justice NGO world run the same script, and from it they draw a fairly strong conclusion: full disclosure is the healthy option, and the countries that bought silence — Spain's pact of forgetting is the perennial exhibit — are storing up a relapse. The essay wants me to endorse something in that neighborhood. Worth noting the psychoanalytic vocabulary isn't an import, it's baked in: the Mitscherlichs' book on postwar Germany was called the inability to mourn, and Vergangenheitsbewältigung is already a working-through metaphor. So the analogy has a lot of authority behind it, which is exactly why I want it pressure-tested rather than waved off.

Here's what I need from you. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — who is the patient, what plays the repressed memory, what counts as a symptom, what's the talking cure, who is the therapist and where does their authority to diagnose come from, what's relapse, what's health — and then tell me where the mapping breaks, separating the breaks that are fatal to the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely untidy. I want to know specifically what the source domain is smuggling in that the target lacks. Therapy presupposes one continuous subject with a single autobiographical memory who at some level wants to get better; a polity has membership turnover instead of continuity, no memory of its own, and no desire that isn't just some coalition's desire. The patient of 1945 and the patient of today don't share a body or much of a population. And in the case everyone holds up as the cure, the talking started roughly two decades late and only once the children began asking their parents questions — so even the paradigm doesn't have the therapy timeline. Then there's the assignment problem I keep tripping over: in the American South, is the monument the symptom or the repression, and does pulling it down count as acting out or as the start of the talking? The framework can be read both ways with equal ease, and I suspect that's a mark against it, not a neutral feature. Same question for who the patient even is there — the descendants of enslavers, the descendants of the enslaved, both at once? They don't share a psyche, and one group's disclosure is another group's demand.

Then the payoff. Does the disclose-now-or-relapse-later prediction survive the disanalogies, or was the analogy carrying the whole weight? Spain is the hard test: amnesty, thirty-odd years of official quiet, then a judge poking at the mass graves and getting shut down, then the historical memory law and a second one fifteen years later. Is that a relapse, a delayed session, or a third thing — a negotiated settlement that got renegotiated once the balance of costs shifted? And if the therapy frame really can't carry this, give me a better comparison for the same material, something that keeps the temporal thickness without needing a unified mind — and say what that alternative would license instead, since whatever survives is what I have to build the essay around.
