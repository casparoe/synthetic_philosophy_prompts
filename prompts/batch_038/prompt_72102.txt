I've been going back and forth between the inverse RL literature and classical revealed preference theory in economics, and I keep having this nagging sense that they're the same project in different clothes — and that they hit the same wall and then climbed over it in opposite directions.

Both want to read a preference/utility/reward function off observed choice behavior. Both run into a trivialization problem. With revealed preference, a single observed choice can be rationalized by any old utility function that puts the chosen bundle on top, so the theory only has content once you demand consistency across many different budget sets (the WARP/SARP/GARP family). With IRL, the analogous problem is that the zero reward makes every policy optimal — literally any trajectory is optimal under some reward — so the problem is only well posed once you regularize the reward space or commit to a particular bounded-rationality model of the demonstrator (max-entropy style action noise, soft value iteration, that sort of thing).

My working hypothesis is that the two fields escaped in structurally different ways: revealed preference restricted the *choice function*, and thereby got falsifiable content — you can actually catch a person violating GARP in the data. IRL restricted the *hypothesis space plus the model of the actor*, and thereby got a well-posed estiamtion problem but almost no refutation: if the inferred reward predicts behavior badly you just say the human was being suboptimal, which is unfalsifiable-ish, theres no observation that counts against the model.

Is that the right way to see it? Compare the two properly and then take a side — I want to know which of these escape routes is actually better for the thing I care about, which is inferring what a person values from the mess of what they actually do (alignment flavored, not consumer-theory flavored). In particular:

(a) Is there any real analogue in IRL of the testability GARP buys you, or is the prior doing all the work and the data just selecting among prior-permitted rewards?

(b) Revealed preference famously can't separate the agent's preferences from the constraints/feasible set they face — it just takes the choice set as given. IRL's move is to put the constraints in the MDP (transitions, available actions, what the agent can observe) and estimate only the reward. How much of the original problem does that solve versus relocate? Especially: what happens when the MDP you assumed is wrong, or when the human's own beliefs about the MDP are wrong, and how does the two-field comparison help you tell those apart from a wrong reward?

(c) What does all this imply about treating observed human behavior as evidence of preferences at all, given humans are demonstrably bad planners with incoherent time preferences and self-control problems — cases where a revealed-preference economist would say an axiom is violated and an IRL person would say the reward is misspecified? Is there a principled difference between "revealed preference violation" and "reward misspecification," or is one just a relabeling of the other?

I'd much rather get a defended comparison with a verdict than a neutral survey of both positions. No need for references or a reading list — just argue it.
