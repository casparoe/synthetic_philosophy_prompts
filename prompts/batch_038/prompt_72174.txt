For about a year I sat on the committee that wrote the crisis-standards triage protocol for the medical ICU I run — 14 beds, regional referral hospital, we recieve transfers from four smaller sites. So allocation under scarcity is basically my native language: eligibility screens, priority scores, tie-breakers (lottery, first-come, most life-years saved), futility thresholds, no-worse-off clauses, load balancing between units, the transfer agreements with the hub, what we owe somebody we physically can't take. I've read almost no political philosophy — I know utilitarian and deontological and roughly what side-constraint means, and that's it.

Here's the thing I want. The refugee/asylum debate keeps getting described as having two halves: who counts as a refugee at all, and who is obligated to take them in. Triage has the same two joints — who gets on the list, and which unit carries the patient — so I figure the debate should be restatable in my vocabulary. Try it, position by position, as though we were arguing about the protocol instead of about borders. Roughly these:

- the fight over whether the persecution-based definition in the 1951 Convention actually picks out a morally real category, or whether it should be stretched to cover anyone whose basic needs can't be met at home (famine, climate, just being catastrophically poor) — i.e. the economic-migrant line and whether it's a line or a dial;
- non-refoulement: is that a duty to admit, or only a duty not to push someone back, and can a state discharge it by funding somebody else's ward?
- the claim that a political community may exclude people because it's *their* community, which I suspect is something like the existing census getting a vote on admissions;
- the fair-shares picture, where the duty is owed collectively and each state only owes its slice — plus what happens to my slice when other units flatly refuse theirs;
- and the surrogate-protection idea: protection is a stand-in for what your own state should have given you, so the obligation is the international community's and lands on whoever is geographically nearest.

For each one, tell me what maps cleanly onto allocation logic, what only maps if you bend something (and exactly where you bent it), and what has no expression at all in protocol-writing language. Flag the bends explicitly. Don't quietly load in triage's own assumptions — one decision-maker with authority over the whole pool, a fixed and knowable capacity, patients who have no say in being there, a good that can be measured in the same units for everyone — and then hand them back to me as neutral translation. If one of these positions only sounds coherent once you've smuggled those in, say so plainly.

Then the leftover, which is the part I actually care about. Two guesses going in. First, that the right-to-exclude argument has no hook whatsoever in my framework, because nobody on my committee thinks the current occupants of the ICU get to veto an admission. Second, that non-refoulement will turn out to be a constraint on where a patient goes *afterward*, which is structurally unlike anything in allocation, since allocation is entirely about who comes *in*. Tell me whether I've got those right, wrong, or half-right.

And end with a verdict rather than a shrug. For each piece that fails to translate: is the failure a limit of my vocabulary — triage is just missing a concept the refugee debate genuinely needs — or is it evidence that the original position wasn't really making a claim at all, but expressing an allegiance or leaning on a premise nobody ever argued for? Take a side per item and defend it. I'd much rather have a conclusion I can push back on than a balanced tour of both readings.

No reading list or citations at the end, please — I'm not going to look anything up and I don't want invented titles.
