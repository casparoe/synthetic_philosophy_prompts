I am a high school senior, and this spring I have been chosen to present a position paper at a university's young scholars symposium in philosophy. My paper concerns what is usually called the responsibility gap for autonomous systems, and my conclusion is unfashionable: I argue that the gap is considerably smaller than it appears, and that what genuinely remains of it is not a problem about responsibility at all. I have spent two years on this in an independent study, but I have never taken a university course in tort law or in machine learning, and my session has three assigned respondents who will each speak before the floor is opened to the audience. What I need, therefore, is a preparation sheet for the question period rather than another defense of my view.

The respondents are these three:

1. A professor of tort law who works in the economic-analysis tradition. She thinks about liability in terms of incentives, cheapest cost avoiders, the difference between the level of care an actor takes and the level of activity they engage in, and the case for making enterprises bear losses simply because they are in the best position to spread them.
2. An artificial intelligence researcher who builds assurance cases for deployed autonomous systems. He thinks in terms of failure-mode analysis, distribution shift between a testing environment and a deployment environment, emergent behavior, and systems that satisfy their specification in ways nobody intended.
3. A moral philosopher in the tradition that treats resentment, blame, and apology as forms of interpersonal address rather than as devices for allocating costs.

My view has three pieces, and I set them out here so that you know what the respondents will be aiming at.

First, I distinguish attributability from accountability. On my account, a harm is attributable to an agent only if it issues from that agent's own practical judgment, meaning some assessment of reasons made by them, however implicit. A harm is something an agent is accountable for if they are the proper target of a demand to answer for it and to make repair. I claim these two come apart for autonomous systems, and that much of the literature slides between them.

Second, I propose a principle I call the foreclosure principle. An agent who deploys a system whose later behavior they cannot predict cannot then plead that unpredictability as an excuse, because they are the author of the situation in which their own ignorance became operative. Deploying is a way of voluntarily giving up a defense. But the foreclosure runs only as far as the risk knowingly undertaken: it licenses holding the deployer accountable up to what I call the foreseeable envelope, the range of failure modes the deployer knew of, or was positioned to know of, in advance.

Third, I argue that harms falling outside the envelope are genuinely nobody's. They belong to no agent, including the machine. I do not attempt to blame anyone for them. I hold that they should be met by compensation drawn from a pooled fund financed by deployment itself, and that whatever moral remainder survives this should be understood as regret with no addressee rather than as blame awaiting a target.

The paper turns on a matched pair of cases. An autonomous sidewalk delivery robot, validated in one suburban setting, is operating in a second city when it exhibits a failure mode that no one at the company anticipated: I hold that compensation is owed and that no one is properly blamed. The same robot is redeployed in that second city after the company's own engineers flagged that its obstacle classifier had never been validated against a particular kind of urban clutter, and the deployment proceeds anyway: I hold that the company is blameworthy and that the resulting harm falls inside the envelope. I also argue against giving autonomous systems a legal status of their own on the model of corporate personhood, on the grounds that a corporation can be addressed because it has members who act in its name, whereas a system has nobody answering in its name.

What I would like from you is the following. Give me the ten or so objections I should actually expect, ordered by the damage they would do to my argument rather than by the order in which they will arrive. For each, tell me which of the three respondents is most likely to press it, and phrase it the way that person would phrase it, in their own vocabulary, so that I can recognize it when I hear it. My suspicion is that the torts professor will open with something about incentives, and that the AI researcher will ask whether the foreseeable envelope is even a determinate thing, but I would rather you corrected me than confirmed me. For each objection, supply a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, an honest note on where that reply is thinnest, and a judgment about whether I should concede the point in my opening talk instead of absorbing it from the floor. Then tell me which single reply has to land if the paper is to survive the session at all, and which of my concessions would be cheapest to buy early.

Finally, tell me the question I am hoping no one asks: the objection that would be hardest for someone with my preparation to answer, and how much of it I can defuse in advance by rewording something in the paper itself. Please gloss any legal or technical terms as you go, since I know most of this vocabulary only from reading about it secondhand. And please do not send me a reading list or a set of references. I would much rather have your best reconstruction of what these three people will say to me than a list of sources I will not be able to track down before the symposium.
