I'm writing an essay on whether pure time preference can be defended and I want you to write it, not advise me on it. Here's the setup I want it to run through. Grant the Ramsey-style decomposition: the rate applied to wellbeing is ρ + ηg — pure time preference, plus the elasticity of marginal utility times growth in wellbeing per head. Now take a world where g is negative, so earlier generations are reliably worse off than the ones that follow. Same formula, opposite sign: the impartial planner gets told to weight a future person's wellbeing more heavily than a present one's.

I want a verdict on two things. First, does the fact that the formula just flips under negative growth show that ρ isn't doing any independent normative work at all — that its really a fudge term whose job is to keep the answer near zero when g happens to be positive? Second, if negative discounting is what impartiality requires when g < 0, what survives of the usual defenses of ρ — the appeal to survival risk, the claim that we shouldn't have to sacrifice for people who'll be richer anyway — and can any of them be rebuilt so they still bite in the shrinking case? Take seriously the objection that being located later in time is a morally irrelevant property, no better a reason to discount than being born on a different continent. But also take seriously the worry that a view which lets the planner's impartiality track the sign of a macroeconomic variable is as arbitrary as the one it replaces. Don't sit on the fence — weigh the arguments both ways and then commit.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning after that, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
