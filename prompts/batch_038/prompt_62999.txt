I am drafting a section of a paper arguing that demandingness objections are not a problem peculiar to consequentialism: if the charge is that a theory asks too much of ordinary agents, then some moderately demanding deontological views are exposed to it more severely than act consequentialism is, in entirely ordinary circumstances. I need a case that makes this vivid and that a threshold deontologist cannot dismiss as a straw man. Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

**The scenario.** One ordinary-life situation, a single paragraph, set somewhere concrete (a hospital, a firm, a family) rather than in a trolley-problem vacuum. An agent has violated an agent-centered constraint — deception, a broken promise, a betrayed confidence, not mere negligence — and one identifiable person is badly off as a result.

**What the two theories must say.**

- *Act consequentialism:* the past violation is a sunk fact, and the agent must do the best she can from here. Its recommendation must be genuinely costly to her — she gives up something real, though not everything — and it must not amount to walking away and letting someone else handle the mess.
- *Threshold deontology with an agent-centered prerogative:* the agent may refuse moral demands that would cost her more than some specified, moderate amount, but she owes this particular victim a personal, non-substitutable duty of repair. On the view, that duty is not discharged by third-party compensation and cannot be traded off against greater aggregate good elsewhere.

**The flip.** The threshold view must require substantially more of the agent — more of her income, her career, and her projects — than act consequentialism does, *despite* the prerogative. The extra demand must arise from the personal and non-substitutable character of the reparative duty, not from punishment, guilt, remorse, or any perfectionist add-on. Note that the hinge is an asymmetry: the prerogative limits duties to promote the good but does not limit duties of repair for wrongs the agent herself committed. Make that asymmetry explicit and support it in a sentence or two rather than assuming it.

**Further constraints.**

- No infinite or astronomically large values, no far-future stakes, no fanaticism; the comparison must be between finite, commensurable costs.
- To keep the arithmetic determinate, assume that each dollar she directs to the most cost-effective global health charities does roughly forty times the good of a dollar spent on restoring her victim, and that an insurer and her employer will cover the victim's ordinary medical needs whatever she does, though not his full restoration. Do not let these assumptions carry the case: show that the flip survives if the ratio is only five to one.
- State the prerogative's threshold explicitly, in dollars or as a share of income, and itemize what the agent gives up under each theory, so that "more demanding" is a claim about the case rather than a slogan.

**Deliverables, in this order.** The scenario; then a walk-through giving each theory's verdict and the reasoning that produces it, showing precisely where the deontological demand exceeds the consequentialist one; then the single feature of the case doing the work, together with a check on what happens when it is removed (for instance, if the victim is anonymous rather than identifiable, or if the duty can be delegated to the insurer); then the two strongest replies a moderate deontologist would make, each with an assessment of whether it saves the view or quietly concedes the point; and finally an honest flagging of every place where the case stipulates a verdict instead of generating it from independently plausible commitments — if any stipulation is load-bearing, say so and propose the minimal repair.

Begin your response with the scenario itself: no title, no preface, no restatement of the task, and no commentary before it. Then open the analysis with a single sentence stating whether all the specifications are met and which one was hardest to satisfy. Write in American English.
