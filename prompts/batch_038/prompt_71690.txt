I spent 34 years as a fishery biologist doing population surveys, and since I retired ive gotten into philosophy pretty hard, mostly by accident — podcasts first, then the actual books. Right now I'm chewing on Bernard Williams and his attack on the idea that you can always justify what you do by totting up the consequences: the integrity stuff, including his point that if a man is pulling his wife out of a river, you don't want him to have spent the moment working out whether the rescue was impartially permitted.

Here's why I care practically. For four years I've been the callout coordinator for a volunteer swiftwater and search team — about 60 of us, roughly three callouts a month, usually at night. When I task someone and they say no, the reason is normally logistical: shift work, out of the county, car won't start. That's easy, I go down the list. But two or three times a month somebody gives me a reason that isn't logistical, and we have no way to handle it. Currently we do one of two things: accept it silently and quietly downgrade that person in our heads as flaky, or push back and make them justify themselves, which always ends badly. Both feel wrong and I can't tell whether they're wrong in the same way.

Complication worth knowing about: roughly half our income is a paid standby contract with an energy company for flood response at their sites, and that contract obliges us to field the most qualified available people, not just any warm body. So a decline isn't free.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run:

- the questions to ask, in order, on the phone, in five minutes, at 2am, by a duty officer who has never read Williams and never will;
- what counts as a sufficient answer at each step and what doesn't;
- how much explaining the member should have to do — my reading is that demanding they show their reason stacks up impartially is exactly the alienating move, but I also can't run a rescue team on "because my conscience says no," so where's the line and what can I legitimately ask for;
- where the procedure ends in accept, where it ends in accept-with-conditions, and where it hands off to somebody else (the chair, the training officer) because the question isn't mine to settle;
- which distinctions have to survive being compressed onto a laminated card in the truck, and which ones I'm allowed to drop;
- where my scientist's instinct to tally costs and benefits is legitimate and where it's precisely the thing Williams thinks is a mistake.

Then run it on these four, all basically real:

1. Our best boat handler has spent twenty years publicly campaigning against the energy company that holds our standby contract — testified against them, letters to editors, the lot. A standby tasking comes in for one of their sites and he declines, saying he won't work for them and doing it would undercut everything he's built. Contractually they're entitled to our most qualified person and he is it. I want him to go. He's not being difficult, he's completely polite, he just won't.

2. A member declines a 2am callout because she promised her granddaughter she'd drive her to a wedding first thing in the morning, four hours away. That's all she says: that she made a promise. The odd thing is the team treats this one worse than case 1, like she let us down, whereas they mostly shrug about him. I think that's backwards and I don't know whether a good procedure would confirm my view or explain theirs.

3. A member declines a specific task rather than the whole callout — he won't rig a particular rope system that our own protocol calls for, says its a matter of conscience, he's not putting his name to it. Sounds like integrity to me. Also sounds like a technical objection wearing a costume.

4. Someone who was on a bad call a few years back, the one where we didn't get there in time, now declines anything involving a child. Nothing to do with principles, they say. They just can't.

Be honest with me about whether such a procedure can actually tell integrity apart from fear, from preference, from burnout, from a grievance in disguise — or whether it just registers whatever story the person tells and I'd be fooling myself. And one of these four should come out against me: tell me which, and don't soften it. Also say what the procedure can't do. I assume it can't tell me whether to keep somebody on the roster, and I assume it has nothing useful to say about the case where accepting a decline might mean someone dies, but tell me if I'm wrong about either. Finally: would Williams himself regard the whole project of a laminated card as a misunderstanding of his point, and if so is that fatal for what I'm asking or just a caveat I learn to live with?
