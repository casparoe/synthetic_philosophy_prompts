I co-host a grad-student podcast out of my department and next week's guest is a moral philosopher who denies that all-things-considered betterness is transitive — she's on the Temkin side of the Rachels–Temkin spectrum arguments, and she thinks the right response to the standard objections is that betterness is essentially comparative, so there's no pair-independent fact about how much good an outcome contains. She's charming and she rambles, and she has two go-to moves that shut down a line of questioning almost every time: when the money-pump stuff comes up she says a pump is just a sequence of choices, not a sequence of betterness facts, and when someone pushes on whether her view permits actual cycles she says cyclicity is a red herring because she never claimed anything is better than itself. My own dissertation chapter is on aggregation and I've been privately worrying that the intransitivist move does more work than it can bear, so I'd like the interview to be structurally awkward for her in a friendly way. We've got 45 minutes and nothing in the list should require her to explain what a spectrum argument is — the opener should already presuppose that she's an intransitivist and go straight at a consequence.

Write me eight to ten questions, numbered, each short enough to read aloud in two breaths (three sentences max), and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, with that answer spelled out in a clause so I can pattern-match live. I want coverage of at least: (a) whether her view allows genuine cycles or only non-cyclic failures of transitivity, and if only the latter, what structural feature of betterness is doing the work of ruling cycles out when asymmetry plainly doesnt; (b) the money-pump objection in its strongest form — a sequence that leaves her strictly worse off with no compensating gain and no foresight required — and then, if she bites the bullet and says betterness simply isn't choiceworthiness, what betterness is *for* and why we should think her intuitions in the torture-against-many-headaches cases track betterness rather than tracking what one should choose; (c) whether the essentially-comparative premise makes the view unfalsifiable, i.e. whether any pattern of pairwise verdicts can be absorbed by postulating a pair-relative weighting, and if she says the weightings are constrained by the ideals in play, whether those ideals are themselves pair-indexed or whether that smuggles back a comparison-class-independent structure; (d) the vagueness/sorites diagnosis of her arguements, and which single premise she would give up last and why transitivity is supposed to be cheaper than that one; (e) why not settle for quasi-transitivity, or for the semi-transitivity/interval-order weakening that sits between quasi-transitivity and full transitivity, since those block the pumps and preserve a well-behaved choice function — what exactly does full intransitivity buy that they don't; (f) what she says about the equally-good-as relation and the small-improvement cases, including whether she needs a fourth comparative relation and whether that relation is transitive; and (g) the population-ethics payoff: if the ordering needn't have a maximum, what does a normative theory actually output when somebody asks what to do.

Order them so the deflections get progressively harder to deploy — most talk-around-able first — and end with a question about what evidence or what intuitive datum, if it turned up, would make her abandon intransitivity rather than explain it away. Write that last one so she can't retreat into saying that careful judges who converge on a complete transitive ordering of the spectrum have merely adopted a frame that hides the pair-relativity; I want a version that makes that retreat cost her something. And please no "what drew you to this" filler, and no closing question asking her to recommend books for listeners — we cut that segment years ago and I'm not reviving it.
