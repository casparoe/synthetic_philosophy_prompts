I'm on a placement at a county record office two days a week, mostly reboxing and typing up catalogue entries for the surviving papers of a Victorian estate and the little brickworks that went with it. Last week I asked the senior volunteer (lovely man, been doing this since the eighties) whether the fact that we have literally nothing written by the tenants or the kiln hands — only the agent's correspondence and the wage books — meant anything on its own. Like, could you infer something from the shape of the gap, or from which kinds of document never happened? He said 'history is written by the victors, son', and that was that, and the whole table nodded. Then on Saturday a mate of mine used the exact same sentence at me in the other direction, in a reading group about propoganda, to argue that basically everything in the mainstream record is the winners talking and so none of it can be used as evidence for anything. Same words, doing two completely different jobs, and I couldn't tell whether they can both be right or whether one of them is just mush. I know every second website credits the line to Churchill without ever giving a reference — please don't try to settle where it comes from, that's not the question.

Can you unpack the sentence for me? I want the distinct precise claims it could be standing in for: producing the records in the first place is definately not the same thing as controlling which ones survive, which isn't the same as controlling what later people get to read, which isn't the same as setting the terms the story gets told in — and I want a verdict on each, whether it's a platitude nobody would deny, a substantive claim that would need evidence, or actually false. I've got a nagging memory that some of the most famous ancient histories were written by people on the losing side, sometimes by exiles who happened to get access to both camps, which seems to sit badly with at least one of the readings. It's also worth saying something about both halves of the sentence separately: who counts as a 'victor' in a case like mine, where there was no war, just an estate and a brickworks and a very ordinary imbalance in who could write and who had to ask permission; and whether 'written' is even the right verb when most of that paper was never composed with the intention of becoming history, it was just administration.

Format: three or four sentences up top with the bottom line — can the record office use and the reading-group use both be right? Then a table with these columns: reading (one line naming it), what_it_says (a precise paraphrase, no vagueness), kind_of_claim (empirical / methodological / conceptual / normative), verdict (platitude / contested but defensible / probably false / incoherent), why (a sentence of grounds), whose_use_it_supports (record office, reading group, both, neither). Then finish with a short paragraph on what I could actually have said back to each of them without being insufferable about it. British English please, and no bibliography or further reading — I'm not writing this up, I just want to hold my end of two conversations.
