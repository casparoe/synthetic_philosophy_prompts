For some years now I have been reading about thermodynamics and information theory on my own, without any university training in either, so I hope you will indulge an amateur's way of putting the question. What I want is a careful comparison of two rival accounts of where the bill gets paid in Maxwell's demon — that is, at which step of the demon's routine the entropy debt is discharged so that the second law comes out unharmed.

The first account is the one I meet most often. On this view, finding out which side of the partition a molecule happens to be on can in principle be done at no thermodynamic cost, because a measurement is just a reversible coupling that leaves no trace anywhere except in the state of the demon's memory. What cannot be avoided is the clearing of that memory. A demon that means to keep working must at some point restore itself to the internal condition it started from, which means wiping the record, and wiping a given number of bits back to a blank condition carries an unavoidable minimum cost — a quantity of heat dumped into the surroundings, proportional to the temperature and growing with the logarithm of two for each bit. On this view the demon runs a profit for a while and then settles the whole account in one lump at the erasure step, and over a complete cycle the books balance.

The second account puts the cost earlier. On this view, acquiring information about a molecule is not free at the demon's own temperature, because resolving something as small and as fast as a gas molecule against a background of thermal noise takes an intervention that produces more entropy than the information is worth. The usual illustration is that the demon would have to illuminate the molecule, and a photon energetic enough to stand out from the surrounding blackbody radiation costs more than the resulting bit ever returns. On this view erasure is a distraction: the second law is already safe at the moment of acquisition, and the demon never gets into credit at all.

What has made me want these two set side by side is a case of my own devising, which I cannot resolve. Suppose the demon is fitted not with a single reusable register but with a notebook containing a billion fresh pages, so that during ordinary operation it never has to overwrite anything. It observes a molecule, notes which side it went to, takes its small dividend of work, and turns the page. My own arithmetic says that for the entire working life of the notebook the device turns heat from a single reservoir into work with nothing entered on the other side of the ledger, and only when the pages are exhausted does anything come due. That conclusion feels plainly wrong to me, but I cannot locate the error, and I suspect the two accounts above fail my case in different ways. The second account ought to forbid the profitable run from ever beginning. The first seems to concede the profit and merely postpone the whole bill to a single terminal erasure, which, if I make the notebook thick enough, can be deferred longer than I am prepared to wait.

I would therefore like four things from you.

First, set the two accounts against each other and state as sharply as you can what each is committed to about measurement, about memory, and about what it takes for a process to count as a completed cycle.

Second, say which of them stands better today. In particular, is the losing account simply mistaken, or is it right about a genuine physical effect that just turns out not to be the load-bearing one? If the disagreement is less settled than textbook summaries suggest, tell me where the pressure points are.

Third, work through my notebook case. Is the profitable run real? If it is, explain why a finite stock of blank pages does not amount to a machine that beats the second law for as long as anyone would care to run it. I am especially curious whether the honest description of such a device is a heat engine running on information, or something closer to a battery that was charged at the moment the notebook was manufactured blank — and if the latter, charged by whom.

Fourth, tell me whether there is a third way to state the constraint, one in which what the demon knows about the gas is itself treated as a thermodynamic quantity that shifts the balance of the inequality, rather than as a ledger that must eventually be wiped, and how such a formulation relates to the first two accounts.

Please do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I have no way of chasing them down and would much rather have your reasoning laid out in full. Where you judge that my amateur formulation is not merely imprecise but actually wrong, say so directly rather than repairing it silently. Write the whole answer in American English.
