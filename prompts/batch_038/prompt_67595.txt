I can handle the mechanics of Jeffrey conditionalization on my own; what I cannot do is separate a defensible simplification from a subtle error that changes what the rule is supposed to establish. Here is the passage I have transcribed, with the book's worked example:

> Ordinary Bayesian updating has a hidden and implausible requirement: that observation hand you a proposition you can be certain of, so that you can conditionalize on it. Jeffrey showed this is a needless constraint, because perceptual certainty is impossible in any case — when you squint at a tile in bad light, you are never sure whether the hairline crack is really there. His rule lets you update anyway. Suppose the crack is there with prior probability 0.25, a flawed tile is a cracked tile with probability 0.9 and an unflawed tile a non-cracked one with probability 0.1, so your prior for the flaw is 0.3. Your bad light leaves you 60% confident there is a crack. Then your new probability for the flaw is just that 60% applied to the 0.9: 0.54. That is the whole rule — conditionalize, then multiply by however confident you are in the evidence. The one condition is rigidity: your confidence in the evidence has to stay put while you update, so an agent who keeps changing her mind about the crack cannot use the rule at all. It also does not matter what order uncertain observations arrive in, and in fact any Jeffrey update can be reproduced by ordinary conditionalization on a suitably chosen proposition, which shows the rule is not a new kind of updating but a notational convenience. Its real significance is philosophical: since certainty never enters the picture anywhere, Bayesianism was never a theory about evidence at all, only a theory about keeping your books tidy.
