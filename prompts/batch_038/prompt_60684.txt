so I run a small weekend reading group, mostly AI-governance and rationalist-adjacent people, and next session is on safe Pareto improvements and precommitment between agents that can inspect each others decision procedures. Half the room has heard of the prisoners dilemma, nobody has seen the formal literature. I want to hand out a decision tree — "which view on getting cooperative gains through commitment do you actually hold?" — built out of the logical space first rather than out of a list of names.

Root of the tree: "you accept that two agents in a game with several good-but-conflicting outcomes (two drivers heading at each other on a narrow road, two countries squabbling over a shipping lane that just opened up when the ice melted) could each do better by binding themselves in advance, as long as the binding leaves each of them no worse off than theyd have been by default." Every node under that should be a question a person can answer by imagining a concrete two-agent scenario, without knowing which leaf it leads to. No jargon in the nodes, and no node that just asks "are you in favor of commitment?"

The axes I care about, roughly in this order:
- what the commitment actually is: rewriting your own decision procedure, handing the game to a delegate with instructions, announcing something intrinsically meaningless in cheap talk that youve both agreed in advance to treat as meaningful, or committing only after learning what the other's default turned out to be
- what "no worse off than by default" gets measured against — one particular default profile you have in mind, every default the other agent could possibly run, the worst case, or your own subjective expectations about them
- whether the guarantee has to hold with certainty in every outcome that can actually occur, or just on average given your beliefs
- which assumptions about how games get resolved are being smuggled in (obviously terrible options never get played, structurally identical games get played identically, some specific equilibrium gets picked out of several)
- whether the binding is simultaneous and mutual, sequential, or one-sided, and whether going in sequence quietly reintroduces the race it was supposed to defuse

The axis I most want represented, because my group conflates these constantly: who eats the risk when you turn out to be wrong about what the other agent's default really is.

Deliverable: about seven branch questions, no more than twelve leaves. Each leaf gets the standard name if it genuinely has one and "unnamed" if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then mark every cell your questions open up that no leaf sits in, and say whether its empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive but coherent, or just because nobody's gotten around to it. Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really tell apart. And add one line per leaf on whether it survives if you drop the assumption that agents can read each others source code and all you have left is public announcements.

No bibliography, no references, and please don't invent any — names only where a name is real common currency.
