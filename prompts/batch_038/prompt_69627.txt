I host a small podcast on philosophy and political economy, and last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about Keynes's 1930 essay on the economic prospects of his grandchildren. Her line, which I could not budge: the closing injunction — that we must hold on to avarice and usury as our guiding values a while longer, since only they can carry us out of the tunnel of economic necessity — is not the transitional ethic its admirers make of it but a concession that the good life Keynes describes is reserved for those already spared the labour that produces the abundance. I answered that she was overreading and that the two claims can be held together; neither of us won. Next week's guest is a philosopher who works on Keynes and the good life and who, I suspect, thinks we both missed the point: on his view the error is structural rather than moral, because the essay treats abundance as a quantity that dissolves the economic problem once it crosses a threshold, whereas the problem is indexical, so that no quantity of it can be enough for anyone.

Write me nine or ten interview questions that turn on the content of that position rather than on his career. I want at least one on Keynes's distinction between the needs we feel regardless of how others fare and those we feel only insofar as they lift us above others — and on why, having conceded that the second sort may be insatiable, Keynes still expects the problem to be solved; one on the fact that the newly rich chose income over leisure, and whether that indicts his psychology rather than his forecasting; one on what would show that a threshold account is simply the wrong form for the claim, and what the indexical alternative predicts that the threshold account does not; one on the purposive temperament Keynes distrusts and his remark that only those who refuse to sell themselves for the means of life will enjoy the abundance; and one that makes him say whether, on his view, the economic problem could ever be solved for anyone. Give a follow-up for each, keyed to the answer he is most likely to give — and since he answers hard questions about motivation by retreating to the claim that he is merely describing the structure of the problem, at least two of the follow-ups should close off that retreat.
