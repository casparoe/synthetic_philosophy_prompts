**The question, as I set it (20 marks):**

Nadia has spent three years telling her younger brother Tomas, in terms the rest of the family find humiliating, that he is careless with money. Tomas has now learned that Nadia has been concealing her own overdrafts from her husband for two years. He says to her: "You've no right to call me careless."

(a) (5 marks) Distinguish the claim that a hypocrite's moral claim is false or unjustified from the claim that a hypocrite lacks the standing to make it. Say what you think the second claim amounts to.

(b) (8 marks) Apply the distinction to the case. Is Tomas's remark right, and in what sense?

(c) (7 marks) Would your answer change if Nadia were angrier with herself about the overdrafts than she has ever been with Tomas? Explain.

**Her answer:**

(a) The first thing to separate is the truth of what Nadia says from Nadia's own position to say it. That she has been hiding debts from her husband and that she tells her brother he is careless with money are two different facts, and the second does not make the first untrue. Someone who cheats on their taxes and says that cheating on taxes is wrong has said something true; the wrongness of tax cheating does not depend on who states it. The traditional name for the mistake is *tu quoque*: answering a claim by pointing at the claimant. So "no standing" cannot mean "not true," and I do not think it means "not justified" either, since Nadia is in as good a position as anyone to know both that concealing debts from a spouse is a bad thing to do and that carelessness with money is a fault.

What "no standing" does mean, I think, is that something about the blamer's own relation to the standard makes her blaming the wrong thing to do, even where the standard is correct and her application of it to him is correct. The problem is not the content of the judgement but who is entitled to deliver it, and how.

(b) Tomas's remark has force because of fairness. For three years Nadia has held him to a standard she has not held herself to. She has taken the benefit of the standard — the authority to sit in judgement, the superiority it gives her in that family — without paying its cost, and free-riding on a moral standard is precisely what makes the hypocrite's blame unfair rather than merely unfortunate. It also carries a claim about the two of them, that she is the sort of person who is above this fault and he is not. That claim is false, and it is the falsity of that claim, not of "carelessness with money is a fault," that undercuts her.

There is a second thing wrong with her blaming which has nothing to do with hypocrisy, and it is worth keeping separate because the case is built so that you run them together. The family finds her remarks humiliating. Blame delivered as humiliation is not moral address at all: it does not invite the other person to see anything, it merely ranks him below you. A person of spotless financial conduct would be doing something objectionable in blaming this way. So Tomas has two complaints, and they come apart; he could have the second without the first.

Is his remark right? Yes, in a limited sense. He is right that she is in no position to say it to him. He is wrong if he thinks that shows he has been misdescribed.

(c) Suppose Nadia were angrier with herself about the overdrafts than she has ever been with Tomas. I would say the hypocrisy goes away, and the standing problem with it. Hypocrisy is essentially a matter of insincerity: saying what you do not believe, or at least saying what you do not do while presenting yourself as someone who does. A woman who berates herself is not presenting herself as anything. She is applying the standard across the board and saying exactly what she thinks, so there is nothing unfair left in her blame and no question of standing arises. Tomas's objection loses its target, and the only complaint still open to him is the one about manner.

One thing that I think is often missed in all this: once we accept that Nadia has for three years treated a standard as binding on her brother and not on herself, the standard loses its grip on the relationship in a way that reaches past her right to speak. A charge brought by someone in her position is not merely one that Tomas may refuse to hear; it is one he may decline to treat as applying to him, and once it is declined in that way there is nothing left for him to feel guilty about. The carelessness stops being a fact about him in the sense that matters. That, I think, is the real cost of hypocrisy: it does not only silence the blamer, it dissolves the blame.
