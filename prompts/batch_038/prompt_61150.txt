I'm the junior analyst on the red team at a foreign policy shop, and before anything goes out it has to clear our senior person — call her K — who signs off on every historical analogy in a memo. She's on leave for three weeks and I've got to write the recommendations in her name, so I'm trying to reverse engineer her rule from her track record.

Here's the last year or so, in order:

1. Memo comparing an adversary's annexation of a neighbor's peninsula to Munich 1938. Rejected. Her note said the memo was using it to make one option look like the only option.
2. Memo comparing a rising power's naval buildup to the Anglo-German dreadnought race before 1914. Accepted, but she made the author cut the "this is how great wars start" part and keep only the bit about how each side's shipbuilding estimates were set by the other side's published program. That one was a background piece with no recommendation attached.
3. Red-team diagnostic, no policy ask, comparing a dispute over missile deployments in a third country to the Cuban missile crisis. The analogy was about how the two leaderships communicated, not about how it would end. Rejected anyway — her note said 1962 came out the way it did because of a trade neither government could have counted on in advance, plus a clock that doesn't exist anymore.
4. Memo comparing a possible run on the weak member of a currency union to the collapse of the big Vienna bank in 1931 and what happened to the German banking system next door. Accepted unchanged.
5. Internal post-mortem on our own botched rollout of a data-sharing system, compared to a fairly obscure 1970s government computing failure. Mechanically it was a genuinely good match — same vendor lock-in, same requirement creep. Rejected, because the post-mortem was written by the office that had defended the rollout and had a stake in the conclusion.
6. Memo comparing a rival's covert influence operations to Soviet-era "active measures." Accepted with a carve-out: fine for explaining how they segment an audience, not fine for predicting the campaign will fail, because we have no idea how many such campaigns failed quietly.
7. Somebody floated the fall of Rome as an analogy for institutional decay at home. Rejected, basically without reading it.

Our shop has in-house jargon for this. A **transfer claim** says a mechanism worked a certain way in the source case and there's reason to expect it here; an **outcome claim** says the ending will be the same. A use is **hot** when the analogy is load-bearing for a policy somebody already wants, **cold** when it's descriptive or diagnostic.

New memo. It's from an analyst who is a well known hawk on this file — everyone knows it, she isn't hiding it. It's commissioned by the planning office as a stress test, there's no recommendation section at all, and her argument cuts against her own standing position: using the Pearl Harbor intelligence failure, she says a stream of ambiguous indicators gets sorted into whatever pattern the receiving side already expects, and that our current posture is the one exposed to that error. Honestly it's the best mechanism argument I've read all year. But Pearl Harbor is about as loaded a name as there is, the thing we remember it for is precisely that the signals were missed, and it's not obvious the outcome wasn't a pile of coincidences.

What would K rule? More usefully: what rule is she actually running? Lay out the two or three candidate rules that fit all seven past verdicts equally well — notice that some of her rejections look like one-way vetoes rather than if-and-only-if tests, and say which. Then say what each candidate rule says about this memo, and commit to a prediction with your reasons. Also name one hypothetical eighth memo that would cleanly separate the candidates you're weighing, and say what principle our shop would be adopting if I overrode her and let it through.

Last line exactly: Verdict: accept / reject / accept-with-reframe — one of those three.
