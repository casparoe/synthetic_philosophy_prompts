I'm the note-taker for a hospital patient-experience committee (not a member, I just type up the minutes) and last month we burned two whole sessions choosing between plans for the outpatient clinic's waiting situation. The plans differ on exactly two things: expected wait before you're seen, and how nice the waiting area is. So we ended up with this ladder of forty plans where each one adds a minute of expected wait and one small improvement on the last: Plan 1 is four minutes on a plastic chair in a fluorescent corridor next to a water fountain that doesn't work, Plan 2 adds a potted plant, Plan 3 a second plant, Plan 4 real chairs, Plan 5 a window, and by Plan 40 it's forty-three minutes in a lounge with recliners, a skylight, an espresso bar and somebody playing piano. Two people on the committee, Farah and Owen, got into a genuinely nasty argument about what the ladder shows, and they've now dragged me into it by email. Roughly the exchange:

(1) Farah: nobody in the room thought Plan 40 was better than Plan 1 — a 43-minute wait is a 43-minute wait and a nicer room doesn't buy it back. But she also wouldn't say that any single step in the ladder is a step down, because the steps are all structurally identical and if you can't say where it breaks you haven't actually said anything. So 'better than' isn't transitive, and the committee should stop pretending its producing a ranking at all.
(2) Owen: that's refusing to do the work. "One more minute is offset by this improvement" isn't something you can see at a step and then iterate; it's a claim about an exchange rate between minutes and comfort, and if you actually had a rate the ranking would come out transitive automatically. What's going on is that we never fixed one, so some pairs simply have no determinate answer. That's incompleteness, not a cycle, and the difference matters.
(3) Farah: incomparability rescues nothing, because we did rank the endpoints, quickly and unanimously. And an incomparability that evaporates the moment you add one more plant is a cycle wearing a disguise.
(4) Owen: you're running together a vague one-place predicate and a precise two-place one. 'Is a better plan' is vague the way 'is bald' is vague, and vague predicates famously don't transmit across a chain — but nobody concludes 'taller than' is nontransitive just because 'tall' is vague. And notice the ladder isn't even vague in its real dimensions: the wait times are exactly comparable and the comfort differences are exactly specifiable, since I can list them. The only fuzzy thing is the trade-off, which is one number.
(5) Farah: fine, then explain the actual vote. We shortlisted three at the end — P (six minutes, tidy room with a window), Q (twenty minutes, recliners and coffee), R (forty minutes, the full lounge). P beat Q 6-3, Q beat R 6-3, and R beat P 6-3. That's not a thought experiment, that's in my minutes.
(6) Owen: that's a Condorcet cycle — a fact about majority voting over nine people's separate, individually transitive preferences. It tells you the committee doesn't have one ordering; it tells you nothing whatsoever about betterness. And if the nontransitivity you want were real, the committee would be pumpable: you could sell it back to its own starting point for a fee, over and over. Nobody believes a hospital can be bled dry by nicer chairs.

Farah's reply to the last bit was that a pump needs a single chooser who can be made to pay, and a nine-person body that cycles isn't incoherent, it's just plural — but she also said (4) concedes the whole game, because if you can restore transitivity by plugging in a number then betterness was never nontransitive, only indeterminate, which is what she said in (1). Owen said he'd be delighted to plug in a number and couldn't say which one. Farah said that's the point. They've both cc'd me twice and I honestly can't tell who's confused. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each of the six steps — I want an actual verdict on each one, rate them if you like, not a "both have a point." Then separately: is there real nontransitivity of better-than in this case, or is it vagueness, or incompleteness, or something else entirely; does the 6-3 cycle have anything to do with the ladder; and is the money-pump objection a real objection or does the "no single chooser" reply dispose of it? And what are the two of them both missing? No reading lists or philosopher names, please — I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.
