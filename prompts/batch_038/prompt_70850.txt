Last night a friend and I argued about biological function until well past two in the morning, and I woke up unable to say what was wrong with his reasoning, although I am confident that something is. He is careful and fair, and the argument is his own reconstruction rather than a summary of something he read, which is why I cannot simply look up the standard reply. He grants that function talk earns its keep in medicine and biology; his claim is that neither of the two live theories of function can account for the one thing function talk is chiefly hired to do.

He set it out as follows, naming (2) the Cummins-style causal-role account and (5) the Wright-style etiological account.

(1) An adequate account of biological function must explain how a trait can malfunction — how a diseased kidney fails to do what kidneys are supposed to do — since grounding that sort of failure is the main reason biologists and clinicians trade in functions at all.

(2) On the causal-role account, to say that the function of X is to φ is to say that φ-ing figures among the causal contributions X actually makes, within some containing system, to capacities of that system that we happen to care about.

(3) A kidney that no longer filters makes no filtering contribution, so under (2) the diseased kidney has no function to filter; the causal-role theorist can report only that it does not filter, never that it fails to.

(4) So the causal-role account cannot satisfy (1).

(5) The etiological account avoids (3) by making function historical: the function of X is that effect of X's ancestors which explains why descendants like X are still around.

(6) But selection can credit a trait only with effects the trait has already produced in reproducing ancestors, and there are perfectly functioning traits whose relevant effects are too recent to have been credited — a population that has just acquired an enzyme able to break down a novel plant toxin, or indeed a trait considered in its very first bearer.

(7) In those cases (5) withholds any function at all, even though we would without hesitation say the enzyme's job is detoxifying and that a mutation disabling it would be a defect.

(8) So the etiological account fails (1) as well, in the opposite direction: it cannot ground malfunction where the history has not yet accumulated.

(9) These two accounts exhaust the options, since a function must be located either in what a trait presently contributes or in how the trait came to be there.

(10) Therefore there are no facts of the matter about biological functions, only descriptions we find convenient, and the apparent objectivity of malfunction is an illusion.

I refuse (10), and I refuse it for reasons I regard as more than squeamish: if nothing has a function, then neither pathology nor adaptation is a real feature of organisms, and I do not see how biology could survive that. Yet when I tried to say where his chain breaks, I found myself defending premises I am not sure I believe, and he found a gloss for each of my objections that seemed to preserve the argument intact. If I am forced to keep one thing, I keep (10)'s negation; among the rest I would most like to keep (1) and (5).

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. The five items should be, in order: the step you reject and the precise diagnosis of what goes wrong there; an explanation of why that step looked innocuous when I first heard it; what rejecting it costs, stated as a concrete commitment I would have to abandon or revise; the runner-up premise you would give up if I insisted on keeping the one you reject, with its price; and your verdict on whether the dilemma can be repaired into a sound argument once the flaw you identified is fixed.
