Software engineer here, reading old automata/cybernetics stuff for fun — mostly von Neumann's little book The Computer and the Brain (1958) and things written about it. There's a move in there that I keep running into and can't find a name for, and I've started to think its a general argumentative pattern rather than something peculiar to him.

It goes like this. First you establish a quantitative difference in how reliable or precise the parts of two systems are. In his case: a nerve impulse is a coarse all-or-nothing event, neurons misfire and have recovery periods, and the numerical precision the nervous system as a whole manages to work with comes out at something like two or three significant digits (my recollection of his estimate, might be off), against a dozen or more for a machine of his day. That's a measurement. But the conclusion drawn from it isn't measurement-shaped. It's that the two systems have to be talked about in different *kinds* of language — that the formalism appropriate to the brain can't be the language of exact arithmetical operations at all, only something statistical, and that the logic governing it is a different logic, not merely a different set of axioms inside the same logic. A number about error bars has become a claim about kinds of description.

Then, and this is the part I find weirder, the very same sloppiness gets used to license a *shallower* theory. Because the components can only hold a couple of digits, you don't need a deep mathematical apparatus to characterize what they build; a high-precision machine needs a much more elaborate one. So the error budget does double duty: it forces the switch to a different formal language and simultaneously means less structure is required. My intuition runs the other way — I'd have expected noise to make a theory harder, since now you need distributions and tolerances layered on top of everything.

I know the neighbourhood, and none of it seems to be this. Multiple realizability is about the same higher-level thing being built out of different stuff, not about precision. The analog/digital distinction is a claim about representational format, whereas here the format claim is supposed to *follow from* an error estimate. Levels of description and coarse-graining are closest, I think, except coarse-graining is something a theorist chooses to do for convenience, while this claims the system's own unreliability dictates which formalism applies. And the old cliché about a big enough quantitative difference turning into a qualitative one is too vague to be the concept.

So: is there an established name for inferring a change in the kind of description — and in the depth of description required — from a measured gap in component precision? I'm not after a bare label, I want the carving. What exactly does the inference license and what does it not? Is it sound, or is it a sleight of hand where a modelling choice gets dressed up as a discovery about the system? If a genuine concept exists, tell me how it differs from each of the four things above and which one actually fits. If nothing established covers it, say that plainly and coin a term, with a definition tight enough that both halves of this case fall under it while something ordinary like "our sensor readings are noisy, so we model them probabilistically" clearly doesn't — plus one or two boundary cases where reasonable people could argue it either way.
