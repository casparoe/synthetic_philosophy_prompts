I run a small workshop on institutional design attached to our political philosophy seminar, and this term it's degenerated into a two-person grudge match. One side is a sortition maximalist: thinks the fix for our legislatures is to hand genuine lawmaking power to bodies chosen by lottery. The other is what I've taken to calling a professionalist: thinks career politicians are a feature rather than a bug, that a standing class of people who run for re-election, accumulate expertise, and can be voted out is the only thing generating answerability, and that sortition buys descriptive resemblance at the price of accountability. They've been circling the same three points since September and neither has moved an inch. To give them something concrete to fight over I made them both work from a draft proposal two of my students wrote, which I'll call Draft 4-B, and which is deliberately strange: a second chamber of 300 members selected by stratified lottery (the strata being age, region, education, and — this is the contentious one — a self-reported interest-in-politics scale, because without it the people who actually turn up are the usual suspects), one four-year term, never eligible again; the chamber cannot introduce legislation at all, but it can veto any bill the elected chamber passes, force a referendum on any bill, and sack ministers on a two-thirds vote; members are paid a judicial salary; every contact with a lobbyist is logged and published; and for ten years after leaving, a former member may not take paid lobbying or advocacy work. One of them keeps invoking the Athenian boule as precedent — selection by lot, one-year terms, a cap on how often a man could serve in a lifetime — and the other keeps saying the analogy is worthless, because the Athenian pool consisted of men over thirty who had put themselves forward and who had to render a full account of their service on the way out, whereas Draft 4-B's members are unaccountable precisely because they never have to face anybody again. There's also the old Aristotelian tag that getting chosen by lot is the democratic mode of appointment and getting elected the oligarchic one, which one of them wheels out at least weekly.

What I want from you is the document these two should have produced six months ago: the joint statement of a properly run adversarial collaboration. Write it as the rapporteur both sides agreed to in advance — you're an expert in democratic theory and constitutional design, and your job here isn't to referee but to make the disagreement precise enough that it could in principle be settled. Concretely: (1) a numbered list of claims both would genuinely sign — about what lotteries do to the composition of a body, what elections do to incentives, what Draft 4-B actually stipulates, and what we do and don't know; be ruthless, and if a claim only looks agreeable because it's ambiguous, leave it out; (2) a disagreements table with four columns — the issue, the sortitionist's own formulation of their position, the professionalist's own formulation of theirs, and what would have to be true for one of them to give way — where each side's wording is one they'd recognize as theirs, not a strawman and not diplomatic mush; (3) the cruxes, i.e. the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move someone — my bets are on whether descriptive resemblance can carry legitimacy without authorization, whether the threat of losing office does informational work that a one-term lottery member structurally can't do, and whether a logged-and-cooled-down lottery body is harder or easier to capture than an elected one, but tell me if you think the real cruxes sit somewhere else; and (4) for each crux, the kind of case, thought experiment, or empirical result both would accept as bearing on it, plus each side's prediction about it, recorded before the fact.

Think carefully, and before you draft anything, spend a few lines brainstorming the claims each side would flatly refuse to sign — the veto-only power is a big one, and so is whether career politician is even a coherent category — then make sure none of those sneak into section 1. Format the deliverable as that numbered agreed-claims section followed by the table, then the cruxes. Flag any disagreement you think turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, and say which words are doing the damage; my suspicion is legitimacy and possibly representation, but I could be wrong. Please don't name studies, authors, or any country's actual reform experiments, and give me no reading list — describe the shape of the evidence instead (something like a panel of lottery members surveyed before and after deliberation on a distributive question, or a comparison of amendment rates in chambers with and without single-term rules) rather than pointing at anything you'd have to cite, since half of what gets cited in this literature is contested anyway. And don't wrap up by splitting the difference or telling me both sides have a point; seperate out the disagreements that are real from the ones that were only ever about words, and leave the real ones standing, exact. I want to hand this to both of them and have each say yes, that's me, in their own column.
