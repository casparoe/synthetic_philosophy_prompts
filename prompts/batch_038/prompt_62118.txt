I co-teach an upper-level seminar on the ethics of well-being that draws roughly equal numbers of philosophy and literature students, and in week four we read the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov (1880) — Ivan's poem, as he tells it to Alyosha in the tavern — followed by Aldous Huxley's Brave New World (1932). The pairing is a familiar one: Mustapha Mond's long colloquy with John the Savage is regularly treated as a secular restaging of the Inquisitor's monologue, and my students arrive expecting precisely that, ready to conclude that both texts say the same thing about trading freedom for happiness. I want to hand them something harder, and I would like to work out the harder version with you before I write the handout. Please produce a close reading of Huxley's novel through the argument of the Grand Inquisitor, treating the comparison as a hypothesis to be tested rather than as a settled result.

Begin by reconstructing the Inquisitor's argument in a form precise enough to be argued with: what exactly does he claim human beings cannot bear, what does he offer them in its place, what work is done by the triad he says the Church took up where Christ refused it — miracle, mystery, and authority — and what role does bread play, both as the first temptation and as his insistence that free men will eventually lay their freedom at the feet of whoever will feed them? Then ask, element by element, whether Huxley retains it. Two conjectures of my own deserve your attack. First, that the two bargains differ in kind: the Inquisitor's arrangement requires a lie sustained by a small number of people who know the truth and accept the burden of knowing it, whereas Mond's requires no lie at all, because the population has been made such that the truth no longer has a point — and I cannot decide whether that removes the arrangement's principal cost or reveals that the cost was never where the argument's force lay. Second, that the Inquisitor's case is hostage to scarcity in a way Mond's is not: Huxley's world has solved production and taught people to want only what they can get, so if the freedom-for-happiness trade still looks compelling there, that suggests Dostoevsky's old man was arguing about something other than bread. Say whether these conjectures survive, and where they break. Then handle the mapping of miracle, mystery, and authority onto Huxley's own apparatus — soma, hypnopaedia and the feelies, the Controllers and the cult of Ford — and identify at least one place where the mapping fails in a way that matters rather than merely loosening the analogy. Finally, compare the two replies. Christ answers with a kiss and says nothing; John answers by insisting that he be permitted to be unhappy, a demand Mond concedes with something like indifference and that the closing chapters then spend themselves against. I want an account of what John's demand actually is — non-interference, or authorship of one's own desires (noting that his desires reach him through Shakespeare and through the reservation's own cruelties, so that he may be no less conditioned than anyone in London), or the claim that suffering is constitutive of meaning — and of whether the novel's ending at the lighthouse rebuts Mond or concedes to him.

A few constraints. Work by paraphrase: locate passages by scene, speaker, and what happens rather than quoting, since the class is split across several Dostoevsky translations and I would rather not send students hunting for wording that does not match their editions. Do not reduce either text to an illustration of the other; if the honest answer is that Huxley is doing something the Inquisitor's argument cannot accommodate, or that the comparison smuggles in a theological premise about Christ's presence and silence that a secular restaging cannot inherit, say so plainly and show where. Close with a short section addressed to me rather than to the students in which you separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these books: flag which of your claims about the function of the poem inside Dostoevsky's novel — whether the rest of the book refutes it, endorses it, or leaves it hanging — and which of your claims about Huxley's own sympathies rest on readings that are disputed rather than agreed, and mark any point where the reading you have been asked to test depends on a contestable interpretive choice. I would rather give the students an argument with its weak joints visible than a tidy comparison they could have assembled from a study guide.
