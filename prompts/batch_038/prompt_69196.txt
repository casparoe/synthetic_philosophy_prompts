You are a philosopher of action who also publishes in behavioral economics, and you are comfortable working with the formal apparatus of intertemporal choice. Please think carefully before drafting; the difficulty here is entirely in the construction, not in the exposition, and a case that merely asserts its verdicts rather than generating them from the two frameworks is worthless to me.

**What the case is for.** I am co-teaching a joint session — half philosophy graduate students, half behavioral-economics graduate students — on whether the two literatures on self-control failure are describing one phenomenon or two. The philosophers work with Davidson's account of incontinent action (the agent who, at the moment of acting, judges all-things-considered that A is better than B, intentionally does B, and was free to do A). The economists work with present-biased preferences — the quasi-hyperbolic β–δ family, where β < 1 produces dynamic inconsistency and the familiar common-difference reversals — together with the Strotz typology of naive, sophisticated, and precommitted agents, and with non-discounting rivals such as visceral-state and projection-bias accounts. I need a designed pair of vignettes that drives a wedge between these two vocabularies in both directions at once, so that each half of the room is handed a case its own toolkit cannot handle.

**Deliverable: two matched vignettes plus a full audit.**

*Vignette I — reversal without incontinence.* Construct a case in which:
1. The agent's choices across at least two moments constitute a textbook present-biased preference reversal, and you can exhibit a specific parameter setting (β, δ, magnitudes, delays) under which the quasi-hyperbolic model reproduces it exactly.
2. At the moment of the choice that the philosophers will want to call the failure, the agent holds and sincerely avows an all-things-considered judgment in favor of that very choice, so that Davidson's conditions are not met.
3. The sincerity of the avowal is not secured by fiat. Give me something internal to the case — how the agent responds to questioning, what they would accept or decline by way of a costless commitment device, what they predict about themselves — that independently licenses trusting the report.
4. The akratic phenomenology is absent: no avowal of "I know I shouldn't," no distress at acting, no sense of being overcome.
5. The explanation of why things go badly is *not* weakness of will. Say what it is instead (a prediction error about one's own future behavior is the obvious candidate, but you may choose otherwise, and if you do, say why).

*Vignette II — incontinence without reversal.* Construct a case in which:
1. All three Davidsonian conditions are satisfied at the moment of action: a contemporaneous all-things-considered judgment favoring A, intentional performance of B, and the unforced ability to have done A.
2. The intertemporal structure is one that a behavioral economist would instinctively model — the agent is weighing something available sooner against something available later — and yet **every** stationary discount function with parameters held fixed across the episode ranks A above B at the moment of action. Do not assert this: write out the two prospects with their delays and magnitudes and demonstrate the ranking, ideally under both an exponential and a quasi-hyperbolic parameterization.
3. The agent's *beliefs* about the outcomes, the delays, and the probabilities are identical at the moment of judgment and at the moment of action, so the flip cannot be cashed out as a belief revision.
4. Something other than the discount function does the work, and the vignette makes it visible without naming it. If you use a visceral state, a shift in salience, or a change in how the very same outcome is described, make the mechanism legible from what happens rather than from what the narrator says.

*Shared constraints.* The two vignettes should feature the same agent in the same domain, with surface details matched as closely as possible so that the divergence is attributable to the single variable you are isolating. Realistic throughout — a workplace, a clinic, a household, a training regimen; no thought-experiment machinery, no perfect predictors, no exotic neuroscience. Roughly 150–200 words each. No theory names, no technical vocabulary, and no winking at the reader inside the vignettes themselves: they should read as two ordinary paragraphs about one person.

**Then the audit.** After the vignettes, provide:
- A condition-by-condition check of Davidson's three requirements for each case, with the specific sentence or detail in the vignette that satisfies or defeats each one.
- The formal work for Vignette I (the parameterization that generates the reversal) and for Vignette II (the demonstration that no fixed-parameter stationary discount function generates it).
- Identification of the single feature doing the work in each vignette, plus a counterfactual test: state what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed or dialed down.
- Two attacks. First, from someone who holds that discounting plus self-prediction error is the whole story of self-control failure, against Vignette II — and address explicitly the move of rescuing the model by letting utility itself become time- or state-varying: does that rescue the account, or does it empty it, and if it empties it, where has the explanatory work relocated to? Second, from someone who wants to preserve akrasia, against Vignette I — for instance by arguing that a standing policy or a prior intention, rather than an all-things-considered judgment, supplies the missing contrary evaluation. Say whether that objection succeeds and what it costs.
- A frank closing note on where the construction is stipulative rather than generated, and on whether Vignette II is a genuine counterexample to the discounting framework or merely a case that falls outside its domain. I would rather hear this from you than have my students discover it in session.
