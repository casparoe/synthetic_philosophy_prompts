ok so my reading group has been chewing on the moral equality of combatants — the view that a soldier fighting for the aggressor and a soldier fighting against him have the same permission to kill each other, and neither is a murderer for doing it. My flatmate defends it every single week with the same picture: a duel. Two men, seconds, a code, a field at dawn. One of them is genuinely in the right about the grievance, one is in the wrong, but once they take the field both have exactly the same right to fire, and it would be absurd to say the wronged man may shoot and the wrongdoer may not, or that the wrongdoer commits murder by hitting back. His point is that the permission comes from the reciprocal structure of the contest — two agents who have both entered a situation in which each is trying to kill the other — and not from who's right about the quarrel. So the jus ad bellum question sits outside the fight entirely, and the fight carries its own symmetric rules, which is also, he says, why the very same code can forbid shooting at surgeons or at people in the town nearby, just as the code duello forbids certain moves. Which is why he thinks the revisionists who compare unjust combatants to armed robbers are just refusing to look at the structure.

Can you audit the thing for me rather than argue with him? I want the correspondences laid out explicitly — what in the duel plays the role of the declaration of war, the combatant, the code, the seconds, the spectators, the field, the bystander who gets hit by a stray ball — and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually bite on his conclusion and the ones that are just surface decoration. In particular I want to know what the duel quietly imports that a war doesn't have: mutual voluntary entry, the fact that there are only two agents and no third parties whose rights get engaged at all, symmetric risk, a quarrel that's about honor between individuals rather than about ruling over anybody, and the fact that the loser can walk away or surrender and the contest is over. Does his move — relocating the permission from the cause into the reciprocal structure — survive once you strip those out, or was the structure doing all the work because it was secretly consent plus symmetry plus no bystanders?

Test case for you to run it against: an eighteen-year-old conscript in the invading army, drafted, told almost nothing, fighting men who are shooting at him right now. Under the duel picture is he a duellist, or does he look more like someone's tool, or a person being used as a shield, or a driver whose brakes have gone? Say which of those pictures handles him best and what each one then implies about whether he may shoot back.

And then tell me what the duel analogy is actually good for, if anything. My own suspicion, which I can't defend, is that it earns its keep for the in bello rules — discrimination, proportionality, the idea that a code can bind both sides — and does nothing whatsoever for the equality claim, and that people slide from the first to the second. Say whether that's right or whether I'm splitting hairs.

Finally: if the duel's the wrong picture, what's a better one? All I've managed is a boxing match where each fighter has been told the other is trying to kill his family, which seems worse, and two guys trading shots from opposite ends of a lifeboat, which seems to prove far too much. Tell me whether either of those can be repaired into something that carries the argument, or whether the honest move is to stop reaching for contests and games altogether and describe war as something else entirely.
