I am a junior at a private day school and a staff writer for our student newspaper. Last year our school piloted an optional mid-day study hall, and in June the dean of students circulated a one-page report recommending that it be made permanent. A group of faculty circulated a reply the following week. The argument has since been through two faculty meetings and one board presentation, and after nearly a year neither side has shifted an inch.

Here is the data. Both sides accept it, and I have recomputed it myself from the registrar's spreadsheet. Of 600 students, 240 enrolled in the study hall and 360 did not.

- Of the 300 students who ended the previous year above the school-wide average GPA: 200 enrolled, mean final GPA 3.60; 100 did not enroll, mean 3.70.
- Of the 300 students who ended the previous year below the school-wide average: 40 enrolled, mean 2.60; 260 did not enroll, mean 2.75.
- All 600 students together: enrolled, mean 3.43; not enrolled, mean 3.01.

So the aggregate comparison favors the program, while every within-group comparison goes the other way.

The dean's argument is that the aggregate is the only figure that answers the question the board actually asked, which was whether to keep funding the block, and she adds that the same aggregate result survives slicing the data by grade level or by whether a student has an IEP, so it cannot be an artifact of one convenient choice of categories. She also says the critics' own numbers prove nothing, because within each group the students who volunteered may have differed from those who did not in ways the registrar never recorded. The faculty critics say the disaggregation is the only honest comparison, since enrollment was voluntary and heavily concentrated among students who were already succeeding; one of them now describes the report as documenting a program that looks good mainly because it recruited good students. They add that whatever the aggregate says, the students the block was created to help did worse when they attended it.

Nobody is accusing anybody of arithmetic errors, and nobody has any new data. What kind of disagreement is this?

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the two sides are answering different questions under the single heading of whether the study hall works — one a question about a funding decision, one about a causal effect — and that is why no computation can move anyone. Is that the right diagnosis, or is it something else: a hidden empirical question about how enrollment was determined that could in principle be settled, a dispute about which conditional is the right one to report, or a normative disagreement about what counts as a fair comparison that no data could resolve?

Please ground the diagnosis in what each side is actually committed to, and say what each would have to concede for the other to win. Then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a different study design, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and why. I already understand the mechanics by which a reversal like this can arise, so please do not spend the answer re-deriving it, and I am not looking for references or a reading list. Our paper follows American usage, so please write your response in American English.
