I cover science and ideas for a national magazine — mostly health and risk, plus the occasional big prediction piece. My problem is not the arithmetic. It's that nearly every number that reaches my desk is a percentage attached to some group of things, and the group almost never gets spelled out, so we end up printing a figure that's technically accurate about a population nobody in the story is a member of. Our existing fact-check rule is basically "is there a study behind it," which is useless here, because the studies are fine and the numbers still mislead.

I've been told this has a name — the reference class problem, the old point that a single case belongs to indefinitely many classes with different frequencies in each (Venn's consumptive Englishman and whether he lives to sixty-one, and somebody's later rule about using the narrowest class you actually have reliable statistics for). Knowing the name hasn't helped me at 6pm on deadline. So: turn it into a procedure my desk can run. That's me plus one part-time fact-checker who's had a single stats course, no in-house statistician, twenty minutes per claim, and the number either runs or it doesn't — so it has to terminate in a verdict, not a shrug.

What I want in it:

- the questions in order, with what counts as an adequate answer to each (starting from something like: who's in the denominator, and who got excluded, and was that exclusion decided before anyone saw results)
- how to tell a class that was built backwards from a number somebody wanted from one that was chosen for a defensible reason and then happened to produce that number
- what to do when two classes are both legitimate and give wildly different figures — I'm not willing to just print the bigger one, and I'm not willing to average them either
- a fallback for when no class is worth trusting: what does the sentence say instead of a percentage
- the steps that are really judgment calls wearing a procedure costume, flagged as such, so I don't kid myself about how mechanical this is
- and the one question on the phone that reliably gets a source to concede their class is shaky

Then run it on three cases:

1. A carbon-removal startup tells us that of the 40 previous attempts to get direct air capture to its target cost, 3 succeeded, so the base rate is under 8 percent and they intend to beat it. Digging in, maybe fifteen of those 40 were demonstration units funded as research that never had a cost target to hit at all.
2. An epidemiologist says 12 percent of people infected with a given virus report fatigue a year later. The class is people who sought testing. Meanwhile a survey reports 5 percent of adults generally reporting the same fatigue over the same year. Our graphics desk wants one of those numbers on a chart and I can't decide which.
3. A think-tank line I ran last spring: one in five first-time founders who take a certain accelerator route end up losing control of their company. The denominator was about sixty organizations in the think tank's own panel, most of whom had signed up because they were already interested in governance. Nobody complained, but if the procedure is any good it should have killed that number before it ran. Tell me what the sentence should have said instead.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do — where a determined source can answer every question honestly and still leave the reader with a false impression, and where the right move is just to print their number with the class stapled to it in the same sentence and let readers do the work. I'd rather know the failure modes up front than discover them in a correction.
