I'm a distributed-systems engineer. Philosophy is something I read on my own, badly, and the only relevant thing I've read is the part of Parfit where he introduces the teletransporter and concludes that identity is not what matters in survival. Out of my own working domain I have built an argument that appears to refute his reductionist view, and I have come to distrust it, because its conclusion is that my employer's storage-retention configuration can kill people. I would like a diagnosis rather than a defense.

**The apparatus.** Treat this as a thought experiment in the spirit of the teletransporter; I am not claiming it is or will be implementable.

- A person's mental processes run as a single distributed job across a cluster. I stipulate that the psychological facts — memory, character, intentions, the ongoing stream of experience — are exactly the ones at issue, and that no information is lost or added except where I say so.
- Live migration: to move the job from node A to node B, the system copies state page by page while A keeps running, then performs a cutover: A is paused, the residual changed state is shipped, B resumes. For roughly three seconds both images are capable of accepting input; our implementation quiesces one, so only one does.
- Retention: after cutover, the paused image on A is snapshotted and kept for R days before garbage collection. R is a configuration value, set by the platform team for storage-cost reasons. It is currently 30 and has been changed twice in the past year. A retained snapshot can be resumed.
- Resumption: if we resume a snapshot on day d after cutover, the resumed stream has quasi-memories of everything up to cutover plus d days of experience the running instance lacks; the running instance has d days the resumed stream lacks. Both are psychologically continuous with the person who was migrated.

**The argument.** I have tried to keep each step to something I would have accepted before I started.

1. Reductionism: a person's existing over time consists in nothing beyond physical and psychological continuity and connectedness, holding in the right way, and not branching. There is no further fact.
2. Connectedness comes in degrees, and continuity is just overlapping chains of it. So the facts of this kind are graded facts.
3. Identity is not graded. So identity has to be recovered from graded facts by means of a cutoff.
4. Given (1), there is nothing further for the cutoff to track. So the cutoff is laid on top of the facts rather than read off them: which graded profile counts as identity is settled partly by stipulation.
5. Stipulations can be revised, and several can be equally admissible; by (1) the reductionist has no resources with which to prefer one.
6. In my apparatus the choice is not merely verbal. Call the relevant parameter the divergence tolerance: how far two streams may drift before they count as two people rather than one. At three seconds and fifty milliseconds of drift, everyone, the reductionist included, will call the migration one continuing person. At ten days of drift on both sides, everyone will call it two. There is therefore a cutoff somewhere between, and by (4) nothing decides where it sits.
7. In our system that cutoff is effectively decided by R, since R determines how long a second stream can be brought into existence and sustained.
8. And this is what makes the argument more than a complaint about vagueness: shortening R does not merely relabel the case, because destroying the snapshot changes what exists. If the snapshot is destroyed on day 3 and never resumed, there is no branching and identity holds. If it is retained and resumed on day 10, there is branching and identity fails. On the reductionist's own account, then, whether the person who entered migration on day 0 survives turns on a configuration value chosen to save money on storage.
9. The change can also be made after the fact. On day 40, noticing that R should have been 90, we recover the image from cold backup and resume it. By (8) it thereby becomes true, forty days later, that the person who entered migration on day 0 did not survive.
10. But whether you survive cannot depend on my employer's storage policy, and it cannot be that a decision taken in week six alters who was alive in week one. A view with that consequence is wrong.
11. Therefore reductionism is false: identity is a further fact, or at any rate the graded facts underdetermine survival in a way that only something further could close.

**What I want from you.**

- Tell me whether the chain is valid as written. If it is, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and what I lose by surrendering it. If it is not, identify the step and say what it slides from and what it slides to.
- Tell me why the defective step looked acceptable to me. I suspect (4) and (8) are not doing the same kind of work, but I cannot articulate the difference.
- Tell me whether the argument survives if I drop the retroactivity move in (9) and (10) entirely and rest on (8) alone. My sense is that (8) is the strongest thing I have and may be strong enough by itself; I want to know whether that is right, and if it is, what a reductionist is entitled to say about it.
- Tell me whether any step depends on my using a philosophical term in a non-standard way. I have no training, and I would much rather be told that I have built the argument out of a misuse of "stipulation" or "consists in" than be answered on the merits of a position I have misdescribed.

I would like to keep (1) and (10) if I can: further facts smell to me like souls, and I do not want to concede that survival is at the mercy of bookkeeping. If both cannot be kept, say which one has to go and what the argument then proves.
