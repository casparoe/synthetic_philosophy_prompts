For an independent study this year I designed my own Bongard problems — the puzzles with twelve small boxes, six on each side, each holding a simple black-and-white figure, where you have to work out what is true of all six figures on the left and false of all six on the right. I read about them in a book on machine intelligence and thought it would be interesting to invent twenty of my own, give them to the twenty-four students in my class, and record what actually happened. I have the results, and I cannot turn them into anything general. I would like to know whether there is a principle behind them.

Here is what happened.

**1. One hole.** Left-hand figures each contain exactly one enclosed region; right-hand figures contain none, or two, or more. Nineteen of twenty-four people had it inside three minutes. Several said they did not reason about it at all, they just saw it.

**2. Connected.** Every left-hand figure is a single piece; every right-hand figure comes in two or more separate pieces. Also solved fast. But the descriptions people gave were completely different from one another: "one piece," "no gaps," "you could trace the whole thing without jumping," "nothing has fallen off it." I do not know whether that variety means they had one concept or four.

**3. Mirror symmetry.** Left-hand figures are symmetric about a vertical axis, right-hand ones are not. This took most people fifteen minutes or longer, and six of them landed on a confident, wrong rule about relative size and would not abandon it even after I pointed out a box that contradicted it.

**4. Odd number of strokes.** Left-hand figures are made of an odd number of straight line segments, right-hand ones of an even number. Essentially nobody got it. Of the two who were told the answer afterwards, one said it felt like cheating: "that could be the rule for anything."

**5. One stroke, no retracing.** Every left-hand figure can be drawn in a single continuous pass that covers each line exactly once; no right-hand figure can. Two people got it, after a long time, and both immediately said it was obvious in retrospect. Everyone else had given up or decided the problem was broken. This is the result that bothers me most, because it looks like a counting rule just as much as number 4 does, and yet it behaved nothing like it.

**6. An accidental rule.** On one problem I had drawn the left-hand figures with a slightly heavier pen. About half the class correctly identified that difference, and I had to tell them it was not the rule. They were angry. Several said the problem was unfair, and one said that if an answer is not the sort of thing the designer could have meant, then it is not an answer.

**7. Taller than wide.** The rule was that each left-hand figure is taller than it is wide. People kept hunting for properties of the shapes themselves rather than a relation between a figure's own dimensions, and did badly. One said out loud, "you aren't allowed to compare a box to itself," which I take to be a rule of the game they believed in but could not have stated.

**8. An unstatable rule.** One problem I made had a real difference that I could not have put into words without pointing at the pictures — something about how the parts were arranged with respect to each other. Nobody solved it, and three people told me afterwards they were certain there was no rule and that I had simply drawn two random sets.

Two further observations. When I gave problem 3 back to the same people two weeks later they solved it in seconds. And when I hinted at problem 4 with the single phrase "think about what is missing," two people solved it immediately, which makes no sense to me, since that phrase has nothing to do with counting strokes.

My own hypothesis, which I expect you to attack: a Bongard problem is solvable from a handful of examples just in case the property separating the two sides is one that human vision already computes for other purposes. But symmetry is surely computed, and 3 was hard; parity of stroke count is surely not, and 4 was hopeless, which fits; 5 is also a counting rule and yet was solvable; and 6 shows that visual salience cannot be sufficient, since heavier strokes are about as salient as anything gets and were rejected outright. So I think my principle is either false or missing a second factor entirely.

What I want from you:

State a principle, or a small number of competing candidates, precise enough that I could use it to predict whether a new problem of mine will be solved, and say which of my results motivates each clause of it. Then tell me honestly which of the results it cannot accommodate, and whether you think any of them should be reinterpreted or simply discarded rather than explained — in particular the pair 4 and 5, and the pair 6 and 7, where my classmates rejected answers that were true.

On 6 and 7 specifically: is the difficulty perceptual, or is something normative going on, a constraint on which hypotheses are admissible answers at all? If the latter, state that constraint as clearly as you can, and say whether it is a fact about human minds, a fact about the conventions of the puzzle genre, or a fact about what a rule has to be in order to be worth calling a concept.

Finally, tell me what your principle predicts for problems I have not tried: one where every left-hand figure has at least one line segment that no other segment touches, and none of the right-hand figures does; and a modified version of problem 4 in which the left-hand figures have exactly one stroke and the right-hand figures have exactly two. If your principle gives the same verdict for both, I think that is a problem for it, and I would like you to say so.
