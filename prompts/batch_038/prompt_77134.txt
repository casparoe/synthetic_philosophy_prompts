I am in the second year of a PhD in legal philosophy, and the chapter I am struggling with concerns constitutional rules that purport to govern their own amendment. My running example is Article 79(3) of the German Basic Law, which declares certain principles — the division of the Federation into Länder, their participation in legislation, and the principles of Articles 1 and 20 — inadmissible to amendment, while remaining silent about whether 79(3) itself could be repealed by the two-thirds majority that the rest of Article 79 makes available. The literature on the general problem splits into two camps that have, so far as I can tell, not moved against each other since Alf Ross posed the self-reference puzzle in *Mind* in 1969 and Peter Suber answered it at book length, and my reading group reproduces the deadlock with almost comic fidelity. The formalists hold that a norm-conferring rule can confer only powers it itself possesses, so an amendment clause A cannot be the source of authority for its own replacement by A′: either the change is invalid — which makes tolerated inconsistency structural to every constitutional order that has ever amended its amendment clause — or the authority of A′ must come from outside the system, a fresh presupposition of the basic-norm kind, which means the system has silently been replaced rather than amended. The institutionalists reply that legal validity is not derivability, that what makes A′ law is that officials identify, apply, and accept it as such, and that the paradox survives only because the formalists have imported a deductivist model of a legal system that no actual system satisfies even in its most routine operations.

What frustrates me is that the two sides agree on everything I would have expected to be doing the work. They agree that self-amendment happens constantly and that courts generally uphold it; they agree on the text of the clauses in the standard examples; they agree that a successful coup and a valid constitutional replacement differ normatively and that the difference matters. The exchange then collapses into a pair of standing accusations. The formalists say the institutionalists have described a change of fact and called it an account of authority — that "officials accept it" is precisely the explanandum, and that on the institutionalist's own resources there is no principled difference between a valid self-amendment and a coup that happens to stick. The institutionalists say that if the formalist standard were the right one, ordinary delegation, the repeal of statutes by statutes, and arguably promulgation itself would all come out paradoxical, so the standard cannot be a standard for law at all; and they add that the formalist is entitled to no comfort here, since on any deductivist reconstruction the eternity clause that fails to entrench itself is simply repealable, a conclusion no constitutional lawyer in any jurisdiction will say out loud. A colleague of mine tried to break the tie by suggesting that the whole thing turns on whether the authority relation between A and A′ is synchronic or diachronic, but neither side took the bait and I am not sure he knew what he was saying.

So I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than an adjudication of it, and certainly not a summary of the debate or a list of what to read next — I have the bibliography and I am not asking you to reconstruct it. Take the two positions as I have described them and tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal, resting on two senses of validity or of authority that each camp uses without marking the difference? Is it substantive but mislocated — that is, is the real dispute about whether a legal system is the kind of object whose norms stand in derivability relations at all, in which case self-amendment is merely where that much larger disagreement happens to surface? Are the camps answering different questions under one heading, a question about the coherence of a normative system on one side and a question about the efficacy and continuity of a practice on the other? Is there a hidden formal or temporal question — something about indexing rules to times, or about self-embracing versus continuing conceptions of rule-making power — that one side is tacitly assuming and that could be settled rather than argued about? Or is the impasse evidence that the question is ill-posed, and the very idea of a rule grounding its own replacement a category mistake? My own working hypothesis, which I hold loosely and want tested, is that the disagreement is genuine but not about self-amendment: that the camps are divided over the correct model of a legal system, that neither will concede anything at the level of amendment clauses, and that the stalemate would dissolve only if the model question were settled elsewhere. Tell me whether that is right, and if it is not, where the self-amendment-specific content lies. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result such as a dynamic or temporal deontic framework in which a rule at one time authorizes its own supersession at a later time without contradiction, a change of question, or a judicial decision that would force one camp to describe its own commitments differently — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why. I would like the diagnosis defended by reference to what each side is committed to saying, including where each is vulnerable to the other's best objection, and I would like you to be explicit about whether both camps could be right in their own terms and, if so, whether that is a comfortable place for me to leave my chapter.
