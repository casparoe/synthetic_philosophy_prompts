I have been going around the responsibility-gap material for years, and what I now distrust is not my intuitions but my ability to systematize them. So I want to put my verdicts on a set of cases on the table and have you tell me what principle, if any, they add up to. Treat this as an exercise in reflective equilibrium rather than a survey: argue from the cases, not from authorities, and do not give me a map of who has written what. I am not looking for a reading list.

The cases, with my verdicts.

A. A Level 4 delivery van, dry road, midday, maintenance current, all validation the regulator required completed, executes a maneuver nobody on the safety team anticipated and kills a cyclist. Verdict: no individual is blameworthy. But I also think the operating firm owes the family compensation and a public accounting, and I am unwilling to let the event be filed as sheer accident.

B. Same van, same outcome, except the incident review finds the perception stack was never validated in heavy rain, the team knew that gap was open, and they shipped anyway to make a demo date. Verdict: whoever decided to ship is blameworthy, and their blame is not much diminished by the fact that the probability of this particular crash was low.

C. A hospital network licenses a triage model. It quietly deprioritizes patients from one demographic. Each vendor engineer did competent local work; no clinician at the hospital inspected the model. Verdict: somebody is blameworthy, and my instinct ranks the administrators who adopted it unvalidated above the engineers who built it.

D. A platform's ranking system, tuned on engagement alone, walks an isolated teenager toward violent extremism over eighteen months; he attacks a stranger. Verdict: I want to say nobody at the platform is blameworthy for the attack. I recognize that C and D look structurally identical to me — a firm deploys a system it does not model, harm arrives by an unpredicted route, the firm profits, no individual has a guilty mental state — and I have failed to find a difference I am willing to defend.

E. A judge adopts a recidivism score, knows its methodology is contested, and follows its recommendation for the harsher sentence in essentially every case for two years. Verdict: blameworthy; "the tool suggested it" is no defense. What troubles me is the asymmetry with D: her causal contribution is smaller than the platform's, and she at least looked at individual defendants.

F. A drone operator engages a target the system designates, having been told the designation is reliable; it is a wedding party. Verdict: blameworthy only if she had some reason to doubt the designation; otherwise the blame goes to whoever certified the system. I am comfortable here.

G. At noon, sober, a man decides to drive home drunk. At 1 a.m., severely intoxicated and unable to do otherwise, he runs a light and kills someone. Verdict: fully blameworthy for the death.

H. An engineer ships a system that keeps learning in deployment, whose future decisions she cannot predict in detail and cannot reconstruct afterward; one of those decisions causes a death. Verdict: not blameworthy for that death. I cannot separate G and H without special pleading: in both, an agent with full capacity at t0 starts a process that removes her capacity at t1.

I. A lab releases a capable model's weights, sincerely hoping for beneficial use; a downstream actor fine-tunes it and causes serious harm. Verdict: I flip. Some days the release was reckless and blame attaches to the releasers. Other days the downstream choice severs the chain, and blaming the releaser is like blaming a locksmith. Tell me which day is right, or whether the flipping tracks a real distinction I have been unable to name.

One suspicion I want tested rather than flattered: several of my verdicts may not be about blame at all, but about compensation, or about who owes an explanation, or about a role obligation that survives the absence of fault. Separating those relations may dissolve some of the tension. Say plainly where that move genuinely resolves a conflict and where it is an evasion — relabeling a hard case so that it stops looking hard.

Deliverable: a single JSON object, no prose outside it, conforming exactly to this schema. Fill every field; use null only where a field is genuinely inapplicable. case_id values must be drawn from A through I.

- bottom_line: string. Three to six sentences: the principle you recommend, and which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon.
- normative_relations: array of objects, each with relation (string, e.g. culpability, liability to compensate, answerability, role obligation — name them yourself), definition (string, precise enough that a third party could apply it), and cases (array of case_ids where this relation, not culpability, is doing the work in my verdict).
- is_separating_relations_legitimate: array of objects, each with case_id, verdict_before (string), verdict_after (string), and honest_or_evasive (string: "honest" or "evasive", plus one sentence of justification).
- candidate_principles: array of exactly three objects, each with:
  - name: string.
  - statement: string. A formulation precise enough to be applied to a case nobody has described yet, with the conditions explicit rather than gestured at.
  - clauses: array of objects, each with clause (string) and motivated_by (case_id plus one sentence on why that case forces the clause in).
  - case_verdicts: array of objects, one per case A–I, each with case_id, verdict (one of "blameworthy", "not_blameworthy", "partial", "blameworthy_under_other_relation", "indeterminate"), matches_asker (boolean), and note (string, one sentence).
  - unaccommodated: array of objects, each with case_id, my_verdict (string), principle_says (string), and why_the_conflict_is_real (string). Empty array if it saves everything.
  - complexity_cost: string. How many primitive notions or disjuncts the principle needs, and whether any of them look designed to fit a particular case.
  - novel_case: object with case_description (a case I have not listed), verdict, and reasoning. Choose, where you can, a novel case on which your three principles disagree, so the prediction carries information.
- hard_pairs: array of two objects covering C-vs-D and G-vs-H, each with case_a, case_b, tension_is_real (boolean), diagnosis (string), and what_must_be_given_up (string).
- flip_case: object with case_id "I", recommended_verdict (string), and decisive_difference (string — the distinction that settles it, or an explicit statement that there is none and my flipping is noise).
- recommendation: object with adopt (string), overturn (array of case_ids), keep_at_a_cost (array of case_ids), and residual_worry (string — the case where you think my surviving intuition is still probably wrong).
- self_check: object with rejected_alternatives (array of objects, each name and why_rejected) and least_confident (string).
