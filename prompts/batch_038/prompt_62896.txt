I am a doctoral candidate in political philosophy, and this term I am marking for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on justice between generations. One of the two written assignments was a 500-word response to a single prompt, worth 15 points; the rubric weights two things equally, an accurate reconstruction of the problem and a defensible assessment of the conclusion drawn from it. I have read the submission twice and would like an independent read before I assign points. Please give a grade out of 15 and then a paragraph-by-paragraph set of comments: what is sound, what is not, and specifically which inferential steps fail and what the student would have to say instead to earn the remaining marks. I want comments the student can act on, not a general impression.

The assignment prompt was: Explain the non-identity problem as it arises for climate policy, and assess whether it shows that high-emission policies do not wrong future people.

The student's submission, in four paragraphs:

1. The non-identity problem arises because large-scale policy choices affect not only how well future people live but which particular people exist. Emissions decisions reshape economies, labour markets, migration, and the timing of relationships, so the specific individuals alive two centuries from now are a function of the policies we choose. A high-emission path leaves those future people worse off than the people who would have lived under a low-emission path — but they are not the same people. As long as their lives are worth living, none of them is worse off than she would otherwise have been, because the only alternative for each of them is never to have existed at all.

2. This looks devastating for the standard case for mitigation. If wronging someone requires making that person worse off than she would otherwise have been, then a high-emission policy wrongs no future person: all of them have lives worth living, and they owe those lives to the policy. Nor can we lodge a complaint in their name, since the individuals in question would not exist to benefit from our restraint. The claim that we owe future generations a habitable climate therefore collapses.

3. Parfit's own remedy is to compare the two futures impersonally and prefer the one containing the greater total of well-being. On this total view the high-emission future is worse even though nobody in it is worse off, and the policy can still be condemned. This preserves the verdict we want about climate change without abandoning the idea that morality is fundamentally about people.

4. The consequences for policy are narrower than they first appear. Since future generations cannot be wronged, the real victims of high emissions are those already alive or already certain to be alive, whose identities our choices do not affect. Their interests are handled by ordinary cost-benefit analysis: emissions should be cut wherever abatement costs are lower than the resulting damages, and the far future simply drops out of the calculation. This is a welcome conclusion, because it means climate policy need not be driven by speculative claims about people we will never meet.
