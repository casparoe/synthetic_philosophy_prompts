For next week's seminar I want to float a slightly heretical proposal about the problem of old evidence, and I'd like you to play the role of a working Bayesian epistemologist who is willing to defend it in public. Forget the usual repertoire of fixes (learning the entailment relation itself as the new fact, Jeffrey-style reweighting, weakening the certainty in E from the start, etc.). Here's the rule I want you to champion — call it the Rewind Rule. When you discover that some hypothesis H you're now taking seriously logically entails a piece of evidence E that you'd already assigned credence 1, you do not conditionalize on the news about the entailment. Instead you (i) go back to the credence function you held immediately before you first came to believe E, (ii) conditionalize that function on everything you've learned in the meantime *other* than E and other than the entailment fact, and (iii) finally conditionalize the result on H ∧ E, treating E as though it were arriving for the first time. That output is your new credence function.

Your job: argue that the Rewind Rule genuinely resolves the old evidence problem, rather than just papering over it. I want a real position, not a survey — the upshot should be that this is what a careful Bayesian ought to do, and that the standard complaints (diacronic Dutch books and money-pumps, the charge of flagrant ad hocery, order-dependence in step (ii), the sheer psychological impossibility of recovering your old credence function, and the worry that H gets confirmed by something you already knew all along) are either survivable or misdirected. Start with a chunk of rough brainstorming about where exactly the rule is most likely to snap, pick the two or three threats that actually bite, and then build the argument around defusing those. Be careful and be concrete: work through at least one worked numerical example with made-up but consistent numbers so I can see the rule doing its thing, and say plainly what the rule commits you to about the epistemic status of your own past credence functions. No references needed, just the reasoning.
