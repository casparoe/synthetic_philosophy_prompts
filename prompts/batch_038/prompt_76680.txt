Ok so I'm in week 7 of a war ethics module and I've talked myself into a position I can't quite defend. My tutor said as much in tutorial, only nicer. I'd rather get the blunt version.

The position: the moral equality of combatants is true — fighters on both sides have the same permission to target each other and the same liability to be targeted — but not for the reasons anyone in my seminar keeps giving. I don't buy the excuse/coercion story, since most soldiers aren't literally forced and the account then only covers conscripts. I don't buy the epistemic story, that nobody can really tell which side is just, because it obviously doesn't hold in a lot of actual wars. And I don't buy the convention story where the equality is just an artifact of the laws of war plus everyone's agreement to be bound by them, because that makes the whole thing a legal fiction rather than a moral fact.

My version: liability isn't a function of what a soldier believes, or of how much they individually cause, or of how culpable they are. It comes from voluntarily taking up a role inside an institution whose entire function is fighting. Because there are two such institutions facing each other, participation generates a reciprocal relation — each side's fighters, by taking up the role, authorize the other side's use of force against people in that role. Call it the mutual authorization account. It's supposed to block the revisionist move (that liability has to track moral responsibility for an unjust threat, which would make just combatants non-liable and every killing of them murder) without conceding that just and unjust soldiers are identical in every other respect. It's also supposed to dodge the threshold dilemma: if you set the responsibility bar for liability high enough that only the genuinely culpable clear it, then in a real army almost nobody does — not the 19-year-old, not the driver — and the just side can't fight at all. My account doesn't make liability a matter of degree, so there's no threshold to set.

So: what would count against this? Sorted, not just listed. Seperate the objections that would actually kill the view from the ones that are merely uncomfortable, and tell me which of them someone already committed to the view would have to concede as real rather than wave away. I don't need references or a reading list, I want the arguments.

Things I'm already worried about, feel free to say I'm worrying about the wrong ones:

- The transparent war. An international body rules against the invasion before shooting starts, the aggressor's own troops are briefed on the ruling and know their state lost. There's no epistemic excuse and no coercion. Does my view say these people are exactly as liable as the conscript across the border? If yes, haven't I given up the one thing that made revisionism feel right — that some people are more liable than others? If no, I'm smuggling responsibility back in through the door.
- Where the role stops. Drone operators, intelligence analysts, the contractor running the depot, the munitions workers. If role-membership does all the work, does my view make civilians liable too, and if I have to draw the line with something else, is the account still distinct from the ones I rejected?
- Non-state wars. If authorization needs an institution on both sides, what happens when one side has no army, no uniforms, no chain of command? Am I offering a theory of combatant equality or just a theory of interstate war?
- The collapse worry, which I think is the biggest one. If the reciprocal authorization relation has to be a real social fact to explain anything, I've rebuilt the convention account. If it's a moral relation that holds regardless of what anyone does or agrees to, then nothing could ever show it failing, and I've insulated the view until it's unfalsifiable. Give me a straight verdict on which horn I'm on and whether that's fatal or survivable.

Also, what does my account predict that the excuse and convention accounts don't? Mercenaries, volunteers versus conscripts, soldiers who desert, a just side that fights by atrocities — anywhere the three views come apart, because that's the only way I'll know whether I've got a view at all or just a repackaging. And does it stand or fall with something outside war, like a general claim about how liability in self-defence works? I'd definately be most persuaded by an objection that showed the account can't handle the atrocities case, so press hardest there.
