I've been reading the actual text of the sentience laws that have started appearing over the last decade or so and there's a pattern in them I can't get my head around.

The EU treaty provision says that when the Union and its members make agriculture, fisheries, transport, internal market and research policy they shall, "since animals are sentient beings, pay full regard to the welfare requirements of animals" — and then, in the same sentence, says this is to be done while respecting national provisions and customs relating in particular to "religious rites, cultural traditions and regional heritage." The UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 does something different: it sets up a standing committee that can review a government policy and report on whether it has an adverse effect on "the welfare of animals as sentient beings," and the government then has to answer the report. The Act covers vertebrates plus cephalopod molluscs and decapod crustaceans — the invertebrates got added after a commissioned review of the evidence — and it applies to wild animals as well as domesticated ones.

Meanwhile basically everyone involved says sentience comes in degrees: more of it in an octopus than a shrimp, shades rather than a line. But the legal apparatus is a membership list. You're on it or you're not, and once you're on it there's no such thing as counting for half as much.

So: reconstruct the theory of moral status this apparatus presupposes. A few things I want pulled apart specifically. What is the difference between animal welfare and animal welfare "as sentient beings" — is the second phrase narrower, or just decorative? What work is the "since" clause doing inside an operative provision rather than a preamble — does it make the duty hostage to the facts, revisable if the evidence changes? And why does the committee's remit run to harms that flow from government policy, so that a wild animal's suffering only comes into view when state action is implicated? That looks like it's tracking who caused the harm rather than whose harm it is.

My own guess is that the list isn't a theory of value at all, it's a jurisdictional device — a way of saying which animals this committee may look at — and the whole thing is a claim about who gets to speak rather than about what matters. But if that's right I can't explain why the treaty bothers to give a reason.

Tell me what the practice strictly requires versus what it merely sits comfortably with, which rival accounts it actually rules out (species-level or ecosystem views, relational standing, indirect-duty views, straightforwardly graded views) versus just fails to express, and where it's philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me what an institution built on genuinely scalar status would have to look like — weights per taxon, confidence-adjusted — and what would break first when someone tried to run it.
