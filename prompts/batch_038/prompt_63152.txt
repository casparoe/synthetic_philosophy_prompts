I direct a graduate seminar in the philosophy of time, and this term I have set aside three weeks for the interface between general relativity and the metaphysics of temporal passage. Before I circulate my framing handout, I should like to test the reasoning behind its central claim, and I would welcome a candid assessment — including, if it comes to that, a demonstration that I have gone wrong somewhere along the way.

The claim is this. Gödel's rotating cosmology of 1949 is an exact solution of Einstein's field equations: pressureless dust together with a cosmological constant tuned to the density of that dust, with closed timelike curves running through every event. Two consequences seem to me to follow. First, because the model is a bona fide solution rather than a mathematical contrivance, journeys into the past are something the laws of nature permit, and the grandfather paradox is accordingly a live conceptual difficulty for physics rather than an amusement. Second, and more important for my present purposes, the empirical objection my students reliably raise — that we have detected no cosmic rotation and have good reason to think there is none — cannot get off the ground. Gödel's spacetime is perfectly homogeneous: it has no center, no axis, no privileged location, and every observer is situated exactly as every other is. Rotation of that kind would be a purely global property without local signature, so there is no measurement we could make, and no pattern we could hunt for in the cosmic microwave background, that would distinguish a rotating cosmos from a non-rotating one. Absence of evidence in this case would not be weak evidence of absence; it would be no evidence at all, and my students' objection would therefore be beside the point.

My question, then, is this. Granted that no observation could settle whether our universe is Gödelian, how should I have my students weigh the upshot Gödel himself drew from the model — that the lapse of time is not an objective feature of reality — against the rival reading on which the very existence of closed timelike curves instead vindicates passage, since on that reading the past is quite literally a place one can travel to? I am especially interested in whether the second reading is available at all to someone who accepts Gödel's premises, or whether it collapses back into the first.

Please compose your reply in American English.
