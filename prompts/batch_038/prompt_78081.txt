I chair the appraisal panel for the Kestrel Row Conservation Area, designated under our municipal heritage ordinance in 1987. The ordinance authorises designation where an area possesses "special historic or architectural character," and authorises withdrawal where that character has been "substantially eroded." Neither phrase is defined, and the panel has no interpretive guidance beyond its own past decisions.

The facts. The 1987 designation report described the terrace as "almost wholly intact": 63 properties, predominantly 1840–1910, with consistent cornice lines, brick or stone facades, and original sliding-sash windows. Since designation the panel has approved 41 applications for alterations — replacement windows, render over brick, dormers, porches, two garages, a rear extension — each on the express finding that it would cause no material harm to the area's character. Our officers' survey this spring records 22 of the 63 properties as substantially unaltered, 19 as rendered, and 31 as having non-original windows; the cornice line is broken in four places. A cumulative review has now been triggered by a residents' petition, and the ordinance requires the panel to reach a decision within fourteen weeks. Separately, an application is before us to replace the last surviving pair of original timber shopfronts on the terrace with a glazed frontage; that decision falls due in six weeks, and the applicant has asked for certainty either way.

The panel is split in a way I have not been able to resolve. One member holds that whether the character has been substantially eroded is a question with a right answer even if no one could say where the line falls, that our ignorance of the line is not indeterminacy in the facts, and that declining to act on the ground of borderline-ness is itself a decision — one that licenses further loss. Another proposes what she calls an invariance rule: the panel should withdraw designation only where withdrawal would be correct on every admissible way of making "substantially eroded" precise, and should otherwise defer, on the ground that in a genuinely borderline case there is nothing for the panel to get wrong. I suspect that rule is self-defeating in precisely the cumulative cases it is meant to protect us in, but I cannot yet say why in terms the panel would accept. There is also a difficulty nobody has raised: if admissible precisifications are constrained by how the term has actually been applied, then our own 41 approvals have narrowed the range, and the invariance rule would allow the panel's past leniency to determine what now counts as borderline.

Please draft the memorandum I will circulate before the meeting, 900–1,200 words. It should recommend one course of action — withdrawal, retention unchanged, retention coupled with removal of the permitted-development rights that currently allow minor alterations to proceed without consent, or deferral — with a timetable that meets the fourteen-week deadline. It should deal separately with the shopfront application and say whether the answer to it ought to depend on the answer to the designation question. Give reasons the proponent of the invariance rule could engage with rather than dismiss, and address the endogeneity difficulty above. State explicitly which account of vagueness your recommendation presupposes, and what adopting it commits the panel to when the next cumulative case arrives. Finally, propose the decision rule we should write into guidance for future cumulative reviews, in language an officer could apply to a survey return without consulting the panel. Argue from the case; do not supply citations or a reading list.
