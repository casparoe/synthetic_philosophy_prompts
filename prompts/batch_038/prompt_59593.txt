I have been asked to give a talk to a regional naturalist society on how biologists decide what counts as a species, and while preparing it I came across the following sidebar in a widely read trade book on biodiversity. The author is a science journalist rather than a biologist, and the book is generally well regarded, so I do not want to build my talk on this passage until I know how much of it to trust. It reads:

> Biologists used to think of a species as a group of organisms that can breed with one another and produce fertile young, and that are reproductively isolated from every other group — isolated, that is, by living in different places and never meeting. Ernst Mayr put this criterion on a firm theoretical footing in the 1940s, and it remains the working definition for zoologists. Its weak point is that nature keeps supplying exceptions: in gulls and in salamanders there are rings in which population A breeds with B, B with C, and so on around the loop, until the population at the far end no longer breeds with the one at the start, so the definition contradicts itself. Philosophers of biology have in any case concluded that species are individuals rather than classes — historical entities with no defining essence — which means there is no fact of the matter about where one species stops and the next begins. The upshot is liberating: the number of species on Earth is a bookkeeping choice, and disputes over whether two populations are one species or two are disputes about words.

I am inclined to think the final sentence overreaches, but I suspect I would also be conceding too much if I accepted the sentences leading up to it at face value, and I cannot tell where the rewriting happens. Could you audit the passage sentence by sentence? For each, tell me what a working systematist would sign off on, what is a harmless simplification that costs nothing, and what is mistaken — flagging in particular any place where a technical term is being used in its everyday sense rather than its biological one. Then address three points specifically:

(a) Whether the criterion as the author states it is what Mayr's biological species concept actually says, and which of the author's phrasings changes its content rather than merely abbreviating it;

(b) What ring species are generally taken to show about the concept, and whether "contradicts itself" is the right description of the difficulty they create;

(c) Whether the claim that species are individuals rather than classes carries the skeptical consequence the author draws from it, or whether it is standardly argued in service of the opposite conclusion.

Finally, tell me which of the author's conclusions survive once the rest is corrected. In particular, I would like to know whether I can grant that species counts and species boundaries involve genuine judgment on the taxonomist's part without granting that such disputes are merely verbal — and if those two things come apart, what it is that keeps them apart.
