I'm three years into a philosophy PhD, mostly metaethics, and our reading group has been stuck on the cross-cultural stuff for two weeks now — the WEIRD critique in psychology, where the published sample is basically American undergrads and everybody else comes out different on fairness, on harm, on obligations to kin, on whether norms apply to strangers at all or only to people you'll have to look in the eye again. I've been volunteered to press the anti-objectivist line at next week's session and I'd rather spar with a real opponent beforehand than debut it cold. So: play a committed moral objectivist — someone who thinks moral facts obtain independently of what any given culture endorses, and who has actually read the anthropology instead of waving it away. Sharp, not a strawman. You can and should concede the small stuff: that a lot of the cross-cultural findings are measurement artifacts, that a translated vignette may not mean the same thing it means in English, that universal agreement was never a criterion of objectivity, that plenty of apparent moral variation is really variation in non-moral beliefs about how the world works. But not the main point. Specifically you don't get to dissolve the whole challenge by retreating to "they only disagree about the non-moral facts, so there's no moral disagreement after all" — I'm going to argue that move is itself a concession, so meet it head on. Stay in character until I say stop, keep replies to roughly a paragraph so it reads like a conversation and not a position paper, and if you need a detail about some study to make your point, ask me for it as an opponent would rather than assuming it.

Here's my opening. The cross-cultural record doesn't just show people disagreeing about morality in the way they disagree about whether the earth is flat, where the objectivist can happily say one side is simply wrong. It shows the disagreement tracking exactly the variables you'd predict if there were no fact of the matter — market integration, how much of your life runs through dealings with strangers you'll never meet again, whether your religion has a god who watches and punishes. People in small-scale foraging and horticultural societies make offers in bargaining games well below what a lecture hall of undergrads would make, and they mostly shrug at low offers instead of paying to punish them, and both of those things move together with market integration across societies.

Now suppose you give the standard reply: the forager and the economics student agree on the moral principle — give people their due, don't free-ride on the cooperative scheme — and differ only about the non-moral facts, namely who counts as a partner in any scheme and what the spirits are going to do about it. Fine, grant it for argument's sake. But then *which* principle is operative in a given situation gets settled by contingent empirical facts about that situation, and by the local culture's own picture of those facts, and once you've said that the anthropologist has almost everything she wanted: the moral truths that bind here don't bind there, not because morality is relative, but because the world the morality applies to is different in the two places. So where exactly, in that story, is the objectivity doing any work? Tell me what your view still buys you that "the facts on the ground differ" doesn't already buy, because right now it looks like you've hidden the relativism in the non-moral layer instead of getting rid of it.
