I am in the third year of a philosophy degree and currently taking a seminar on the normativity of social rules; my term paper is meant to defend a positive account, but my supervisor has told me to specify first what such an account owes us before I commit to one. For this task, please take the role of a professional moral philosopher who works on social norms, convention, and the boundary between the conventional and the moral. Think carefully rather than reaching for the first available framework, and before you write the list itself, spend one short paragraph brainstorming what is genuinely puzzling about this territory and where ordinary intuitions pull against one another.

The deliverable is a specification: the desiderata that any adequate theory of the moral status of everyday conventions must meet. By everyday conventions I mean the sort of thing at issue in tipping, queueing, jaywalking, holding a lift door, standing distance, or what one brings to a shared meal — locally settled norms of conduct that nobody enacted, that are sustained by informal sanction rather than law, and whose violation typically produces no identifiable harm to an identifiable victim.

For each desideratum, give me the requirement in one sentence, the concrete case or consideration that motivates it (a scenario, not an abstraction), and your verdict on whether it is non-negotiable for any theory in this area or whether it is something a particular camp demands and others may reasonably refuse. Ten to twelve desiderata, no fewer than eight. In particular, tell me which of the things people routinely demand here are actually prejudices of one camp.

The list must be able to handle the following pressures. Treat each as a test the theory has to pass, not as an illustration, and tell me where you think I have mischaracterised the issue:

- The visitor. A tourist in a city where the bus queue is scrupulously observed steps into a gap. Nobody is delayed, nobody is harmed, everyone sees it. Is there a moral complaint, and if so whose?
- The dead convention. In a city where nearly everyone crosses against the light when the street is clear, a visitor waits obediently at the kerb and holds up the person behind them. Does a norm need a threshold of general compliance before it generates obligations, and does the theory need to distinguish a convention that is locally alive from one that is locally binding?
- The exploitative convention. Customary restaurant tipping that functions not as a bonus but as the mechanism through which employers pay less than they otherwise would. The theory must be able to deny this one force without simply appealing to the fact that it is unjust, or the injustice clause is doing all the work and the account of convention is redundant.
- The shifting convention. I move somewhere the customary standing distance in a queue is half what I grew up with, and after six months my old instinct starts to feel like rudeness. Does obligation attach to the convention as it actually is, as I take it to be, or as the majority currently enforces it?
- The asymmetric convention. A practice that costs one group far more to comply with than another, where the burden is invisible to those who bear it lightly. Does the account need a fairness clause, and would that clause collapse the view into a general principle of fairness rather than a claim about convention as such?

It must also refuse to collapse these distinctions, and I want to know if you think any of them are not real distinctions: moral obligation versus social pressure; a convention being violated versus someone being wronged; the reason-giving force of a convention versus the reasons to believe a convention exists; being a participant in a practice versus merely being subject to one; and a convention that is arbitrary but salient (which side of the road) versus one that is arbitrary and uncoordinated (which dish to bring).

Then give me your view on the structure of the list. Which desideratum or pair of desiderata do you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied? I suspect that explaining the visitor's obligation and refusing to make obligation hostage to the visitor's own endorsement of the practice pull against each other, and that any threshold-of-compliance clause collides with the exploitative-convention case; tell me plainly if I am wrong about either. And which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidate theories?

Finally, and briefly, score three views I have sketched for the paper. Treat them as stipulated, and say so if any turns out not to be a distinct view:

1. Salience. Conventions matter only insofar as they solve coordination problems, so they bind only where there is a coordination problem and only to the extent required to solve it.
2. Expectation. What binds is the reliance a stable convention generates in others; violating it wrongs the particular people whose expectations you defeat.
3. Membership. Taking part in a shared practice is itself a way of standing in a relationship with a community, and violation is a failure to give co-participants their due, whether or not expectations are defeated or coordination fails.

For each, name the desiderata it plainly satisfies, the one it most clearly fails, and whether that failure looks repairable from inside the view.

Two constraints. First, no bibliography, no reading list, no citations or named authorities anywhere — I want the reasoning, not the references, and you should not have to guess at sources. Second, keep the whole response to roughly 1,400 to 1,700 words. A table is acceptable for the desiderata themselves, but the motivating cases must be in prose.
