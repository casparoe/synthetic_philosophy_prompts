I'm about two months into a PhD in history of science and I've gotten wedged between two people I respect. My advisor marked up my first chapter — its about an eighteenth-century naturalist who sorted human beings into varieties — with "presentist" down both margins, meaning I kept writing that he was wrong about race and kept using words for what he was doing that he'd never have used himself. Then at a workshop a senior person argued close to the opposite: that the anti-presentist norm is impossible anyway, since you pick your topic and your questions from wherever you're standing, and morally convenient besides, since "he was a man of his time" is doing live work in current arguments about statues and reparations. Our reading group has the same fight every couple of weeks and nobody moves. What strikes me is that both sides keep agreeing on the facts — everyone agrees anachronism can wreck an argument, everyone agrees we write from the present — and they still don't budge.

So what kind of disagreement is this? Is "presentism" doing triple duty as a slur — one person using it for anachronistic vocabulary, another for moral evaluation of the dead, a third for letting present concerns pick the research question — so that the fight is verbal? Or is it substantive but about something other than history, like what the discipline owes its readers: explaining why a smart person believed X, versus reconstructing X in its own terms, versus saying whether X was true? Or is it bedrock — an intuition about whether you can understand a view and condemn it at the same time that neither side can argue for?

I've got a hypothesis I want you to pressure-test rather than just repeat back at me: that the real fork is over where a text's meaning lives, in what the author could have intended or in what the words went on doing, and that once you split that question out the two sides stop disagreeing about history and start disagreeing about a philosophy of language they never signed up for. Tell me if that's right, wrong, or too cute. Either way, argue the diagnosis from what the two camps actually say rather than from a survey of the field, and tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, a test case that would seperate the positions, or an honest argument that there isn't one and I should stop expecting it. No reading list, I can find that myself. American English please.
