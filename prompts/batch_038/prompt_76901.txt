I am convening a graduate seminar on cosmological design arguments, and I intend to devote the first meeting not to the arguments but to the standard they must meet. Write me that standard: the desiderata for an adequate treatment of fine-tuning, where "treatment" covers both halves of the task, since an account must first say which physical claims qualify as fine-tuning data at all before it can say what, if anything, explains them. Fix the purpose as follows. I do not want the standard for a merely coherent cosmological picture; I want the standard for an account good enough to let one say, in front of physicists, whether the fine-tuning data constitute evidence for design. Requirements that bear only on other purposes — predicting new physics, completing a natural theology, satisfying a working cosmologist's taste in explanations — should be identified as such rather than passed off as central.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and classify it: is it a constraint any account must satisfy, or a demand pressed by one side of the debate that the other side has no reason to concede? Among whatever else you judge to belong, the list should address the following, and I would like to see your own judgment about whether each is genuine or a prejudice in disguise. First, invariance under reparameterization: the apparent narrowness of a life-permitting window can be an artifact of the variables or units in which it is stated, and an account must not let the size of the surprise depend on a coordinate choice. Second, the methodology of the sweep: a life-permitting region in a space of many constants need not be a product of narrow intervals along each axis, since a shift in one quantity may be offsettable by adjusting another, and single-parameter sweeps — hold everything else fixed, vary one — may therefore misdescribe its extent and its shape. Third, a requirement I will call pre-registration: an account should be able to say which regions of parameter space would have counted as surprising before the relevant physics came in, on pain of the data having been fitted to the conclusion afterward. Add whatever else you think more fundamental than these.

Then tell me which requirement is most selective, in the sense that failure to meet it most reliably disqualifies a candidate, and say whether any coherent-sounding subset of the list cannot be satisfied all at once; if the specification turns out to be unsatisfiable, I would far rather learn that in week one than in week nine. Close with no more than a few lines on how the designer reply, the multiverse reply, and the reply that denies any probability attaches to the constants fare against the list. Give me no bibliography and no names — I know the literature considerably better than my students do, and it is the requirements, not their provenance, that I am after.
