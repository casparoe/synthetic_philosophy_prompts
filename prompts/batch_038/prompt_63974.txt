I am preparing a written statement of my own position for a bioethics reading group on reproductive selection and enhancement. Each member has been asked to set out their commitments in advance so that the others can examine them, and I would like a rigorous consistency check on mine before I circulate the document. Here they are, ordered as they occur to me rather than by importance.

1. Procreative beneficence in something like its standard form: when prospective parents can choose among the children they might have, and possess information relevant to how those lives would go, they have a genuine moral reason — not a mere preference — to choose the child whose life can be expected to go best.

2. That reason is not enforceable. No state may compel a selection, forbid one, or attach penalties to particular choices; nor should the state fund enhancement.

3. I accept the social model of disability. Disability is constituted to a large degree by socially erected barriers and by other people's attitudes rather than by impairment as such, and being disabled does not in itself make a life go worse. The testimony of disabled people about their own quality of life counts as strong evidence on this question.

4. Prenatal screening followed by selective termination is permissible, and often the right course, where the diagnosed condition is what I would call serious — by which I mean one involving chronic pain or a drastically shortened life.

5. The non-identity objection does not trouble me. Where an alternative child would have had a better life, choosing the child with the worse life wrongs no one, since that child is deprived of nothing they would otherwise have had; and a child who is never conceived cannot be wronged at all.

6. I am a welfarist about what makes one expected life better than another: expected well-being, and nothing else. I reject perfectionist lists — achievement, knowledge, agency, species-typical functioning — as independent grounds for selection.

7. Enhancement beyond therapy is permissible, and justice requires that access to it be genuinely equal, so that enhancement does not entrench or amplify existing inequality.

And one case verdict, so that these abstractions have something to bite on: given a choice between an embryo predicted to have a mild cognitive impairment and one predicted not to, I think the parents have a real moral reason to choose the second, and I do not think that reason is exhausted by the social costs the family would otherwise bear.

I have no settled view about which of these I would abandon, so please do not guess at my priorities or reconstruct the set as coherent out of charity. What I want is an audit. Which of these genuinely pull against one another, and which only look as though they do? Rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. For each, say precisely what would have to be given up or narrowed, and what the least costly repair is. Then tell me which single revision would clear the greatest number of problems at once. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or for references — only for a tracing of what commits me to what.
