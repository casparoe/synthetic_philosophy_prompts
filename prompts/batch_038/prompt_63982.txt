(1) Ben: the usual calculation is fine. Call whatever is in the envelope you're holding X. The other one has either 2X or X/2, each with probability 1/2, so its expected value is 1.25X. Switch. And notice it doesn't depend on what X turns out to be, so it still works after you open the envelope and look, and it works again if you swap back and re-run it — which just shows this is a real paradox and not a puzzle with a tidy solution.

(2) Nadia: it's not a paradox, it's an equivocation. Your X is doing two different jobs, and the 1/2s are only 1/2 if you assume the organizer was equally likely to put any amount in there. There's no such assumption available: no probability distribution is spread evenly over all the possible amounts. So the 1/2s are made up out of nothing.

(3) Ben: I don't need a distribution. Do it before anybody opens anything. Two envelopes, one has twice the other, I get handed one at random. Whichever I'm holding, the other is either double or half, and there's no asymmetry between the two envelopes, so it's 1/2 each way and I gain. Symmetry alone gets me there.

(4) Nadia: symmetry alone gets you the opposite. Before you look the envelopes really are exchangeable, so the expected gain from switching is zero, not positive. You can't have it both ways: either the symmetry holds and there's no reason to switch, or it fails and then you owe me an actual prior over the amounts. And if you hand me any prior with a finite average amount, you can check the ex ante gain from switching comes out exactly 0, so your 1.25X has to be broken somewhere.

(5) Ben: fine, granted, always-switch-before-looking is dead. But the case I care about is after you look. Say the organizer tells you the smaller amount is a whole number of dollars from 1 to 100 and every one of those was equally likely, and you open your envelope and see 40. Then you're either holding the smaller one, and switching wins you 40, or you're holding the bigger one, and switching costs you 20. Equal odds, 2:1 payoff, so you switch. And you'd switch at every amount up to 100 for the same reason. So looking genuinely buys you something you didn't have before, there's a concrete policy that beats never-switching, and anybody who says "give a proper prior and the whole thing dissolves, there's never anything to gain by switching" is just wrong.

(6) Nadia: no. Your policy only looks better because you're comparing it to never-switching, which was never the live alternative — the value of the envelope you're holding is what it is either way. And your 40 example assumes each number 1 to 100 was equally likely, which is the same indifference move I just objected to, only with a ceiling painted on it.
