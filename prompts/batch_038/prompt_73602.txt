I'm running a reading group (mostly cog sci grad students, one or two philosphy people) and we're working through a pop book on moral psychology. Here's its paragraph on the chairman cases:

"Knobe asked people to imagine a chairman who is told that a new program will raise profits but will also [harm / help] the environment. He says he doesn't care about the environment at all, only about profits, and goes ahead anyway. Asked whether he harmed the environment intentionally, most people said yes; asked the same thing about helping it, most said no. The two stories are identical except for whether the side effect is good or bad, so what's really driving the judgment isn't the chairman's mental states at all — it's the reader's own moral evaluation. This shows intentionality is a moralized concept: we don't figure out what someone believed and wanted and then decide whether they acted intentionally, we decide whether the outcome was bad and then work backwards. The effect replicates in preschoolers and turns up in non-Western samples too, so it isn't a quirk of American undergrads. And there's a payoff for ethics: if ordinary people's intentionality judgments get warped by blame this much, the line between intending a harm and merely foreseeing it — the line the doctrine of double effect is built on — can't be doing any real work."
