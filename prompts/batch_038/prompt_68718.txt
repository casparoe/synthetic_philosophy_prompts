I chair the Standing Committee on Customs at a small residential college. Our remit is narrow: members propose that some long-standing practice of the College be discontinued or altered, and we determine whether it should be retained, retained with modification, or discontinued. Our charter gives us exactly one instruction — that no practice shall be discontinued merely because its purpose has not been identified — and is silent about grievances. In eleven years we have issued five determinations. I would like help working out what rule we have actually been applying, and what we ought to do with the sixth.

The record:

1. **The Founder's Charge**, read aloud at every installation of a new head for some two hundred and fifty years. A member proposed dropping it as an hour of dead air. Neither proposer nor defenders could state what the reading did; the defenders said only that it had always been read. No member reported harm from it. Its wording is quoted in the installation oath and appears on the College seal. **Retained.** Our minute records that our inability to say what the reading did was, in our view, the strongest reason not to stop doing it.

2. **The Stint**, a compulsory term of work on the estate, instituted forty-one years earlier by a resolution stating its purpose: to teach surveying and woodland management, then central to the College's mission. Nothing of the kind had been taught anywhere in the College for over a decade. The requirement occupied a fixed block of the autumn term, so a partial exemption would have meant redesigning the term. Three members filed written complaints in one year that it had delayed their degrees. No rite, phrase, or calendar entry anywhere in the College referred to it. **Discontinued.** Our minute records that we knew what the Stint had been for, were satisfied it was no longer doing it, and considered that sufficient.

3. **The Common Lunch**, two hours daily since the foundation. A proposal to cut it to forty minutes for timetabling reasons. No complaint from any member; the College's own survey showed satisfaction. Elections, welcomes, and the annual toast all happen at the long table. **Retained unchanged.** Our minute records that many practices which cannot be justified can still be described, and that this one could not be described either: we did not know what happened at the long table, and would not shorten it in order to find out.

4. **The Night Walk**, in which new members walked the College boundary after dark with a senior member, about ninety years old. A minority called it humiliating; one member's written account described genuine distress. Abolition was proposed. The boundary is walked again in the installation rite, and the phrase appears in the letter sent to every admitted member. **Retained but made optional**, with a daylight alternative offered and no record kept of who took it. Our minute records that we would not abolish a practice whose function we could not state, but that a grievance actually recorded had to be given something.

5. **The Office of Keeper of the Gate**, a ceremonial office one hundred and fifty years old whose duties had been transferred elsewhere by statute thirty years before. Its purpose was articulable and demonstrably spent; the title appeared in no other rite, oath, or calendar entry, and its insignia had been in a cupboard for two decades. The incumbent favoured abolition, and a junior member who was in fact performing the duties without title or pay filed a written complaint; the statute had extinguished the office's duties and left the title with no legal content, so recognising that junior member was not available to us. **Discontinued.** Our minute distinguishes it from the Founder's Charge on the ground that here the office stood for nothing and was attached to nothing.

Two members of the committee now disagree about the character of the whole enterprise: one says it is entirely a business of fences, the other that fences are precisely the wrong image for practices nobody ever built.

The proposal before us this term concerns **the Saturday Silence**. Instituted in 1897: all resident members assemble for twenty minutes of silence before the evening meal. Its founding resolution states its purpose plainly — to make visible that the College is one body, including members who are not friends and who are at odds — and the defenders have satisfied us, from attendance records and from testimony on both sides of last year's bitter dispute over admissions, that it still does exactly that. Two of those witnesses said it was the only occasion on which they had been in the same room on equal terms. Universal attendance is, on that evidence, load-bearing: the practice is said to work because nobody is absent. It is also attached to a good deal else — the installation oath refers to it, the College calendar is anchored by it, and the annual memorial for deceased members is held inside it.

There is, however, a recorded grievance. Eleven members, most recently admitted, filed a joint statement saying that compulsory silent assembly is experienced as a demand for visible conformity; two say their objection is religious; one describes a dated incident in which her absence during a period of bereavement was noted aloud. When we offered the Night Walk device — an opt-out, with no record of who took it — the eleven refused it, on the ground that an opt-out would mark them out and turn the injury into a public fact. The junior dean's consultation note accepts that reasoning and adds that, because the stated function depends on universal attendance, any relief that satisfied the eleven would degrade the practice for everyone else. The proposer asks for outright discontinuation and has explicitly declined to offer a substitute.

What I want from you:

(a) State the candidate rules we may have been applying — at least three, as genuine decision procedures, each taking as input the practice's age, whether its function is known, whether it is known to be performing that function, whether it is attached to other practices, and whether a grievance has been recorded, and each returning retain / retain with modification / discontinue. Show that each accounts for all five determinations, and flag the determination that strains it most.

(b) Say what each predicts for the Saturday Silence and where they part company. I expect at least two to diverge. If you think a rule can be refined so that all five precedents still fit while its prediction here changes, show me the refinement rather than asserting it.

(c) Give me your best prediction of what we will in fact decide, reasoning from the language of the minutes and not only from the outcomes — the outcomes are consistent with more than the reasoning is.

(d) Then tell me what we *should* decide if our aim is coherence with our own record. Identify the closest analog among the five and say precisely where the analogy gives out. Be candid about the principle we would be adopting either way: if we retain, what we are saying about a practice that functions and injures people who can point to the injury; if we discontinue, what we are saying about attachment and about the burden our charter places on proposers; if we modify, what we are saying when the modification is one the injured have told us will not help them.

(e) Finally, name the next case that would discriminate among the surviving rules — a concrete proposal of the sort this committee could actually receive, specified along the same dimensions, with what each rule would say about it. Construct it so the rules you want to separate genuinely separate; a case on which they all agree tells me nothing.

Please do not give me a history of the relevant ideas or a roster of authorities. I want the analysis of our record and of this proposal.
