You are an economist and philosopher who works on information aggregation in markets: you know the microstructure literature on adverse selection and the no-trade results, and you also know the epistemology of markets — the case for treating prices as knowledge-aggregating devices and the standard objections to that case. You have refereed a great many graduate seminar arguments. Please think carefully before answering, and where a disputant's move turns on the exact hypotheses of a theorem, state those hypotheses explicitly rather than assuming the disputant has them right.

I am the faculty adviser to my college's student-managed investment fund. At Tuesday's meeting two of our senior analysts, Elena and Marcus, argued for the better part of an hour about whether the fund should accept an offer, and I stayed out of it. Here is the situation, and their exchange as faithfully as I can render it.

Background: The founder of a small publicly traded medical-device company is stepping down as chief executive but will remain on the board for two more years. Through an intermediary she has offered to sell the fund a block of shares equal to roughly four percent of the company, at the prevailing market price, with no discount. Separately, the fund received a large unrestricted donation this year and is under pressure from its board to put the cash to work.

1. **Elena:** Decline. The no-trade reasoning says that if agents share a common prior and it is common knowledge that everyone is rational, speculative trade cannot happen, because a willingness to trade would leak whatever the trader knows. She knows more about this company than we ever will. That she will sell at the market price rather than at a discount means she expects the price to fall. Her willingness to be our counterparty *is* the bad news.

2. **Marcus:** The hypotheses are false here, and you know they're false. She has overwhelming non-informational reasons to sell: her career, her reputation, and most of her net worth are already tied to this single firm, so diversification alone justifies the sale. And the block is far too large to sell into that thin market without pushing the price down on herself; a negotiated sale at the quoted price is the cheapest exit available even to someone who is bullish. Once you admit non-informational motives, her offer carries no adverse-selection signal on its own.

3. **Elena:** On your method the theorem can never be applied at all, because whenever trade appears you can always nominate some liquidity story and declare the inference void. Its whole content is that you must settle in advance which motives you will treat as operative. And look at what she actually chose: she could have hedged, or sold in small pieces over a year. She selected the one structure that lets her hand a large block to an unsophisticated counterparty at an unchanged price. The choice of mechanism is itself informative.

4. **Marcus:** Then check it against what the market did. The sale was disclosed in a filing six weeks ago. The stock has not fallen; it has drifted up slightly. If informed selling were underway, the price would have absorbed it. Prices aggregate the judgments of participants far better informed than either of us, and our task is to take that aggregation as given rather than to second-guess it from a seminar table.

5. **Elena:** That inference is self-undermining. Suppose every investor reasoned as you just did — deferring to the price instead of investigating. Then nobody would be doing the work that makes prices informative in the first place, and the fact that the price didn't move would mean only that nobody looked. Generalize your rule and it destroys the very signal it tells us to trust.

6. **Marcus:** Generalizing it is the mistake. We are a four-million-dollar fund; whether we investigate does not move that price by a cent. Your objection concerns a whole economy of would-be analysts, and it is a fallacy of composition to apply it to a single price-taker's decision. It is also unstable in my favor, not yours: if enough people deferred, prices would degrade until investigating paid again, so the world you describe can't persist. Meanwhile, if we veto every trade with an insider-adjacent counterparty on principle, we aren't improving price discovery anywhere — we're only refusing to be paid.

Neither budged, and I could not have adjudicated it if I'd tried. Please do the following.

(a) First, briefly set out what the no-trade reasoning actually requires: which of its conditions are load-bearing, which the fund's situation plainly violates, and which are merely arguable here. Do this before you grade anything.

(b) Then take the six moves one at a time. For each, state in a single sentence the proposition it asserts and what it depends on, and rate it: *clearly mistaken*, *partly right but overstated*, *substantially right*, or *clearly right*. Justify each rating in a few sentences, and say explicitly wherever a move reaches a defensible conclusion by an indefensible route.

(c) Then give a bottom line on the decision itself: should the fund take the block at the offered price, decline it, or seek different terms? Your recommendation must follow from your adjudication rather than be stapled onto it.

(d) Then say what both of them missed — including any consideration already sitting in the background facts above that neither of them used, and any point on which they were talking past each other rather than disagreeing.

(e) Finally, name the one further fact about this particular offer that would most sharply discriminate between Elena's reading and Marcus's, and say what each reading predicts about it.

Do not supply references, reading lists, or citations; argue from the content of the positions themselves. Be willing to conclude that one of them is substantially right and the other substantially wrong — I am not looking for a diplomatic split — but do not let a rhetorically forceful move pass for a good one.
