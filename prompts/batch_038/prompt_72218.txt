I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I also sit on the exhibitions advisory panel of a science center that is building a permanent gallery on mind and matter. The centerpiece now before us is a working model of Douglas Hofstadter's careenium: a shallow hemispherical bowl roughly four feet across, filled with several hundred steel balls, each etched with a word or a picture. When a visitor speaks into a microphone, a few balls are knocked loose; they ricochet into others, which set still others rolling, and the bowl hums. The designers' draft label promises that the visitor will watch symbols push their own substrate — that one labeled ball makes other labeled balls move, and that this, rather than anything happening merely underneath it, is what a thought doing work looks like.

One panel member, a physicist, will argue the label asserts a falsehood: every ball moves only because another ball struck it, the mechanical story is already complete, and the gallery risks teaching schoolchildren that meanings shove matter around. Another argues that scrubbing the label teaches the opposite error, that the symbol level is an inert gloss with no purchase on the world at all. We vote Tuesday: approve the text as written, approve it with amendments, or return the piece to the designers.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate in advance, recommending one of those three and giving reasons the designers could actually reply to. I want you to say what the disagreement between my two colleagues really turns on, not merely that they disagree; to put the strongest form of the case against your own recommendation in its best light rather than noting that it exists; and, if you recommend amendment, to propose the replacement wording for the sentence in dispute. Write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter to a committee — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
