My friend and I argued for most of last night about the Anthropocene and neither of us budged, so what I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another tour of the candidate start dates. The facts we both accept are these: the working group spent some fifteen years assembling a case for a formal epoch beginning around 1950, marked by bomb-test radionuclides and anchored to the sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario; in 2024 the Quaternary subcommission voted that proposal down by a wide margin, the rejection was duly confirmed, and the geological bodies said in effect that the term would go on being used informally by scientists and the public alike; meanwhile 1610, 1780 and the Neolithic each retain defenders.

My hypothesis is that the impasse is not factual but concerns what a geological time unit is supposed to be — individuated by a single synchronous marker in rock, or by the character and trajectory of the interval as a whole, in which case the Anthropocene is an event rather than an epoch, and the 2024 vote decided a question about definitions that everyone mistook for a question about the Earth. Is that right? Test it against what each side actually argues, including the objection that a unit with only decades of sediment and an upper boundary still being deposited cannot be assessed for duration at all, so ratifying it now would be a wager on the future, and including the reply that the vote reflects nothing deeper than the conservatism of stratigraphic procedure. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis, and whether the genuinely unresolved question is the second half of the title — who decides: stratigraphers applying their own criteria, or Earth-system scientists and historians following the causal story. Please write in British English.
