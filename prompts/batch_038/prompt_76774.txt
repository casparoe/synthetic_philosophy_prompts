Suppose a colleague proposes a general framework for deciding, trait by trait, whether an organismal feature is an adaptation, a byproduct of something that is an adaptation, an exaptation, a retained vestige, or simply the residue of drift and ancestry. Before the proposal is evaluated at all, I want the standard it will be measured against. Write that standard.

The deliverable is a specification of desiderata: what any adequate framework of this kind must be able to do, with the case or consideration attached to each requirement, and with an explicit verdict on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the local commitments of one camp in the controversy. Organise it as you judge best, so long as the motivating case is never separated from the requirement it generates. I want the list to cover, at minimum, the following territory.

Representational range. The framework must be able to express, without forcing them into one another: finely tuned adaptive design; a feature arising as an obligatory consequence of something selected elsewhere in the organism (the whiteness of bone; a structure entailed by the geometry of another); a feature inherited unchanged because nothing in the lineage had occasion to alter it; a feature whose present use differs from the use that shaped it; and a feature present because sampling in a finite population, rather than selection, settled the matter.

Distinctions that must survive. Origin versus current utility, above all. Good design versus selected design. A necessary byproduct versus a merely contingent one. A genuine constraint versus an absence of data. Optimality versus mere adequacy. Having a function versus having a history of being selected for one.

Defaults and evidence. Say what the framework's null expectation should be, who carries the burden of proof, and what stops the framework from licensing accounts that cannot be wrong. Treat this difficulty directly: the demand that non-adaptation be the default may not be a requirement at all, but the adaptationist's own burden of proof restated by his opponents. Decide, and say why.

Consistency with results it cannot override. That relatives are not independent data points. That what selection can reach depends on standing variation, genetic architecture, and population size, so that identical selective pressures leave different traces in large and in small populations. That optimality reasoning earns its place as a way of testing whether selection dominated, not as a premise.

Two further demands. First, the central term of this controversy has been used strictly — a byproduct of definite and necessary form — and loosely — any non-selected feature that happens to be of use. Its strictness has been attacked, notably by Dennett, on the ground that necessity is necessity only relative to a space of alternatives, and that the alternatives (other ways of spanning the same gap) were available and preferred for reasons of their own. State which sense your desiderata are indexed to, and specify what must be held fixed for a claim of necessity to be evaluable at all. Second, identify any subset of your requirements that cannot be satisfied jointly — if the list is itself a no-go result, that is what I most want to see — and name the single requirement whose failure disqualifies a framework regardless of its other merits.

Close with a short assessment, a paragraph each and no more, of how strict adaptationism, a pluralism weighting constraint and drift heavily, and a programme that treats adaptationism as a research strategy rather than a thesis about nature fare against the specification. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list: argue from the concepts alone.
