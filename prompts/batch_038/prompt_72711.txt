I retired six years ago after thirty-eight years in analytical chemistry — method validation, inter-laboratory comparisons, and an unending stream of referee reports — and I have since been reading Indian philosophy with a small group that meets Thursday mornings at the public library. I come at these texts as a methodologist rather than a historian, so what keeps arresting me is not the doctrine but the procedure: the rules by which the work is supposed to be done. The tradition we are on at present is Nyāya, whose foundational text is a collection of aphorisms attributed to a figure known as Gautama or Akṣapāda. That text does something I find startling, which is to legislate philosophical argument the way a standards body legislates an assay. It recognises three sorts of discussion: one whose avowed aim is simply to settle what is the case; a second in which each party defends a thesis with victory as the declared object; and a third in which one party advances no thesis of its own and confines itself to pulling down the other's. It requires that a proponent's case be set out in five ordered steps — the thesis, the reason, an appeal to a familiar instance in which the connection between the reason and what is to be proved has actually been observed (a kitchen hearth, where smoke and fire are found together, or a lake, where neither is), the application of that instance to the matter in hand, and a restatement of the thesis. Asserting the general rule by itself is not sufficient; you must point to something anyone could go and look at. And it enumerates grounds on which a debater may be declared defeated — misdescribing an opponent's words in order to trap him, offering rejoinders that turn on a superficial resemblance rather than on the actual reason, falling silent, saying again what has already been said — with an assembly present to judge.

Here is my difficulty, and it is not a scholarly one. The same school's theory of knowledge does not look like a theory that would license that institution. Nyāya holds that there are four irreducible sources of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison, and the word of a reliable speaker — and treats a cognition as knowledge when it is true and when it was produced by one of those sources. What is decisive is therefore the causal pedigree of the belief, not anyone's ability to defend it before an audience. A true belief that arrived by a defective route is not knowledge on that account, however eloquently it is argued for; and, symmetrically, a person who is outmanoeuvred in debate may retain knowledge he simply could not protect. The institution thus appears to make dialectical performance the criterion at precisely the point where the theory says dialectical performance is beside the point.

I would like you to reconstruct the epistemology presupposed by the practice, as distinct from the doctrine, and to be as careful as you would be with an instrument whose calibration you did not design. What does the demand for a witnessed instance presuppose about the relation between inference and perception — that all warrant terminates in observation, that reasons must be publicly checkable, that a general claim unsupported by an instance is not yet a claim at all? How is that to be squared with the school's own inferences to things no one could ever observe (a self, atoms, an ordering cause of the world), and with its granting the word of a reliable speaker an equal and independent standing? What do the grounds of defeat presuppose — that an inability to answer is a mark of error, or merely that adversarial testing is the cheapest available proxy for truth? And what does the threefold division of discussions tell us: if trickery is licensed in the victory-seeking form yet counts as defeat in the truth-seeking one, does the tradition thereby concede that only one of the three is epistemic, and if so what are the other two — a sport, a trial of nerve, a training exercise for the first? Please separate sharply what the institution strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, since I suspect a reliabilist, a coherentist, a pragmatist about truth, and a social epistemologist could each sign off on the same five steps and the same list of defeats; tell me where the practice is underdetermined in that way and where some specific rule actually rules one of those views out. Finally, tell me what the institution would have to become — which of its rules dropped, which invented — if it were rebuilt on a straightforwardly probabilistic view of evidence, in which a reason need not be tied to any observed instance and defeat means a failure to revise one's confidence rather than a failure to reply. Please end with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states in one sentence the view of knowledge this practice most strictly presupposes, or says plainly that it presupposes none and is compatible with several.
