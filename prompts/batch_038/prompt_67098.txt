Here is the piece's summary, with its sentences numbered as they appeared:

1. Weil's case against parties is usually mistaken for a plea for better behaviour, but it is really a structural claim about incentives.
2. A party, she says, is a machine whose one true objective is its own growth without limit, so every question it touches becomes a recruiting question.
3. It therefore exerts continuous pressure on the minds of its members, until a member stops asking whether something is true or just and starts asking whether it is good for us.
4. From this she concludes that every party is totalitarian in embryo, whatever its programme, and that the condition is not curable by reform, better leadership, or cooperation between parties.
5. Her remedy is institutional rather than moral: abolish parties, let candidates stand as individuals, and fill the assembly by lot from ordinary citizens, so that nobody owes their position to an organisation or to a reputation.
6. She also thought this was easier than it sounds, and that the only real obstacle is the unexamined assumption that parties are natural.
7. Underneath all of it is a deeper objection to the idea of collective will: for Weil, politics is not about adding up what people want but about attending to what is just, and any organisation with a will of its own blocks that attention.
8. That is why she reads as a forerunner of today's anti-partisanship movement — her argument applies with even more force to a two-party system in which both sides treat every issue as a membership drive.

For what it is worth, I am already inclined to believe sentences 2, 4 and 8, and I suspect the piece is flattering that inclination rather than reporting Weil, but I cannot say where the seam is.
