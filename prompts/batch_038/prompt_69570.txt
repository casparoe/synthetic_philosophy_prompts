I've been reading a trade-press book on truth, and its chapter on deflationism opens with the following passage:

"Deflationism starts from a modest claim about the truth predicate: its job is expressive, letting us endorse claims we cannot spell out in full, as when we say that everything the witness testified to is true. On this view truth has no hidden nature to investigate — to call a sentence true is simply to assert it again, so 'Snow is white is true' says nothing over and above 'Snow is white', and in principle the word could be deleted from any sentence containing it. The doctrine is usually traced to Tarski's Convention T, which defines truth for a language by means of biconditionals of exactly that form. But Tarski's own theorem shows that no language can contain a truth predicate for itself, which means the deflationist cannot after all have a theory of truth for English as it is actually spoken. The liar sentence finishes the job: the biconditional for 'This sentence is not true' is contradictory, and since the biconditionals are the whole theory, restricting them to escape the paradox leaves nothing to be deflationist about. Which is why, in practice, deflationists end up saying that to call a claim true is only to record your own endorsement of it — a pragmatist or relativist account on which truth is whatever we find ourselves able to assert."
