Write a short story of between 1,500 and 2,500 words, and give it a title.

**The situation to dramatize.** Two people are wintering alone at a remote observation station — an avalanche hut, a tide-gauge house, a fire lookout, whichever suits you — sharing a single room with two bunks and one lamp. The lamp's switch is on the wall beside one resident's bunk; that resident keeps the station log, and the station's charter gives him sole authority over the lamp. The other resident carries a tin whistle, and the charter gives that one sole authority over whether the whistle is played after lights-out. The charter is explicit on two points, and both points are things the residents themselves asked for.

First: on each resident's designated matter, what that resident decides stands. The other may argue, complain, bargain, and beg, but may not overrule, and no vote between the two of them may overrule.

Second: the sponsoring office has appended a clause it considers unobjectionable — where both residents would rather have one arrangement than another, the arrangement both prefer is to be preferred; neither man is to be held in a state they agree is worse.

Before the pass closes, the inspector asks them to agree on and record a single standing arrangement for the season: lamp on or off, whistle played or not. Four arrangements are available. Here is how each resident ranks them, best to worst.

*The whistler:*
1. Lamp off, whistle silent. The hut dark and still; the rare night nothing is asked of him.
2. Lamp on, whistle playing. He will play only to a waking companion — the light is the sign that the other is up and, in effect, willing, so playing then is a gift given and received.
3. Lamp on, whistle silent. The light costs him his sleep, but at least he imposes on no one.
4. Lamp off, whistle playing. Music in the dark fills the hut and the valley with sound nobody consented to; he would consider it unforgivable and would be ashamed afterward.

*The log-keeper:*
1. Lamp off, whistle playing. He loves the music, but only in the dark, where he can lie still and give in to it unwatched, with no lamplight reminding him of the columns still to fill.
2. Lamp on, whistle silent. A proper working night: light enough to write, quiet enough to think.
3. Lamp off, whistle silent. A wasted night — too dark to work, too still to enjoy.
4. Lamp on, whistle playing. The light pins him to the log while the music makes the log impossible; the worst of both, and he must pretend otherwise.

Note what these rankings have in common: each resident's view of his *own* designated matter turns entirely on what the other is doing with his.

**Requirements.**

1. Each of the four arrangements must be inhabited for at least one night on the page, and the run of nights must form the loop that the charter's two clauses make unavoidable — every night's arrangement displaced by a complaint the charter protects, until a night arrives that displaces an arrangement back toward where the winter began. Derive the order from the rankings; do not narrate the derivation.
2. Exactly one of the displacements must come from the inspector's second clause rather than from anyone's authority over his own matter, and the story must make plain that this appeal is legitimate under the charter and still does not bring the loop to rest.
3. The winter must run out with the two of them still unable to write a standing arrangement into the log. No compromise discovered, no wise third party, no spring thaw settling the question.
4. Neither resident may be a fool or a saint, and the ending may not turn on a change of heart, a rescue, a death, or a confession.
5. Include at least one attempted fix — a resident offering to surrender his authority over his own matter, a proposal to settle it by vote, a rota, a schedule of alternating nights, a letter to the inspector — and show precisely what the fix costs or why it fails.
6. No character and no narration may name or allude to the theoretical result being enacted. Do not use the vocabulary of the literature (paradox, Pareto, social choice, rights-based, impossibility, equilibrium, cycle in any technical sense), do not mention any economist or philosopher, and do not let either resident say anything equivalent to observing that they are going around in a circle because of a theorem. A reader who knows the result should recognize it at once from the events alone; a reader who does not should simply find the winter oddly closed, like a staircase that returns to its own landing.
7. The reasoning must be carried by scenes — the complaints, the bargaining, the small cruelties and small concessions of two people with nowhere else to be. Keep the point of view fixed throughout, and let the setting do the atmosphere rather than the prose.
8. End with an entry in the log-keeper's own hand; the entry, or its last line, must be the last line of the story. It should read like something a careful clerk writes when he cannot mark a thing final.
