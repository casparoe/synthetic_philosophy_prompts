I was at a leaving-drinks thing last week and two people I know — call them M and T — got into a long argument about whether the world is getting less violent, and I mostly stood there holding a warm beer. It went a few rounds and neither of them backed down, and honestly I couldn't tell who was winning. Here's the exchange as best I remember it:

(1) T opened by saying the whole decline thesis is a denominator trick. In a foraging band of thirty people, one killing is more than three percent of the group; the identical event in a city of two million doesn't register at all. So the per-capita rates being compared across deep time aren't measuring the same quantity — they're an artifact of rounding tiny populations up.

(2) M said that's definately why you use rates instead of raw counts, since raw counts grow with population no matter what people are like. And the small-numbers worry is just sampling noise: pool enough sites across enough centuries and it washes out.

(3) T said pooling doesn't fix a biased sample. The bones come from excavated cemeteries, and a site where somebody found a mass grave full of bodies with embedded points gets excavated, written up and talked about, while a site with nothing dramatic in it doesn't. So the pool has been filtered for exactly the thing under measurement. That's publication bias, not noise.

(4) M granted a margin of error but said it can't touch a difference of a factor of ten, and pointed out that the European homicide decline doesn't rest on bones at all — it comes from court and coroner records in several countries independently, and they all slope the same way over centuries.

(5) T's reply was that homicide is a moving target. What got recorded as homicide in 1300 includes killings we'd now file as lawful self-defence, execution, or accident, and whole categories of victim — infants, slaves, wives — were counted differently or not at all. Worse, we haven't abolished violence so much as relocated it: into prisons and policing, and overseas into supply chains and remote warfare. If you define the numerator as private, domestic, person-on-person killing, of course it falls, because the state took over the job. That's reallocation, not reduction.

(6) M said reallocation only matters if the amount actually borne by victims stays constant, and it doesn't: state violence is procedural, targeted, and per head much smaller than feud, massacre and conquest. And the historically biggest killers — mass atrocity, conquest, deliberately induced famine — are near historic lows on both the absolute and the per-capita measure.

(7) T then said M was treating war deaths as though they came from something bell-shaped. They don't: the distribution is heavy-tailed, and for a heavy-tailed process the sample mean is dominated by events rarer than the observation window, so eighty years of great-power quiet is close to no information about the underlying rate. You cannot tell "the tail has been tamed" from "we haven't drawn a tail event yet." Reading a trend off that is like concluding a casino is safe from the fact that nobody's been hit by a meteor.

(8) M said that argument self-destructs: it would make every claim about rare-event rates unknowable, including T's own claim that state violence is smaller per head than feud. Heavy tails widen error bars, they don't blank out inference; and the mechanism story — deterrence, trade interdependence, a norm against territorial conquest — is evidence that isn't just extrapolation from the same sample.

(9) T's last word was that M had just conceded the real point. The interesting claim was never "the numbers went down," it's "something structural changed so they'll stay down," and numbers alone can't carry that.

(10) M's last word was that numbers plus mechanism is ordinary science, and that if a trend only counts once it's guaranteed permanent then no historical claim survives — including T's.
