I spent eight years as an epidemiologist working on diagnostic-test evaluation before I moved into journalism — screening programmes, cut-points on continuous risk measures, sensitivity and specificity measured against imperfect reference standards, latent class models for the situations where no gold standard assay exists at all. I now cover science and ideas, and I have been commissioned to write a long feature on a question that has recently stopped being a seminar-room hypothetical: which computations matter morally. The piece has to handle several live controversies at once — the claim that moral patienthood tracks some intrinsic quantity of information integration in a system rather than what the system is made of; the rival claim that what matters is a particular global-broadcast or higher-order architecture; the objection that only living tissue of a certain kind can be conscious at all; the invertebrate and welfare-policy debate, now that several jurisdictions have begun writing animal sentience into statute and into the requirements for policy review; and the neural-organoid question, where researchers are asking themselves whether the dishes in the incubator might deserve consideration. My editor's standing instruction is that I explain these disputes without turning the feature into a survey, and my own difficulty is that I cannot stop hearing all of it in the vocabulary I used to earn a living with. When a philosopher tells me we should attribute sentience on precautionary grounds because the cost of a false negative falls on a being that cannot appeal the decision, I hear a loss function with wildly asymmetric misclassification costs and a threshold being dragged far below the point of balance. I do not know whether that hearing is an insight or a tic.

So: I would like you to restate this debate, as faithfully as you can manage, in the vocabulary of diagnostic evaluation and screening policy — index tests and target conditions, reference standards and their absence, case definitions and how they are fixed by committee, prevalence and what it could mean here, sensitivity and specificity and the trade-off between them, spectrum and ascertainment effects, the conditional-independence assumptions latent class methods require, positive predictive value as a function of prior probability. Take the positions one at a time rather than in aggregate. For each, tell me what the target condition is supposed to be, what the index test actually is (behavioural markers? self-report? neural or computational correlates? architectural features of the system itself?), whether there is a reference standard even in principle — and if there is not, what that does to the entire apparatus, since almost everything I know about test evaluation is organised around the assumption that some better measurement exists behind the cheap one. Then tell me what happens to the precautionary argument when it is written as a threshold choice: who bears the cost of the false positive in this setting, given that the entity wrongly enrolled cannot be harmed by being enrolled, and whether that makes the precautionary move look like sound screening policy or like something with no analogue in screening policy.

Finally, the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Sort your translations into three piles: what carries over cleanly; what carries over only if I quietly accept a distortion, and what that distortion costs; and what cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. I suspect at least three candidates for the last pile and would like you to test them rather than take my word: the difference between whether something counts morally and how much it counts, since my framework issues binary case status and has no natural home for graded weight; the first-person character of the condition itself, which means the only test available for a system that talks is a test whose markers were acquired from the reports of the population we are comparing it to, so that assay and target are not independent in the way my methods require; and the possibility that sentience is not a latent trait waiting to be estimated but a cluster concept with no single underlying quantity, in which case the whole search for a reference standard is misdirected from the start. For each item in the third pile, give me a verdict: is the residue a defect in the epidemiological frame, a sign that the philosophical position is less determinate than its proponents sound, or a genuine third thing? I would also like you to watch yourself for smuggling — the moment you find yourself using my vocabulary's presuppositions (that there is a fact of the matter to be measured, that the measurement problem is separable from the ethical one) as though they were neutral translation choices, say so out loud.

Be as definite as you can afford to be, and mark the places where you are extrapolating past what the frame licenses rather than dissolving every claim into fog. And if you think this request rests on a confused premise — that sentience attribution is not a measurement problem in any sense worth the name, and that dressing it in the language of screening policy will produce something confident-looking and hollow — then tell me that in your first two sentences, plainly, instead of handing me a courteous translation with the objection buried at the end. Under nine hundred words, continuous prose, and no reference list or reading recommendations: I want sentences I can quote, not a bibliography I have to chase.
