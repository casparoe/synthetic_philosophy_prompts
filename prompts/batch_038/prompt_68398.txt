I'm a third-year in a philosophy-of-computation seminar and I've been assigned a paper on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis — not on the thesis itself, on what kind of claim it even is. My supervisor keeps saying the interesting question is what an account of its status would have to deliver, and I want that spec written out before I read another forty pages of commentary.

So: write the desiderata for an account of the epistemic status of CT. What does such an account have to be able to do to count as adequate — which phenomena it must explain, which distinctions it cant collapse, which results it has to be consistent with — with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Concretely it had better handle at least: why nobody has ever produced a counterexample, and whether that's inductive support or a symptom of the thesis being unfalsifiable; the difference between the mathematical thesis about effective procedures and the physical thesis about what the universe can do, and what each owes separately; the convergence of all the formalisms (Turing machines, lambda calculus, general recursion, Post-style and register machines) and whether that's independent evidence or one notion wearing five costumes; what an actual counterexample would have to look like and whether it can be described without begging the question, since anything describable enough for us to check is going to look like an algorithm itself; the results that derive an upper bound on what a mechanical process can do from assumptions like local causality, finitely many states and bounded precision, and whether those are proofs of CT or of something adjacent; the failures of the exotic machines (Zeno-style machines finishing infinitely many steps, real-valued machines storing exact reals, machines with an oracle) and whether those failures are conceptual or merely engineering; and the fact that CT does double duty in philosophy of mind, so that if it's analytic then "the mind is a Turing machine" goes trivial and stops saying anything.

Tell me which of these are non-negotiable and which are just hobby-horses of one camp, and flag any subset that cant be jointly satisfied — I have a suspicion that "must be falsifiable in principle" and "must be an analysis of a pre-theoretical concept" can't both be kept, and I want to know whether you agree. Then score, briefly, the three families I keep running into: CT as conceptual analysis of "effective", CT as empirical hypothesis about physical process, and CT as stipulative definition.

Purpose, so you know what "adequate" means here: I want an account good enough to tell me whether the thesis could ever be overturned by evidence rather than by re-definition. A table for the requirements is fine (requirement / what it has to handle / why that's a requirement). Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which single requirement excludes the most candidates, and whether the three families can jointly meet the list — then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back at me.
