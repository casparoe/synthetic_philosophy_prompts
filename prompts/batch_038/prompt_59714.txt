I am a first-year psychiatry resident preparing to present at our journal club, where the paper under discussion concerns the steep rise in US diagnoses of bipolar disorder in children and adolescents beginning in the mid-1990s, and the later introduction in DSM-5 of a new category for chronic, non-episodic irritability as a partial corrective to it. When I asked in supervision whether that rise should be read as a genuine change in the population or as an artifact of the classification system, the attending waved the question away with a line he uses often: these categories are self-fulfilling prophecies, and that is all "rising prevalence" has ever meant. I am fairly sure that one sentence is doing several incompatible jobs at once, and that at most one of them is defensible, but I cannot say which is which.

Could you produce a menu of the distinct, precise claims that "psychiatric diagnosis is a self-fulfilling prophecy" might be making? For each reading, state the mechanism it posits — who believes or applies what, to whom, and what thereby changes — and then give a verdict: platitude, substantive but contested, or false, with a sentence on why. I need the discriminations rather than a summary of the debate.

The distinctions I most need drawn are these:

1. Whether the prophecy is Merton's — a belief that is false when held and becomes true because it was held — or something structurally different, in which no belief need be false and nobody need expect anything. If the looping account of human kinds does not have Merton's shape at all, say so plainly and explain what it has instead.

2. Whether the loop runs through the patient's own self-conception, through the clinician's habits of noticing and recording, or through institutions: eligibility for services, school accommodations, reimbursement codes, forensic use. The third can move prevalence figures without altering anyone's psychology, and I suspect my attending has not separated it from the first. Tell me which of the three, if any, deserves the name "prophecy."

3. Whether a rise in recorded cases reflects a change in the world, a change in the instrument, or a change in the kind itself — and whether those three loci are distinguishable in practice or only in principle, given that we have no classification-independent access to the kind.

4. Whether a loop can make a classification false, in that it fits no one until it is applied, or instead can make it true, in that a diagnosis erroneous at the moment of application becomes correct as the person grows into it — and whether these two outcomes should share a label at all.

5. Whether loops can run in reverse, making a category progressively less applicable to the people who once received it, and what a self-defeating loop would do to the idea that the effect is one-directional and cumulative.

6. What would have to be the case, on each reading, for the pediatric bipolar rise to count as real — and whether "real" here means independent of the classifier or something else, such as having stable explanatory and predictive purchase on suffering, course, and response to treatment. Note also, if it is worth noting, whether the slogan undercuts itself when turned on itself, since the taxonomy of looping and non-looping kinds is itself a classification.

Close by telling me which of your readings would actually license the attending's dismissal, which reading I think he is in fact invoking when he says it, and what he would have to establish empirically — not rhetorically — to be entitled to it. Please organize the whole thing as a numbered list I can carry into the session. I do not want references, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I want the readings, the mechanisms, and the verdicts.
