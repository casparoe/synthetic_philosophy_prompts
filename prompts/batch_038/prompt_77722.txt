Last weekend I ended up in the usual 1am argument at my local alignment meetup — half the room has been reading LessWrong since the sequences, half of them found the paperclip maximizer on youtube last month — and this time it was about impact measures. One guy, call him Dev, insisted the whole "can you define side effects without a full specification of value" debate is overthought, and he reached for an analogy I hadn't heard used this way before: Leave No Trace. His pitch was that backpacking ethics already solved the problem. You don't need to know anything about what a given hiker wants in order to know they shouldn't trash the campsite; the rules are leave what you find and pack it in, pack it out, and they're mechanical and checkable — you can audit a site afterward without consulting anyone's utility function. So, he said, low-impact AI is just LNT for machines: define a baseline, measure the deviation from it, penalize it, done, and the entire objection that you need to know what we value in order to know what counts as a side effect just evaporates. He even sketched an operationalization on a napkin, something like penalizing the agent in proportion to how much worse a random sample of human goals became after its action, and claimed that's still LNT in spirit because the goals are only used as measuring sticks and never as the thing being optimized. I lost the argument in real time and my friend says I'm being pedantic, but I don't think I am.

So: audit the analogy for me, and please don't just give me a tour of the impact measures literature, I've read the same posts as everyone else and I know the vocabulary. What I want is the correspondence table first — what plays the hiker, what's the campsite, what counts as a trace, who's the ranger, what stands in for the trip's purpose, what's the equivalent of the rule about staying on durable surfaces, what's the AI's "pack it out" — and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually threaten the value-free claim versus the ones that are just cosmetic mismatch. A few places I already suspect are load-bearing and want pressure-tested: whether "as you found it" secretly requires a counterfactual rather than a snapshot, and if so what the baseline even means when the world drifts on its own (weather, other hikers, the bear that came through at 3am) while the agent is acting over months; whether the reversibility flavor of LNT — put the rock back where it was — is the same idea as the reachability-style penalties or a genuinely different one, since putting the rock back and preserving your ability to reach lots of futures are not obviously the same constraint; whether the rules were authored by humans who already agreed about what's worth preserving, so the analogy is smuggling in precisely the shared value function it's advertised as making unnecessary; and the case where leaving the site exactly as you found it is clearly wrong — there's a leaking drum of something nasty at the campsite, or a leg-hold trap set on the trail, and strict LNT says don't touch it. Also, Dev's claim that using a sample of goals as measuring sticks rather than as the objective keeps everything value-neutral feels like the shakiest step to me, and I'd like you to say whether it survives contact with the offsetting problem, where the agent does something cosmetically nice to cancel out the penalty.

Finally, tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it only partly survives, how much: is there a defensible weaker claim in the neighborhood, something like "you can pin down most of the impact constraint with a lot less value specification than a full utility function, and LNT is evidence that such constraints are usable in practice by agents who disagree about everything else"? And if the analogy has to go, what would be a better one for what these penalties are actually doing — ideally one that isn't quietly doing the work of a hidden value function itself, which is the sin I think LNT commits. Assume I already know relative reachability, attainable utility preservation, and the toy gridworld where there's a vase in the room and the agent can either walk around it or smash through it to save two moves, so don't re-derive any of that from scratch; do tell me which of those the LNT framing picks out correctly and which it misses entirely, and where the mismatch has practical consequences rather than just being philosophy.
