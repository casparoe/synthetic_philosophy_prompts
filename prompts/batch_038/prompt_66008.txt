I'm on a seven-person advisory panel in a small river town — call it Kettleford, it's basically a composite of a couple of real places so don't go looking for it — and we vote in six weeks on something I don't think we're ready for.

Here's the situation. The main crossing is the Aldous Vance Memorial Bridge, named in 1901 for a mayor who got the free public library and the waterworks built. Last spring a county archivist turned up a letter showing that Vance personally drafted an 1884 ordinance that drove the town's riverside Traveller families off the commons and had their boats burned, and argued for it in explicitly racial terms in his own hand. Until then the town's standing line had been that he only signed what the council sent up to him. Descendants of those families still live in the area and two of them have addressed the council. Nobody on the panel wants to leave things exactly as they are, and nobody wants to act like the library and the waterworks didn't happen.

The options actually on the table, more or less in the order people brought them up:

1. Keep the name, do nothing at all.
2. Keep the name, put a plaque on the bridge that gives the full record including the ordinance.
3. Rename it after the families collectively — something like Riverside Families Bridge, no individual attached.
4. Rename it after a new individual, picked from public nominations; the current front-runner is a nurse who ran the flu ward in 1918.
5. De-commemorate entirely: revert to a purely functional designation (Kettleford River Bridge No. 2) and spend the money on the library's local history program instead.
6. Keep the name on the bridge but have the council formally reclassify it in the register — declare that as of a certain date it's a historical label and not an honor, with a recorded statement of disavowal. No physical change, no cost.
7. Do 4 and also leave a small stone on the riverbank path naming Vance and the ordinance, so his name survives somewhere as a marker of what happened rather than as a tribute.

I want numbers, not vibes. For each of the seven, give me two scores from 0 to 10: expressive honesty — how well it makes what the town says match what the town actually believes right now — and durability — the chance it's still in place and unchallenged in thirty years. Then put all seven into tiers overall, one sentence each on why it lands where it does.

Three arguments keep coming up at our meetings and I'd like you to engage them head-on rather than route around them. First, that a street or bridge name is a low-attention honorific — almost nobody driving over it thinks about who Vance was — so both the harm of keeping it and the value of changing it are tiny, except that for the families it's the single highest-salience object in town, which seems to break the argument. Second, that the honor was already consumed by the audiences who received it in 1901, so removal is purely prospective and can't actually retract anything. Third, that maintaining a name is an ongoing act performed every year by the current council, not a passive inheritance, which would make option 1 not a default but a decision.

Tell me which of those three is doing the most work in your ranking, and if you think one of them is simply bad, say which and why.

Separately: should the letter change the ranking? If commemoration expresses the values of the people doing the commemorating, then how culpable Vance personally was looks beside the point — the town is endorsing or not endorsing a name today either way. But the archival discovery clearly moved public opinion here. Say whether it should shift any of your scores, and by roughly how many points, and explain what you think it's actually evidence of if not of his guilt.

Last thing, one number: if the council can only fund one intervention, what fraction of the commemorative effort should go into changing the name on the bridge rather than into changing what gets said and taught around it? Give me a figure like 0.3 or 0.8 and your reasoning, even if you think the question is a bit of a trap.
