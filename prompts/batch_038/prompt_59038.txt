I'm putting together handouts for a graduate pro-seminar on metaethics next term, and one of my TAs — who is much better at the formal stuff than I am — dashed this on the board after class last week and announced that it was basically a proof that the is-ought literature has never had a coherent subject matter. I don't buy it. But I also couldn't find the bad step standing there in front of him, and I want to give it to the students as a puzzle, which means I need to know the answer myself first. Here it is, tidied up from the board:

(1) In a valid argument the conclusion can contain no content that wasn't already in the premises — that's just what validity amounts to.
(2) 'Ought' is not a logical constant, so no rule of logic can introduce it; it has to come out of the premises themselves.
(3) Therefore no set of purely descriptive premises entails a conclusion containing 'ought' — call this Hume's Law.
(4) But consider: "the tide comes in twice a day at Mont-Saint-Michel" is purely descriptive, and it entails "either the tide comes in twice a day at Mont-Saint-Michel or everyone who eats shellfish ought to donate a kidney" — by disjunction introduction, which nobody disputes. That conclusion contains 'ought', and it is not equivalent to anything purely descriptive.
(5) So (3) is false.
(6) The only way to rescue (3) is to insist the disjunction in (4) isn't really a normative conclusion. But then whether a conclusion is normative is not settled by its vocabulary or its logical form — and vocabulary and form are the only things (1) and (2) ever appealed to. So Hume's Law has no precise statement as a thesis about logic.
(7) If it isn't a thesis about logic, the only candidate left is an epistemic one: purely descriptive evidence could never rationally support a normative belief.
(8) But if that's right, a person's normative beliefs could be totally unresponsive to everything they ever learn about the world with no failure of rationality, and there'd be no such thing as moral evidence — nobody's findings about suffering, cooperation, incentives or human nature could count for or against any moral view.
(9) That's clearly wrong; it makes a mystery of the obvious fact that learning about the world does move people's moral minds, and it collapses the difference between moral disagreement and a difference in taste.
(10) So (7) is false — and since (7) was the last refuge for (3) once (5) went through, Hume's Law is either false or meaningless, and decades of argument about it have been argument about nothing.

I'd much rather keep (3) in some recognisable form than swallow (10), and I don't want to walk into week one telling a room of first-years that the whole debate was empty. But I won't pretend I know where the crack is, so tell me rather than reassuring me. Which step do you reject, and why did it look fine — what's the reading on which it's true and the reading on which the inference goes through? Then price the alternatives: for each of (1), (3), (6) and (7), say what I'd have to give up if I rejected that one instead, and whether the cost is a technical patch, a substantive concession, or a change of subject. I'm particularly interested in whether there's a principled way to state Hume's Law as a formal claim that survives (4) without the restriction looking gerrymandered to dodge the counterexample, or whether the honest answer is that the interesting thesis was never formal to begin with — and if it wasn't, say what it was instead, precisely enough that I could put it on a slide. Please don't send me off to the literature or list citations and authors; my students will have the handout and nothing else, so everything needs to be worked out from the argument itself. One small thing: a couple of your steps in my numbering above may be sloppy — if you think a premise as stated isn't doing what I take it to be doing, say so and restate it properly before you argue about it.

Finish with a line beginning "Verdict:" naming the single step you'd reject and, in one clause, what rejecting it costs.
