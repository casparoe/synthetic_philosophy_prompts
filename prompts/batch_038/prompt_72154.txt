I'm a physics undergrad who's been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group this semester, and after a few weeks on thought experiments I've basically talked myself into thinking the whole business is epistemically empty — vivid restatements of arguments with the scenery left on. My reasoning is roughly this. Take the classic one where you imagine tying a light cannonball to a heavy one and reasoning about how fast the pair falls: on Aristotle's rule the light one should lag and retard the heavy one, but the pair is heavier than the heavy one alone so it should fall faster, and you get a contradiction, so speed can't be proportional to weight. Fine. But you can write that out as a chain of steps from assumptions everyone already granted before they imagined anything. And if the conclusion is already sitting inside premises you accept, then the conclusion isn't news — it's a restatement with the scenery left on. The imagining just makes you notice something you'd implicitly committed to all along, the same way that knowing a number's digits already settles whether it's divisible by three without teaching you anything about numbers that wasn't already true. So my working view is that calling a thought experiment a source of knowledge about the world is a category mistake: it's a mnemonic and a rhetorical device, nothing more.

Here's the thing that won't let me rest easy with that view, though. If the conclusion really was already in the premises, how come nobody before Galileo believed it? Tying rocks together is not a novel technology, and plenty of very sharp people had thought about falling bodies for two thousand years without drawing the obvious inference. Either that's just a fact about how dumb people were, or the act of running the scenario in your head is doing some genuine work that the explicit argument somehow doesn't do — and if it's doing work, then maybe it's contributing something, not just decorating. I can't tell which. There's also something that bugs me about my own reasoning: I seem to be assuming that a valid argument can never give you knowledge of its conclusion, and if that were right then no deductive reasoning in mathematics or logic would ever tell anybody anything new, which I'm pretty sure is false. So am I onto a real point about thought experiments, or am I just sliding between two different senses of "new" and calling it an argument? I'd rather get that sorted out than be reassured.

Separately, and this is where I'm least confident: the group kept saying Galileo settled the falling-bodies question from his armchair, without dropping a single thing. That can't be literally right, can it? It seems to me like the two-stones scenario at most shows that one particular Aristotelian claim is incoherent, and that's a long way from establishing that all bodies fall at the same rate, let alone anything about how fast. And in air a feather and a hammer obviously don't behave alike, so somewhere in there a quiet idealization is getting smuggled in, along with assumptions nobody flags — like whatever you're supposed to say about the speed of a tied-together pair relative to its parts. So I guess what I want is for you to explain, as carefully as you can, where exactly the imagining stops and the background assumption starts in a case like this, how much of the heavy lifting is really being done by premises that aren't visible in the story, and whether that vindicates my "it's just window dressing" line or undercuts it.
