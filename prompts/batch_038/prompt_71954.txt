I have no academic training in philosophy or statistics, but for about four years I have kept a spreadsheet in which I record degrees of belief about bird identifications from my garden, mostly at dusk, when the light is poor. I also settle small bets with a friend on the basis of those numbers. What began as a hobby has pushed me into a problem I cannot solve on my own, and I would like a classification instrument that shows me where my own thinking actually sits.

The problem: in bad light I never learn outright that the bird was a common redstart. What happens is that my confidence shifts to something like sixty–forty between redstart and black redstart, and I then want to know how the rest of my probabilities ought to move. I have worked out a recipe that leaves my conditional probabilities untouched — the probability that it nested in the hedge given that it was a redstart, and so on — and redistributes everything else in proportion to the new sixty–forty split. It seems to work. Three things trouble it. First, two poor-light sightings in a row do not appear to give me the same final numbers in the two possible orders. Second, over one season I got noticeably better at separating those two species, which means my conditional probability of redstart given that the bird looked like that changed; my recipe seems to assume such conditionals are never disturbed by the experience itself. Third, sometimes I want to take an update back and return to where I was, and I cannot tell whether that is legitimate bookkeeping or a way of cheating myself.

Please write a decision tree of the positions on how to revise degrees of belief when the evidence itself is uncertain. My requirements:

1. Take the root to be a commitment I already hold: that belief comes in degrees, and that when I do learn something outright I should conditionalize on it. Everything below that is in play.
2. Build the tree from the space of possible answers rather than from a list of views: first state the branching questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate actual positions within that space.
3. Each branch node must be a question I could answer about the garden case without knowing any technical vocabulary and without being able to guess which branch it leads down.
4. Ten to fourteen leaves. Each leaf gets the name of the position if it has one, or the label unnamed if nobody defends it, one sentence on what holding it commits me to, and one sentence on its most awkward consequence.
5. At least two branch nodes must turn on my three troubles above — the order in which successive vague observations arrive, the failure of the assumption that conditionals stay fixed when I become a better discriminator, and the admissibility of reverting — rather than on the uninteresting question of whether evidence can be less than certain.
6. One leaf must be the view that a single number per proposition is simply the wrong format for evidence of this kind; the tree should route me toward or away from it by a question about the case, not by a question about my taste in mathematics.
7. Mark every combination the questions permit that no position occupies, and say in one sentence whether the gap exists because the view is unstable, because it is merely unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored.
8. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate.

I cannot look anything up, so please give me no references, no bibliography, and no reading list, and do not invent any; attach a person's name to a position only where you are confident it belongs there. Keep the prose readable for someone who has never met a measure-theoretic argument, though formulas are welcome wherever they earn their place.
