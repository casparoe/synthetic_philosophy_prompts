I'm giving a talk in a couple of weeks at a history of economic thought workshop and what I want is a proper Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view. The thesis is mostly negative: the now-standard line that Berkeley's Querist anticipates chartalism -- money as a creature of the state, with its value riding on the sovereign's power to demand it -- gets him wrong in an interesting way. On my reading money for Berkeley is a counter or a ticket whose authority comes from its role in coordianting and quickening labor inside a domestic economy; the bank and paper-credit schemes are about circulation and employment, not about state money as such.

Audience: mostly historians of economic thought who came to Berkeley through Knapp and the chartalist revival; one early modernist who works on Locke and the recoinage debate and will insist I'm just restating Locke with an Irish accent; and two or three monetary economists who'll ask what's actually left of a quantity-theory reading once I strip out the state.

So: give me maybe eight objections this crowd would genuinely raise, in their own idiom, ordered by how much damage they'd do. For each, a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is thinnest, plus which ones I should just concede up front. Flag the places where the real answer is that Berkeley is vague or flatly inconsistent rather than that my reading wins. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. American English please, and skip citations and query numbers -- I'll have the text on the podium.
