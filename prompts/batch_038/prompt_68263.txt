ok so I TA a small reading group on the paradoxes of infinity and our first session is on supertasks. Problem is half the room walks in already convinced that obviously you can't do infinitely many things and the other half walks in convinced that the convergent series settles everything and wonders why we're meeting — and nobody in either half can tell me which question they're actually disagreeing about. So I want to build a diagnostic, a "which supertask person are you really" thing, that people fill out before the session. Help me design it.

What I want:

- Ten to twelve questions, each one a concrete case, each with two to four answer options. Nobody should be able to answer by recognising a label or by knowing which camp a question belongs to, so here's the constraint I care about: no option may contain the words *impossible*, *indeterminate*, *ill-posed*, or *actual infinity*. Those are the things I'm trying to measure, so they have to fall out of the answer pattern, not out of the wording. Same for the questions — don't ask "do you think the task can be completed?"

- The cases. I want a specific menu, because the ones people have a rehearsed answer to are no use to me. Please use: (a) the ordinary on/off lamp toggled at 1/2, 3/4, 7/8 ... with the question asked specifically about the instant t=1 and not about the intervals before it; (b) the same lamp but with a dimmer instead of a switch, so brightness goes 0, 1, 0, 1, ... and one of the options is something like "half brightness, obviously, that's the average of what it was doing"; (c) a machine that copies a word, shuttles one letter from tray to tray, doubles its speed each time, and at the end the letter isn't in any particular place; (d) an infinite stack of little balls that collide so the whole cascade finishes in a finite time — and then the exact same thing run backwards, where balls appear out of nowhere; (e) infinitely many would-be killers, the n-th one scheduled to strike a bit before noon if you're still breathing, so that you can't possibly survive and yet no killer ever acts; (f) someone falling toward a black hole who watches a computer far away grind through an unbounded number of steps in what feels to them like a finite amount of time; (g) Zeno's runner, but where the question is not "does he arrive" — it's "what does the sum of that series have to do with it"; (h) the same lamp, except the toggling is done by a person instead of a machine and the person is asked afterwards what the last thing they did was.

- Then the leaves. Roughly eight to ten of them, each with a short name you make up (two to four words). No philosopher's name attached to any leaf — I don't want a genealogy, I want positions described by what they say. For each leaf: the answer pattern that lands you there, the one most awkward thing that position is committed to, and — this is the part I care about most — whether you're confident this is a stance people genuinely hold or one you built to fill out the grid symmetrically.

- The branching should key on the questions that actually cut the space up. From reading my room, there are at least three axes people conflate: whether the description of the task picks out a unique final state at all; whether the obstacle, if there is one, is physical, metaphysical, or conceptual/linguistic; and what happens when you run the task in reverse. Build on those, but tell me if you think I've got the axes wrong or that there's a fourth one that matters more.

- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, and say what question you'd have to invent to separate them. Don't paper over it.

- Note the unoccupied cells: combinations of answers that look coherent but where you think nobody actually stands, with a guess at why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Format: the whole thing as one JSON object with this schema, and then a couple sentences of prose after it if you need to argue with me.

```
{
  "axes": [{"name": "", "what_it_measures": "", "how_it_gets_conflated_with_the_others": ""}],
  "questions": [{"id": "q1", "case": "the scenario, in plain language", "stem": "what the taker actually reads",
                 "options": [{"key": "a", "text": "", "what_this_answer_reveals": ""}]}],
  "leaves": [{"label": "", "answer_pattern": {"q1": "a", "q2": "c"}, "position": "",
              "most_awkward_commitment": "", "is_this_a_real_stance": "high/medium/low plus one sentence why",
              "occupied": ""}],
  "inseparable_pairs": [{"leaves": ["", ""], "why": "", "question_that_would_separate_them": ""}],
  "empty_cells": [{"combination": "", "diagnosis": "incoherent | unattractive | unexplored", "note": ""}],
  "settled_vs_contested": {"settled": [""], "genuinely_contested": [""],
                           "where_this_tree_sneaks_in_a_side": [""]}
}
```

About that last field: please keep straight for me which parts of this debate are settled among people who work on it and which parts are wide open, and don't present a contested answer as the consensus. Specifically, I don't want a tree that quietly treats one verdict about the lamp as the default and sorts everybody else into deviant leaves. If some leaf is close to the mainstream, say how close; if two leaves that look opposed actually agree on a sub-question, say that too. And the "where_this_tree_sneaks_in_a_side" field is a real request, not a formality — every diagnostic like this smuggles something in somewhere, so tell me what yours does.
