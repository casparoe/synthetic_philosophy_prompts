I am a professor of philosophy and, for the past four years, the editor of an interdisciplinary journal covering ethics, philosophy of science, and social epistemology. We receive roughly four hundred submissions a year; between me and two graduate student triage editors we handle fourteen or so new manuscripts a week, and our desk-rejection rate has drifted to something near seventy percent, which I regard as both too high and too inconsistently applied. A striking proportion of those rejections — and of the arguments my triage editors and I have about the borderline cases — turn on one recurrent family of moves: manuscripts that insist their claim is true rather than merely well supported, useful, or widely accepted; manuscripts that accuse their opponents of collapsing truth into consensus; manuscripts that announce in the abstract that some distinction is not verbal but a difference in what is the case. I have come to think most of these moves are confused, and that the confusion is diagnosable in advance by anyone who has properly absorbed the deflationary line — that the truth predicate is transparent, that its entire raison d'être is to let us generalize over, endorse, and reject claims we cannot or need not state one by one, and that once that job is described there is nothing left for a substantive property to do. My triage editors have not absorbed this, will not, and should not have to. Worse, our editorial policy forbids desk-rejecting a manuscript simply because it adopts a metaphysics of truth the editor finds unpromising, so the procedure I want may not smuggle in the correctness of deflationism. Its tests must be ones that an author who holds a robustly substantive view of truth would themselves accept as identifying a place where they owe an explanation.

Please write that procedure as something we can actually run on a submission in twenty minutes and keep to a single sheet. For each test, give it a name, the question it puts to the manuscript, what counts as a sufficient answer to that question, and the disposition that follows (desk reject; reject with an invitation to resubmit; send to referees; send to referees with a specific question flagged for them). Order the tests so that the cheap and near-decisive ones come first, and say where the procedure stops and hands the manuscript to me rather than to a triage editor, on the ground that the remaining question is metaphysical rather than editorial. Mark plainly which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of mechanical steps. Then tell me which distinctions from the deflationist's toolkit survive this compression and which have to be dropped, why dropping them is tolerable at triage though not at review, and what the procedure systematically cannot do — including how it behaves on manuscripts about the Liar, on alethic pluralism, on claims about what is true but unknowable, and on attributive or non-declarative uses of the word (true to the data, a true friend of the movement), and in which direction it will err in each case.

Apply it to four sentences now sitting in the queue:

1. From a submission on replication in psychology: We do not claim only that the original result failed to replicate, nor that it is unhelpful for theory construction; we claim something stronger, namely that the original finding was not true.
2. From a submission in social epistemology: Because the two camps accept the same data, the same model, and the same background history, they cannot really be disagreeing about any of these; their disagreement is about truth, and about truth alone.
3. From a submission in the philosophy of mathematics: The system's own resources cannot say of its Gödel sentence what we are able to say of it from outside — that it is true.
4. From a submission on scientific realism: There are features of the world that make our best theories true, and no formulation of any theory, however complete, could state what those features are.

One of these should come out badly for me, and I would rather hear it than have it explained away: our standing scope statement reads that this journal is committed to publishing work that aims at truth rather than at consensus. If your procedure implies that this sentence is either a redundancy dressed as a principle or a piece of unearned metaphysics, say so directly and tell me what I should replace it with. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; what I need is the procedure itself, written so that a graduate student with one semester of logic and none of this background could apply it this afternoon, and so that I can defend each of its steps to an author who complains that we rejected them for their metaphysics.
