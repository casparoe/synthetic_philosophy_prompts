Six months ago a private equity buyer closed on the consumer auto-finance group where I work — roughly 900 employees, paper originated through some forty dealerships across three states — and I came across with the deal as deputy general counsel, having spent eleven years at the company, most of them in collections. Post-closing diligence confirmed something that had been widely assumed and never documented: for most of the 2010s, a substantial share of our sales staff "packed" payment, meaning they slipped add-on products into installment contracts — extended service contracts, GPS trackers, prepaid maintenance plans — without itemizing them, and then quoted the customer the monthly figure the customer had been led to expect. Some of this was individual improvisation. Some was trained from above; at least two district managers ran it as an explicit target, and one of those two is now our VP of operations. The practice stopped in 2021, when the previous owners rewrote the commission plan. Nothing is being prosecuted, the cars were delivered and they run, and on most of the affected contracts the limitation period has expired.

The new owners want a complete accounting of how far the practice went, both because they carry residual exposure and because they would like to be able to say credibly that they cleaned the place up. Their proposed mechanism, which I have been asked to design and which I am uneasy about, is a sixty-day window: any current employee who gives the panel a full, on-the-record account of what he did and what he was instructed to do receives a written guarantee that the company will not terminate, demote, or pursue civil recovery against him on account of anything disclosed inside the window. Accounts go to a two-person review panel — me and an outside consultant — are summarized to the board, and are otherwise confidential: no report, no names, no publication, and no disclosure to third parties except where we are legally compelled. Two complications sit underneath this. The customers who were overcharged cannot be identified, and so cannot be refunded, without the operational records, and those records live in a legacy servicing platform that exactly two people understand — both of whom are implicated and both of whom are needed for a ninety-day system migration. Separately, a former sales associate who was terminated in 2018 for what her file calls performance issues has been in touch with counsel; she says she was fired for refusing to pack payment and for complaining about it, and she wants the exercise to produce something public that clears her name.

I would like help thinking this through, and I want to be blunt about the parts that bother me most. The guarantee covers only what the company controls: it cannot bind a state attorney general, and it cannot extinguish anything owed to the customers who were cheated, most of whom still do not know they were. So a meaningful part of what we are selling, we do not own. Is it dishonest to sell it anyway, or is a partial clean slate a legitimate thing to trade for information, provided we are clear about its limits? Second, does the confidentiality defeat the point? If the accounting exists to establish what happened, what is it doing for anyone when nobody outside the boardroom ever reads it — and is there a difference between what it does for the company, what it does for the customers, and what it does for the record itself? Third, the two people whose knowledge we need: does our dependence on them change what they are owed, or only what we can get out of them, and is it worse to purchase immunity from the people we cannot replace than from the ones we could dismiss tomorrow? And fourth, the associate who refused — does she hold a claim on this process that the packers do not, and can we honor it without giving up the confidentiality the guarantee depends on? One last thing I should put on the table: I sat in the regional meetings where those numbers were read out and I did not ask what was in them, which makes me wonder whether anyone inside this company is well-placed to grant a clean slate at all. I am not asking for legal advice, and I am not asking whether the practice was illegal; outside counsel owns both of those questions. What I want is an assessment of whether this bargain is fair, to whom, and what it costs if it is not.
