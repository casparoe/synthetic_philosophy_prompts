Thirty-five years running mass spec in an environmental lab, retired two years back, and now I read philosophy for fun, which turns out to be harder work than the lab ever was. Currently wedged on the Sleeping Beauty coin problem. I'll assume you know the setup and won't type it out: fair coin, heads means one awakening, tails means two, memory drug, she's asked each time for her credence that the coin came up heads.

Here's what got me going. I built a spreadsheet, 10,000 simulated runs, to check the betting frequencies rather than argue about them like a philosopher. Result: if Beauty is offered one bet per *awakening* at even odds on heads, holding credence at 1/2 bleeds her, and 1/3 comes out ahead. But if the payout is counted once per *run* no matter how many times she's asked, the fair price looks like the halfer's 1/2. So the whole 1/3 seems to come from the fact that tails gets to vote twice. Either that's a genuine diagnosis of what the fight is about, or I've walked into a confusion that got sorted out fifteen years ago and everyone knows about except me.

What I want is your judgement, not a survey. Rate these six positions 0-10 for how likely each is to be the correct view of the case, then put them in order:

1. The halfer line (Lewis) — being woken teaches her nothing relevant, credence stays 1/2.
2. The thirder line (Elga) — 1/3, via the indifference/long-run reasoning.
3. The double halfer — 1/2 both before she goes under *and* after she wakes up, which means giving up some ordinary rule about conditionalizing on what you learn. Someone defends this; I've lost the name.
4. The deflationist line — there's no single proposition she's being asked about, so the two camps are answering different questions and both can be right.
5. The self-indication style argument, where you're supposed to already favor hypotheses that contain more people like you in them, and which I gather is tangled up with the Doomsday argument.
6. The "stop asking about credences and just write down a decision rule" line.

Score them against criteria I made up on the bus, because the ones I see argued about online all seem to beg the question:

- Does the view survive scaling up to a case where counting is unambiguous — say a thousand runs of the experiment going at once, and we ask about the share of all awakenings that fall in heads runs? If it can't handle large N, it's out.
- Does it give the same answer regardless of how the protocol is *described* to her? I don't want rational credence turning on the wording of the lab notebook.
- Does its update rule generalize to other self-locating situations, or is it a patch cut to fit this puzzle only?
- Does it tell me which single step the two camps are actually disputing? A view that diagnoses the disagreement is worth more to me than one that just plants a flag.
- Bonus points if it explains why my spreadsheet gives two different answers under the two counting conventions without implying I'm an idiot.

Then, separately: give me a number out of 10 for how much weight my counting worry deserves as an objection to the thirder, and tell me which of the six you'd personally bet on and how much of the field you'd say is overrated. Don't bother with references or a reading list — my library card works fine and I can hunt things down. I want the reasoning.
