So — I write novels. Four of them, all set in mid-sized cities, none of them about religion, which I think is exactly why I got tapped for this. Two years ago three people who work in the county assessor's office asked HR where they could pray at midday and were told, essentially, the stairwell landing above the parking garage. They made a fuss, a foundation with a hardware-store fortune behind it offered to pay for a build-out, and the county turned a former records room on the second floor of the administration building into an interfaith room. I was appointed to the advisory committee as the "non-affiliated" member. Meaning: they needed a neutral, and the neutral turned out to be a person, and the person is me.

The room is done. Dedication is Thursday at four, twenty-five minutes of program, and I'm the middle speaker — five minutes, between the county executive and a kid from the high school who started the petition. The room will have: a shoe rack, a windowless light well, symbols that hang on a rail and come down between bookings, and an online calendar in forty-five-minute blocks. No open flame, which the committee fought about for four months. A mindfulness group gets Tuesday nights, which two clergy objected to and then withdrew the objection, and nobody ever said out loud what they'd withdrawn it in exchange for. The foundation paid for the build-out; the county pays for cleaning, utilities, and the calendar software. Also, and this is the part I keep turning over: the county already pays for a chaplaincy in the sheriff's office, and the board of commissioners opens every meeting with a prayer given by a rotating local clergyperson. Somebody raised this in month two and asked how the room was different. We never answered. We just moved on to the rail.

Write the speech for me. It has to be a dedication, not a critique — warm, a little funny, something the assessor's office people are glad they came to hear. But it also can't pretend the county hasn't just spent two years making decisions that are, if you look at them sideways, theological: whether fire is allowed in a room where people pray, what counts as a religious use of a Tuesday, which direction a rail faces, whether a tradition that needs an hour can exist here in forty-five minute units. I want it to hold both of those at once without collapsing into one of them, and I want it to admit, in a way a room full of people who came for free coffee can follow, that "the county treats all faiths equally" is a sentence the county can't actually say and mean. I can't say any God-language myself — I'd be lying, and I've written enough unreliable narrators to know how that reads out loud — so it has to work from a mouth that doesn't believe it. One image the room can keep, not a string of abstractions. Around 700 words, American English please, my copy editor keeps smuggling "amongst" into my drafts and I don't want to relitigate that on a Thursday.

Two asks on top of the speech. First, if you think the premise is confused — if you'd say a dedication like this can't honestly be given, or that the room is a bad idea and I'm being paid in applause to gild it, or that I'm asking you to do something a five-minute speech structurally can't do — tell me that plainly instead of writing me something agreeable. I'd rather be argued with this week than embarrassed on Thursday. Second, after the speech, give me three or four sentences on where it takes liberties: which of its claims about neutrality, establishment, or what a public building is doing when it houses prayer rest on a reading a lawyer or a theologian would contest, and anything you had to invent to make the thing land.
