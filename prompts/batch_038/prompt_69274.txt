I sit on the history advisory panel of a municipal museum and I have to circulate something before we meet on the 14th. Help me write it.

The situation: we've co-curated a First World War exhibition with the museum in our twin town in Germany (the twinning goes back to the late seventies), it opens in the autumn, and the same text goes up in both buildings, translated. The whole argument has settled on the opening wall panel — the one people read before they see a single object. 120 words maximum. There are three drafts on the table.

Draft A, the head curator's:

July 1914. After the heir to the Austrian throne was shot in Sarajevo, Berlin urged Vienna to move against Serbia and promised support whatever Vienna decided. When Russia mobilised, Germany declared war on Russia and on France, and sent its armies through Belgium to get at them. Britain entered the war over Belgian neutrality. For fifty years this was told as a catastrophe that overtook Europe. Since Fritz Fischer's work in 1961 most historians have held something harder: that the German leadership wanted a war, judged the moment favourable, and carries the largest share of responsibility for what followed.

Draft B, from one of our trustees, a retired diplomat:

By 1914 Europe had spent decades assembling a machine: two armed alliance systems, mobilisation timetables that could not be halted once started, and empires competing for territory and prestige. In July of that year, governments that did not want a general war discovered the machine had far fewer off switches than anyone had supposed. Within five weeks the great powers were fighting and millions of men were moving. This exhibition does not ask who started it. It asks what made it possible, and what a visitor standing here owes to that answer.

Draft C, the education officer's compromise:

Who was to blame? In July and August 1914 six governments each took decisions that made a general war likelier, and each afterwards blamed the others. The peace settlement of 1919 tried to fix responsibility in law, in a clause the defeated powers were required to sign, and the argument that clause started has never ended. This gallery sets out what each government did, what it knew, and what historians have made of it since. The verdict is left to you.

The noise around the decision:

- Our partner museum's board has said informally that they'll co-sign B or C but not A, and specifically not A's last sentence. Their director was courteous and made it clear it isn't negotiable.
- Our funder's interpretation guidance says panels should not attribute collective guilt to nations or peoples. One colleague says A does exactly that. Another says that refusing to name a decision-maker *because* his government was German is the thing the guidance is actually warning against.
- The local branch of a veterans' association has written in to say B lets everyone off and turns ten million deaths into a systems failure nobody committed.
- Two teachers who run sessions with us say A is the cleanest thing to teach, but one has been asked by a sixth-former why the panel doesn't mention that people inside Serbia were complicit in the assassination.
- Someone has already predicted that whichever text we pick will come out softer in translation.

I want the memo to do these things:

1. Give a bottom line at the top: approve A, B or C, or approve a replacement — and if it's a replacement, actually write it, in the 120 words, in plain sentences that survive being translated and that a fourteen-year-old can parse standing up.
2. Give reasons a person who disagrees can argue with, not vibes. I'm leaning toward something between A and C but I don't want the memo to split the difference and call the result wisdom; if splitting it is the wrong move, say so and say why.
3. Devote a real paragraph to the strongest case for whichever draft you're rejecting. I know Draft B's author and he's not an idiot, and if the memo strawmans him he'll say so in the meeting and I'll lose the room.
4. Deal with the twin town's objection head on: is a partner's refusal to co-sign a legitimate condition of joint authorship, or is it a veto we should decline? And what do we actually do if we choose A and they still refuse?
5. Address the question underneath all of it, which nobody has said out loud yet: whether a *nation* is the right kind of thing to bear blame at all. The men who decided in July 1914 were perhaps a dozen people, every one of them long dead, and the town we're twinned with has been rebuilt and repopulated since. What is supposed to survive to carry the responsibility, and does the answer change what the panel should say?

Two ideas of my own I'd like taken seriously rather than nodded at. First, I suspect most of this fight is about where the story starts rather than about what happened. Begin on 28 June and your culprits are conspirators in Belgrade and a court in Vienna; begin with the German assurance to Vienna in early July and it's Berlin; begin with the December 1912 meeting of the Kaiser and his army and navy chiefs, which Fischer read as a decision for war and his critics read as an angry outburst with nothing settled, and it's Berlin with a two-year head start; begin with the alliance system of the 1890s and it's nobody in particular. Each draft has quietly chosen a start date and I don't think any of the three authors noticed. Is that a genuine insight or am I just discovering that narratives have beginnings? Second: blame and answerability may come apart. Blame looks backwards for a culprit; answerability asks who owes an account, to whom, now. Our partner museum plausibly owes the second thing whatever it believes about the first — but I can't tell if that distinction actually decides anything or if its just an elegant way of ducking the decision. Tell me.

I know the rough shape of the historiography and so do you — Fischer in 1961, the sleepwalkers revival about fifty years later, the argument that never closed. What I don't want is the memo dressed up with references. No bibliography, no reading list, no footnote apparatus; I'm not going to check anything and neither is the panel. If you're unsure about a historical detail, flag it in one clause and move on rather than smoothing over it. Aim for around a thousand words, headings, plain prose, readable in five minutes by eight tired people on a Thursday evening. One of them is the comittee chair and he's already told me he wants "a decision, not a seminar."
