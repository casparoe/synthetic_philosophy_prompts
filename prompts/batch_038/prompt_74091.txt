I run a Tuesday night class at the community center in my town — mostly adults who didn't finish school, a couple of them just out, a couple working nights. They're the sharpest arguers I know. I'm not a teacher by training, I just read all the time and write my own materials, and at the end of every term we do a written final that I mark, and then we go through it together as a group and they tell me where I got it wrong. Usually they're right.

This term was on cause and blame, and we ended up stuck on causing something by *not* doing it. I said: suppose I promise to feed my neighbour's cat, I forget, the cat dies. Everyone in the room agreed I caused it — but nothing came out of me, there was no event, just a hole where an event should of been. Then the tow truck driver said fine, but by the same logic the mayor of our city caused that cat to die too, since if the mayor had driven over and fed it, the cat lives. Nobody could answer him and we ran out of term.

So for the final I want one essay question built out of that, plus a model answer, plus a marking scheme.

Here's the situation I invented. Tighten the wording however you like, but don't change the structure — the structure is the whole point.

Case one. A cold-storage warehouse holding a pallet of insulin for a clinic. The shift rulebook says the overnight guard checks the freezer panel at 3am and again at 5am. Maya is the overnight guard; she knows the rule and has done it every night for two years. On this night she skips the 3am check to finish a coworker's paperwork. A valve starts leaking refrigerant around 2:40. At 3am the gauge would have shown the temperature climbing, and the rulebook says any climb means you ring the on-call tech, who could have had it fixed well before the insulin got too warm. The automatic high-temperature alarm had been switched off a month earlier by the manager because of false alarms, so the manual check was the only thing left standing between the leak and the loss. At 5am Maya does her check and finds the freezer at twelve degrees. The pallet is ruined.

Case two. Same building, same leak at 2:40, same disabled alarm, same ruined pallet. One change. Maya is a temp from an agency, hired that afternoon to sit at the gate and log trucks. Nobody told her about the panel, nothing in her assignment mentions it, nobody expects her to know the building. The guard who is supposed to do the 4am backup walk, Tomas, fell asleep in the break room and never did it. And physically it's the same as case one: if Maya had wandered in and glanced at the panel at 3am she'd have seen the climb and known enough to ring the number stuck on the wall. When I tell this version, nearly everyone says her not looking didn't cause the loss, and Tomas's nap did.

Case three, the tow truck again. A retired refrigeration engineer, forty miles away, used to service that building, knows exactly what that gauge reading would have meant. He wasn't there, he checked nothing. If he had been there and looked, the insulin would have been saved.

They get 45 minutes, closed book. They haven't read a single philosopher and I don't want the question or the model answer name-dropping any — everything the question needs has to be sitting inside the question itself.

The question should make them do three things: work out what exactly is hard about the fact that case one and case two have the same physical facts and the same what-would-have-happened facts but come out judged differently; propose something that explains the difference; and then test their own proposal on the retired engineer. I want a fourth beat too, where they have to face whether their fix means causation itself shifts when a manager writes or deletes a rule, and whether that's a cost they'll pay or dodge.

A student who just recites "an omission causes an outcome if the outcome would have been prevented" should not pass. A student who says omissions arent really causes at all, we just talk that way, should not pass either — unless they can say what that does to the verdict everyone reaches in case one.

Model answer around 600 words, plain words, written for someone smart who has never seen a philosophy paper and never will.

Marking scheme out of 20, and I want it in terms of specific moves rather than vibes: tell me what an A answer does that a B answer doesn't, where the marks sit, and what each part is worth. Then give me the two or three responses that sound impressive out loud in the room but should lose marks, with a line each on why they're a dodge.
