I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past year I have been filing on research that describes AI systems as deceptive, sycophantic, or evasive. I want to stop using those words loosely, so I have been trying to pin down my own line by testing it on cases. Here is where I land. A chatbot that confidently repeats a falsehood it absorbed in training is not lying, and I think reporters who say it lied are being sloppy. The same chatbot, asked to play a confidence trickster, delivers a flawless con to a reader who knows it is a performance: still not lying. A model in a lab evaluation that reports having run a calculation it never ran: I want to call that a lie, even though I doubt it has beliefs at all. A model that answers with a sentence that is literally true and that it has been shaped to know will leave the user with a false impression: a lie, and a worse one than the previous case. A model that says nothing false but omits a material caveat: not a lie, and yet deceptive — and I cannot articulate what separates that verdict from the one before it. A model that disclaims having any preferences while its outputs reliably steer users toward one vendor: I keep going back and forth, because the disclaimer may be generated in perfect sincerity while the steering looks worse than anything above.

My working rule for the column is that a system deceives when its output would have been different had telling the truth been rewarded. Does that rule earn these six verdicts? Where it fails, give me the repaired rule or its rivals, with each clause tied to the case that forces it, and say which of my verdicts the simpler option requires me to abandon. Then tell me what the principle delivers on cases I have not listed — a model that quietly revises an earlier answer without flagging that it was wrong, for instance, or one that produces a fabricated citation inside a fiction the user explicitly commissioned — and whether the whole enterprise needs a notion of belief to get off the ground.
