Last night I lost an argument I had been confident of winning, and in three weeks I have to deliver a speech that depends on which of us was right.

The argument concerned Athens, and it went like this. My friend designs sortition processes for a living — she has staffed citizens' juries in four cities — and when I told her I had been asked to give the opening remarks at the first session of a new one, she spent two hours explaining why I should not say a single encouraging word about deliberation. Her evidence was Mytilene. In 427 BCE the Athenian assembly decreed that every adult male in Mytilene should be put to death and the women and children enslaved, as punishment for revolt; the next day, at the request of some Mytilenaeans and their sympathizers in the city, it met again and reversed itself; the show of hands was nearly even; and the reversal reached Lesbos only because a second trireme, launched in haste a day and a night behind the first, met no contrary wind, was fed barley cakes steeped in wine and oil by the men who had provisioned it, rowed in shifts without stopping, and arrived just after the general Paches had read out the sentence and prepared to carry it out. Meanwhile the first ship had made no haste, being on so sad an errand. And roughly a thousand ringleaders whom Paches had already sent home were executed regardless, the walls were razed, and the fleet was confiscated. So, my friend said: the outcome was not the assembly's judgment arriving somewhere. It was a coin flip plus a weather report plus a head start. If I stand in front of forty randomly selected residents and tell them their deliberation carries epistemic weight, I am telling them something Mytilene does not support.

My answer — which I could not get out cleanly, and which is the answer the speech needs to make — was that the second ship is not the interesting part of the story. What has to exist before a city can reverse itself overnight is someone willing to ask for the meeting to be reopened the next morning, and someone else willing to stand up and argue the losing side again in front of a room that is furious and has already decided. That is not luck. It is a practice, and it is rare, and it is exactly the practice forty strangers are about to spend five weekends learning.

The occasion, precisely: our city council has convened a forty-member citizens' assembly, drawn by lot from the municipal voter roll, meeting over five weekends, to recommend whether the city should close its two least-used fire stations and redirect the money into a civilian crisis-response unit. Their output is advisory; the council votes afterward. The room at the first session will contain the forty panelists, none of whom have heard of Cleon; the council staff and two council members who commissioned the thing and are privately nervous about it; one reporter from the local paper; and two colleagues of mine from the department, one of whom works on Thucydides and will consider the speech a failure if Cleon appears as a cartoon villain.

I need roughly 900 words, six or seven minutes, spoken aloud, in the first person, addressed to the panelists rather than to the officials.

Structurally, the speech has to do four things. First, it has to let Cleon be right about something. He told the assembly — in an archaic early-modern English translation I have in front of me, which you should render into plain modern speech rather than read aloud in that diction — that they behave as "spectators of words and hearers of actions," and that "overcome with the delight of the ear, you are rather like unto spectators sitting to hear the contentions of sophisters than to men that deliberate of the state of a commonwealth." He also argued that delay only benefits the wrongdoer, that the city hands out the prizes for rhetorical victory while bearing the danger of having been persuaded, and that a city with fixed, even mediocre laws is stronger than one where clever speakers keep overturning settled decisions. Some of that is an attack on the very institution I am opening. The speech should meet it head-on, because the panelists are about to become the thing Cleon was complaining about, and they will be more useful if they know what that complaint is.

Second, it has to show what Diodotus actually did, which was not to appeal to mercy. He told the assembly, in the same translation, that "For how great soever be their fault, yet I would never advise to have them put to death unless it be for our profit," and that "We plead not judicially with the Mytilenaeans so as to need arguments of equity, but we consult of them which way we may serve ourselves of them to our most advantage hereafter." He asked them not to trade the advantage of his counsel for the attractive pretexts of Cleon's and for their own present anger. Then I want the speech to pose, without resolving, the question that my friend would press: the man who saved a city saved it by refusing to appeal to the city's conscience. Is that an instruction to a panel of strangers who have to agree about something, or a warning?

Third, no inflation of scale. The vote was nearly even. A thousand men died anyway. The reversal was not the same thing as a good outcome, and the speech must not let the panelists leave thinking that getting a second look is the same as getting it right.

Fourth, it may name what came next — the destruction of Melos in 416, the Sicilian expedition of 415 to 413, the trial of Socrates in 399 — but only if the naming does work. Two sentences at most, and the work is that the same institution that reversed itself also destroyed itself repeatedly; a panel is not a guarantee. If you cannot make that land without turning the occasion into a lecture about why popular deliberation fails, leave Melos out entirely.

Things the speech must not say: that the wisdom of crowds will settle this; that the panelists are wiser than their elected representatives, two of whom are sitting in the second row; that Athens invented democracy as we know it; anything about the panel's historic importance. And do not put any words in quotation marks and attribute them to Thucydides, Cleon, or Diodotus other than the two passages I have given you — describe their arguments instead of quoting them.

Give me the speech, and then, after it and clearly separated, three sentences telling me which of my friend's objections you decided not to answer and why.
