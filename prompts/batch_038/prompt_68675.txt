I am a doctoral student in philosophy, and next month I am co-teaching a week of a graduate aesthetics seminar whose topic is Schopenhauer's philosophy of music, paired with an evening screening of Damien Chazelle's Whiplash. The pairing was my idea, and it is now beginning to look like a liability. Half the seminar is analytic aestheticians who have read the music sections of The World as Will and Representation closely; the other half are two musicologists from the performance faculty, one of whom is a working jazz pianist and has already told me, not unkindly, that the film is about practice and not about metaphysics. A friend of mine who plays drums professionally says something similar: on his view the film's real subject is the economics of conservatory prestige, and any philosopher who walks into that material carrying Schopenhauer is going to come out with a parable. I want to pre-empt that objection rather than absorb it in the room, and I want to know whether my own reading survives contact with someone who thinks it is overreach.

My reading runs as follows, and I would like you to test it rather than endorse it. Schopenhauer's claim is that music alone among the arts does not copy the Platonic Ideas but copies the will itself, so that what we hear is striving, satisfaction, and the pain of deferred satisfaction, without any of the particular objects that in ordinary life give those states their content; he ties register and motion to grades of that objectification, so the bass moves slowly because inorganic mass moves slowly, and he treats dissonance and its resolution as the image of desire and its quieting, with words in vocal music a subordinate foreign addition. Now the film is saturated in exactly the vocabulary that account seems to forbid: it is about tempo, about a body in time, about hands bleeding into a bowl of ice water, about a five-hour endurance session on a single tune, about a teacher who humiliates a student for a tempo he cannot hear, about a former student whose death is announced as a traffic accident and is later revealed as a suicide, about a bar conversation in which the teacher justifies everything by an anecdote of a cymbal thrown at a young saxophonist, and finally about a festival stage on which the teacher cues a piece the student does not know, the student walks off, comes back, seizes the tempo himself, and plays an improvised solo over which the teacher's expression passes from contempt to something like recognition while the student's father watches with awe and horror. My distinction, which I would like you to use and then try to break, is between music as symptom and music as exertion: on the first, the listener hears the will from nowhere in particular, as Schopenhauer describes it, in a contemplation that has temporarily suspended willing; on the second, music is an episode of willing in a body, and its meaning is inseparable from the effort, rivalry, and damage that produced it. My thesis is that the final sequence is not the will-less contemplation Schopenhauer's aesthetics promises but the naked will with no quietive residuum left in it, so that the film splits Schopenhauer against himself: his metaphysics of music may be the best available account of what the audience in that hall is undergoing, while his ethics of resignation and compassion condemns every condition that made the performance possible. I want to know whether that split is stable or whether it collapses, and in particular whether Schopenhauer's own aesthetics already depends on the striving his ethics repudiates.

What I would like from you is a critical reading of the film in these terms, in continuous prose only, with no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, and no tables, written the way one would write a long letter to a colleague. Begin by stating your verdict on my thesis in a few sentences, then argue it out. Take the philosophy seriously as philosophy: say where Schopenhauer's account genuinely illuminates specific moments, including whether his linking of tempo and register to grades of objectification gives any purchase on a film whose central humiliations are about tempo, and where the account has nothing to say. Take the film seriously as a film: attend to the fact that the phenomenology here is delivered through editing, sound mixing, and physical detail rather than through anything represented in the music's content. Say explicitly where you would resist me, including whether my symptom/exertion distinction is doing real work or is just a relabeling of the old listening-versus-performing divide, and whether Schopenhauer's low estimate of performance relative to composition and his impatience with virtuosity as display undercuts the whole exercise from the start. Do not reduce the film to an illustration, and do not distort Schopenhauer to make the pairing tidier than it is. End by saying what the film contributes to the debate about music and the will that argument alone would not have produced, if anything. Please do not include a reading list, bibliography, or citations, and do not put words in quotation marks from either the film or Schopenhauer; paraphrase instead, since I have both texts in front of me and I would rather have your judgment than your references.
