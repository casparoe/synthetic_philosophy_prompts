I co-direct a workshop series in which every funded adversarial collaboration must close with a single joint statement bearing both parties' signatures. This term's pair has spent four years on the question of whether self-deception is possible in the strict sense, and I need that document drafted now.

The parties are as follows. Party A, whom we call the Divisionist, holds that genuine self-deception requires two things: first, that at some point in the episode the subject is in a state carrying the content not-p while sincerely avowing p, the two being kept apart by a partitioning of the mind of roughly the kind a broadly Davidsonian account invokes; and second, that the episode be sustained by aim-directed activity — the subject's own information-handling is organized toward producing the belief that p, though that organizing aim is not itself avowed. Party B, the Continuist, holds that no such machinery is needed. On their view every case we describe as self-deception is fully explicable by ordinary motivated cognition operating on evidence — biased search, selective attention, disproportionate weighting of confirming data, motivated forgetting, and confabulation about one's own reasons — compounded by a folk habit of reporting the outcome in the vivid idiom of deception. The Continuist adds that the static and dynamic paradoxes are artifacts of importing the interpersonal-deception template, where there are two people and a moment of knowing.

The collaboration has produced a pre-registered study whose results are sealed and will not be opened until the statement is signed. The design is this. In stage one, every participant performs an evidence-based judgment task with a determinate but non-obvious answer: which of two opaque containers holds more marked tokens, on the basis of a recorded series of draws they inspect at their own pace. In stage two, participants are randomly assigned either false diagnostic feedback, to the effect that participants who select the first container score highly on a measure of cognitive resilience they will care about, or neutral feedback unconnected to any trait. In stage three, three measures are collected in fixed order: an explicit belief report with a confidence rating; a speeded forced-choice between the two containers under a 750-millisecond response window; and a free-recall prompt asking them to list everything they remember about the sequence of draws. In stage four, two subsets receive further probes: a misattribution probe, which raises the possibility that trait feedback can shift judgments and asks whether that happened here; and a contradiction probe, in which the experimenter sets the participant's own recalled draws alongside their stated belief and asks them to reconcile the two.

Draft the joint statement. It must contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — what both parties accept about the phenomenon under dispute, about the ordinary logic of third-person ascriptions of self-deception, about whether the static and dynamic paradoxes impose real constraints, and about the limitations of this study (including the worry that a token-counting task may not engage the sort of investment that real cases involve).

2. A disagreements table. For each row give the topic, a formulation of the Divisionist position that the Divisionist would sign, a formulation of the Continuist position that the Continuist would sign, and a verdict on whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. Nothing may be worded so that a signatory concedes more than they intend.

3. Three to five cruxes: sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party. For each, identify the outcome pattern in the sealed data that both accept as bearing on it, and record each party's prediction in advance. Then, for each crux, state the outcome pattern each party has agreed in advance would count against their own view — and where a party cannot name one, say so plainly and explain what that asymmetry does to the collaboration's evidential value.

4. A dedicated treatment of the retreat problem. The Divisionist objects that any pattern the Continuist finds — dissociation between the speeded and explicit measures, failure to recall contrary draws, insistence under the contradiction probe that the recollection rather than the belief was faulty — can be redescribed by the Continuist as bias plus confabulation, leaving the Divisionist no possible observation. The statement must either exhibit an outcome the Continuist has agreed cannot be so redescribed, or concede that none exists.

5. A short closing section on the case both parties have been arguing about since the first meeting: the department chair who maintained for a year that a departed colleague would return, who was visibly startled when shown the colleague's forwarded acceptance letter, and who within a week was telling people he had always thought the return unlikely. Give the two readings, each in its proponent's preferred wording, and say whether this case is diagnostic or merely evocative.

Constraints: write for signatories, not for an audience, and keep it between 1,400 and 1,900 words. Do not supply a bibliography, and do not cite or name studies other than the one described above; refer to positions by their commitments rather than by their authors. If you mention the paradoxes of self-deception, characterize them rather than quoting anyone's formulation. Do not invent results — the predictions are what is wanted, and they must be specific enough to be checkable.
