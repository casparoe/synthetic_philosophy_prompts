I work on evals at a mid-size AI company — I'm an engineer, not a philosopher, so bear with me if the framing comes out clumsy.

Part of my job is running human-or-model discrimination tests. Setup is basically the Loebner thing: a crowdworker chats through our own UI with either a human contractor or one of our models, ten-ish turns, then picks which one it was. We're pretty strict about the protocol — identical font and avatar, no usernames, no timestamps rendered, nothing that tells the judge anything about the plumbing. The stated principle in our internal doc is something like "the judge should only ever see the words."

Here's where it gets awkward. Model replies stream in token by token and land in about a second; our human contractors take eight to forty seconds to type, and the typing indicator just sits there the whole time. We never controlled for that, and a coworker ran an informal side analysis and could apparently call the model from response latency alone at like 90%. So the thing we said we were shielding, we're not shielding, and nobody on the team is particualrly bothered, which strikes me as weird.

The reason I'm asking you: I was reading Turing's 1950 paper in Mind — computing machinery and intelligence — and he has that bit about ESP as one of the last objections, which I'd never noticed before. As I recall, and I'm not going to pretend I remember the wording: he says telepathy would wreck the game, so you'd have to put the competitors in a telepathy-proof room, and then something to the effect that you wouldn't need to screen for the other varieties of ESP (clairvoyance, precognition, psychokinesis?). He also apparently thought the statistical evidence for telepathy was overwhelming, which is... a choice.

But the shape of his worry looks identical to mine. Both are about information reaching the judge through a channel that isn't the content of the conversation. So: what does our "only the words" rule actually presuppose? What theory of evidence-about-minds makes a shielded text channel the correct instrument — is that behaviorism, functionalism, something else entirely, or is it not a philosophical commitment at all, just experimental hygiene that any theory of mind would endorse? Which rival accounts of mind, or of what counts as evidence for one, does the rule quietly rule out? And is the practice consistent with itself given the latency leak — is a response-time cue just more behavior, in which case fine, or does it break the very thing the rule was there to protect? Then: if we swapped in a view where the channel doesn't have to be shielded, what would the protocol concretely have to look like instead?

Treat this as a real puzzle, not a request for a survey of the Turing test debate — I don't want the Chinese Room or any of that. Start by brainstorming out loud: list the candidate commitments the rule could be resting on, and say which ones you think actually fit before you commit to an answer. Then work through it carefully and land somewhere.

One more thing. If you think I've built this on a confused premise — if Turing's ESP business isn't really about the same problem as mine, or if "what does this presuppose" is just the wrong question to ask about a lab protocol — tell me straight instead of politely playing along. And please flag which parts of your answer you're confident about versus which parts you're extrapolating.
