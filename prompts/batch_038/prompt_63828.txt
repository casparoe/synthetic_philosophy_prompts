I work in the research department of a small open-economy central bank — think Nordic-ish, nine-person monetary policy committee, definitely not the Fed — and in about three weeks I'm presenting a twenty-minute staff memo to the committee itself plus a room of maybe fifteen researchers. The memo's claim is unpopular and I know it: that we should deliberately make our own communication *less* informative. Concretely I propose we stop publishing the full staff forecast path and the two-decimal nowcast, and instead release a deliberately coarsened three-category signal (roughly below / around / above trend) with a disclosed noise band, plus randomized rounding on the handful of numbers we'd still publish. The argument has three steps. First, Grossman and Stiglitz's 1980 impossibility result: prices can't be fully informative, because if they were nobody could earn a return on the costly business of becoming informed, so the informativeness of asset prices — and of the business-contact surveys and dealer chatter our own forecasters feed on — depends on somebody continuing to earn an informational rent. Second, we have become by far the loudest public signal in this economy, and there's a crowding-out channel: Morris and Shin's 2002 paper says more precise public signals can hurt welfare, and yes, I know Svensson's 2006 comment argues their result is actually pro-transparency once you plug in realistic parameters, but Svensson's bite comes from a coordination game and my channel is information acquisition, not coordination, so I don't think his calibration transfers. Third, Milgrom and Stokey's no-trade theorem, which I want to use as a diagnostic rather than as a prediction: if our published forecast were genuinely fully revealing, there'd be nothing left to trade on and volume in our domestic rates and FX markets should collapse toward zero, and the fact that it hasn't is our best available evidence about how much private information is still being produced. So the policy is to preserve some of that rent on purpose. I've been calling the mechanism epistemic crowding out and I'm attached to it, though a colleague has been telling me for two months that it's just an argument for obscurantism with a model taped to the front.

Here's the room, and this is the part I actually need help with because I keep prepping for the wrong audience. The chair and two external members are former academics who know rational expectations and market microstructure cold — one of them has written a well-known paper on information aggregation — and they will not be impressed by a stylized-fact story; they'll want to know whether this bank has any private information worth protecting at all, given that most of our inputs are published by the national statistics office before we see them, whether my third step secretly needs common priors, and how big the effect is supposed to be. One external member is an ex-financial journalist who cares almost entirely about legitimacy and will say I'm proposing institutionalized vagueness, that noise, once discovered, becomes the news story, and that credibility is a stock that takes a decade to build and one memo to spend. The head of communications sits in too and thinks in terms of message discipline: her worry is that markets will read coarsening as the bank loosing confidence in its own forecast, and she'll ask why rounding a number isn't itself a signal that gets decoded within a week. Our parliamentary liaison will point out that publishing forecasts and explaining decisions is close to a statutory obligation here, so transparency is not a free choice variable for me to optimize over. And in the back there are two or three young DSGE modelers who'll say the whole thing is a finance-market microstructure channel with no bite in an economy where the marginal agent whose expectations matter is a household or a firm setting a price, not a trader, plus one experimental postdoc who has run lab asset markets and will say the Grossman-Stiglitz effect shows up but is tiny and gets swamped by noise traders.

So: draft me the prep sheet, not a defense of the memo. I want the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise — in their vocabulary and in roughly the order they'd come up, with the ones from the two academic members and the journalist distinguished from each other rather than lumped together as skepticism — ranked by how much damage each would do to the proposal if I fumble it. For each one give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing up, a blunt note on where that reply is softest, and what I should concede early so I don't look like I'm defending the indefensible. Tell me which single reply has to land for the memo to survive the meeting at all. And include the question I'm praying nobody asks, which I suspect has something to do with the memo being self-undermining in the same way I'm claiming our publications are, but say it the way the chair would say it and tell me honestly whether there's an answer.
