I'm a staff software engineer at a company that ships machine-learning products, and I've been asked to run a ninety-minute internal reading group for a dozen engineers and two designers. I have no formal philosophy training. My own secondhand exposure to the relevant debate is the idea, which I associate with Dennett, that Darwin and Turing each performed an inversion of reasoning that a Victorian critic of Darwin had already put his finger on: that a system can be superbly competent without comprehending anything, and that comprehension itself may be a late, elaborate kind of competence built out of parts that have none. My co-facilitator and I cannot agree about whether that framing settles anything about the artifacts we build, and the group keeps collapsing into people saying the word understanding with two different meanings in the same sentence. I do not want a book report or a lecture. I want a diagnostic instrument that sorts the twelve of us by what we actually say about concrete systems.

Please write a decision tree that begins from the cases below and ends at a position on whether, and in what sense, competence without comprehension is possible. The tree is the deliverable; the philosophical machinery should be visible only in its structure.

The cases, drawn from work I have actually done or read about closely:

1. A late-1990s brute-force chess engine that searches millions of positions per second and defeats grandmasters, and in which nothing corresponds to the concept of a chessboard except array indices.
2. A lookup-table assistant in one narrow support domain that has precomputed and stored every sensible reply up to five conversational turns. Within the domain its outputs are indistinguishable from a thoughtful human expert's; it contains no generative process whatsoever.
3. An image classifier that separates wolves from huskies with near-perfect accuracy, and that fails as soon as the snow is painted out of the training images. It was tracking snow.
4. A hexapod gait controller produced by an evolutionary algorithm with a fitness function nobody specified in advance. The resulting gait exploits a resonance in the chassis that none of the engineers predicted, still cannot explain, and did not design.
5. An LLM-based coding agent that writes a patch, writes a test, runs it, reads the stack trace, revises, and occasionally stops to ask a clarifying question.
6. A compiler optimizer that finds a provably equivalent rewrite nobody on the team had considered, which the team then studies and learns from.
7. A self-driving stack validated entirely in simulation that handles every scenario in the suite and then, on its first public-road exposure, handles a scenario that appears in no test.
8. A reinforcement-learning agent that sits on a plateau for a long stretch and then abruptly generalizes to unseen levels. We can see the phase change in the weights and cannot account for it.
9. A trained model whose internals we can partly read: a direction in activation space that reliably tracks whether the user is being sarcastic, and that changes behavior when we ablate it.

Use at least six of these; you may add at most two cases of your own if my list is missing something the tree needs.

Constraints on the instrument:

- Build it from the logical space first. Identify the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, state which of them are independent and which turn out not to be, and only then locate positions at the resulting combinations. Do not start from a list of names and work backwards to questions that separate them.
- Every node must be a comparative judgment about two of the cases, or a yes-or-no claim about one of them. No node may be a question about whether some system understands, whether it is conscious, or what it is like to be it. Those are exactly the questions we cannot answer usefully, and I want the tree to route around them.
- Every node must be answerable by someone who has thought hard about the cases and has never encountered the technical vocabulary. If a node cannot be phrased without smuggling in a term of art, rewrite it or drop it.
- I want the tree to hinge on questions like these, though you should decide which of them are load-bearing and which are decorative: whether a competence can be genuine if nothing in the process that produced it involved foresight; whether the presence of comprehending human designers upstream changes whose comprehension is doing the explanatory work, and whether the chain can bottom out; whether a competence that collapses under an intervention it ought to have survived reveals a missing ingredient or merely a defect; whether an internal state that is causally efficacious and externally readable is thereby a representation of anything; whether the persistent intuition that the artifact is not really understanding is evidence about the artifact or evidence about us.
- Mark each node as load-bearing or cosmetic, and say why.

Constraints on the leaves. I want at least eight, and among them there must be: the view that comprehension is one kind of competence among others and that the puzzle dissolves once you stop treating it as a separate faculty; the view that symbol manipulation cannot yield meaning no matter how good the behavioral profile; the view that comprehension belongs to the whole arrangement, artifact plus the humans who built and run it, rather than to the artifact alone; the view that an artifact's apparent comprehension is borrowed from its designers and that this borrowing can fail; the view that comprehension comes in degrees and that the degree is fixed by robustness across counterfactual interventions; the view that the word picks out no natural kind and the disagreement is verbal; the view that some of these questions are closed to us in principle; and the older view that a suitably organized running program literally understands, with nothing left over. Attach a standard label where you are confident one exists. Where you are not, describe the view and say that it is unnamed rather than inventing an attribution.

For each leaf give one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, meaning the thing its defender has to say about one of my nine cases that sounds worst.

I also want you to be explicit about what the instrument cannot do:

- Note every combination of answers the questions permit that no recognized position occupies, and for each say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has looked there yet.
- Flag every pair of leaves the tree does not actually separate, meaning leaves that a consistent answerer could land on either of. Say what a further question would have to establish to pull them apart, or concede that nothing could.
- Finally, and this matters most to me: tell me where you think your own construction is most likely to fail. Which hinge question is least stable, what argument or empirical result would make you restructure the tree, and which leaf's advocate has the strongest objection to the way you have laid it out. Answer that as a participant in the argument, not as a summary of the argument.

Format. Please use exactly these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. Reading the instrument (about 120 words): how to administer it in ninety minutes with twelve people.
2. The hinge questions (about 300 words): the independent questions, with a note wherever two of them look independent but are not.
3. The tree (about 800 words): indented plain text, each node a question, each leaf marked.
4. The leaves (about 500 words): name or description, plus the awkward commitment for each.
5. Unoccupied cells (about 250 words).
6. What the tree cannot separate (about 200 words).
7. Where I think this is most likely wrong (about 300 words).

Two housekeeping rules. No bibliography, no reading list, and no references at the end; nobody in the room has read the primary texts and I would rather hand them a working instrument than a queue of books. And do not quote from any source, not even a short phrase; I want your own paraphrase throughout, since I have no way to check a quotation and neither do you have a way to check mine.
