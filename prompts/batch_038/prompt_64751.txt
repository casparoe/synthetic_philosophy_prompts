Four hours on this with a friend and we both came out more confused than we went in, so now I'm farming it out to a third party. Topic is whether a reasoner can trust its own upgrade — the Löbian obstacle business.

Toy setup, which we both agreed to. W is a proof-checker agent: it only ever asserts sentences it has actually derived inside its own system, and it's sound, so nothing it asserts is false. W wants to build a successor S. S plays by the same rules — only asserts what it derives, also sound — but has a bigger toolkit: everything W can derive, S can derive, and some things S can derive that W can't. Both are strong enough for the usual diagonal stuff and for Gödel's theorems to apply.

W wants to adopt a deference policy toward S: whatever S asserts, W counts as settled and asserts too. My friend says that's impossible, and here's his argument numbered the way he wrote it on the napkin:

1. If W adopts the policy, then for every sentence φ, W derives "if S derives φ, then φ."
2. S derives everything W derives, so for every φ, S derives "if S derives φ, then φ."
3. Take φ = ⊥. Then S derives "if S derives ⊥, then ⊥," which is just S deriving its own consistency.
4. By Gödel's second theorem, a system that derives its own consistency is inconsistent.
5. So S is inconsistent — contradicting the assumption that S is sound.
6. Therefore W can't adopt the policy. No sound weak reasoner can defer to a strictly stronger one.

Then, to make it hurt, he ran the same argument with S = W: W derives "if W derives φ then φ" for every φ, so W derives its own consistency, so W is inconsistent, so W isn't sound. Which seems to show no reasoner can trust its own derivations at all. That can't be right. I relied on my own derivations to follow his argument, he relied on his to produce it, and if (6) is true then neither of us was in a position to establish (6). I told him that was self-undermining and he said no, it's just the honest conclusion, and we went around in circles for an hour after that.

I proposed two patches and he says neither survives. I'm not convinced.

Patch A, restricted deference: W only defers on a narrow class, say Σ1 sentences about the output of one specific subroutine it wants S to run. Then (1) hands W reflection only for that class, and (3) can't be run, because ⊥ isn't in the class.

Patch B, a trust ledger with graded confidence: W doesn't prove anything about S, it just assigns 0.99 credence to whatever S asserts, and S assigns 0.99 to whatever its own successor S′ asserts, so W's credence in S′'s assertions is 0.99 × 0.99 = 0.9801, and so on down the ladder. My thought is that the diagonal step needs credence 1, or an actual derivation, to close, and once trust is graded each application of it costs a little, so instead of collapsing the whole thing just decays. He says S can derive sentences about its own credences, so the ladder diagonalizes anyway and the decay is fake.
