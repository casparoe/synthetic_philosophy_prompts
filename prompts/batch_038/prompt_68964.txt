I am preparing a handout for a reading group in our epistemic game theory sequence, and I want a decision tree rather than a survey. The starting commitment should be minimal and shared by everyone in the room: the reader accepts the standard apparatus (a state space with partitions, a knowledge operator for each player, common knowledge as the greatest fixed point), and accepts two results without contesting them — that two Bayesians with a common prior who come to know each other's posteriors for an event must assign it the same posterior, and that backward induction yields the unique subgame-perfect play in the centipede game. Everything downstream of those two acceptances is what the tree should sort.

The branches must be questions about epistemic hypotheses, not about solution concepts: whether rationality is known at the relevant state or merely true; whether the knowledge in play is mutual to some finite depth or common in the fixed-point sense; whether the players are assumed to share a prior; whether a hypothesis may be retained at a node off the equilibrium path, given that reaching the node is itself evidence bearing on it; and whatever further splits you judge belong on the tree. Each node must be phrased so that a reader can answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to, and each leaf must carry the name of the position or concept it corresponds to — rationalizability, iterated admissibility, common-knowledge backward induction, the impossibilist line on which the hypothesis fails at every non-initial node, and so on — together with one sentence on that leaf's most awkward commitment.

Two matters I want explicitly addressed. First, a colleague of mine defends a view I need the tree to be able to locate: in a game with n decision nodes per player, reasoning should proceed with mutual knowledge of rationality to depth exactly n and no further, which reproduces backward induction in short games but sustained cooperation in a long centipede. Is there a leaf for it, and can your questions separate it from the view that denies common knowledge while retaining arbitrarily high finite depth? Second, I suspect the finite-depth/fixed-point distinction is not a genuine branch in finite games, since the two should coincide there; if you think I am wrong about this, say where the separation arises.

Finally, please list the combinations of answers that your questions permit but that no one occupies, and say for each whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or merely an unexplored corner of the space.
