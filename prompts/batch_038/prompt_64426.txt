Had four hours of it with my mate Dev on Saturday and my head is still going round, so I thought I'd get a second opinion that isn't him.

Context: I'm the governance manager at a mutual — about 60,000 members, we do savings and a bit of insurance — and part of my job is turning member rule-change proposals into a board paper ahead of the AGM. Most years they're dull (quorum, proxy forms, one about the car park). But I get maybe four or five a year that are properly constitutional, either formally submitted or as a sounding-out email from a member group asking whether something is even viable before they draft it, and this year they're all versions of the same arguement: should the members who know what they're talking about get more say than the ones who don't.

Dev's line, for the record: you wouldn't let 60,000 people vote on whether an aircraft is airworthy, so why let them vote on whether our solvency margin is adequate. My line: because it's the passengers who fall out of the sky. His counter: so make the test about facts rather than values and you've got yourself a competent electorate. My counter: whoever writes the exam already runs the place, and anyway the people who do know the facts don't agree about them. That's where we got stuck and that's roughly where I want your help — I want a screening procedure I can run on a proposal like this, not not an essay on whether epistocracy is a good idea. Something I can put in front of me with a notepad and work down, and reuse when the next one lands.

What I've got: six weeks between a proposal landing and the board paper going out; me, plus a part-time company secretary who's read law but not philosophy; the governance committee signs the paper off; and then the whole membership votes on it, so anything I write has to survive being read aloud by someone who's convinced it's a power grab. Stakes vary from the embarrassing to the solvency-threatening. I've done a bit of background reading and got as far as Mill's plural votes, the old point about a jury only being wiser than its members if the members are more often right than wrong, a phrase I think was "qualified acceptability" — something about a rule only being legitimate if the people it binds could accept it even when they lose — and an idea about drawing a random sample of members and training them up instead of testing anyone. If I've got any of that wrong, or it's better known under another name, tell me. If there's something else that would genuinely change the shape of the procedure, fold it in rather than listing it.

Then the cases. Case one: a member group wants the decision on whether we divest the investment portfolio from fossil fuels taken only by members who pass a two-hour online test on corporate finance and climate risk, with votes weighted by score. I'll be honest, I'm soft on this one — I've read the arguments and I think they're probably right about the assets — so be straight with me about whether the procedure rescues it or kills it, and if it kills it, say what the kill shot is. Case two: a proposal that the decision on closing two regional branches goes to a panel of 60 members drawn at random, paid for their time, briefed by staff, given two weekends to deliberate, with the board bound to accept whatever they recommend. Case three: a proposal that our chief actuary and the chair of the risk committee get a joint veto over any AGM resolution that would take our regulatory capital below a threshold, on the grounds that the membership can vote for anything except ruin. And one I don't know what to do with: a group representing our older members wants two board seats reserved for members over 70 — is that even the same question, or does my procedure not apply to it?

Please structure it as:

- **The short version** — about 100 words on what the procedure actually turns on.
- **The distinctions that matter** — 250 words or so: which distinctions the procedure depends on, and why the collapse of each one breaks it.
- **The procedure** — the main event, roughly 800 words, numbered steps in the order I should ask them, what counts as a pass at each step, and which steps end the thing with a no rather than carrying on.
- **Where this isn't really a procedure** — about 200 words: the steps that look mechanical but are actually me making a judgement call, and how I should own that in the board paper instead of dressing it up as the procedure deciding.
- **Run on the three** — about 700 words across the three cases, plus a couple of lines on the over-70s one.
- **What it can't do** — about 150 words: how it'll misfire in practice, and what a determined member group could do to game it.

Where a step turns out to be a question about values rather than facts, I'd rather the procedure said so and handed it to the committee or the membership than pretended to settle it. No reading list and no references at the end — I wouldn't be able to check them anyway.
