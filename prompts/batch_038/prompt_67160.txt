Ok so I've been chewing on the symbol grounding problem — Harnad's thing about how a system that just shuffles symbols around needs those symbols to actually mean something *to it*, not only to us outside observers looking in. I keep landing on the idea that the problem is mis-stated, and I want you to argue for my version rather than just weigh it up.

My version: grounding isn't a property a single system either has or lacks. It can be held by one party and spent by another. A language model's symbols are grounded the way a banknote is backed — the paper is only good because a whole separate system of people who really do have causal contact with the goods stands behind it, and the note can be cashed out into their activity. So call it hosted grounding, or borrowed grounding.

Please make the strongest case you can that hosted grounding is real grounding and not a cheat: that a system whose symbols inherit their content from the embodied practices of the humans whose writing it absorbed genuinely has those symbols grounded, in the same sense that a cashier genuinely accepts a banknote she didn't mint.

Then deal with the obvious comeback, which is that the cashier has skin in the game — she can be fired, sued, robbed of her savings — and the model has nothing at stake. Is having something at stake actually necessary for grounding, or is it a separate virtue that people sneak into the argument because it sounds rigorous? Pick a side and defend it. No need for citations or names, just the argument.
