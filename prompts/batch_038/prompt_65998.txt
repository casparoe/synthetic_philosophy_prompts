You're a philosopher whos also spent real time around master-data systems in healthcare. Think it through carefully and brainstorm a bit out loud before you write the actual procedure, because the conceptual part matters more to me than the checklists.

I run the master patient index queue for a group of six community hospitals. Every day my team gets a few hundred pairs of records that an automated matcher flagged as possible duplicates, and we have maybe two minutes per pair. Merge two different people and somebody gets the wrong blood type or a drug they're allergic to; don't merge two records that are really one person and you get a fragmented chart, a missed allergy, a duplicated radiation plan. Constraints: the person deciding has no philosophy or statistics training, every "maybe" has to become a real action by end of shift, and escalation to a senior reviewer counts as a failure of my process because we only have one such person.

Here's the thing I keep coming back to and cant shake. I've been reading about the identity of indiscernibles — the idea that two things sharing every property are just one thing — and about that thought experiment with two exactly similar spheres alone in an otherwise empty universe (a guy named Black, I think). Someone explained the standard reply to me: each sphere stands at a different distance from the other, so they do differ in a property, so they aren't genuinely indiscernible after all. If that reply works, then in my database nothing is ever a true duplicate either, because every record has its own record ID and its own created-at timestamp. But if I let those count as differences I can never merge anything and the whole job is pointless. So I need an answer to which properties are allowed to count and which have to be excluded as mere bookkeeping — and I need it as a rule my team can apply, not as a discussion of haecceity.

So: write the procedure. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it lands on merge / don't merge / escalate, and mark every step that's really a judgment call dressed up as a rule. Then run it on three cases: (a) two records matching on name, date of birth, address and phone, which turn out to be identical twins registered at the same clinic in the same week; (b) two records matching on everything except that one carries a middle initial and one has a nine-month gap in insurance history; (c) a pair identical on all fields where one record was created by the ER overnight and the other by a registration clerk the next morning. On at least one of these give me the answer I don't want — I'd like to auto-merge anything scoring above a threshold and I suspect you're going to tell me I can't.
