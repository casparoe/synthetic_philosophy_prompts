I am preparing the seminar paper for a graduate proseminar in philosophy of mind whose final two sessions were given over to the dispute between Searle and Dennett about original and derived intentionality: Searle's claim that the states of a computing machine have only an aboutness borrowed from its programmers and users, since formal symbol manipulation is never sufficient for semantics, and Dennett's counter that this borrowing story has no non-question-begging cash value, which he presses with the two-bitser — a bistable device whose state comes to be about a coin landing heads only in virtue of the role it plays in a larger design, and which is about something else entirely if you unplug it and install it elsewhere. I mentioned over dinner that a friend of mine who maintains legacy industrial control systems had told me about plants that keep running for years after the last person who understood them has retired, and that remark suggested to me a case that might serve as a discriminating test between the two views rather than as another intuition pump of the sort both sides already have. I have drafted the case below, and I would like your assessment of it.

The case is this. Sometime in the 1970s a horticultural research station commissions a climate-control machine for a sealed glasshouse: several thousand two-bitser-like elements, each a physical device whose state is reliably caused by some condition (soil moisture, ambient temperature, light level, the position of a vent) and whose state is in turn used by the machine's control logic. The station's engineers build, wire, and maintain the machine, and their working practices fix which element stands for which condition; a wall chart in the maintenance room, cross-referenced to a printed manual, records the assignment. Now suppose the station is abandoned: the last of the staff retires or dies, the manual and the wall chart are destroyed in a fire, and the glasshouse is thereafter visited by no one. The machine nevertheless runs on a solar array and a bank of batteries that outlast everyone who understands it. Its sensors continue to track soil moisture and temperature, its actuators continue to open and close the vents, a paper tape at the far end continues to print a running log that piles up in a bin nobody reads, and a diagnostic lamp continues to flash in an empty room. Nothing in the machine's physical composition or causal organization changes at the moment of abandonment. Two stipulations matter for what follows. First, the log is a genuine record: a competent engineer who found it a century hence could reconstruct the climate history of the glasshouse from it, and the reconstruction would match independent evidence from tree rings or ice cores. Second, the machine's internal states admit of more than one equally good interpretive mapping — an alternative assignment of elements to contents on which the machine is about dew point and pressure in a systematically shifted way — and while the staff were alive, the choice between mappings was fixed only by their practices, not by anything intrinsic to the hardware.

Please write an essay that answers the question the case is designed to raise: after the last person who understands the machine has died, do its states still have content, and does the answer favor Searle or Dennett? I want you to engage the strongest version of each side's response rather than a caricature. The Searlean line presumably runs through the thought that a tape nobody reads is not a record of anything but only a potential for someone to decode it, so that the machine's aboutness is doubly parasitic — on minds that once conferred it and on minds that might recover it — and that the case therefore shows precisely what derived intentionality is. The Dennettian line presumably runs through the thought that the pattern in the glasshouse is there whether or not anyone is in a position to see it, that real patterns do not require readers, and that the abandonment alters only our epistemic access to the machine and not the machine. Address, as part of your argument, two subsidiary questions that I think the case makes sharp rather than merely decorative: whether content can lapse and then return, if a future archaeologist reconstructs the log (and whether a verdict of lapse-plus-return is coherent or is just a way of hedging), and what fixes the mapping of the interpreter's own states if interpreters are needed to fix the mapping of the machine's — whether the demand for a non-interpretive content-fixer leads to Searle's biological brains, to a regress, or to nothing at all. I am asking for a committed verdict, not a survey: state which side the case favors, say what you think happens to the machine's states when the last engineer dies, and give your reasons, including reasons why the opposing verdict fails. Then flag, in a short closing passage, the one or two places where your own argument is most exposed, and say what would have to be true for the other verdict to be right instead. You need not cite anything or supply a bibliography or list of further reading; I would much rather you argue from the case and from the two positions as you understand them than gesture at sources.
