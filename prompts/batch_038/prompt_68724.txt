The student is a fourth-year who has done well all term; the exam was in class and timed, with a suggested length of four hundred words for this question. The question appeared exactly as follows:

Question 3 (20 points). Two spots of light are flashed in quick succession a few degrees apart, the first green and the second red. Subjects reliably report seeing a single spot move smoothly from the first location to the second, changing color somewhere around the midpoint of its path. (a) Set out the two accounts of this report that Dennett and Kinsbourne label Orwellian and Stalinesque. (b) Explain why they think the subject's own reports cannot decide between these two accounts, and say what they take the deeper difficulty with the question to be. (c) On their view, what would have to be true for a question about when a given content became conscious to be answerable at all?

The answer, reproduced in full: "(a) In the color phi case the subject reports a change of color at a point in the trajectory where nothing of the sort was ever on the screen: at that moment the green spot has already gone out and the red spot has not yet come on. The puzzle is that the report concerns an event at a time when the information needed to generate it — the color and the position of the second flash — was not yet available to the system. Two accounts are possible. On the Stalinesque account, the visual system holds off producing a conscious experience of the sequence until the second flash has arrived, and then gives the subject an experience of a single spot moving and changing color mid-path, so that she has an experience as of something that never happened. As with the show trials, the outcome is fixed before the performance begins. On the Orwellian account, her experience is closer to what was on the screen — a green spot there, then a red spot here — but the memory of it is revised afterwards, so that when asked she sincerely reports a change of color partway across. As with the rewriting of the records, the alteration happens after the fact. What the two accounts agree about is that the subject's report is a faithful account of the experience she had; they differ only about whether that experience was assembled before it was had or reconstructed afterwards. This is why the reports cannot decide between them: no amount of introspection will disclose a difference that shows up nowhere in what the subject is disposed to say. (b) Dennett and Kinsbourne's conclusion is that the Stalinesque account should be preferred. The Orwellian account must posit a second process whose only job is to alter the memory, whereas the Stalinesque account needs only the delay that processing time already imposes on the system, so it is the more parsimonious of the two. The deeper difficulty is that the question assumes there is a definite moment at which the processing becomes conscious, a moment that the two accounts are competing to place themselves before or after. If there is no such moment, then it follows that no physical fact corresponds to the question either: nothing in the brain settles when the color change was experienced, because nothing there could settle it. (c) What would make such a question answerable is a third-person method for locating the point at which the erroneous content enters the causal chain — imaging, or single-unit recording, that shows where in the visual system the mid-path color change is represented. Once we can point to that place, we can say whether it lies before or after the moment of consciousness."
