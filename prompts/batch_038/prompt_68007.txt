I am a graduate student, and my seminar group has spent the better part of a year going in circles over the absent-minded driver case. Let me set it up exactly as we use it, because the disagreement turns on the details.

A driver is on a highway at night with two exits, A and then B. Taking A pays 1, taking B pays 4, and passing both so that the road runs out pays 0. He is drowsy: when he reaches an exit he cannot tell A from B, and cannot tell whether he has already passed one. His only available policies are therefore behavioral — at each exit he continues with probability p and exits with probability 1 − p, drawing independently at each exit. At planning time, before he sets out, this yields (1 − p)²·1 + p(1 − p)·4 = 1 + 2p − 3p², which is maximized at p = 1/3 and gives an expected payoff of 4/3. Everyone in the group accepts all of that. The fight is about what to say once he is at an exit.

**Ravi.** At an exit, the driver's alternatives are not "exit here" and "continue here." He has no way to select an alternative that applies at the exit he is standing at but not at the other one, since he cannot tell them apart. So the honest comparison is between behaving in a way that gets him off at A, worth 1, and continuing now and leaving the rest to his mixture, which brings him to B and pays 4 with probability 1 − p and 0 otherwise. Continuing is worth 4(1 − p), exiting is worth 1, so he should continue whenever p < 3/4. At p = 1/3 the plan is stable and there is no conflict between planning and acting. The competing calculation is a double count: it lets the driver treat his present decision as though it governed only the exit he happens to occupy while simultaneously crediting him with his planned behavior at the other one.

**Nour.** Ravi's asymmetry is the fallacy. If "exit" means exit at every exit, then it is worth 1 only because he leaves at A — but that framing throws away the one thing the driver actually has at the exit, which is evidence: the evidence that he is at an exit. Given his plan he reaches A for sure and B with probability p, so the centered odds are 1 : p and his credence that this is B should be p/(1 + p). Exiting is then worth (1 + 4p)/(1 + p) and continuing 4(1 − p)/(1 + p), and exiting is better exactly when p > 3/8. At p = 1/3 we both say continue, which is why nobody noticed the split for a year; but at p = 1/2 I say exit and Ravi says continue, and one of us is wrong about a well-posed question rather than merely talking past the other. The fact that the plan beats my action-time advice when my advice is turned into a policy does not refute me: the planning-time optimum was computed on the assumption that he sticks to p, and if he is going to reconsider at every exit, the object to be optimized is a different one.

**Teodora.** You are both answering a question with no subject. At the exit the driver is not choosing; the lottery he set up before setting out is landing. "What should he do now?" presupposes that there is now a decision, and there is not one — there is only the plan and its realization. Your two quantities, 4(1 − p) and (1 + 4p)/(1 + p), are both things nobody is in a position to act on, which is why you disagree and why nothing hangs on it. If you want a real action-time question, give him a way to precommit, or a way to tell the exits apart, and there is nothing left to argue about.

Our moderator observed that Ravi and Nour coincide at the planning optimum and diverge only for p between 3/8 and 3/4 — that is, only for plans the driver would not adopt if he is rational at planning time. Nour replied that this assumes precisely what is in dispute, since whether he can stick to a plan is what the argument is about.

My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the dispute is verbal. Ravi and Nour are answering different questions — what policy should the driver have adopted, and what should the driver do given only that he is at an exit — and the appearance of contradiction comes from applying "should" and "rational" to both. If that is right, no argument will settle it, and the live question becomes which of the two questions decision theory is supposed to answer. I suspect this is too easy, or wrong, and I want to be told so if it is.
