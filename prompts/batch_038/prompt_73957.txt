I'm in a seminar on probability and its applications this term (stats major taking it for the credit, most of the others are philosophy people) and we just spent two weeks on anthropic reasoning — the doomsday argument, self-sampling vs self-indication, the whole thing. There's a guy in my reading group, a physics PhD student, who refuses to engage with the SSA/SIA material at all because he says the doomsday arguement is just the German tank problem wearing a fake beard. His pitch: during the war Allied statisticians estimated German tank production from the serial numbers on captured tanks and their parts, and when the German records got opened up afterwards the statistical estimate turned out to be dramatically closer to the real production figures than anything the intelligence services had guessed. So, he says: your birth rank in the human sequence is a serial number, the total number of humans who will ever exist is the factory output, and there's literally nothing new here — it's an estimation problem with a known success story, no indexical weirdness required. The statistician never had to ask whether they were a randomly selected member of the population they were estimating, and that's his whole point: the self-location business is what manufactures the fake paradoxes. On top of that he says SIA is obviously the presumptuous one (the presumptuous philosopher thing, where you apparently settle whether the universe is finite or infinite just by noticing there are lots of us), because SIA lets your own existence count as evidence about how many observers there are, and the tank analogy never does anything remotely like that.

I sort of buy it and sort of don't and I can't tell which of my doubts are real. Can you lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine: serial number, factory output, the sample of captured tanks, the estimator, the statistician standing outside the population, whatever else matters — and say what plays which role on the doomsday side. Then go through the disanalogies and seperate the load-bearing ones from the cosmetic ones. The ones I keep circling back to: (a) in the tank case there's an actual physical sampling procedure, somebody went and captured tanks, whereas nobody sampled me out of humanity, I just happen to be one; (b) the statistician isn't a member of the population being estimated but I am, and being inside seems like exactly what makes SSA and SIA come apart; (c) the tank likelihood doesn't care how many tanks there were in any deep sense, but under SIA the fact that I exist does; (d) "German tanks built in month X" is an unambiguous reference class and "human beings" famously isn't. Which of those actually break the inference, and which would a Gott-style defender just shrug at?

The part I care about most is whether his conclusion survives, and I think there are really two conclusions tangled together — that the doomsday reasoning is trustworthy because it has this empirical track record, and that the SSA/SIA debate is a philosopher's artifact you're allowed to skip. My suspicion, which I can't defend, is that the analogy is doing more work than he admits and is quietly assuming something like SSA (reasoning as if you'd been drawn at random from your reference class), which would be pretty embarrassing given that's the very assumption he thinks is unnecessary. Also: is there a better source domain? The urn version, where you pull one ball out of an urn of consecutively numbered balls and it says 7 so the urn probably doesn't have many balls, feels closer to me, but I can't articulate what the urn has that the tank factory doesn't — or whether the urn just hides the same problem more elegantly. And if the tank case really is the right model, what would a doomsday skeptic have to give up to keep their hands clean?
