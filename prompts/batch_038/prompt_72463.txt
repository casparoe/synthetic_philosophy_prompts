At my workplace two people on my team, Dana and Marcus, have a long-running argument about whether a hypocrite can legitimately blame anyone. It began with a real incident: our department head gave a stern talk about padding expense reports after having done exactly that himself for two years, though he had admitted it and repaid the money before the talk.

Round 1. Marcus said the head had no standing to blame anyone, so the talk was morally void and should be treated as noise. Dana said that is plainly wrong: everything he said was true, several people really were padding their reports, and a standard does not stop applying because the person invoking it is himself a sinner.

Round 2. Marcus said the issue is not truth but entitlement — blaming means holding someone to a requirement you refuse yourself. Dana said the head was not refusing it; he admitted the violation and made restitution, which shows endorsement, and if imperfect endorsement disqualified you, no one could ever blame anyone.

Round 3. Marcus said Dana then owes an explanation of why the same words from a colleague with a clean record would have landed differently, since the staff's resentment rested on no factual error. Dana said the resentment tracks social meaning — it reads as a status play or a deflection — and that this is precisely what shows standing is not a property of the blamer in the way Marcus assumes: the head's standing ought to have been worse toward the two people who knew about his reports than toward the new hires who did not, and the fact that everyone was equally angry suggests they were reacting to an insult rather than tracking standing. Marcus answered that if standing varies with what each hearer happens to know, then it is not a fact about the blamer at all, and saying he lacks standing is simply the wrong description of the situation.

Round 4. Marcus said standing must be something that can be lost and regained, and that on Dana's account there is nothing to lose or regain, so the concept does no work. Dana said it does plenty of work, just not the work Marcus wants: it governs who ought to speak and when, not whether what they say counts.

Please adjudicate the exchange round by round — who is right at each stage, and where each of them overreaches — and then say who is more right about the underlying question and what, if anything, both are missing. Give your answer as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "rounds", an array of four objects each containing "round" (1 through 4), "dana" and "marcus" (each an integer from 1 to 5, where 5 means clearly right about that round and 1 means clearly mistaken), and "reason" (one or two sentences); "underlying_winner", one of "Dana", "Marcus", or "Neither"; "what_both_missed", a string of at most three sentences; and "new_case", a string stating what your preferred account implies about a colleague with a spotless record who privately intends to pad his own reports next quarter and denounces the department head loudly.
