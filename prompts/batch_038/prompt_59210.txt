I'm a PhD student working on the mid-century dispute over whether dreams are experiences, and I want to pressure-test a counterfactual against Malcolm's actual argument rather than the caricature of it. His case turns on two connected claims: that a person who is asleep is, on any criteria we possess, not undergoing experiences, and that the sense of a claim about someone's having dreamt is already fixed by the impressions he has on waking, so there is no episode in the night for the report to be about. Since he wrote, the empirical situation has changed in ways he never had to face. Trained lucid dreamers answer an experimenter's questions and do simple arithmetic from inside verified REM sleep, using pre-arranged patterns of eye movement and facial muscle activity, and afterwards report the exchange as having occurred within the dream. Sleep neuroimaging classifies the content of a sleeper's imagery from the activity pattern alone, with the classification checked against the report given minutes later. And some patients physically enact the violent scenarios they later describe having dreamt. Reconstruct the reply Malcolm would most plausibly make, in his own conceptual terms. I suspect it would not be a clean concession: intra-sleep behaviour, he might say, only adds further criteria to a concept whose criteria the waking impressions were already supplying. But tell me where that defence has to give way — which of these three developments genuinely damages the argument, which he can absorb, at what dialectical cost, and whether what survives amounts to anything more than a stipulation about what counts as being asleep. Then say briefly how the same evidence would cut for or against Dennett's objections to him.
