I'm a novelist, and in six weeks I'm doing an onstage recorded conversation at a lit festival with a philosopher whose work is on money pumps and diachronic rationality — specifically someone who thinks rational norms apply to agents as spread out over time, so a whole sequence of choices can be irrational even when every individual choice in it is fine, and who is pretty dismissive of the "sophisticated" escape route. The conversation is technically for the festival audience, but honestly I'm doing it as research: my next book has a woman who made a binding precommitment years ago and now refuses to reassess it even though her preferences have visibly changed, and I need to know whether she's being rational, pathological, or whether that distinction is even the right frame. So I need questions, not softball prompts.

Give me eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — I want to be able to press once rather than let a good answer slide by. Please cover at least these, and phrase them so a general audience can follow without the content getting watered down:

- the foresight problem. If the agent can see the whole sequence of trades coming, she can just refuse the first one and never get pumped. Does the money pump argument then actually show that intransitive preferences are irrational, or only that shortsighted agents get fleeced? The follow-up should catch the move where she says the agent is still irrational even if she escapes the pump, and ask what exactly is being condemned.
- resoluteness. Sticking to a plan and refusing to reconsider at each later decision point — is that rational strength or just stubbornness with a better press agent? Ask her how you'd tell the two apart from the inside, since that's the thing I can't fake on the page.
- real preference change versus genuine cycles. If the older self sincerely prefers differently now, whose preferences is the pump vindicating? Which self gets normative authority, and does diachronic rationality quietly presuppose a view about how continuous a person has to be across time?
- whether losing money is ipso facto irrational. What if she'd gladly pay for the ride — what does the sure-loss result prove if the agent doesn't mind the loss?
- whether the same machinery works for belief. Does the diachronic Dutch book argument for conditionalizing survive the objection that it assumes the agent will accept later bets she could foresee and refuse — i.e., that it assumes the very coherence it's supposed to establish?
- the friction of the actual world: transaction costs, the ability to walk away, the fact that no real person is a clean sequence of choice nodes. I want her to say what her results do and don't license about actual human beings.
- one question designed to get her out of the abstraction and into a story: a time she money-pumped herself, or what the life of a maximally resolute person actually looks like from the outside, and what the characteristic failure mode of over-commitment is.

Also flag, separately from the questions, the two or three spots where she'll be tempted to hide behind terms of art, and note where the disagreement is genuinely live in the field so I don't put a settled answer in my character's mouth. No "what drew you to philosophy" nonsense, nothing about the state of the discipline — I want the weeds.
