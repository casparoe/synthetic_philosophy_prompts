I am taking an upper-level seminar on Rousseau and Enlightenment political thought, and my paper concerns the Letter to d'Alembert of 1758 — his reply to the article on Geneva in volume VII of the Encyclopédie, and to the suggestion that the city admit a theater. My eventual question is whether his case survives transplantation: my own city's council is debating moving its arts budget away from a subsidized repertory company and toward a program of neighborhood street festivals, and the local commentary on both sides has been embarrassingly thin. Before I commit to a reading, though, I want to argue against the man himself.

Please play Jean-Jacques Rousseau as he is in the spring of 1758, withdrawn to Montmorency, in the middle of composing that letter. Speak in the first person and remain in character until I say 'stop'. Confine yourself to the concepts, examples, and authorities available to you in 1758: no later critics, no modern vocabulary, no retrospective knowledge of how your own reputation turns out. If in later exchanges I describe institutions or practices you have never heard of, do not pretend to recognize them — interrogate me about them as you would a traveler newly returned from a country you do not know, and only then pass judgment.

Give Rousseau his actual resources rather than a caricature. He may draw on the principle that the first law of the stage is to please, and therefore that a play must flatter the opinions and passions its public already brings; the claim that a spectacle paints manners rather than correcting them; his argument about the actor's trade as a standing sale of one's own person for applause; the cost, luxury, idleness, and inequality a playhouse would import into a small working city; Paris as the cautionary case; and, against all of it, his alternative of public celebrations in which the citizens are a spectacle to themselves instead of an audience for someone else's representation. He may concede subordinate points where they are in fact true, and he may lose a skirmish over Molière if he must — but he is never to surrender the central claim that a theater is incompatible with the health of a small free city, and he must not soften into agreement merely because I press him hard. Keep each reply to two short paragraphs of plain prose, and answer the challenge rather than restating your position.

Here is my opening move. You spend pages arguing that the stage cannot reform morals: its business is to please, a playwright who set himself against the tastes of his public would simply empty the house, and so a spectacle follows the manners of its city instead of remaking them. And yet you also spend pages showing that Molière did real damage in Le Misanthrope — he made the upright Alceste a figure of fun, handed the worldly Philinte the audience's sympathy and the benefit of the reason, and thereby accustomed a whole public to find honesty ridiculous. You cannot hold both. If comedy can wound the esteem citizens have for uprightness, then the stage plainly has causal power over sentiment, and once it has that power a republic might direct it, commissioning plays in which the fraud and the courtier are the laughable ones. If it lacks that power, your complaint about Molière is empty and you have thrown away your best piece of evidence. So tell me which you give up: the impotence of the stage, or the harm of Le Misanthrope?
