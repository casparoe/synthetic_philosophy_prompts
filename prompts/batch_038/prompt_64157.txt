Here's the passage, more or less in full: 'The Nyāya school built its epistemology around a practical question: how do we actually come to know something, as opposed to merely thinking we do? Its answer is that knowledge always arrives through a means, a pramāṇa, and that there are exactly four such means — perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. None of the four reduces to any of the others; the Buddhist epistemologists, who recognised only perception and inference, argued for centuries that the rest were dispensable. Inference turns on a pervasion between a reason and what it is meant to establish: smoke is never found where there is no fire. You get hold of that pervasion by assembling instances — kitchens, smithies — and by checking that it fails to break down anywhere you look. Comparison is what happens when you reason from one thing to another on the basis of their resemblance: told that a gavaya is like a cow, you work out what sort of animal you're dealing with when you meet one in the forest. Testimony is the least exotic of the four, since it rests on nothing more than your having observed that this speaker has been reliable in the past; here Nyāya lines up with the reductionists in contemporary social epistemology. And because a Naiyāyika holds that you know something only when you can exhibit your grounds for it, the theory is best read as a standing demand for reflective certification — knowledge as cognition whose credentials you can produce on request. Since those credentials are ultimately enumerative, the whole edifice stands or falls with the question that undid Hume's account of induction: whether a finite list of cases can warrant a universal.'
