I direct a small charity that funds corrective surgery for children born with a treatable heart defect, and in six weeks I have to give the seven-minute appeal at our annual donor dinner. The complication is that I sincerely hold Taurek's view: I do not believe the deaths of five are impersonally worse than the death of one, I do not think there is a sum of lives saved that makes our work better than that of a smaller charity, and for years I have kept our materials free of the familiar arithmetic — this much money, that many children rescued. My board knows this and backs it. The room will be two hundred donors, nearly all of whom have been trained by every other appeal they have ever heard to expect precisely that arithmetic, including a table of foundation officers who will ask me afterward what our cost per life saved is.

Write the appeal. It may not aggregate: no totals, no comparisons between groups of beneficiaries, no per-outcome figures, no claim that a gift goes further with us than elsewhere. It must make the case for giving to us by taking the children we treat one at a time, and it must say why that way of proceeding is a form of respect rather than an evasion. It must also meet, out loud and without flinching, the objection from the floor that refusing to count is refusing to choose, and that if we decline the arithmetic someone else's children get the resources; where that objection is right, I want the speech to concede it. Finally, a family whose daughter died on our waiting list before we reached her will be at the front table and has asked that she be named — what I say about her has to be consistent with what I say about the children we did reach, since I cannot explain her death by pointing to a larger total. Make it followable by a room that should never notice it is being given an argument.
