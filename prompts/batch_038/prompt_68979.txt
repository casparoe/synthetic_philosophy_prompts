I am assembling a graduate seminar on explanation in modern history, and one argumentative configuration has repeatedly surfaced in our discussions for which I cannot find an established designation. I should be grateful for help locating it in the literature, or, if it genuinely lacks a name, for carving it out with enough precision that I can teach students to recognise it.

The configuration arises in debates over substitution counterfactuals — the family of questions of the form "would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler?" One party maintains that the outcome was structurally overdetermined: the economic collapse, the terms of the peace settlement, the willingness of conservative elites to hand over power, the erosion of constitutional constraints. Pressed to show what that claim amounts to, they offer a substitution: remove the individual and the position is occupied by someone else — a different occupant, a regime of the same kind. The other party then asks what "the position" is and what fixes its boundaries. This is where the difficulty lies. The description of the office to be filled appears to be drawn from the outcome it is invoked to explain. The structuralist characterises the post functionally — what the movement required was a leader able to hold its rival factions together, convert economic distress into nationalist grievance, and be tolerable to the conservatives as a junior partner — and each of these specifications turns out, on inspection, to be recoverable from a close study of the man who actually held the post. When this is pointed out, the structuralist tends to retreat to a disjunctive consequent ("an authoritarian nationalist regime of some kind"), which then begins to look unfalsifiable; when they decline to retreat, they owe a functional description fixed in advance of the appointment together with a prediction about the class of persons who could have satisfied it, which they rarely supply.

The same pattern recurs in the debate about whether the Soviet terror of the 1930s required Stalin. The structural case rests on one-party rule, a security apparatus already built under Lenin, and the exigencies of forced industrialisation, and it is cashed out as the claim that any successor would have presided over something of the same character. But the specification of what the succession required — a general secretary positioned to staff the apparatus with clients, say — is a description that only picks out its referent once one knows how the succession actually went.

I want to stress that the defect may be symmetrical. The great-man theorist also fixes the granularity of the outcome, insisting that what needs explaining is not a regime of a type but this regime with these particular crimes and these particular dates, and at that level of specificity almost any individual becomes indispensable. In both cases it looks as though the relata of the counterfactual are individuated by whichever side of the debate the speaker occupies.

Candidates for the nearest established vocabulary occur to me, and each seems adjacent without being on target. The standard objections to functional explanation concern the direction of benefit and the mechanism by which an effect feeds back to sustain its cause; here the worry is not feedback but the individuation of a role. The methodological constraints that practitioners of counterfactual history impose — that the antecedent be a small, plausible alteration of the actual past, and that the imagined sequel be theory-guided rather than invented — govern the antecedent and the machinery of the scenario, whereas my difficulty concerns the description of the consequent and of the position the antecedent vacates. Hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy describe an epistemic tendency in the reasoner, not a structural feature of the argument as such. Survivorship reasoning and the image of a marksman painting a target around the bullet holes are close in spirit, but they concern selection over cases rather than the boundaries of a description within a single case. And the older dismissal of "might-have-beens" as a parlour game, associated with E. H. Carr, simply refuses to engage with substitution counterfactuals rather than diagnosing what goes wrong inside them.

So: what is the nearest established concept or body of work here, and precisely how does the configuration I have described differ from it? If it is unnamed, propose a term and a definition, and state the boundary conditions, since that is where my interest lies. In particular, tell me whether the defect is present, absent, or attenuated in each of the following variants: (i) the functional description is documented from material predating the appointment — a party programme, contemporaneous speculation in the press about what the post demands; (ii) the structuralist explicitly commits in advance to a class of outcomes rather than to the outcome, and states the class's membership conditions; (iii) the substitution is run the other way, on a case in which structural conditions persisted across a leadership change but the regime's character altered markedly; (iv) the office is highly institutionalised, with formally defined and legally constrained powers, as against a movement in which authority is personal to its holder.

Finally, I should like to know whether the same thing arises outside historiography — in functionalist explanations of institutions, in reasoning about key-person risk within organisations, in the treatment of simultaneous independent discovery in the sociology of science, where the fact that several people arrived at the same result is often taken to show that any one of them was dispensable. If it does arise there, that would tell me whether I have identified a local vice of counterfactual history or a general one attaching to role-and-occupant reasoning.

Please do not content yourself with a label. What I need is the phenomenon stated exactly enough that I could hand it to a student along with an unfamiliar argument and have them determine reliably whether the argument exhibits it — together with an account of what it is not.
