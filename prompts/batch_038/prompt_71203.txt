So, awkward situation, and it's basically the plot of the book I've been circling for three years, except it's happening in my kitchen. I write family sagas. Two daughters, Iris is twelve and Nora is nine. Iris reads like a vaccuum cleaner — chapter books on her own at four, and she's now in her school's "enrichment" stream, which mostly means harder worksheets and a smug expression at dinner. Nora is a completely different creature: good with her hands, extremely funny, stubborn as a fencepost, draws constantly, and cannot get through a page of text without losing the thread of it. Three years ago we moved from Montreal to a small city in the Netherlands for my partner's job, and between the move and a long bout of illness Nora missed something like seven months of school, and she's now being taught and tested in a language she only started speaking at six. She came back near the bottom of her year in reading. Her maths is fine, by the way. Fine!

A friend of ours — I'll call him Dave, he does data work for a pharma company, the kind of person who reads the actual papers, unfortunatly for me — cornered me after a dinner party and told me I'm about to throw money away. His case, as best I can reconstruct it: reading ability is about seventy percent genetic, that figure has been measured properly in twins, so the gap between Iris and Nora is mostly baked in, and spending a few thousand a year on a reading tutor for Nora is roughly like paying someone to change her eye colour. Same money aimed at Iris's enrichment, he says, has a much better return, because you're feeding a trait that's already there instead of fighting one that isn't. When I said but they grew up in the same house with the same books and the same parents and the same bedtime stories, he said exactly — you held the environment constant, so whatever's left over is the genes. He also said the figure barely moves between countries, so it's about as solid as things get in that field, and he added that he'd read the number comes out *higher* in comfortable families like ours and lower in poor ones, which he took to mean poor kids get squeezed by circumstances and mine don't, so I can relax. I could feel something wrong in it, the same way I can feel a sentence lying to me in a draft, but I couldn't have said where.

So: is Dave right? I don't want the abstract "every child blooms at their own pace" thing, and I don't want you to be gentle with me because I'm the worried parent — I want to know whether each of those steps actually follows from the one before, and if one doesn't, exactly where the chain snaps and what the number he's quoting would and wouldn't license him to say about my specific kid. Part of me wants him to be wrong for reasons that have nothing to do with statistics, and another part of me, the tired part, wants him to be right so I can stop lying awake about it, so I'm not a reliable narrator here and you should assume I'd rather be corrected than comforted. Please also mark clearly which pieces of this are things people who work on it have broadly settled, and which are still genuinely fought over by people who know the field — I'd rather know where the evidence is soft than be handed a contested position dressed up as consensus, especially if the contested one happens to be the flattering or the convenient one. And for what it's worth, Dave is going into the novel eventually. I want him to be wrong in an interesting way, not wrong in a way that makes him an idiot, so if there's a version of his argument that's actually defensible — a narrower claim he could have made and been right about — I'd like to hear that too.
