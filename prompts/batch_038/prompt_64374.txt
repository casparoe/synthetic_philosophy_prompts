I am a senior at a public high school, where I edit our student-run social science review and run the Thursday discussion society — about twenty members, all aged fifteen to eighteen. Several have read a popular book on behavioral economics and now consider the rational-choice framework refuted; a few, trained for policy debate, maintain that a preference simply is a choice and that talk of inner states is noise. I believe both camps are collapsing at least four distinct questions into one, and I want to open the semester's first meeting with an instrument that sorts members by what they actually believe rather than by the side they claim.

Please write a self-sorting quiz of ten questions. Each should be a short, concrete case about an ordinary choice: no gambles with stated probabilities, no laboratory curiosities, none of the stock paradoxes. Members must be able to answer with no economics background, so the questions themselves should carry no technical vocabulary — all of it belongs in the scoring key — and no question may ask about definitions or labels. Each question offers two or three responses, every one of them defensible and every one of them something a thoughtful seventeen-year-old would genuinely pick.

Where possible the cases should drive wedges between three things my members treat as identical: what a person chooses, what they say they want, and what they report feeling afterwards. I have three of my own, to include or adapt: a student who deletes a short-video app on Sunday, reinstalls it on Wednesday, and deletes it again the following Sunday; a student who pays a fee to have an exam result sealed for a month when opening it would cost nothing; a student who orders the fries after sincerely saying they prefer the salad, and afterwards reports enjoying the fries more. Build the remaining questions in the same spirit.

The distinctions the quiz should track, in descending order of importance to me: whether a choice later repudiated by the chooser still reveals a preference at the moment it was made; whether a preference can exist that has never been expressed in any choice; whether the object of preference is an option, an outcome, or an experience; whether paying to remove an option is evidence about one's preferences or about one's preferences concerning one's preferences; and whether a change of description that leaves every option physically identical can change what is preferred.

Then the scoring key, mapping answer patterns onto descriptively named positions. At minimum I want these leaves: choice constitutes preference; preferences are inner causes that choices track imperfectly; preferences are assembled during the act of choosing; the chooser is a sequence of distinct agents over time; welfare is a felt quantity independent of any choice; the whole dispute is idle provided prediction is unaffected; and what is good for a person is the satisfaction of their preferences. Give a conventional label for a position where one exists, but do not attach any leaf to a particular author unless you are confident, and do not pad the key with references — I will supply those. Each position should be described functionally, so the key works for a reader who has never encountered its name.

Three additions I have not seen in anything of this kind. First, list the combinations of answers the questions permit that no position on your list occupies, and say for each whether that gap exists because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, name any pair of positions the questions cannot actually separate, and state what further case would separate them. Third, identify the single question most likely to fail as a discriminator — the one where nearly everyone chooses the same option — and say what to do about it.
