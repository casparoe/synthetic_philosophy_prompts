I'm the volunteer board chair at a small food bank in a mid-size Ohio town — eleven paid staff, most of them part-time, plus about sixty regulars who unload trucks and stock shelves. Our executive director of nine years left in the spring of last year and I've been running the search for her replacement ever since, which means its been fourteen months and the place is starting to fray at the edges. We post on Idealist and the state nonprofit jobs board and we get somewhere between 80 and 140 applications a round, which is way more than three people working evenings can actually read.

Here's the part I'm a little embarassed about. Early on I made up a system to keep from drowning, and I've been using it for four rounds now. Stage one: everybody gets screened against a one-page list of hard musts (five-plus years managing a budget, supervised staff before, can work a Tuesday evening shift when needed). Stage two: I interview in the order the applications come in. Stage three: at the end of each interview I score them on a ten-point rubric I also made up, and the first person who lands a 7 or higher gets an offer, and the search stops. Everyone on the board hated this at first, but it worked. We hired a woman who'd been running a pantry two hours north and she was genuinely good — organized, got along with the drivers, rewrote our whole intake process. She lasted about nine months before taking something closer to her mother, which I don't blame her for, but she'd been feuding with two of the board members near the end and afterward I decided the real lesson was that a 7 just wasn't a high enough bar.

So round two I moved the cutoff to 8. Nobody hit it. Round three I moved it to 8.5 and I also cut the search off after twelve interviews instead of going through everyone who cleared stage one, on the theory that if the right person hasn't shown up by then they aren't coming. Round four I'm at a 9. For the record, in the last eleven months the highest score anyone has gotten on my rubric is an 8.2, and that was a guy who withdrew to take a job in Indiana. Meanwhile the salary line for the role got trimmed in the last budget because we're mid-way through a capital campaign for a new walk-in cooler, and we can't offer relocation, so I'm not sure a 9 exists at what we can pay.

My husband says I'm being lazy and that you don't get to pick a number out of thin air and call it good enough when there are ninety more applications sitting unread — the pantry deserves the best person, and if that means reading all of them, read all of them. My friend Deirdre, who does hiring for a hospital system, says basically the opposite: she thinks the original rule was sound, she thinks the empty chair is the actual emergency here, and she specifically thinks raising the bar after a bad outcome is backwards, that the bar should move the other way. So — who's right? Is it irrational to make an offer to the first candidate who clears a cutoff I invented on a Tuesday night, when I could keep going? And is Deirdre right that the moving-bar thing is the bug in the system, or is that just her professional bias talking? I'd also honestly take some advice on how you're supposed to set a bar like that in the first place, because right now the 9 doesn't feel like a standard, it feels like a number I picked when I was annoyed.
