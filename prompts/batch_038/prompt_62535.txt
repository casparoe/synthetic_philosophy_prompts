I work in a behavioral science department where the heuristics-and-biases people and the fast-and-frugal people share a hallway and have been having the same arguement since roughly the mid-90s. On paper it's settled. Everyone now concedes the Kahneman/Tversky line was too quick to label something a bias, everyone concedes the Gigerenzer line is right that context matters, so we all agree it depends on the environment and then go back to our own labs. In practice nothing has changed. Linda-type tasks still get published as evidence of a conjunction error, the natural-frequency reformulation still gets published as evidence there was never an error to begin with, and neither side cites the other. There's now a third group saying people are approximately sampling from a probabilistic model, which somehow makes both older camps angrier.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: this isn't a disagreement about whether human judgment is rational, and it isn't even a disagreement about the normative notion of rationality at work (coherence vs. correspondence). It's a disagreement about what counts as *the environment* — a hidden reference-class problem. To say that take-the-best or the recognition heuristic is ecologically rational you have to fix an ecology, but the ecology can be carved as finely or as coarsely as you like, and for pretty much any heuristic you can find a redescription of the environment that licenses it. If that's right, no single experiment can falsify the ecological claim, and the truce isn't a resolution, it's a mutual agreement to stop asking.

Is my hypothesis right? Diagnose the actual structure of the stall from what each side does when it runs an experiment — what counts as data, what the comparison is, whether they care about process (which cues the person looked at) or only accuracy against a benchmark, and how they treat the objection that the interesting question is *who* fails, since failure tracks cognitive ability. Sort out how much is verbal, how much is a real methodological split about representative versus adversarially-chosen task design, and how much is bedrock neither side can argue for. Then tell me what would count as progress: some distinction, a formal result, or a type of experiment that actually pins down the environment parameter — or should I accept that the truce is permanent and stop trying to get my two postdocs in the same room?
