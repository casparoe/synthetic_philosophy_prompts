I'm auditing a graduate seminar in the philosophy of history, and our week on causation produced the kind of exchange where everyone leaves convinced they agreed with everyone else. Two students both insisted that Hitler was not necessary; one meant that some authoritarian German regime was likely in the early 1930s whoever led the radical right, the other meant that a different man could have led the NSDAP to the same place, and nobody noticed that these are distinct claims resting on distinct evidence. I want to pin the disagreement down by running one well-documented episode through the great-man-versus-structure opposition: the two months between Hitler's appointment as chancellor on 30 January 1933 and the Enabling Act of 23 March, including the Reichstag fire of 27 February and the emergency decree that suspended civil liberties the next day. Take the following facts as given so that the analysis does not turn on anything I would have to check: the cabinet contained only three Nazis among twelve members — Hitler, Wilhelm Frick at the Interior Ministry, and Hermann Göring as minister without portfolio — while Papen took the vice-chancellorship, Hugenberg of the DNVP took economics and agriculture, and Neurath remained at foreign affairs; the body was a presidential cabinet governing by decree under Article 48, the arrangement every German government had used since Brüning in 1930; the party was electorally declining at the moment of appointment, having lost seats in November 1932 after its July peak; the conservatives who engineered the appointment believed the distribution of offices would bind Hitler and were wrong within weeks; and the Enabling Act cleared its two-thirds threshold with the votes of the Catholic Centre and the exclusion of the Communist deputies.

Now the lens. I want Hook's distinction in The Hero in History between the eventful man, through whom history happens to pass, and the event-making man, whose own capacities redirect it — set against Carlyle's hero-worship on one side and Spencer's reply that the great man is a product of the society that produced him on the other — with the later historiographical quarrel folded in: the intentionalist and structuralist accounts of the Third Reich, and the two mechanisms the structuralist side made its own, Mommsen's cumulative radicalization and Kershaw's idea of working toward the Führer. Before you apply any of it, separate four claims the seminar keeps fusing: (i) onset dependence, whether the regime would have come about at all without Hitler; (ii) substitution equivalence, whether another occupant of the same office would have produced a comparable trajectory; (iii) convergence under drift, whether the structure pushed whoever held the office toward those policies irrespective of his intentions; and (iv) lock-in, whether the structure made reversal harder than acceleration, so that a theory can fail about onset and succeed about persistence. Note as well that a Nazi Germany without Hitler is not one counterfactual but two — coarsely described, as some authoritarian German state, the structuralist may win; finely described, as that state with that racial policy on that timetable, the great-man theorist may win — so state the grain at which each of your verdicts is offered.

Answer in exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, in this order, with no introduction and no conclusion: 1, what the structural account actually predicts about these two months, at a grain you specify; 2, the single feature of the episode that gives the event-making reading its strongest purchase, and why the conservatives' miscalculation does not by itself settle the question; 3, the substitution test run against a Schleicher-led or DNVP-led authoritarian alternative with the description of the outcome held fixed; 4, whether cumulative radicalization and working toward the Führer explain anything inside this window or only after the seizure of power, and which of the four claims each mechanism actually bears on; 5, what the episode does to Hook's distinction — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the distinction was never built to handle. Close every item with a bracketed tag, either [settled] or [contested], and wherever you tag something contested, name the disagreeing camps in a phrase. I want a visible line between what historians and philosophers of history treat as established and what is genuinely live among people who know the material; do not dress up a contested position as the consensus, and do not soften a settled one into a matter of taste.
