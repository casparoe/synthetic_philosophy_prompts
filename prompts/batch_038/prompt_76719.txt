I sit on the nine-member board of a private foundation with an endowment of roughly $400 million and an annual granting budget of about $25 million. Our charter commits us, in language the founders chose deliberately, to using the foundation's resources to produce the greatest amount of good we can identify, and it does not restrict that good to any particular population, time period, or kind of beneficiary. At next month's meeting we must rule on a single request, and the board is divided badly enough that whatever we do will be treated as precedent.

The request is $18 million over seven years for a research and deployment program in AI alignment. I will stipulate the program officer's expected-value analysis rather than defend it. She assigns the program about a 1-in-500 chance of substantially reducing the risk that advanced systems cause a permanent, civilization-ending catastrophe; conditional on success, she values the averted catastrophe at 10^14 future lives. The expected value is therefore on the order of 2 × 10^11 lives, or roughly 10^7 lives per dollar. She adds, correctly, that even if we cut her probability estimate to 1 in a billion, the expected value still exceeds the entire near-term alternative.

That alternative is to spend the same $18 million extending an existing global-health portfolio at an estimated $5,000 per life-year — about 3,600 life-years, delivered through documented supply chains, with measurable results inside the grant period.

Two features of the case have made the board's disagreement intractable, and I need the memo to confront both rather than paper over them.

First, in the same cycle we received an unsolicited proposal from a small group seeking $400,000 for a scheme they say has a 1-in-a-billion chance of averting the loss of 10^22 lives. That is an expected value of about 10^13 lives, or more than twice the lives-per-dollar rate of the $18 million request, produced by exactly the same arithmetic. Every member of the board finds it absurd. Several conclude that a decision procedure which obliges us to fund the absurd proposal whenever one is offered must be self-disqualifying, and that the alignment grant should be declined for the same reason; others reply that the ridiculousness of one proposal says nothing about the merits of another, and that declining the alignment grant means knowingly forgoing the largest identifiable good available to us — a choice that would look indefensible if the tail risk turns out to have been real. A third member's objection is different again: she thinks our estimates of very small probabilities are unreliable in a systematically skewed direction, since the scenarios we find impressive are the ones we are most likely to have overpriced, and she doubts the foundation can audit its own enthusiasm.

Second, the members disagree about whether far-future lives should be discounted at all, and nobody wants the grant to turn on relitigating that. I need a recommendation that can survive the disagreement, because we cannot vote it away.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the board ahead of the meeting. It should:

1. Reach a clear bottom line — approve, decline, or approve on stated conditions — with the conditions and timeline concrete: what we commit now, what phases or tranches are gated on what, what we revisit at the annual review, and what would trigger an early review.
2. State the strongest case against that bottom line in its own best terms, at a length and with a seriousness that makes clear it was not written to be demolished, and then explain what the recommendation concedes to it.
3. Address the second proposal head-on: whether a decision rule must be judged by the worst case it licenses; and if so, which modification survives that test without also disqualifying the $18 million request. Consider at least a bound on the utility function, a ceiling on the weight any single low-probability branch may carry, a discount applied to chained or self-reported probability estimates, and a fixed portfolio allocation to high-variance programs that is set independently of any individual proposal's nominal expected value. Say which of these you think actually works and which merely relocates the problem.
4. Show how the recommendation would change if the board adopted a positive pure rate of time preference, and identify precisely which parts of it are invariant to that choice.
5. Close with a short annex proposing the standing rule the foundation should adopt for future longshot requests, so that we are not reconstructing this argument from scratch every cycle. The annex should be written so that a program officer can tell in advance whether a proposal clears it.

Aim for roughly 1,200 words of memo plus the annex. Write it as an internal governance document addressed to lay trustees, not as a survey of the philosophical literature: name positions only where a name saves space, and include no bibliography, footnotes, or references.
