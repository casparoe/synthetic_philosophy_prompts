I sit on the disciplinary council of a national judiciary that has just restored constitutional rule, and we must decide whether to remove Judge A from the bench. From 2019 to 2023 she presided over eleven prosecutions under an emergency decree criminalizing the publication of information said to undermine national confidence — a decree our constitutional court has since struck down and declared void from the outset, holding that it deliberately inverted the presumption of innocence and was drafted to silence one named opposition paper.

Judge A's defence has three strands: the decree was validly enacted and in force when she applied it; she imposed the statutory minimum in all eleven cases and, in two published judgments, construed the operative phrase so narrowly that the prosecutors could not stretch it again; and when a colleague on the same bench refused to sit under the decree, he was replaced within a week by a judge who handed down maxima, so her seat was not a free seat.

The bar association, pressing for removal, replies that eleven people served time; that her narrow constructions were issued as reasoned judgments and thus gave the decree a legitimacy a flat refusal would have denied it; and that she accepted a promotion in 2022.

Draft the opinion I will circulate before Friday's sitting. It should state a bottom line the council can actually vote on — removal, retention, retention with a public finding of compromise, or some other disposition you think is available — followed by the reasons, ordered by weight. I want you to say which of her three defences does real work and which is a rationalization, to tell us whether treating her judgments as law at all is the right frame or a way of evading a choice we would rather not make, and to treat the bar association's legitimacy objection at its strongest rather than dispatching it. Close with what your recommendation implies for the colleague who refused to sit, whose own petition is next on our docket.
