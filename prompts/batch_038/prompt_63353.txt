I'm putting together a chapter for an edited volume on diachronic choice theory, and I want to stress-test the central claim before I commit to it, because a couple of my grad students have been circling it in seminar without quite landing a hit. The claim is this: the money-pump argument doesn't actually do the work people think it does. On the standard telling, an agent with A preferred to B preferred to C preferred to A gets walked around a cycle of small-fee trades and ends up where she started minus a few cents, and that's supposed to show intransitive preference is irrational, full stop. My view is that the argument's normative bite is carried entirely by a prior, quite substantive diachronic principle — something like the authority of the agent's own earlier deliberation, the idea that a plan adopted at the outset licenses binding your later self. Give me that principle and the pump bites, because the agent is made worse off by her own lights, taken as a temporally extended planner. Withhold it — be a thoroughgoing consequentialist about choice, for whom only the node you're actually at carries normative weight — and the pump stops being an argument about rationality and becomes a report about how an expensive agent can be made, which is a claim about prudence and transaction costs, not about the coherence of her preferences. So transitivity isn't being derived; it's being smuggled in alongside a contested picture of agency. I know the sophisticated-chooser move is supposed to blunt pumps too, by having the agent reason backwards and refuse the first trade, and I also know people dispute whether that really works in general — but that's downstream of my point either way, since sophistication is itself a way of granting the planning principle.

So: what would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from genuinely fatal to merely awkward, and I want them sorted by kind rather than dumped in a list. Some things I'm specifically worried about and would like you to take a run at. Is there a version of the pumping argument that doesn't presuppose any diachronic principle at all — one that works on a single-shot choice, or one that exploits only facts the agent endorses synchronically at each moment, so that even a hard-line consequentialist about choice has to say something has gone wrong? Does my view collapse into a rival, i.e. is "the pump only bites given a planning principle" just a relabeled statement of the planning principle, making my position unfalsifiable-by-construction? And the thing that keeps me up: any case you throw at me I can redescribe as "here the agent wasn't governed by her plan," which sounds like the classic immunizing maneuver. Is that a real problem for a thesis of this type, or is it the kind of interpretive latitude that views about the structure of agency get for free? I'd also like cases where my view and the standard reading genuinely come apart in their predictions — places where the planning principle and the transitivity axiom pull in different directions, so that the two positions issue different verdicts about the same agent.

Finally, tell me what a defender of the orthodox money-pump argument would themselves concede counts against me — not just what a hostile critic says, but the concessions they'd make in good faith, e.g. whether they'd grant that pumps on forgetful or myopic agents are evidentially weaker, or that the argument looks different for bounded agents who can't represent the whole sequence. Say plainly whether my thesis risks anything at all, and if it does, name the single thought-experiment or result that would most move me. I'd rather hear "this position is well insulated and here's why that's fine" than a polite list of objections I could swat away. Don't bother with references or a bibliography — I'll fill in the citations myself — just give me the arguments and cases.
