I am in the second year of a philosophy MA, and this term my seminar has been working through the family of views on which a mental state is conscious only in virtue of the subject's being suitably aware of it — that is, views on which the awareness in question is a further, distinct state aimed at the first one, rather than a built-in feature of it. We spent a couple of sessions on the split between the versions that treat this further state as a kind of thought, requiring concepts the subject must possess, and the versions that treat it as a kind of inner perception, an interior scanning faculty in the old lineage of reflection as a second source of ideas. A classmate of mine, who is writing his term paper in defense of the perceptual version, has been leaning hard on an image he calls the flashlight view: the mind is a dark room full of objects, most of which are simply not lit; a first-order state is one of those objects; the higher-order state is the beam; and for the state to be conscious is nothing more than for the beam to be on it. He uses the image to do three distinct pieces of work. First, he says it explains why unlit objects are no less real for being unlit, so the view gets genuinely unconscious mental states for free rather than by stipulation. Second, he says it handles the regress objection, since one does not need a second light in order to see by the first. Third — and this is the move I find both the most interesting and the most suspect — he points out that a beam can be swung onto an empty corner of the room, illuminating nothing at all, and he argues that the theory therefore predicts phantom conscious states: cases in which it seems to you that you are in some state when there is no such state in you at all, on the model of a phantom limb. He takes this to be a virtue, because it makes the view empirically risky rather than consistent with everything.

I want to know how far the flashlight image can actually bear that weight before I respond to him in class. Which features of the source are supposed to correspond to which features of the target, and which of the correspondences are real rather than decorative? Some disanalogies strike me as serious: when a beam lights an object in a room, the object was there and had its properties before being lit, and anyone else in the room can see it too, whereas on the view the first-order state's being conscious is supposed to consist in its being represented, and it is available to no one but the subject. Others strike me as harmless — the beam's spatial origin and direction, say, or the fact that a real flashlight dims and runs out of battery. I would like the load-bearing disanalogies separated from the merely pictorial ones, and I especially want to know whether the empty-corner move survives: whether the case of a beam pointed at nothing is really analogous to a higher-order state with no first-order target, or whether the analogy is trading on the fact that in the room there is an independently checkable answer to the question of whether anything was lit. I am also unsure whether the image is even available to the thought version of the theory, which advertises itself as non-perceptual, and whether his regress reply quietly assumes that illumination can occur without anyone to whom it is revealed.

Please write your answer in American English, as exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. Use item 1 for the correspondence, stated compactly; item 2 for the disanalogy that does the most damage to the phantom-conscious-state inference; item 3 for a disanalogy that looks damaging but is not, with the reason it is not; item 4 for what the flashlight image smuggles in about the regress objection; and item 5 for your verdict on whether the image can carry his three conclusions, together with a better analogy if you think there is one.
