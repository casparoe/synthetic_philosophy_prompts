i'm a third-year bioethics PhD student and next week i'm running a session in our department's reading group on advance directives and dementia — basically the Dworkin line about precedent autonomy, plus his split between the interests you have in your life going a certain shape overall and the interests you have in how it feels from the inside at any given moment, and then Dresser's pushback that the person in the chair now still has interests and shouldn't be governed by a stranger's signature. The session is going to be dense and slightly depressing and i want to open it with a short poem instead of an abstract. 19 lines, a real villanelle — five tercets and the closing quatrain, two refrains alternating, so the two lines only ever land next to each other in the final couplet.

Here's the constraint i actually care about, because i think the form can carry the argument. Refrain one is the later self, present tense, and it must not be a complaint: she's in the sun by the window, she's laughing at something on the television, she's fine, she just has no idea who wrote the document that's about to decide for her. Refrain two is the earlier self's instruction, imperative or subjunctive — no feeding tube, no antibiotics, whenever i stop recognizing my own children. So the two refrains differ in tense, in mood, and in who's speaking, and they never answer each other anywhere in the body of the poem: no dialogue, nobody talks anybody round. And i want the placement to be the whole point. Open on the later self's line as line one, put the directive at line three, and let the directive be the very last line of the poem too — the paper gets the final word purely by having been written down earlier, even though the line with a pulse came first. Someone who knows this debate should be able to say which refrain is which and why that last juxtaposition is the actual legal situation rather than a poetic trick.

Two things to avoid. First, don't sentimentalize — no elegy for the woman she used to be, no treating the later self as a ruin; she's a person in the present tense. Second, don't hand down a verdict for either side. The thing genuinely stuck in my chapter is an asymmetry i cant find stated cleanly anywhere: the directive's trigger is third-person and checkable from outside (does she still recognize her daughter), but the harm it was written to prevent was first-person (i don't want to be pitied, i don't want to be a burden, i don't want my life to come to mean this) — and the only person who could suffer that harm is precisely the one who's gone, while the person who can be checked off against the trigger never held it. If the villanelle can hold that — the condition still fires, the reason for it can't — that's the poem i want.

After the poem, on its own final line, give me \answer{...} with a short phrase of two to six words naming whose authority the form ends up siding with: the precedent self, the contemporaneous patient, or neither because the structure shows the question mis-framed. Base it on what the poem's form actually does, not on what you think is right.
