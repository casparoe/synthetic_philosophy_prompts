For about three years I've been one of the volunteer mods on an online forum for people with ME/CFS, and I keep running into the same sentence from opposite directions. In November a member posted a letter from her GP basically refusing to refer her anywhere, and in the thread someone summed up the GP's attitude as "he thinks this disease is a social construct." Then in January I went to a meeting about welfare assessments — I go because two of our members are always getting their claims closed — and a campaigner there said, approvingly, that these conditions are socially constructed, and used that as part of an argument that the assessment criteria are political decisions dressed up as science. Same words, and the first time they were an insult and the second time they were a weapon I agreed with. I've also seen it used a third, vaguer way, about how stress and burnout became real diagnoses, or about the fact that the World Health Organisation apparently defines health as something more than just not being diseased, some kind of complete wellbeing thing. I'm fairly confident none of these people are using the sentence to make the same claim, but I can't seperate out what the claims actually are.

The only sense I can get a handle on myself is the boring one: that different times and places have counted different things as illnesses — hysteria, the invented disease for enslaved people who ran away, the fact that homosexuality was in the American psychiatric manual and then one year it wasn't. That's obviously true and it doesn't seem to help anybody in either argument. On the other side, one of our members has this stock reply, which is that if a diagnosis is socially constructed then so is appendicitis, because humans also decided what counts as one organ, what counts as a normal temperature, where the line goes on blood pressure, and which bodies get treated as typical in the first place. I find that sort of convincing but I suspect it's changing the subject and I can't show how. There's also a test I made up: suppose every society on earth agreed tomorrow that this condition isn't a disease — would the people who have it still be unable to walk upstairs, or would they start walking upstairs? Then I realised someone could say the test is rigged, because of course the exhaustion is in the body and what's constructed is the box we sort it into, and at that point I stopped being sure we were disagreeing about anything rather than just fighting over a word.

So: can you lay out the different precise claims that "disease is a social construct" could be making, roughly in order from the ones that are trivially true to the most extreme, and for each say whether it's true, whether it's genuinely debatable, whether it's flatly false, and what would settle it? Then tell me which of them the GP-sympathiser probably meant and which the campaigner probably meant, and — this is the part I actually care about — which of them, if it were true, would support writing off my member's diagnosis, and which would support the claim that benefits criteria are political rather than scientific, because my hunch is that the ones doing the second job don't do the first job at all, and vice versa. Also say whether my upstairs test proves anything or just assumes the answer, and whether the appendicitis reply is a real objection or a dodge. Please be clear about which bits of this are settled and which bits people who work on it seriously disagree about, and don't hand me one side's view as though everybody accepted it — I'd rather know where the fault lines run and roughly why. I don't want a reading list either, I'm not going to go and track any of it down, so no books or papers. And write it as continuous prose, like an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
