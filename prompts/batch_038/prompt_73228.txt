I moderate a baseball stats forum and somebody asked me a question I couldnt answer, and it turned into a thing I keep chewing on.

Here's the practice. The win goes to whichever pitcher was on the mound when his team took the lead for good — a starter has to have gone five innings, and if he hasn't, the official scorer just hands it to whichever reliever he judges was the most effective. The loss doesn't work like that at all: if a pitcher leaves with the go-ahead run on base and that runner later scores, the loss is charged to him even though he was sitting in the dugout when it happened. And then there's ERA, where a pitcher is charged only with earned runs — to decide which runs are earned, the scorer reconstructs the whole inning as it would have gone with no errors, so the guy only pays for what his own pitching caused.

So what theory of credit does the win actually presuppose? On its face it says the outcome is part of what you deserve credit for, but maybe it's really just a crude stand-in for contribution, invented in an era that couldn't measure anything better — and how would you tell those two apart, given that both produce exactly the same stat? Is the win consistent with the earned-run rule sitting in the same box score, or with the way the loss gets assigned, or does one line of a pitching record end up being computed from two incompatible ideas about responsibility? And while you're at it: what does it take for a scorer to be able to reconstruct an inning that never happened and call that the fair one?

Finally, if a league ditched the win entirely for stats that strip out everything a pitcher doesn't control, would it lose something the win was tracking, or nothing? American English please.
