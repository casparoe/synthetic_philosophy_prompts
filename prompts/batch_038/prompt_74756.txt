So I built an argument for my logic reading group and it came out way more destructive than the point I was trying to make, and now I honestly can't tell whether it's sound-but-uncomfortable or subtly broken. Context: we'd just done downward Löwenheim-Skolem and one guy claimed the continuum hypothesis isn't even the kind of thing that could be true or false. I tried to write out why he'd be right, as a chain:

1. If first-order ZFC is consistent, it has a countable model M.
2. M has only countably many memebers, so every set in M is countable — including whatever M takes to be the reals.
3. ZFC proves that some set has no bijection onto ω, so M contains an x with M ⊨ "x is uncountable."
4. By 2, x is countable. So inside M, "uncountable" can only come to: there's no bijection onto ω that M itself contains.
5. And nothing about x considered on its own, apart from M, could make it genuinely uncountable rather than just uncountable-by-M's-lights. The whole difference is which bijections happen to be lying around in the model.
6. The same applies to us. Our claim that the reals are uncountable is made inside some background theory T. If T is consistent it has a countable model N in which our very own x is countable, and N satisfies every theorem of T. So there's nothing we could point to in T that rules N out as the intended interpretation — meaning our "really uncountable" was doing no more work than M's was.
7. Therefore there are no uncountable sets full stop, "uncountable" is model-relative, and CH has no determinate truth value: accepting or rejecting it is just choosing which countable structure to inhabit.
8. And nothing specifically set-theoretic about the move. First-order PA has nonstandard models, and there are sentences — Con(PA) is the obvious one — that hold in the standard model and fail in some nonstandard ones. Since nothing in the first-order language singles out the standard model, arithmetic truth is model-relative in exactly the same way.
9. Generalizing: for any first-order theory, the intended structure isn't pinned down, so our terms have no determinate extensions and there's no fact of the matter what our mathematical claims are about.

I don't believe 9 and I don't believe 7, but every step looked fine to me going down the page. So: where does it break — and I mean the earliest step, not the last one. And why did that step look fine, when it wasn't? Or when it was, if that's your verdict.
