ok so I've been in the same online community for like twelve years now — started in a rationalist blog's comment section, migrated to a subreddit, now it's mostly a discord — and there is one argument that runs every single month and has never once produced anybody updating. Let's call it the good-faith argument.

One side, who'd happily call themselves steelmaners, says: you basically can't read motives off a post, default to good faith, bad-faith accusations are unfalsifiable and function as a way to win arguments by excommunicating the person instead of answering them, and echo chambers are built precisely out of people who started motive-checking instead of content-checking. The other side — idk what they call themselves, maybe "gatekeepers" sounds too villainous — says bad faith is absolutely detectable, there are reliable tells: motte-bailey switching, citing studies only when the topic touches their in-group, vanishing when refuted and coming back a month later with the same claim reworded, procedural objections deployed asymmetrically. And they say treating everyone as good-faith by default is how a discourse gets captured by a handful of people who've noticed that endless demands for rigor are free.

Both sides have anecdotes. Both have been burned. Neither has ever produced a single case where someone said "huh, you're right about that."

Here's my working theory, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion: they aren't disagreeing about whether bad faith exists, they both agree it's common and they both agree people are usually blind to their own motivated reasoning. They're disagreeing about which error is catastrophic. The steelmaners think a false accusation is the fatal one — it destroys the community's ability to update at all, and once you can be exiled for tone you stop engaging. The motive-checkers think false acceptance is the fatal one — it lets a small organized group define the terms of every conversation while the good-faith people politely consider each objection on its merits. If that's right, every piece of evidence gets weighted differently on each side and the whole twelve-year stalemate is downstream of a value commitment nobody has ever stated out loud in the thread, which is why stating it once would feel like a huge deal and also change nothing.

So: is that the right diagnosis? Or is it something else. Maybe they're answering two different questions under one heading — one about an individual reader's epistemic hygiene, one about community-level moderation policy — and the collision is an artifact. Or maybe there's a hidden empirical question doing the work, something like how detectable bad faith actually is in practice, what the base rates on those "tells" are, whether a motivated reasoner's posts look different from a confused person's posts to a blind rater.

I want the diagnosis, argued from what each side actually says, not a survey of both positions like I'm new here. And then, given whatever you land on, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves it, something you could actually measure in a discord of a few hundred people, a change of question. Or, if my loss-function story is basically right, say plainly that it isn't a factual dispute and no quantity of evidence will move it and the realistic ceiling is some negotiated norm — and what that negotiation would even look like.

Small thing but relevant: I lean steelmaner, I've said so in-thread more than once, so if you think my hypothesis is a motivated rationalization in favor of my own priors, that's a live possibility you should take seriously rather than tiptoe around.
