Third-year epistemology PhD here, work-in-progress group in ten days and the framing of my chapter has gone wobbly — could use someone to think out loud with. No references please, I've got a stack of them and I don't want a lit review, I want the conceptual geography.

The map I've been working with. On one side, pragmatic encroachment: whether you know can depend on what's at stake for you, so the same evidence yields knowledge in the low-stakes version of a case and not in the high-stakes one. On the other, epistemic contextualism: 'knows' is context-sensitive, so nothing about the subject's epistemic position changes at all and its the ascriber's standards doing the work. And the standard complaints against each, which I take to be roughly: encroachment makes knowledge turn on something non-mental, which sits badly with knowledge being a mental state, and it makes knowledge unstable in the sense that you gain and lose it as the train timetable or the interest rate shifts; contextualism gets accused of changing the subject from knowledge to a word, and of mispredicting the data about disagreement, retraction and eavesdroppers.

Now the thing I'm actually defending, which I think falls between the two. Call it assertoric encroachment. Knowledge is fixed entirely by evidence, and 'knows' isn't context-sensitive either. What's stakes-sensitive is flat-out assertion: how good an epistemic position you need in order to properly assert p depends partly on what hangs on getting p right in the situation the assertion lands in. So in the high-stakes case you do know, the low-standards context doesn't retroactively make you not know, but saying it out loud is improper. My running case: a junior doctor who read the lab results herself, first hand, so no hearsay worries — her own stakes are basically nil (salaried, no liability, it isn't her call what happens next) but the family she's about to speak to has enormous stakes in the prognosis being right.

Three things I want, and compare the two established views in whatever order makes this easiest to see:

1. Once you strip each framework down to which item is doing the stakes-work and which item is doing the context-work, is my view a species of encroachment with 'knows' swapped for 'asserts', a species of contextualism in a different costume, or genuinely a third thing? Be explicit about what a framework has to do to count as either.

2. Walk the objections through. Which of them transfer to me, which get deflected by the fact that I never touch 'knows', and which get worse. I know one gets worse and I want to see whether you land on it: if assertion is governed by a knowledge norm, a stakes-sensitive propriety condition on assertion looks like it just is encroachment by the back door. So tell me what my norm of assertion has to be instead, and whether giving up the knowledge norm costs me whatever made the view attractive in the first place.

3. The doctor case is meant to force the question of whose stakes count — hers, the family's, the institutional setting's, the conversational context's. I suspect that fork is the same fork that generates the split between indexical and non-indexical treatments of 'knows', so picking a horn may commit me to a parallel view about assertion. Spell out the horns, what each predicts for the doctor, and which one a view like mine can least afford.

If any of this is straightforwardly incoherent, say so early rather than humoring me.
