I sit on the fundraising committee of a small community trust up here in West Yorkshire — eight of us, mostly retired, and I'm the one who takes the minutes. We ran a crowd-funded appeal for a new minibus and about 4,000 people gave, at every whole-pound amount between £1 and £4,000 (the platform let people type in any figure they liked). Our funding policy, written years ago by a trustee who's since died, says that "principal benefactors" get their names on the brass plaque in the hall, and nowhere in it is there a number. So on Tuesday we had to pick one, and two of the others — Gareth, a retired actuary who's been reading Williamson with a pencil, and Sadia, who did a philosophy degree thirty years ago and never quite got over it — spent forty minutes arguing about something considerably more abstract than the plaque whilst I wrote it all down. Neither would give an inch, and when they asked me to referee I ducked it. Could you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right about what? As near as I can render it:

(1) Gareth opened by saying there's already a fact of the matter. Somewhere in that list there is a last donor who isn't a principal benefactor and a first one who is. We can't find it, but that's our limitation, not the world's — it's like the number of people at the carol sing: nobody counted, but there was a definite number, and saying otherwise is just giving up on truth altogether.

(2) Sadia said the analogy was the whole mistake. The carol sing number is determinate because "person" and "was there" have sharp boundaries; "principal benefactor" is a phrase we invented for sorting donations, and we invented it without drawing any lines. So the right description isn't "there's a line we can't see" but "no line was ever drawn". For a stretch of amounts — she guessed anything from maybe £300 up to maybe £3,000 — the phrase simply doesn't decide, and it's not that we're ignorant of how it decides.

(3) Gareth: then tell me what you make of the sentence "there is some whole-pound figure which is the cut-off". It's either true or it isn't. If you say it's neither, you've chucked excluded middle for an ordinary existential, and you're on your way to saying "either £1,200 counts or it doesn't" isn't true, which is absurd. If you say it's true, you've admitted a sharp line and your quarrel with me is only that you can't locate it — my view with extra steps.

(4) Sadia said that was a false dilemma and she'd take the second horn gladly, because she keeps classical logic: every tautology and every valid inference, "either £1,200 counts or it doesn't" included, comes out true on her account. Her trick is that a sentence is true if it comes out true on every admissible way of making the vague phrase precise. Since each such precisification puts the cut-off somewhere, "there is a cut-off" is true on all of them and so true, full stop. But for any particular n, some precisifications put the cut-off at n and others don't, so "the cut-off is £1,200" is neither true nor false. True existential, no locatable line — which she thinks is exactly what the phenomenon calls for.

(5) Gareth said that's the worst of both worlds and it's rotten from the inside. Take the disjunction: the cut-off is £1, or it's £2, or ... or it's £4,000. By her own rules that's true. Yet not one disjunct is true. So she's got a true finite disjunction with no true disjunct, which means truth has stopped being built up out of the parts of sentences, and she hasn't kept classical logic at all, only its tautologies. He added that if it's true that there's a cut-off, there had better be a fact about which precisifications are admissible; and whatever settles that either singles one of them out, in which case she's an epistemicist, or is itself vague, in which case she needs precisifications of precisifications all the way up and never actually tells anybody what's true.

(6) Sadia said she accepts the disjunction point cheerfully — it's the price of the view and worth paying, because the alternative has a hole Gareth keeps walking past. Even if she granted him that nobody could ever know where the line is, that would be an argument about knowledge, not about truth. He needs seperately that every sentence here is either true or false, and his only support for that is that classical logic says so, which is precisely what's in dispute. And he owes an account of why the line is where it is. If the answer is usage, then usage is a matter of how we're disposed to apply the phrase, and our dispositions don't single out one figure out of several thousand; so either the cut-off is a naked brute fact with no explanation, in which case "a line we can't see" buys him nothing over "no line", or it's fixed by usage, in which case it isn't fixed.

(7) Gareth said brute beats incoherent, and that the tu quoque runs the other way: what makes a precisification admissible, if not usage? If usage can fix a whole set of acceptable cut-offs but no member of it, that's a distinction without an explanation, and he'd rather have one unexplainable fact than an unexplainable plurality.

The practical upshot, in case it matters. They compromised at £1,500, and Gareth then offered Sadia £200 out of his own pocket that the trust had got it wrong — that either somebody on the plaque isn't a principal benefactor or somebody left off is. She wouldn't take it, saying that on her view the sentence he'd bet on isn't false but isn't true either, so there's nothing to bet on in either direction. Gareth said a disagreement that shows up as a refusal to bet can't be merely verbal, and that his view at least makes the plaque's correctness a matter of luck, which a committee ought to care about, whereas hers makes it impossible to get anything wrong. Sadia said the exact opposite: on his view it's certain they've got at least one name wrong and there is nothing whatever they can do about it, so the only sane policy is to rewrite the rule as a bare number next year; on hers they can simply decide, and a decision can't be mistaken.

So please: rate each of the seven moves, and the bet business at the end, on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two justifying each rating; say who's right about what and who's more right overall; say whether the £200 bet really does distinguish the two positions or whether it only reflects two different norms about betting on indeterminate sentences; and tell me what both of them are missing. I've since found out these views have names, epistemicism and supervaluationism, and I suspect there are options neither of them mentioned that would have dissolved the row in about five minutes. Don't worry about the plaque, I mainly want the arguements graded.
