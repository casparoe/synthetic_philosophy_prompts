Okay so Condorcet gets dragged into a modern seminar where someone presents the information-cascade result: agents decide in sequence, each gets one private signal about which of two options is right, each sees the choices made before them but not the signals behind them, and once the first two or three go the same way everybody downstream rationally ignores their own signal and copies. Bayes-clean all the way through, and the group can lock onto the wrong answer and stay locked, with extra members adding nothing to accuracy.

The first thing he'll say is that his jury theorem assumes independence, so case closed. But I want you to push past that. Is the cascade result actually a blow to anything Condorcet held, or does it just describe a situation his theorem never claimed to cover -- and what would he answer to someone wielding it as proof that crowds are dumb and the theorem was always a fairy tale?

Then the follow-up result: if signals can be arbitrarily strong, so that occasionally somebody simply knows, wrong cascades always get broken eventually and the crowd does converge on the truth. Given how much of his life went into public instruction, would he take that as the real fix for herding rather than any redesign of how assemblies vote? And where would you expect him to be wrong about that?
