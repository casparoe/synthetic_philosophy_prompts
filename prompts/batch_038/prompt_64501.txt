I'm reading Pascal and I keep coming back to the bit where he says justice without force is powerless, and that since we can't make what's just strong, we make what's strong just. Our property law looks like a working example and it's bugging me. Someone who occupies land that isn't theirs — openly, continuously, without the owner's permission — becomes the owner after ten or twenty years, and it happens all at once: at nineteen years and eleven months they're a trespasser the true owner can throw out whenever they get around to it, and the next morning they hold the title. Meanwhile the same system never lets the clock run out on a murder, voids contracts signed under duress on the grounds that pressure can't create obligation, and elsewhere insists nobody should profit from their own wrongdoing.

What does the limitation rule actually presupose about justice? Is it making Pascal's move — enough uninterrupted possession ripening into a right — or is there a non-Pascalian theory on which it's a claim about what ownership is, something like: to own is to have the standing to exclude, and standing nobody exercised for twenty years was never really yours? Which rival accounts does the practice rule out, rather than merely sit uneasily alongside? And how much of it is underdetermined — quieting stale claims, witnesses dying, rewarding productive use all seem to license the same rule, so tell me what the rule would look like if each of those were doing the work instead, and whether there'd be any observable difference. Finally: suppose a jurisdiction decided the Pascalian reading was the honest one and wanted to stop endorsing it. What would have to change in the rules, and would there be anything left of property law at the end?
