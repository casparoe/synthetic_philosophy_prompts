I'm a clinical psychologist rather than a philosopher, and this term I've been auditing a graduate seminar on skepticism because I wanted to see whether the hinge-epistemology literature could carry some weight in a paper I'm writing about fixed false belief. The seminar ran through the late Wittgenstein's notes collected as On Certainty and then through the contemporary debate they generated: Wright's entitlement account, Pritchard's non-epistemic reading, Coliva's epistemic reading, and the relativism worry all three are in part reactions to. What I took away was the picture on which certain propositions — that the world has existed for a long time, that I have two hands, that my name is what everyone says it is — are not known or justified but held fast, functioning the way a hinge does: the door turns on them and they do not turn with it. Doubt itself presupposes something not in doubt. I wanted to use that picture to explain why a patient's framework can be internally impeccable and yet not on a par with mine, and a classmate and I built the following argument to check whether the picture can bear that weight. It came out proving considerably more than either of us wanted.

(1) Whether an empirical belief is rational turns on whether the reasons available to the believer support it, and reasons bear on beliefs only from within a framework of hinge commitments: hinges are what make reasons reasons. (2) Hinge commitments are not themselves supported by reasons; if they were, they would be ordinary empirical beliefs, and the regress of reasons — the very thing the hinge view is invoked to stop — would start again one level up. (3) Nor can a framework be assessed from outside itself, since any assessment of one hinge system must be conducted from within another, hence by standards the first need not accept. (4) So there is no framework-neutral fact about which hinge systems are rational; call this hinge relativism. (5) Consider a patient of mine, a woman in her sixties who insists that the man living in her house is an identical impostor standing in for her husband, and whose beliefs about everything else — the weather, her medications, the arithmetic of her bank balance — are formed impeccably by any ordinary standard. (6) On the hinge view her impostor commitment functions as a hinge for her: she did not reason her way to it and she would not give it up for a reason, and her remaining beliefs are rational relative to it just as mine are relative to mine. (7) By (4), there is no framework-neutral fact making my system the rational one, so it is not the case that my hinge system is more rational than hers. (8) But the hinge view was introduced to secure the rational standing of ordinary empirical belief against the skeptic, and if it cannot rank frameworks it has secured nothing: it has relocated the problem from beliefs to frameworks. (9) The relocation is no rescue. The brain-in-a-vat challenge is itself an assault on a framework, and (2) with (3) entail that it cannot be met with reasons; so the view concedes that the radical doubt cannot be answered while denying, on pain of (4), that anything at all can be rationally preferred to it. (10) Either hinge commitments are rationally evaluable, in which case (2) is false and the view collapses back into the evidentialism it was meant to replace, regress and all; or they are not, in which case (4) and (7) hold and there is no rational preference between frameworks. Either way hinge epistemology fails to answer skepticism — it either reverts to the problem or concedes it. My classmate's objection is that (4) generalizes further than the argument admits: by the same reasoning there would be no framework-neutral fact about which rules of inference are rational, in which case the argument itself has no claim on anyone's assent. I think that is a symptom rather than a refutation, but I can't show it.

Tell me where the argument goes wrong. Specifically: identify the step or steps that fail and say what kind of failure it is — an equivocation, and if so between which two readings of which expression; an inference that silently requires a premise I never stated; or a false description of the case at (5). Then explain why the step looked acceptable: give me the reading on which it is true, and say what the argument needs from it that the true reading does not supply. Then price the alternatives — for each of Wright's entitlement account, Pritchard's arational reading, and Coliva's epistemic reading, say what that view must claim about the failing step, and what it costs the view to say so, including whether any of them escapes the dilemma at (10) by a route other than a third horn I have not noticed. Two narrower questions I would also like answered. First, does the delusion case belong in the argument at all? I am not asking about the psychiatry, and you may stipulate whatever you like about whether her state is a belief; what I want to know is whether her impostor commitment satisfies the conditions the hinge view places on a hinge, and if it does not, exactly which condition it fails and whether that condition is available to all three readings or only to some — because if only to some, that may be the real dividing line I was looking for. Second, is my classmate right that (4) extends to rules of inference, and if it does, does that refute the argument or merely make it dialectically useless, and to whom? If I have to choose among the steps, I would most like to keep (1) and (5) through (6), since the locality intuition and the clinical case are what I need for the paper; I would happily sacrifice (3), which I never trusted, but I cannot see how to reach (4) without it. Please argue from the structure of the argument rather than from the literature, and do not send me a bibliography.
