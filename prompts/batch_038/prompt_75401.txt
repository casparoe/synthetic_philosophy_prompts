I have no academic training in philosophy. I left school at seventeen, worked thirty years as a county surveyor, and for the last four have sat on the historical commission of the county where I was born. Two years ago I bought a battered paperback of the second of Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, the one on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, because the title described the meeting I attended every month. His three ways of holding on to a past — monumental, antiquarian, critical — gave me names for positions people on this commission have held for years without being able to state them. I want to test those names against one record that I know well, and then use them on a case we have to decide in six weeks.

The chair has held her seat for thirty-one years. Here is what she has actually ruled, and what she said when she ruled it.

1. When the county proposed demolishing the 1870s courthouse for a parking structure, she fought it and won — although she told me afterward, privately, that the building was ugly and that every record inside it had already been scanned.
2. When the chamber of commerce offered to pay for a bronze statue of Josiah Pell, the county's founder, she declined the gift and had the commission install, in the same spot, a panel reproducing the foreclosure dockets showing how Pell acquired his land during the drought of 1893.
3. She has funded a pageant about the 1851 wagon company for a decade running, at the expense of our conservation budget, on the grounds that children need to watch people their own age decide to attempt something impossible.
4. A family asked her to strike their ancestor's name from the cemetery roll because he had deserted and the story was still causing trouble among his grandchildren. She refused: the roll belongs to the dead, not to the embarrassment of the living.
5. She backed the renaming of a road that carried the name of a probate judge who had enforced the county's old segregation ordinances, and she insisted that the ceremony say out loud what he had enforced.
6. She blocked the sale of roughly nine hundred farm ledgers from the 1870s and 1880s that no researcher had requested in forty years, saying that nobody knows in advance what a future question will need.

Now the case in front of us. In 1974 the descendants of Dr. Etta Brandt donated her papers under a sealed restriction that expired this January. The county hospital has carried her name since 1926, and the statue on the green shows her carrying a child out of the influenza ward. The diary confirms all of that: she worked through the epidemic for eleven weeks without pay and died of it. In the same hand it also records that in 1914 she turned away the families of striking miners in the northern valleys, that she wrote about the immigrant households she treated in language the commission could not print in a school textbook, and that she kept a private tally of families she judged unfit, which she forwarded to a state survey. Meanwhile the hospital district is voting on closing three rural clinics — the ones serving those same northern valleys — and both sides of that vote have already invoked her. The closure's opponents point at the statue. Their opponents point at the diary.

Three decisions are on the agenda: whether the diary enters the public catalog complete and unredacted; whether the hospital keeps her name and the statue stays on the green; and whether the pageant and the fourth-grade unit that currently use only the influenza story are revised to take in 1914.

What I want from you:

- Set out the candidate rules her six rulings admit. Treat the three modes as candidates rather than as the answer, and say whether any single one of them survives all six verdicts intact. My own reading is that none does, but I cannot tell whether that means she is inconsistent or that she is running some composite rule, and if so, what its switching condition is — what makes her a preserver in one month and a prosecutor the next. Say whether the criterion in the essay itself, that history is legitimate only insofar as it serves life, supplies that switching condition, or whether she needs something the essay does not give her.
- One of us argues that ruling 2 shows she is a critical historian. Another argues that a monumental historian would have done exactly the same, on the theory that monumental history takes the deed and abstracts from the motive, so that unmasking Pell's motives is no objection to honoring what he built. Which of them has the essay right, and does it change anything about the Brandt case?
- For each candidate rule, state its verdict on each of the three Brandt decisions separately. Where the rules agree, say so; the disagreements are what I need.
- Give me your single best prediction of how she will vote on all three, with the reasons you would put in front of the commission.
- Identify which of the three decisions her record genuinely leaves open, and say what principle she would be committing the county to by voting each way on it.
- Design the case we should put in front of her next year that would separate the composite reading from the best single-mode reading as sharply as possible, and explain what each reading predicts about it.

Please write the analysis in your own words and explain any term you take from the essay; I know the three labels and very little of the argument behind them. I am not looking for sources or further reading — I am looking for whether the vocabulary I picked up actually fits the woman I have been arguing with for four years.
