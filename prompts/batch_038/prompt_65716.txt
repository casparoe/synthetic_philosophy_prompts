I am preparing a paper on personal identity and have been trying, before writing a word of it, to establish what I actually believe about selves by attending to my own verdicts case by case. My working picture is Dennett's: a self is a centre of narrative gravity, an abstractum posited to organise, explain and predict the doings of a human organism, rather than any further ingredient within it. When I test that picture against cases, however, my verdicts do not obviously cohere, and I should like a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, that does the work.

Here are the cases, and what I find myself saying about each.

1. A colleague in the late stages of dementia. She no longer recognises anyone, cannot recount a single episode of her own history, and has lost every project she once cared about. I say she is nevertheless the same person, and that it would be a distortion to describe her as somebody new.

2. A man rendered globally amnesic by an accident, whose character is untouched. He remembers nothing antecedent, yet his humour, his tempers, his tastes and his style of reasoning all persist. I say: same self.

3. A convert, political or religious, who at forty acquires an entirely new set of values, friendships, ambitions and aversions, repudiating the old ones as so much error, while retaining every memory and narrating his life as a single continuous story of error and rescue. I say: same self again. Cases 2 and 3 together appear to disqualify both memory and character as the criterion, and I do not know what remains.

4. A memoirist whose book is largely invented: the childhood she describes never happened, the mentor she reveres never existed, and yet the fabricated account becomes the story by which she, her family and her readers organise her life thereafter. I say she has in an important sense acquired a new self, and that the narrative is doing constitutive work even though it is answerable to nothing.

5. An inarticulate life. A woman who has never framed a life story, keeps no journal, has no answer to the question how she came to be where she is, and would fail any narrative interview one cared to devise. I say she has a self no less than a celebrated autobiographer has. Cases 4 and 5 are the pair I cannot reconcile, and I suspect one of them must go.

6. If an exact physical and psychological duplicate of me were produced this morning while I remained intact, I say he is not me; yet I also find myself saying that had I died in the night, I should say he was. I notice with some discomfort that this makes my verdict depend on something that happens only to him.

7. Two successors after a fission procedure, each narrating my life from the inside with equal conviction and equal apparent memory. I can say neither is me, and I cannot say that I have died. This has the same shape as 6, which troubles me further.

8. My grandmother, dead these forty years: I hold that there is a fact of the matter about what she would have made of various present developments, and that some such claims are true and others false. Of a minor character in a novel I hold that the corresponding claims are neither true nor false but merely licensed or forbidden by the text. On my own account both are centres of narrative gravity, so I owe an explanation of the difference.

9. I say that a self is nothing I could ever encounter, point to, or weigh, and yet I refuse to dismiss it as a façon de parler, since self-ascriptions explain and predict in a way that no mere list of dispositions seems to. That is, I take it, precisely what makes a centre of gravity a real feature of the world rather than a fiction — but I am not sure I have earned the right to say so.

My own tentative formulation is that there is a self wherever there is a narrative that is broadly true of a living organism, broadly unified, and doing explanatory work; I suspect each clause fails somewhere in the list above, and I want to be told where.

So: is there a principle, stated precisely and if need be with several clauses, each tied to the case that motivates it, which delivers all or most of these verdicts? For each candidate principle you offer, please tell me what it says about cases I have not raised — supply two or three of your own and say which of my intuitions each is designed to probe; tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would require me to abandon; and tell me whether the price of saving all nine is a complexity that a good principle ought not to have to pay. If you conclude that 4 and 5 cannot both be kept, say which goes, and what the loss costs me elsewhere in the list. Please write your answer in British English.
