**Superrationality is not rationality plus common knowledge. It's a substitute for it.**

Most people who read a lot of this stuff learned two things in the same couple of years: Hofstadter's superrationality, and the common-knowledge machinery from epistemic game theory. They get filed together in the head as "the material about agents who know things about each other's knowing." I think that filing is a mistake, and the mistake has practical consequences.

Start with the recap. Superrational reasoning in a symmetric game says: the other player is running the same kind of reasoning I am, so we'll land on the same answer; therefore don't maximize over all four cells of the matrix independently, maximize over the diagonal. In a standard prisoner's dilemma that gets you cooperation instead of mutual defection. In the Platonia setup — trillionaire, twenty people, exactly one telegram gets a billion, no communication allowed — it gets you sending the telegram with probability one in twenty. Notice what's missing from that description. Nobody believes anything about anybody's beliefs. The whole thing runs off one assumption about a mechanism, not about a mind modeling a mind.

This is where I want to make a distinction I don't think is usually made. Call them:

**Copy-superrationality.** The symmetry is a fact about implementations. Either you literally are the same program running twice, or you're both instances of "what a careful person does in this situation," and you know which because you know what the procedure is. Cost: one first-order belief.

**Guess-superrationality.** The symmetry is a fact about a social category. He's a smart, honest guy reading the same column I am, so he'll get the same answer. Cost: an entire belief hierarchy, because now I need him to be superrational, and I need him to think I am, and so on up.

Hofstadter's own presentation slides between the two, and I think most of the heat in arguments about superrationality is people defending one while attacking the other. But once you see them as separate, you can price them.

Here's my proposal for how to price them. For any game, there's a fixed budget of mutual information about each other's decision procedures that the players need before they can safely coordinate off the diagonal. Nash reasoning spends that budget on hierarchy — and hierarchy is expensive, because every level is a separate thing that has to be gotten right, and there are unboundedly many levels. Copy-superrationality spends it on a single bit at level zero: we are the same algorithm. So the two are substitutes, and the substitution rate is not favorable to the hierarchy side. Call it the transparency budget. The more you can buy at level zero, the fewer levels you need to survive.

Guess-superrationality then should be the fragile one, and you can see how fragile pretty quickly. Let ε be the probability your counterpart isn't running your algorithm — he's a plain maximizer, or a different sort of thing entirely. With payoffs T > R > P > S, cooperating beats defecting exactly when (1−ε)R + εS > (1−ε)P + εT, which rearranges to ε < (R−P) / ((R−P) + (T−S)). On the usual dollar version of the dilemma that's 99/249, so a shade under 0.4. You can be wrong about your partner's type nearly forty percent of the time and diagonal reasoning still pays. That's graceful degradation. It doesn't look like a cliff at all.

Except that there are cliffs in this literature. Rubinstein's email game: two players have to coordinate, only one of them learns which coordination game they're in, and the message confirming it might get lost with small probability. You can have an arbitrarily long but finite chain of "I know that he knows that I know," and the equilibrium still jumps back to the safe one. Common knowledge doesn't get approximated, it gets had or not had. That's the shape of a discontinuity, and guess-superrationality — being made of hierarchy all the way down — looks like it should inherit it rather than the smooth threshold from the last paragraph.

So which is it? I think the answer is that the two ε's aren't the same kind of quantity. In the threshold calculation ε is uncertainty about *type*: a first-order matter, and it degrades smoothly. In the email construction the uncertainty is about the *state* — which game is being played — and what has to be infinitely deep is whether he knows that I know that. Those are different failure modes, and my threshold formula only measures the first one. Which means the honest conclusion is that the formula is measuring something other than the thing I care about. The real threat to guess-superrationality isn't that your partner is the wrong sort of agent. It's that your partner is the right sort of agent and isn't sure that you know that he is.

That's also, I'd argue, the correct way to read Aumann's result about rational agents with a common prior being unable to agree to disagree. It's normally filed as a curiosity, or as a negative result about opinion. But it's really a statement that hierarchy can't be faked: if two guess-superrationalists each believe the other will send the telegram, and one of them doesn't, then the hierarchy was never actually there. Superrationality on the guess reading is Aumann's theorem repurposed as a coordination device rather than as a impossibility result about disagreement.

The practical upshot is the part I actually care about. If you want off-diagonal cooperation from a real counterpart, don't invest in building more levels of mutual belief, because that's the fragile direction and it's the direction where one dropped message kills everything. Invest at level zero: become the same algorithm. Publish your decision procedure, be boringly predictable, be copyable. It's also why I think the Twin Prisoner's Dilemma and Parfit's hitchhiker are usually misread as exhortations to hope the other guy is like you. They're not hoping. They're deleting the epistemics and replacing them with a fact about implementations.

Superrationality isn't rationality with more knowledge. It's rationality that got out of the knowledge business.
