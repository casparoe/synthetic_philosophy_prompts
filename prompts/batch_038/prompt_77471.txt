Been sat on this for a fortnight and I can't get out of it. It's a Maxwell's demon argument, except I've used the standard resolution as a premise instead of fighting it, which is why I don't think the usual Bennett-style reply touches it. The conclusion is obviously wrong but I can't find the crack.

(1) Szilard's engine: one molecule in a box, one bit of information about which half it's in, and you can pull kT ln 2 of work out of a single heat bath. So one bit is worth exactly kT ln 2 of work.

(2) Now suppose some measurement could hand you a bit for less than that. Measure, run the engine, collect kT ln 2, repeat, and you've built a perpetual motion machine of the second kind. So no physical process can gain a bit for less than kT ln 2.

(3) Landauer: erasing a bit — resetting a memory to a standard state regardless of what it held — dissipates at least kT ln 2 as heat.

(4) And (3) is where the bill in (2) gets settled: the demon's memory fills up, and to keep running in cycles it has to empty it. All of this I accept, it's just the textbook story.

(5) But nothing in (2)–(4) is special to demons with trapdoors. It applies to anything that ends up correlated with something else in a way it can later exploit: a thermometer, a photographic plate, a retina, a tree ring, a lab notebook, a hard disk. Call any of those a memory.

(6) A memory is finite, so a memory that operates long enough has to overwrite, and every overwrite is an erasure, and pays.

(7) Therefore no physical system ever gains information about the world for free. What it can hold, and how fast it can revise what it holds, are both capped by the heat it can dump.

(8) An observer is a physical system: finite memory, finite capacity to dump heat. So the total number of bits of empirical information an observer can acquire over its whole history — cumulatively, since every revision overwrites something and pays again — is bounded.

(9) Testing the second law means acquiring empirical information about entropy-increasing processes, which by (8) is bounded and, for a finite observer with no infinitely cold sink to lean on, exhaustible.

(10) So the second law entails that no finite observer can go on testing it: past some point the evidence simply runs out. Which means the second law can't be an empirical claim at all — it predicts the impossibility of the observations that would confirm it. And the same route works for anything else tested by piling up observations in finite heads and finite notebooks, so nothing is empirical.

I don't want to give up (1) or (3), those are the only solid planks here, and I'm pretty sure I don't want to give up (10)-being-false either. I keep circling (2) and the hop from (5) to (6) but I cant say what's wrong with either — a thermometer really does end up correlated with what it measures, and a real memory really does get overwritten. So: which step do you reject, why did it look innocent when you first read it, and what does rejecting it cost you? If your answer is that the argument is fine and physics really is capped that way, say so, but then I want to know where the absurdity in (10) is coming from.
