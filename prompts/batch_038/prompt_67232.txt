You are an alignment researcher with a background in analytic philosophy whose specialty is building diagnostic instruments for technical disagreements — the kind of artifact that walks a room full of people who believe they are having one argument through the six arguments they are actually having. I am running the opening session of a workshop on scalable oversight, on the question of whether adversarial debate between two model-provers, judged by a limited human, can carry the weight its original proponents placed on it. Everyone in the room already knows the source material, so please do not summarize the literature, do not supply a bibliography or reading list, and do not label any position with a person's name: name positions descriptively (along the lines of "decomposability optimism" or "equilibrium-selection skepticism"), and paraphrase rather than quoting from any paper, since I cannot check quotations and would rather not have them. What I want is a decision tree whose root is a shared starting point stated once, briefly, as four entry commitments: (i) the judge is bounded in time, attention, and domain expertise; (ii) obfuscated arguments exist, in that an untrustworthy prover can produce an argument that is unsound and that the judge cannot recognize as unsound without assistance, with recognition getting harder as arguments compose; (iii) the complexity-theoretic analogy behind the original proposal is a theorem about optimal play against polynomial-time judges, with debate reaching questions that direct judging cannot, and it says nothing on its own about what reinforcement-learned debaters converge to; and (iv) in the human-debater experiments on hard reading-comprehension questions, judges who had never read the source passage did better under two-prover debate than under a single-expert consultancy baseline, and most residual debate errors were traced to mistakes by the honest debater rather than to successful obfuscation.

From that root, branch on seven to ten questions, each phrased so that a participant could answer it on the spot without any idea which position their answer leads to; no node may contain a leaf's name or paraphrase one. The axes I want represented, in whatever order your tree finds most economical: whether the operative asymmetry in a given domain is the ordinary generation-versus-verification gap, where checking a proposed flaw is far cheaper than finding one, or something stronger, where the judge cannot check the flaw even once it is pointed out; whether claims in the domain decompose into what I have been calling atoms — subclaims the judge can settle unaided at negligible cost — and if so whether that decomposition terminates or recurses indefinitely, so that verification depth is bounded or unbounded in practice; whether the protocol merely needs to move the judge's belief in the right direction on average, or needs to let the judge detect the particular falsehood; whether optimal play is the right solution concept at all, given that trained debaters optimize against a judge whose systematic biases they can learn to exploit; whether the relevant comparison class is debate standing alone or debate as one component of a broader amplification-and-distillation scheme; and whether the judge is a layperson or a domain expert with tools. Please also test a suspicion of mine: I have been treating the view that the judge's priors do the real work — so that debate is a search procedure over what the judge already believes rather than a truth-tracking protocol — as an independent leaf, but it may be a redescription of the equilibrium-selection branch rather than a sibling of it; if the two collapse, say so and fold them, and if they do not, build a node that separates them. Nodes should be answered by appeal to concrete cases rather than to slogans, so attach each of the following to at least one node, and use nothing fancier: a patch case, where a model proposes a change to a large codebase no human on the oversight team has read in full, correct on every test anyone runs, with the worry being a branch reachable only by inputs nobody thought to try; a citation case, where a model reports an intervention safe on the strength of many individually accurate citations and the defect lies in what was omitted, so that no single statement put to the judge is false; a passage case, mirroring the reading-comprehension experiments, where the judge has not read the source and the debaters reveal short quotations selectively; and a proof case, where a formally verified derivation ends in a statement that differs from the claim the judge is being asked to accept in a way visible only to someone reading both statements side by side with expertise. If one of these cases discriminates at no node, tell me which and why rather than forcing it in.

The deliverable is the tree plus three things that are the actual point of the exercise. First, the tree itself, with seven to twelve leaves, each carrying a descriptive name, one sentence stating what a person who lands there is committed to, the single most awkward consequence of that commitment, and the observation or experimental result that would most cheaply move them off it. Second, lay out the logical space before locating positions in it: restate your branch questions as a set of axes, enumerate at least four combinations of answers that the axes permit but that no leaf occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has thought to defend it — and treat the combination "obfuscation is decisive for the domains we care about, yet debate remains the best available oversight mechanism" with particular care, since I suspect it is a live position that only looks contradictory by conflating sufficiency with comparative availability. Third, flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate, saying what further question would separate them or why no such question exists, and then identify the single node doing the most sorting work — the one whose removal would collapse the largest number of otherwise distinct leaves into one. Think the structure through before writing: begin by listing the candidate axes of disagreement, note which of them turn out to be dependent on one another, prune to a set that genuinely partitions the space, and only then render the tree.
