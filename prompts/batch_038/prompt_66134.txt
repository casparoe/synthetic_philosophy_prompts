Our Incident Review Board votes Thursday on the record for a four-hour Sev-1, and it is currently split two–two with one member undecided. I am a staff software engineer at a mid-sized card-payments company and one of the five members; I have no philosophy or formal methods training, and I want the memo I circulate to read like an engineering document, not a seminar paper.

**What the board decides.** Our charter gives it three jobs: decide what goes in the incident record's *primary cause* field; decide which remediation items get designated *cause-derived* (funded, owned, tracked to closure with a named deadline) rather than merely *contributing*; and approve the cause statement that is copied verbatim into the quarterly reliability report our enterprise customers and our compliance auditor read. Two further charter provisions matter here. First, findings must not assign individual blame. Second, the cause statement must be a single conditional sentence that a game-day experiment could in principle falsify. There is also an unwritten practice that the internal and external cause statements are identical, and one of my colleagues wants to break that practice in this case.

**The timeline, as the board has reconstructed it.**

(a) Six days before the outage, a release changed a connection-pool default in the authorization service from 200 to 50, to fix a memory leak. It was reviewed, merged, and rolled to the full fleet over two days with no alarms.

(b) A retry library we depend on has had a misconfigured backoff ceiling for roughly eighteen months and retries far more aggressively than intended. Under the old pool size this was invisible. Under the smaller pool, retries saturated it within about ninety seconds of the first latency blip, and retry traffic itself became the load.

(c) The circuit breaker meant to shed that load did not trip. Seven months ago we migrated our metrics pipeline; the breaker's threshold, configured as an absolute error count, is now evaluated against a percentage, so it has been effectively disabled since the migration. It has no test. Nobody noticed.

(d) The on-call page never fired. A rotation-tool migration three weeks earlier dropped the pager binding for that team, and the alert went to a Slack channel nobody monitors. Discovery came from a support escalation twenty-two minutes in.

(e) A separate bug in one of our client libraries, shipped the same morning as the pool-default change, independently drives connection churn. Our post-incident modeling says that had the pool change never shipped, that bug alone would have produced equivalent saturation about an hour later, during the same traffic peak.

(f) Traffic was roughly 1.6× normal because of a planned marketing campaign, announced through the normal channel. Our capacity-review process does not require sign-off for marketing pushes.

(g) Rolling back the pool default did not restore service. Recovery required manually flushing a downstream queue that retry traffic had filled and that did not drain on its own.

**The split.** Two members — our SRE lead and an engineering director — want the record to name (a). Their position is that (a) is the thing that was different on Tuesday, and that the only question the auditor and the SLA-credit process actually ask is whether we would have had this outage without it. When (e) is raised, they respond that (e) rests on modeling they cannot demonstrate, and that they will not put a counterfactual they cannot show into an audited record. Two members — a reliability architect and I — want the record to name (c). Our position is that (c) is the only defect we know of that is live in production right now, is untested, and would convert any single dependency's capacity loss into an outage, whereas naming a config default that has already been reverted teaches nothing and funds nothing. The fifth member, who is not an engineer, is concerned that the promotion packet of the engineer who shipped (a) cites the memory-leak fix as a highlight, and wants the record to establish clearly that the change was correct and well reviewed.

**The rubric.** The board scores each candidate primary cause from 1 to 5 on three axes: *necessity* (how confidently we can say this outage would not have occurred, or would have been materially shorter, without it); *leverage* (how many other plausible failure scenarios the remediation would prevent); and *actionability* (whether a named team can deliver the remediation within one quarter without new headcount). A primary cause must score at least 4 on necessity **and** at least 4 on leverage. No candidate has ever met both, so the rubric has never actually decided anything and we have always voted. I expect this case is the one where it breaks out loud.

**What I need.** A memo of roughly 900–1,200 words that I can circulate before Thursday, which:

1. Recommends a specific entry for the primary-cause field and designates each of (a)–(g) as primary, cause-derived, contributing, or out of scope, each with an owner type and a deadline.
2. Drafts the required single-sentence cause statement and the game-day experiment that would falsify it, concretely enough that I could hand it to an SRE without further explanation.
3. Applies the rubric to every candidate, showing the scores and where the necessity/leverage conjunction forces a choice the board would be embarrassed to defend — including what a necessity-only test does with (f), with (g), and with (e).
4. States explicitly what it does with (e), and whether the "we cannot demonstrate that counterfactual" objection is dispositive for an audited record. If your recommendation depends on a fact we would have to establish experimentally, say which experiment establishes it and what the record should say in the interim.
5. Takes a position on whether the external-facing statement should match the internal one, and if not, supplies both texts.
6. Makes the strongest honest case for the recommendation you reject, in terms the SRE lead would recognize as her own rather than as a straw man, and states what would have to be true for you to change your mind.
7. Addresses the fifth member's concern without either blaming or exonerating the engineer who shipped (a).

If your conclusion is that the rubric itself needs amending, say so and propose the amendment rather than working around it. Please use no citations, no reading lists, and no named theories or authors; keep the vocabulary to what appears above plus ordinary engineering usage, and do not soften the recommendation into a set of options.
