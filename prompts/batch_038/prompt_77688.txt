You are a philosopher of action and decision theory who has also spent years teaching mixed seminars of philosophy and economics graduate students. Please think carefully before writing: this is a design task as much as an expository one, and I would rather have a well-pruned instrument than a comprehensive one.

**Context.** I run a ninety-minute interdisciplinary reading group on whether irrationality can ever be rational — the family of puzzles that includes Parfit's hitchhiker (the stranded traveller who can be rescued only by a driver willing to believe their promise to pay later, and who, being transparently honest, cannot produce a promise they know they will have no reason to keep) and Kavka's toxin (the eccentric's million, paid for forming tonight the intention to drink a harmless but wretched-making toxin tomorrow afternoon, when you need never actually drink it). My group is half philosophers of action and half economists. The philosophers arrive thinking in terms of reasons and intentions; the economists arrive thinking in terms of preferences, constraints and revealed choice. The handout must sort both, using the same questions.

**Deliverable:** a diagnostic decision tree — six to eight nodes, seven to ten leaves — that a participant can work through on paper in about fifteen minutes, together with a short scoring key. Please organise the answer in the following six parts, in this order.

1. **Design notes, written first.** Brainstorm at least ten distinctions that could plausibly separate positions in this debate: for instance, whether rationality attaches to acts, intentions, dispositions or character traits; whether the case is purely intrapersonal or depends on another agent's predictions; whether that other agent can see through you; whether an intention requires a reason for the intended act; whether the agent's own future conduct is already fixed or genuinely open at the moment of decision; whether a theory treats rationality as tracking outcomes or as responding correctly to reasons; whether a payoff for a state of mind can be had without the corresponding action. Then prune the list to the five or six that genuinely do the work, and for each distinction you discard say in one line why it looks important but is not, or is subsumed by one you kept.

2. **The tree.** Every node must be a concrete case, or a question about a concrete case, phrased so that a participant can answer it without knowing which position their answer leads to: no node may contain a label, a proper name, or a term of art that gives the game away. Every branch leaving a node must be a live option that someone in my group would actually take. The wording of each option must be neutral between the philosophical and the economic vocabularies; where the two pull apart, add a footnote to that node explaining how you managed it. Each leaf carries the name of the position as it is usually known (or 'unnamed' if nobody defends it), one sentence on what that position says about the hitchhiker, one on what it says about the toxin, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

3. **Required leaves.** The following must appear: the view that nothing irrational is ever rational and that these cases are simply misdescribed; the disposition- or rule-based view on which cultivating a disposition can be rational even where exercising it is not; the strategic commitment view familiar from deterrence and bargaining, on which credibility is a resource one rationally spends by making oneself unable or unwilling to behave sensibly; the reasons-first view on which one cannot intend an act one believes one will have no reason to perform; and at least one view that dissolves the puzzle by denying an assumption about knowledge or transparency rather than an assumption about rationality. Do not treat this list as exhaustive — add leaves wherever the logical space demands them.

4. **Two cases of your own.** Invent two diagnostic cases that do not exist in the literature, and place one at each of the two deepest branch points. For each, state which pair of leaves it is designed to separate, which feature of the case does the separating, and how you know the separation is real rather than an artefact of your stipulations. Flag both clearly as invented.

5. **The space, not merely the tree.** Lay out the combinations of answers your nodes permit, mark which are occupied, and for each unoccupied combination say whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. Where it is unexplored, sketch in two sentences what a defender would have to say.

6. **Honest limits.** Name any pair of leaves your instrument cannot really separate, and say whether some further case could separate them or whether the difference is not one into which anybody could be sorted by a questionnaire at all.

**Constraints.** British English throughout. No bibliography, reading list, footnote references, page numbers or verbatim quotations of any kind: describe the standard cases in your own words, and if you are unsure of an attribution, say so rather than inventing one. Aim for roughly 1,600 words, and keep the register of a handout that a sharp non-specialist could use with no lecturer present.
