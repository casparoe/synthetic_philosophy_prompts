Okay so I'm deep in the second draft of a novel and I've hit a wall that's more philosophy then craft, and I suspect you're better equipped than my writing group.

My main character, Dolores, is 58, and she's spent eleven years and essentially all of an inheritance pouring money into something that is, by any ordinary measure, hopeless. My beta readers all say the same thing: she's just doing the sunk cost thing, she's a fool, make her see it and quit. And here's my problem — they're right about the economics, and I don't want to write a book where the interesting character is simply wrong in a way a first-year econ student could diagnose in a paragraph. I want the reverse. I want Dolores to be, in some defensible sense, right, and I want her rightness to survive the obvious objections, because if it doesn't my readers will bounce off the whole second act.

So: build me a decision case, to order, in which honoring a sunk cost is genuinely rational, not merely understandable or moving or tragic. Constraints, and please treat these as hard:

1. Realistic. No sci-fi, no magic, no time travel. Something a person in a small coastal town could actually face — a building, a boat, a piece of land, a family business, whatever you like. One or two paragraphs of scenario, no more.

2. The money is truly sunk. Not recoverable, no resale, no salvage value, no tax loss to harvest, no ordinary investment sense in which it pays off later.

3. Block the standard escapes explicitly, and show me you've blocked them: no reputation or signaling (nobody is watching her resolve, nobody will reward her for finishing), no strategic precommitment or burn-the-bridges move, no new information the eleven years of spending has revealed to her, no option value.

4. Continuing has to cost her something going forward. If it's free or cheap, the case is trivial — I want a hard-nosed accountant to be able to say, correctly, that she is worse off on his ledger.

5. No stipulating the verdict. Don't just tell me she's the kind of person who values finishing things and therefore finishing is rational for her. The rationality has to fall out of the structure of the case, so that a reader who doesn't give a damn about Dolores's feelings can still feel the pull.

Then do the demonstration. Walk me through which account of sunk-cost reasoning each constraint knocks out, and tell me where the rationality actually lives once all of those are gone. My own hunch — please tell me if I'm fooling myself — is that it has to be something about the relation between the money already spent and the thing she would do next: that the past cost is irrecoverable but becomes worth having made if she continues, so that continuing retroactively changes what those eleven years were rather than just adding to them. If that's the mechanism, say so plainly. If it's not really distinct from recoverability or from option value, say that too, because that would be a bad sign for the book.

And last, the part I actually need most: give me the best attack from someone who insists sunk costs are never rationally relevant. I expect the version that says I've smuggled the value into the utility function and relabeled a preference as a reason — so tell me whether that attack lands, and if it does, what's left of Dolores that isn't just a woman with expensive tastes. I'd rather hear it from you now than from a reviewer in print.

One more thing. Whatever you give me, keep it in prose I could actually steal. If it comes out reading like a problem set I'll have to rewrite the whole thing anyway, and my deadline does not care.
