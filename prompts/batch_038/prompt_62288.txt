reading a pop-history-of-science book for a seminar and there's a stretch on Newton's religion that goes roughly like this: 'Newton wrote far more words on theology and alchemy than on mathematics and natural philosophy, and he kept almost none of it in print because he was a heretic -- he rejected the Trinity and had convinced himself, by collating manuscripts, that the key proof-texts had been doctored by later scribes. He also believed God had to keep stepping in to stop the solar system from going out of true, which is exactly what Leibniz laughed at him for in the long exchange with Clarke. His refusal to feign hypotheses captures the whole method: he wouldn't speculate about anything he couldn't measure, so when he couldn't say how gravity gets across a vacuum he just said God does it. On the same principle he treated space as God's own extension, which is why his contemporaries called him a pantheist. In short, the founder of mathematical physics was really a theologian whose physics was a spinoff of his religion.'

Some of this I'd have guessed is just true-but-weird-Newton, and I honestly can't tell which bits are the book overreaching. The two I most want pinned down are the claim about feigning hypotheses standing for his general method, and the space-as-God's-extension line -- including what Newton himself actually said about the cause of gravity and about whether God is space or the world's soul.
