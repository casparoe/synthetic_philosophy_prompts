For eleven years I have compiled and self-published a printed almanac of our county: bridge opening dates, church cornerstone years, school board rosters, township boundary changes, mill closures, flood levels. The current edition carries roughly 4,500 separate factual claims. Each of them I assert without qualification, in the same declarative voice, and the front matter includes a standing preface saying that whatever errors remain are mine and that I would be glad to be corrected. I do not doubt that there are errors. Given the size of the book I would put it close to certain that several entries are wrong, and I have said as much in that same preface — same book, same breath.

I have since learned that this situation has a name, and that a version involving a thousand lottery tickets is its cousin. What I have not seen anyone address is the wrinkle that actually keeps me up, so I want to put it to you precisely.

My entries do not rest on uniform evidence. About 3,000 come from a single county record series I have been cross-checking for a decade and trust very nearly completely. Roughly 1,200 are supported by two independent sources that agree. Some 300 rest on one weak source: clipped newspapers with a documented history of typographical errors, or a single elderly resident's recollection of something from the 1950s. I print all three tiers identically, with no hedging on the weak ones.

So: what is your credence that my practice is epistemically permissible — that I am not being rationally inconsistent in flatly asserting each entry while being confident the set as a whole contains falsehoods? I want a single number between 0 and 1, and I want the derivation, not a tour of the positions people have taken.

Please break the question into the sub-questions your number actually depends on and give a rough probability for each, one or two lines of justification each, and then show how you combine them. At minimum I would want you to address: whether flat-out belief is a real state distinct from very high confidence, such that the question is about anything other than arithmetic; whether, if it is real, it is governed by any requirement against believing things you know cannot all be true; whether a rule of the form "assert only what is above some confidence cut-off" can be principled rather than a line drawn in sand; whether my three tiers genuinely correspond to different probabilities or are mostly self-flattery; and whether the printed preface does any real epistemic work or is just good manners. Then tell me which of these sub-questions my overall verdict is most sensitive to.

Two further things. My co-editor, who handles the index, considers what I do plainly incoherent and wants a one-to-five confidence mark printed against every entry; my own instinct is that the marks would wreck the book and would dress up one man's hunches as though they were measured. I put myself at about 0.75 that the practice is defensible; she would be nearer 0.15. Say where you think that gap most likely lives. And tell me whether any of this is merely epistemic: a genealogist once used one of my dates in a citizenship petition, which is why I am asking about permissibility rather than tidiness — if the stakes of a single entry being wrong change the answer, I want that folded into the number rather than appended to it.

I have no training in this subject, so please define any term of art as you go, but do not soften the number on my account. Above all, close by saying what would change your mind: which argument, or which fact about how the almanac is actually made, would move your figure substantially, and at which step in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be going wrong.
