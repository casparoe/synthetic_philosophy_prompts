i TA a section on early modern political thought and next week we're on Pascal's fragments about justice and force — the ones where he says that since we couldn't make what is just strong, we went and made what is strong just, and that the whole authority of justice rests on custom, so that if you trace a law back to its principle you annihilate it. i want to hand out a poem at the start of section to get them into it, and i've decided it should be a pantoum, because the form does literally what Pascal is describing: lines 2 and 4 of each quatrain come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, so the same two lines keep trading places without ever being joined.

so — a pantoum, five stanzas, twenty lines, quatrains rhyming ABAB, with the recycled lines repeated verbatim (no fudging it by changing a comma, my students will catch that). Four or five beats a line, whichever you choose, but keep it even throughout. Stanza 1's lines 2 and 4 should carry the two halfs of that exchange — couldn't make the just strong, so made the strong just — stated plainly, and then those two lines travel the whole length of the poem in swapped slots. The last stanza has to fold stanza 1's lines 1 and 3 back into its own 2 and 4 positions, so the thing ends on the first stanza's third line.

what i want the middle stanzas to do is change what those repeated lines mean purely by changing what surrounds them, so that by the end a reader can feel the exchange going round and round and never landing. put Pascal's own material in there instead of abstractions: the robe and the ermine, the grave magistrate who commands a whole people's respect without ever saying anything worth hearing, the guards that make a king look like a king, the river that bounds a rather plaisant idea of justice, the couple of degrees of latitude that overturn an entire jurisprudence — truth on this side of the Pyrenees, error on the other. and get in his point about the orders: different duties owed to different merits, fear to force, love to what pleases, assent to proof, with tyranny being the demand for something by a road that can't deliver it, which is exactly why force can manufacture obedience but never justice.

dont name Pascal or the Pensées anywhere in the poem, and don't use the word chiasmus — someone who's read the fragments should recognise them from the images alone. and please don't soften the ending into hope; it should land on the fact that the swap doesn't work and that the only thing that would settle it isn't on offer in politics.
