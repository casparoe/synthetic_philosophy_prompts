I'm a 4th-year philosophy PhD student working on the "strange inversion" literature — the Dennettian line that Darwin and Turing are pulling the same conceptual move, that both of them flip our ordinary top-down picture of design and show you can get the competence without the comprehension. My chapter is going to argue the two inversions are not in fact the same move, and before I write it I want to see the strongest version of the arguement laid out. I'll handle the lit review myself, so please don't give me one, and don't cite anything — I just want the reasoning.

Here's the thesis. It's idiosyncratic, so please adopt my framing rather than arguing me out of it:

Say a process is *generative* of competence if the competence it ends up displaying exceeds, in some sense you should make precise, the comprehension built into its initial specification. Natural selection is generative: the variation-and-selective-retention loop nowhere represents the solution it produces, and the organism-environment fit is manufactured by the loop itself. A universal machine running a program is not generative in that sense: the program is a finite specification, and everything the machine does is unpacked from it. So the Turing "inversion" doesn't establish that comprehension can be absent from the production of intelligent behaviour. It establishes only that comprehension can be compressed and deferred — relocated from the moment of operation to the moment of authorship. What looks like competence without comprehension in a computer is really comprehension without presence: somebody's understanding is running the show from a distance, and the machine is a delivery mechanism for it.

Two replies I want you to genuinely wrestle with rather than wave at:

1. The objection that this proves too much. A genome is also a finite specification, and an organism's traits are also just unpacked from it, so by my own criterion the Darwinian loop isn't generative either and the asymmetry evaporates. I suspect the cases come apart over who produced the specification and whether producing it required already knowing the answer, but that's a hunch, not an argument — turn it into one, and be careful about whether "the environment wrote the genome" smuggles in an author.

2. Turing's own child-machine proposal: rather than writing the finished adult behaviour into the program, you build a small program and then educate it through a process of reward and punishment. That looks like installing a Darwinian loop inside the Turing case, which would collapse my asymmetry on the spot. Either argue that it doesn't collapse, or argue that the asymmetry survives one level up — in the choice of the reward function and the construction of the training environment, which is where all the comprehension would then be hiding.

I don't want a balanced on-the-one-hand piece. Make the case as forcefully as it can honestly be made.
