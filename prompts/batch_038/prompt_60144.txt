I sit on the five-member jury of a small private foundation that awards a single fellowship every two years: three years of full salary and laboratory space for one researcher. The fellowship cannot be divided — the salary is calibrated to buy out one person's teaching load in full, so two half-fellowships would release neither recipient from anything.

This cycle began with 214 applications and has produced two finalists. Under our published rubric — four weighted criteria, each scored 0–10 by every juror and then averaged — the two candidates tie to the second decimal place. Because no tie had ever arisen under this rubric, we re-scored both dossiers blind, with names, institutions, and acknowledgements stripped and the files presented in randomized order. The aggregate tie held exactly; the profiles did not. In the first scoring candidate A led on three of the four criteria, and in the blind re-score candidate B led on three, with the total unchanged on both occasions. Across both scorings A leads on originality and significance, B on rigor and feasibility. The rubric's weights have not been revisited since 2016, and no juror has been able to locate a recorded justification for them.

Three facts constrain us. First, the founding deed of 1968 directs that the fellowship go to the single candidate the jury judges most deserving; it makes no provision for a tie, and our counsel has advised only that resolving one by lot would not be unlawful. Second, after a contested award in 2016 the foundation adopted a policy that scoring must rest on merit alone, expressly listing financial need, career stage, institutional prestige, and prior disappointment in the competition as ineligible considerations. Both dossiers mention, outside the scored material, circumstances that policy would exclude: one candidate is within two years of a compulsory retirement date, the other holds a competing offer. Third, that competing offer expires on the 25th — eleven days after our next meeting — and the fellowship begins on 1 September. The unsuccessful candidate may appeal to the board, and the board requires from us a written statement of the grounds of the decision.

The jury is split four ways. The chair proposes a draw from an envelope: on that view any tie-breaker we articulate will be a private preference in formal dress, whereas a lottery is honest about the absence of a ranking and alone tells both candidates that neither has been judged second. One juror holds that a tie is a failure of attention rather than a fact about the world, that we are paid to judge, and that we should continue deliberating until it breaks even at the cost of the September start. One proposes splitting the fellowship. One proposes reopening the weights so that they track the stated purpose of the fellowship, and re-scoring on that basis.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the meeting on the 14th. It must recommend one course of action, set it out as operational steps with dates that meet the 25th, and give reasons the other jurors can argue with rather than merely be informed by. It must address: whether the deed's requirement of a single most deserving candidate can be satisfied by a jury that finds two candidates it cannot rank; whether a tie of this kind is evidence about the candidates or about the instrument that produced it, and what would settle that question on the material we have; and whether, once applications have been received under a published rubric, the jury may alter the basis of comparison at all — and, if it may not, what follows from that. The chair's question about need and career stage should be answered directly: whether a tie-break may draw on material that a score may not, and on what principle the distinction would rest. Finally, the memo must state the strongest case for the position it rejects, in a form that position's advocate would recognize, and say why that case does not carry.

Constraints: no citations, no bibliography, no named philosophers, no invented legal or historical authority. Do not open with the familiar medieval fable of the animal that starves between two equally attractive bundles of hay; it does no work here. Assume your reader knows the facts above and nothing else. Roughly 800–1,100 words, in headed sections, in plain formal prose.
