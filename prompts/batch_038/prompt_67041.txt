I am preparing a lecture-recital for our department's series on nineteenth-century philosophy of music. The final item on the program is a short passacaglia that I will play at the piano while explaining, over the repeating bass, how Schopenhauer's chapter on music in Book III of *The World as Will and Representation* sorts the voices of a texture into grades of reality: the bass as unorganised nature and the crude mass of the planet, slow-moving and incapable of melody; the inner voices as the vegetable and animal kingdoms; and the singing top line as the intellect and striving of the individual human being, the only voice that leads, departs from its starting point, wanders, and returns — a return that on his account buys only a momentary rest before the striving begins again. Rather than close the evening by paraphrasing the argument a second time, I want to read a poem that performs it.

Please write that poem, subject to the following constraints.

**Form.** Five quatrains, twenty lines in all. The fourth line of each of the first four quatrains must be one and the same refrain, repeated word for word, unaltered by a single letter: this is the ground bass. It should be heavy in feel — around four stresses, mostly monosyllables, and free of any image of motion, choice, or interiority, since the bass is not an agent but a mass. It should rhyme with nothing else in the poem: the ground supports the voices above without joining them.

**Ascent.** The three lines above the refrain in each quatrain should climb the grades: first unorganised nature (weight, stone, crystallisation, gravity, the planetary mass); second vegetable life (sap, root, blind upward growth without a mind to own it); third animal life (appetite, pursuit, the heat and blood of the chase); fourth the individual human being, the only stanza in which a voice can be said to lead, to remember, to want, and to regret.

**Dissonance.** Harsh, unresolved, or discordant material is permitted only in the upper three lines. The refrain must stay level and untroubled wherever it falls. Each quatrain's discord should be resolved before the refrain arrives, with one deliberate exception: in the fourth quatrain the resolution may fail, or arrive only as a fresh departure, since the melody's return to its tonic is precisely the satisfaction that does not last.

**The turn.** In the fifth quatrain the ground rises to the top. Its first line is the refrain, and the three lines beneath it should grow successively shorter — fewer syllables, thinner voice — until the last line is one or two words, so that the individual line thins out into silence over a bass that has not changed.

**Vocabulary.** The poem may not use the words *will*, *representation*, *idea*, *objectification*, *Platonic*, *metaphysics*, *pessimism*, or Schopenhauer's name, and it may not name any composer, work, or philosopher, or quote the chapter. The doctrine has to be carried entirely by images of sounding and of weight. It should work for two audiences at once: a listener who knows the chapter should be able to say which stanza is which grade, and why the refrain moves at the end; a listener who has never read him should simply hear a good poem about music and mortality. Keep the register plain and physical, with no Victorian uplift.

Please answer in two sections, in this order:

1. **The Poem** — the twenty lines under a title of your choosing, with stanza breaks shown.
2. **The Score** — 300 to 400 words of commentary: what each quatrain is a grade of and by what device; where you placed dissonance and where you resolved or withheld it; why the refrain rhymes with nothing; what the fifth quatrain's inversion is claiming about the individual and the ground; and, finally, one honest note of where the poem takes a liberty with the chapter rather than translating it.
