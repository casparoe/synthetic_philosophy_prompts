I sit on my university's museum committee, which certifies whether objects still qualify as original under a bequest that funds their upkeep. The case is a nineteenth-century bracket clock. Over four decades one conservator has replaced its components a few at a time — a gear, then a wheel train, then the dial's enamel, then the case veneer panel by panel — every intervention documented, every part made to the original specification, and every one approved by curators who would have been astonished to learn they were authorizing a replica. Perhaps sixty percent of the material is now new. The donor's heirs argue the bequest should lapse; the museum replies that since no single replacement could have made the clock a copy, it is not one, and that the heirs are pressing a distinction there may be nothing to settle.

I have been asked to draft the committee's reasoning, and I would value your counsel on two points. First, is either party's position tenable? I suspect both can be maintained only at some cost I cannot name. Second, and more pressing, we must issue a rule that will bind later cases. One member proposes a numeric threshold — below half the original material by mass, an object is reclassified; another insists we judge each object on its own merits and refuse to legislate at all. Assess both remedies, say which you would defend and on what grounds, and tell me what I might say to whichever party the rule treats least generously. Please compose your reply in American English.
