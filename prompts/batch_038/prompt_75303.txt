I lead the modelling group for a consortium that maintains a shared upper ontology plus several domain ontologies in OWL, and in our governance documents every class we assert is read by downstream teams as a metaphysical commitment, not a notational convenience. We keep receiving requests to reify abstract entities as first-class objects: propositions, so that assertions, evidence, and retractions have a common subject; probability distributions and possible-world-like structures for a new uncertainty module; cardinal numbers and sets for cohort sizes; and a proposed class for functions mapping specimens to assay values. Teams have been answering these ad hoc, and our schema now contains commitments nobody can defend.

Acting as a philosopher of mathematics who has also done standards work, please convert Benacerraf's dilemma into a decision procedure that a modeller with no philosophical training can run in roughly thirty minutes on any proposal to reify an abstract entity. Begin with a short brainstorm of what each horn — the availability of multiple equally adequate identifications of the same structure, and the demand for an account of how beliefs about causally inert, non-spatiotemporal objects can be reliable — actually implies for someone writing a schema, and of what the standard responses (structuralism, fictionalism and nominalist paraphrase, indispensability-driven platonism, ersatz or plenitudinous worlds) buy and at what cost. Then give the procedure: numbered questions asked in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the exit points that terminate in "refuse to reify," "reify under an explicitly documented identification convention," or "reify and treat as settled," and the steps that are questions of engineering, backward compatibility, or community agreement rather than metaphysics and therefore get handed to a named sign-off rather than resolved on the spot. Say plainly which philosophical distinctions you had to sacrifice to make the procedure usable, and why those were the cheapest to drop.

Apply the procedure to three cases: (i) adding a propositions class for our evidence machinery; (ii) reifying probability distributions as objects; and (iii) treating numbers and cardinalities as objects rather than as annotations on existing classes. At least one verdict should go against my preferences — I want propositions as objects badly, because otherwise our evidence and retraction records have nothing to attach to — and where the procedure refuses, state the second-best construction and what it costs.

Close with the procedure's failure modes: the kinds of proposal it will systematically misjudge or fail to discriminate, and any step that is a judgment call dressed in procedural language. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the procedure's main claims and single out the one step you are least sure of.
