I'm part of an interdisciplinary reading group on the philosophy of time — three philosophers, two astronomers, one retired engineer — and last week we worked through the standard argument from special relativity to the block universe, the one that goes back to Rietdijk and Putnam in the mid-to-late 1960s. The version that actually persuaded several of us, though, came not from the papers but from a popular book one member brought in, which explains the relativity of simultaneity with a loaf of bread. Spacetime, the book says, is like a loaf: your "now" is a slice through it, and observers moving relative to one another cut the loaf at different angles, so their slices contain different events — yet every slice is equally a slice of the same loaf, and the loaf is there in its entirety whether or not anyone is cutting it. The book then presses the image with Penrose's Andromeda variant: two people pass each other on a street, one walking toward the Andromeda galaxy and one walking away, and because their slices are tilted differently, for one of them an Andromedean fleet has already set off while for the other the decision whether to launch has not yet been made. From this the book concludes flatly that the future is already real, that presentism is not so much false as ruled out by physics, and that the block universe is a theorem rather than a metaphysical taste.

I want an audit of the loaf. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — saying what in the picture plays the role of the four-dimensional manifold, the slice, the angle of the cut, the knife, the crumb structure, the ordering of slices along the loaf, and above all the standpoint from which the whole loaf is visible at once. Then identify the disanalogies and rank them by how much damage each does to the use the book puts the image to: the fact that a loaf sits in an ambient space in which it is at rest; the fact that a knife can be tilted through any angle while an observer's slice is bounded so that it can never become timelike; the fact that a loaf's slices are ordered by where they fall in that ambient space whereas an observer's family of slices is ordered by proper time along a worldline; the fact that slices of a loaf are all equally present to someone standing outside it, while nobody stands outside spacetime. Which of these are merely decorative — the unavoidable price of any visualization — and which are load-bearing? Our group split on this. The philosophers said the image begs the question, because it depicts the four-dimensional whole as a thing already there to be carved, which is precisely the conclusion; the astronomers said that is cheap, that pictures always presuppose something, and the argument stands or falls on the physics and not on the picture. Tell me which side is right, or whether each is half-right.

Finally, please separate what the picture contributes from what the physics contributes. Make explicit the bridge premise the loaf hides — something to the effect that what is co-present with me is real, that reality is transitive along chains of co-presence, and that the present can be a global hyperplane rather than a merely local notion, which is exactly what some critics of the argument have denied. Then ask whether the Andromeda case gains anything from its scale: at a walking pace the discrepancy across two and a half million light-years comes to only a few days, and it would be a few seconds if the galaxy were much closer — does the argument lose force at that magnitude, or is the rhetorical size of the gap doing all the work? If the loaf fails, suggest a better image for the same physics that does not presuppose eternalism: say what it would have to convey, and what it would inevitably obscure. And tell me what a defender of the loaf-and-block-universe line would himself concede as a genuine cost of the picture, as distinct from what its critics say about it.
