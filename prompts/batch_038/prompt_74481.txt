Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher of perception — someone who works on the argument from illusion, on sense-data theories, and on the disjunctivist response to them, and who is also at home with the methodology of psychophysics, including signal detection theory, though you need not deploy any mathematics. Some background, so that you can calibrate what I do and do not already know. I am a retired sensory physiologist. For close to four decades my working life consisted of measuring detection and discrimination in the human visual system, and I have been reading philosophy seriously only since I retired three years ago. I have no formal training, so it is entirely possible that I am describing something the literature already handles under a name I have simply not encountered.

Here is the pattern. An argument requires a premise of roughly this form: in such-and-such a situation, the subject could not tell that things are otherwise than they seem. In the cases I keep meeting, the situation is a hallucination stipulated to be one the subject could not distinguish, even on reflection, from a corresponding veridical perception. The conclusion drawn is that something must be shared between the two situations, on the reasoning that if the perception contained anything the hallucination lacked, the subject would be in a position to detect that absence. The inference, in other words, runs from the failure of a discrimination to the absence of anything there to discriminate. The same premise reappears in the newer versions of these cases, where a brain is kept in a vat and driven by signals arranged to be exactly those its body would have received, and the argument is that because the vat-dweller could not tell, what the vat-dweller has must be the same in kind as what the perceiver has.

What strikes me further is that this premise does work for both sides of the dispute I have been reading. Those who conclude that perception and hallucination share a common ingredient use it that way. But their opponents also lean on the subject's inability to tell the cases apart, and they use it to argue in the opposite direction: that the hallucination has no positive character of its own that could be captured, since all that can be said of it is that it is one the subject could not distinguish from a perception. Neither side, so far as I can determine, says which sense of could-not-tell is in play, and it is not obvious to me that both uses can be entitled to it.

My difficulty is methodological, and it comes from my former profession. In the laboratory the failure of a discrimination is a fact about the instrument at least as much as it is a fact about what is being measured, and one is never permitted to read the first as the second without first characterizing the instrument. We were trained to keep apart two quantities that ordinary language runs together: what an observer reports when asked whether two presentations differ, which depends both on the observer's sensitivity and on the criterion the observer happens to adopt on that occasion; and what an observer does when forced to choose between them, which can be reliably above chance even when the report is that there is nothing to choose. These two come apart in both directions, and I believe both directions are documented. There are conditions under which observers deny any difference and nevertheless select the correct alternative well above chance, trial after trial; and there are conditions under which observers report a clear difference, with high confidence, yet perform at chance when asked which presentation carried the property, since confidence and accuracy dissociate readily enough. I am describing these from memory of a field I left three years ago, so please treat my characterization of the findings as approximate rather than as something to be relied upon in detail.

The philosophical premise seems to me to require the second quantity, since the conclusion concerns what is there in the experience; but the intuitions that make the premise attractive appear to be drawn from the first, which is only a report, and which is hostage to criteria, to attention, to the wording of the question, and to the resolution of the comparison being made.

So my question is whether this has a name.

I have considered the obvious candidates, and none of them seems to be the thing. Plain equivocation is too crude: the argument does not, I think, use one word in two senses so much as it treats one achievement — the subject's inability to tell — as though it settled a question about the world that it does not settle. And I understand that the opponents of these arguments object to a conception on which perception and hallucination are supposed to share a common factor, with perception being that factor plus a relation to an object; but that conception is the target of the objection rather than the move by which the target is reached, and my concern is with the move. I have also come across a general debate about whether introspective reports are trustworthy, which is a broader claim and a different one: my worry is not that such reports are systematically false, but that they are being read as measurements of something they were never measurements of.

I would not be satisfied with a label, so please treat the following as the deliverables, in order:

2. Whether or not it does, identify the three or four nearest concepts and, for each, say what my description shares with it and exactly where the fit fails.

In other words, tell me whether this diagnosis, if correct, actually defeats the argument, or merely marks a place where more work is owed.
