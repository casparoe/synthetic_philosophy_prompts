Prepping a seminar session on Goodhart's law and specification gaming, and there's a hole in my materials I want you to fill. I need a purpose-built thought experiment, built to a fairly tight spec — so please don't hand me a trolley problem with a KPI bolted on.

Here's the spec. Pick one realistic organization — hospital network, school district, call center, a financial regulator, a police department, whatever — and give me the scenario in a single paragraph. No AI, no sci-fi, and above all no villains: nobody games the metric, nobody fudges or misreads the data, nobody is confused about what's being measured, and the leadership is competent and honest and would have caught any of that if it happened. The metric measures exactly what it was designed to measure. Now the twist: as the organization drives that metric up, the thing it actually cares about gets worse — not out in some weird tail region, but steadily, across a wide range of improvement.

The catch is that the case has to survive the standard four-way taxonomy, which my students will have read the week before: regressional (selecting for the proxy also selects for the noise, tails come apart), extremal (you push the system into a regime where the old relationship no longer holds), causal ("an action on the part of the regulator causes the collapse"), and adversarial (an agent with different goals games it). Adversarial is ruled out by construction and I've banned the other three in the setup. And here's the part I care most about: the metric has to be *locally vindicated*. For any individual patient, pupil, or caller, raising that person's score genuinely improves that person's outcome — a lone unit that improves while everyone else holds still really does better. The aggregate harm has to be emergant, not smuggled in by stipulation.

Then do the demonstration, because a scenario without the check is worthless to me:

(a) go through each of the four in turn and say why it doesn't describe your case;
(b) name the mechanism you actually used, in a phrase and a sentence;
(c) identify the single feature of the scenario that's doing the work, and tell me what happens to the verdict if I delete it;
(d) take the strongest objection seriously — which I expect will be that the taxonomy paper defines causal Goodhart so loosely that anything you build is just causal Goodhart wearing a new hat. Say whether that objection succeeds, and give me a criterion for telling a genuine fifth mechanism from a relabeled fourth one.

So you know what counts as cheating: I'm using this to show the taxonomy is a list of mechanisms, not a proof of exhaustiveness, so a case whose deterioration is asserted rather than generated by the structure fails, and so does one where the harm turns out to come from measurement error, from someone quietly gaming the number, or from a distribution shift into an untested regime. I've already got a stack of those. I also don't want the resolution to be that they picked the wrong metric — assume the metric is the right one, and that its a fact about the organization, not about the measurement, that does the damage.
