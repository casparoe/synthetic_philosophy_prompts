I serve on the governing board of a regional health data cooperative: a nonprofit trust through which twelve hospital systems pool de-identified clinical records into a shared research registry. Contribution was made voluntary at founding, and access to the registry is free to all twelve members whether or not they contribute. After five years, seven systems contribute and five do not. Two of the five are large, well-resourced academic systems whose IT leadership has told our staff that anonymization and record-linkage work is not a priority for them this budget cycle. The registry's scientific value turns heavily on coverage; at present it systematically under-represents rural and safety-net populations, which is the very gap the founding grant was meant to close.

At our March meeting the board will vote on a proposal from the finance subcommittee: impose an access charge on non-contributing members, consisting of a per-query fee plus a tiered annual subscription, waived entirely for contributors, together with a clause under which contribution becomes mandatory in the third year if participation has not reached ten of twelve.

The board is divided along two lines that I do not think anyone has yet separated cleanly.

The first faction argues that the situation is a straightforward collective-action trap of the familiar sort: each system reasons that its own contribution makes no perceptible difference to the registry's usefulness while costing it real staff time, so withholding is the dominant strategy, and no quantity of exhortation will alter that. On this view there are only two levers, compulsion and price, and since a mandate is politically unattainable this year, price is the instrument to use. They add an equity consideration: the seven contributors are effectively subsidizing the five, and because the largest contributors carry the largest absolute costs, the present arrangement is regressive among members.

The second faction argues that the diagnosis is mistaken and that the proposed remedy follows from the misdiagnosis. On their account the five are not defecting against a dominant strategy; several have indicated privately that they would contribute if they were confident that at least nine others would, since their costs are largely fixed while their benefits rise steeply with coverage. What is missing is credible assurance, not incentive. They further warn that introducing money would change the character of the exchange: the seven contributors are unpaid, and they invoke the classic argument about voluntary blood donation to the effect that a market frame can drive out the reciprocal motives on which a scheme like ours depends. They also observe that a per-query charge would fall hardest on the small rural systems, whose patients are the under-represented ones, so the policy could make the registry less representative while raising revenue.

A third confusion keeps surfacing in our correspondence. Several members insist that what we have is a tragedy of the commons. One member objects that the phrase cannot apply at all, because nothing is being depleted: no one's use of the registry subtracts from what remains available to others, so there is no common-pool stock being run down, and the phrase has been doing unexamined work in our deliberations. I suspect this quarrel is not merely verbal and that its resolution bears on which instrument the board should choose, but I cannot yet say how.

Please draft the memorandum I will circulate to the board a week before the meeting, roughly 1,100 words, in the register of a document the board could act on rather than a survey of the literature, with the recommendation stated in a short opening paragraph and the reasoning following. It should:

1. State a recommendation on the subcommittee's proposal — adopt as drafted, adopt with named amendments, defer, or reject — and, if it recommends anything other than adoption as drafted, set out the substitute mechanism concretely, with a timetable that fits a board meeting four times a year.

2. Resolve the diagnostic question. Specify what would have to be true for the first faction to be right, what would have to be true for the second to be right, and what in the facts I have given you — or what inexpensive piece of evidence the board could obtain before the next meeting — would discriminate between them. Then say what the board should do if it cannot tell, and justify that default rather than treating it as a neutral fallback.

3. Address the vocabulary dispute on its merits. Say whether the depletability objection is correct, whether it matters, and, if this remains a genuine collective-action problem despite it, identify what kind of problem it is and explain why the classification changes the instrument rather than merely the label.

4. Give the crowding-out objection its strongest form and then assess it: under what conditions would monetization actually destroy the cooperation it is intended to purchase, and do those conditions obtain in this consortium? Neither dismiss the objection nor concede it.

5. Confront a difficulty neither faction has raised. Any scheme the board adopts will require someone to verify that contributions are complete and current rather than nominal, and that verification is itself costly and itself liable to be under-supplied, since its benefits accrue to all members regardless of who pays for it. Explain who should bear that cost and on what principle, and what the board should do about a scheme whose enforcement depends on the good faith of the parties it enforces.

6. Include a decision rule the board can adopt now for the contingency that its chosen mechanism fails: a pre-committed trigger and a pre-committed next step, so that an unsuccessful first attempt does not return the board to this same impasse a year from now with its options further narrowed.

Throughout, members who disagree with your recommendation should be able to see their strongest consideration engaged and answered rather than restated and set aside.
