My sister does grants admin at a smallish foundation and last month they had an offsite to decide whether to keep using their rubric for picking cause areas — the one where you rate each candidate 1-10 on importance, tractability and neglectedness and multiply the three. Two of the program officers got into it pretty hard and the rest of us just watched. I wrote the argument down afterwards, roughly in order, and I'm curious who's actually right at each step.

1. Nadia (against the rubric) opened with: the three numbers aren't independent, so multiplying them is bogus. Fields get crowded precisely because they look tractable, so anything that scores high on tractability will tend to score low on neglectedness, and vice versa. When two of your three factors move together like that, the product mostly ends up reflecting the remaining one — importance — which is the vaguest and least measurable of the three. So the thing looks like a three-variable calculation but is secretly a one-variable one in a lab coat.

2. Ben (for it) said that's a misreading of what neglectedness is for. It isn't a fourth independent fact about the world, it's the term that converts average value into marginal value: what matters to a funder isn't how much good a cause does in total but how much good the next dollar does, and under diminishing returns that's exactly what "how much attention this already gets" tracks. Drop it and you'll fund the same crowded things everyone else funds, just because they're important and tractable. So the correlation Nadia pointed at isn't a defect, it's the mechanism: if tractability and neglectedness trade off, their product comes out roughly flat across causes, and that's the correct result — it means per-dollar progress is about the same here and there, so importance is legitimately what does the discriminating.

3. Nadia: fine, but neglectedness gets scored at the wrong granularity. A cause area can look neglected at the level you're ranking while the specific thing you'd actually fund is swarming with people, and the reverse. What's neglected is never "global health" or "AI policy" as such, it's a particular approach inside them, and the rubric has no slot for that — so a 3 tells you nothing about whether your grant is the marginal one.

4. Ben: every coarse framework has that problem, and the alternative isn't a better framework, it's no framework — you'd be ranking approaches on the same gut feelings, just less visibly. Besides, the objection is self-defeating against this rubric specifically, because the obvious fix is to apply it at the intervention level, which you're allowed to do; the rubric doesn't fix the level of analysis, so "it's at the wrong level" is a complaint about how their committee happened to use it.

5. Nadia then went for what she took to be the knockout: the rubric is self-undermining the moment you talk about it in public. Neglectedness is the only one of the three factors that's about other people's beliefs and behavior, so any widely-used version of the ranking destroys the very property that made the top-ranked causes attractive. And it's not slow drift, it's a built-in expiry date — the more successful and public the framework, the faster its outputs go stale, and the framework can't detect that, since it's own scores are what caused the change.

6. Ben: two replies. First, expiry is the design, not the failure — a search heuristic that stops pointing at a place once that place stops being neglected is doing its job, and you're supposed to re-score periodically and move on. Second, the crowding effect is negligible at our scale: the number of funders using anything like this rubric is a rounding error next to total global attention on any cause area, so the reflexivity would only bite in fields that are already saturated, which we wouldn't be funding anyway.

7. Nadia's closer: then admit the scores are ordinal and stop multiplying them. Two causes can both get a 7 for neglectedness while one is a factor of three from saturation and the other is a factor of a thousand, and the multiplication turns that difference into literally nothing. The single number the rubric spits out is precise-looking in a way its inputs cant support, and the precision is exactly what makes people comfortable deferring to it instead of arguing.

8. Ben's closer: nobody who's good at this actually multiplies. The arithmetic is a mnemonic for "all three have to clear a bar, and you have to write a number down for each," and its real function is defensive — it forces you to score tractability out loud, which is where the bias lives, because tractability in practice means "will I get clean feedback on this," so interventions with easy metrics score high and long-shot structural ones score low whether or not they'd have worked. The rubric earns its keep by making that bias visible enough to argue with.

They asked the room to referee and nobody wanted to. Flag places where someone lands a real point but for a bad reason, or answers a claim the other person didn't make. Then say who came out ahead overall, and whether there's something structurally important about this kind of rubric that neither of them touched.
