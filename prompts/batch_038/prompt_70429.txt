so I help run a winter shelter and a food pantry in a town of about nine thousand people, and every single year the same fight happens. Half the volunteers think we should turn people away the second we're full, the other half think turning anybody away is basically harming them, and somewhere in the middle somebody always drags out that old lifeboat ethics essay and calls me a Hardinite, and then somebody else calls me a gatekeeper with a clipboard. I don't think I'm either one, but I can't say why, and lately I've started to worry that the reason I can't say why is that there isn't a why. No philosophy training here — eleven years of doing this and reading on my own at night is all I've got.

Here's what I actually believe, roughly in the order it comes up at meetings:

1. There's a number. We have 22 beds and a pantry that can feed maybe 180 households a month. Once we're at that, taking one more person in means giving everybody already inside a worse night. Helping in a way that wrecks the helping isn't helping.

2. Same thing goes for the reserve. We keep a freezer stock and an emergency fund that we don't touch for normal traffic, because the plant closed and we lived off that reserve for four months. Aid that eats down the reserve so there's nothing left when the next thing hits isn't aid, its just a slower failure.

3. I don't buy the "people are greedy, it's human nature" version of any of this. Stuff gets ruined when nobody's in charge of it and nobody has made the rules about who gets what. Put the same resource under a group that actually owns the decisions and sets clear rules and people handle it fine. I've watched that happen here with my own eyes.

4. Need is need. I don't rank people by how they ended up on my doorstep — lost the job, spent the money, walked out on somebody, whatever. Once they're standing there they're a person who needs a bed.

5. But when we're over the line and I have to pick, I give first call to people from here. Longtime residents, the ones who've put in hours at the pantry, families with kids in the school district. Somebody who got off a bus from three states over an hour ago goes on the waitlist, even if he's worse off than the local guy.

6. Nobody who would die or get seriously hurt without us gets turned away. Capacity or no capacity. That one I won't move on.

7. Anywhere I get to choose, I'd rather do a lot of good for fewer people than a little good for more people. I feel the same way about donating — one thing that genuinely works beats ten things that sort of work.

8. I would turn down grant money that came with rules about who we're allowed to serve, even if the money doubled our capacity.

So: go through this and tell me which of these are genuinely pulling against each other and which ones only look like a conflict until you actually sit down with them. Rank the real ones by how bad they are rather than dumping them all on me as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give, what the cheapest fix is, and what that fix costs me. If there's a single change that would clean up the most, tell me what it is. And tell me straight whether "Hardinite" is a fair label for this package or whether I'm doing something that essay doesn't cover at all. Don't send me off to read anything, I just want to know if this holds together.
