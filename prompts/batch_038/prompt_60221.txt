So I'm a second-year doing a joint philosophy and economics degree, and this term I'm on a module called Money, Mind and Markets where we've spent the last three weeks on the eighteenth-century stuff — Hume, Locke on interest, and then Berkeley, who I'll be honest I only picked up properly because my flatmate kept going on about him. Everyone does Berkeley on perception and then stops, but our seminar this week was on The Querist, which is bizarre as a text because it's just hundreds of numbered questions with no answers at all, and I found it weirdly much more readable than the Alciphron dialogue we did before it. Anyway. Long story short, I'm "the philosophy one" in my friendship group, so when the students' union finally got its time bank off the ground they asked me to give the short speech at the launch on Thursday, and I've decided I want to build the whole thing out of Berkeley's theory of money rather than out of the usual stuff about community and reciprocity, because I think he's actually right and nobody in the room will have heard it before.

The scheme works like this: you do an hour of something for someone — cycling someone's bike, teaching them a bit of Spanish, helping them move a sofa up three flights — and you get one unit, and the unit is worth an hour of anybody else's time, and you can spend it on an hour of anything. They've called the unit a bob, which everyone thinks is hilarious. The room on Thursday is going to be a mess: two SU officers who just want the thing to sound fun and inclusive, about thirty students who have come for the free pizza and will be looking at their phones, the university's finance office sending someone to be polite, a manager from the local credit union who has been very kind about it but is quietly nervous that we've set up something that ought to be regulated, a guy from a city crypto meetup who a friend of a friend invited and who has already told me twice that he's "interested in the tokenisation angle", and my seminar tutor, who will be stood at the back with a plastic cup of wine. That last one matters because she'll know exactly whether I've understood the reading. So there are two doors the speech has to shut without ever naming them. First, the crypto one: Berkeley's whole point as I read it is that there is nothing sacred about the metal, that the true idea of money is a ticket or counter, and that's precisely the bit this guy will hear as vindication — but for Berkeley a token only matters insofar as it gets people working, and gold in a vault or a coin hoarded under a floorboard is worth nothing to a nation, so the speech has to make the instrumentalism do the work and leave him nothing to appropriate. Second, the credit union one: the speech must not sound like we're saying money is a fiction or that we're opting out of the real economy, because Berkeley wanted more circulation, not less, and he was pushing for a national bank — the time bank should come across as taking money more seriously than usual, not as a protest against it. You can use these three lines, which are the ones I've got highlighted in my copy, but please don't put any other words in Berkeley's mouth because I can't check them and I'd rather not misquote a bishop in front of my tutor: "Whether there ever was, is, or will be, an industrious nation poor, or an idle rich?"; "whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?"; and the one about the denominations being retained although the bullion were gone, and things still being rated, bought and sold, industry promoted and a circulation of commerce maintained. Also steer clear of the queries where he's being horrid about the Irish poor being content in dirt, or where he wants the legislature to stop leaving fashion to women and fops — I know they're in there and I do not want to be the person who reads those out at a student event.

One thing I can't work out myself, and I'd like you to actually think it through before you write rather than papering over it: our units lapse if you don't spend them within six months, which the SU did deliberately to stop people sitting on them and to keep them moving. As far as I can tell Berkeley never considers a unit that expires — he's happy for money to be a store of value in some of the queries — so I don't know whether his reasoning backs our rule up or undercuts it. Do the argument, tell me which way you think it goes and why in two or three sentences before the speech, and then make the speech take the side that's genuinely his rather than the side that flatters us. Formally: it needs to be about four or five minutes, so somewhere around 650 words, spoken aloud to a room where half the people are eating. I'd like it to borrow Berkeley's own method a bit — putting the questions to the room instead of announcing conclusions — because that's the thing about The Querist that actually struck me, and it's a decent way of not lecturing anyone. It has to have one real joke that lands in the first ninety seconds and that the students and the finance person can both laugh at, and it should end on the bit where the treasurer opens the ledger and the first hour gets entered. Name Berkeley once, maybe twice, never say "instrumentalism" or "fiat" or "heterodox", and don't give me any references or a list of things to read afterwards — I just want the two or three sentences of reasoning and then the speech. Cheers.
