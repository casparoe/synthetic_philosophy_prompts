I was chairing a philosophy-of-language reading group when two members, Nadia and Owen, fell out over an example on the handout. The scenario: a vault is opened overnight with no sign of forced entry; the combination is known to Marta and to two colleagues who were abroad all week; and it is not established who opened it. The two sentences under discussion were (i) 'If Marta didn't open the vault, someone else did' and (ii) 'If Marta hadn't opened the vault, someone else would have'. The group agreed that (i) is true and (ii) false.

Nadia began from that agreement: the contrast shows that (i) is not in the business of being true or false at all. Indicatives obey something like Adams' thesis — one is entitled to assert one just when one's conditional credence in the consequent given the antecedent is high — so (i) records an epistemic state rather than a fact about the vault. Owen objected that we nonetheless argue about indicatives as though they were truth-apt: a member can say of (i) 'that's wrong, she had an accomplice', and we treat that as a substantive correction, not an announcement of revised credences.

Nadia answered with Lewis's triviality result: no non-trivial proposition can have its probability equal the corresponding conditional probability across all credence functions, so Adams' thesis is incompatible with indicatives expressing propositions. Owen replied that triviality only rules out context-invariant contents; on a Stalnaker-style account both (i) and (ii) have truth conditions, but (ii) is evaluated at the nearest world in which Marta stays away, holding fixed the colleagues' absence, while (i) is evaluated within the worlds left open by what the group knows. Nadia said this concedes her point: if (i)'s truth value varies with the group's information state, 'true' is merely assertibility in disguise. Owen said not at all — that is ordinary context-dependence of the kind found in 'here' or 'I', which fixes which truth conditions apply without showing that none apply, and which is why we can happily report someone else's indicative as true.

Nadia's last move was that (i)'s unfalsifiability is a symptom of emptiness rather than a counterexample to it. Owen answered that its near-triviality is an artefact of a rigged example, since the group already knows the vault was opened, and that most indicatives — say, 'if the alarm was tampered with, the silent line still reported' — are nothing like that. Neither budged, and I could not tell who had the better of it.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying which of Nadia's and Owen's points hold up and which do not, and whether either scored something the other never answered? Please also say what, if anything, both are missing about why (i) and (ii) come apart — including whether the vault case really bears on the question they took it to. Write your answer in British English.
