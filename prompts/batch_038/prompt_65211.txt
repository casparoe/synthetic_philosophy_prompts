Six months ago my husband died, and since roughly the third week afterwards I have dreamed of him almost every night: not fragments but whole scenes, with dialogue, in which he is plainly there and plainly alive, and in which I am plainly there too. I have taken to writing these down on the bedside table within a minute or two of waking, and the journal has become the most important document I own. At a philosophy reading group I run out of my kitchen, one of the members — a graduate student, kindly meant — remarked that on a famous account of dreaming there was nothing it was like for me to have those dreams at all: on that account the journal records stories composed at six in the morning, not events from the night. I found this both offensive and, worse, not obviously refutable by anything I actually know. I want to find out precisely what I am committed to, and what my neighbours at the table are committed to, so I am commissioning a diagnostic instrument rather than an essay.

Please build me a decision tree (with a taxonomy behind it) on the question of whether dreams are experiences, in the sense at issue when Malcolm denies it and Dennett entertains the possibility that the narrative is only assembled in or near waking. The handout is for eleven people: two philosophers, one polysomnography technician who will object loudly to anything inconsistent with what she sees on a hypnogram, and eight of us who have never read a word of this literature but who dream.

The construction should run in three explicit stages. First, lay out the axes you take to generate the logical space, each with its available values. I want at least these among them, and you may add more: (a) whether anything was going on for the sleeper during the dream, as against only during the telling; (b) whether the waking report is caused by an experience or is a construction, treated as a question separable from (a); (c) whether the content was immersive — a here, a me located in it, a point of view — as against imagistic or narrative without presence; (d) whether the dreamer formed beliefs in the dream, or only entertained contents; (e) whether experience is all-or-nothing across a night or comes in degrees, so that some stretches are experience-free; (f) what the position says about the unreported hours of deep sleep; and (g) an axis I suspect gets overlooked — whether the theorist is asserting their view as true or merely deploying it to show that our ordinary confidence is unearned, since these are different positions and should not share a leaf. Second, show which combinations of values are occupied, which are empty, and for each empty one say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply got round to it. Third, attach names to the occupied leaves.

The leaves must include: the Malcolm-style denial; the Dennett-style position, with the assertion/deployment distinction from axis (g) made visible in the tree rather than buried in a footnote; the view that dreaming is a kind of imagining and so involves no misleading sensations or false beliefs; the view that makes immersion or phenomenal presence the hinge; a neurobiological account that treats dreams as robust conscious experience and explains their content from sleep physiology; and a position on which deep, report-free sleep is not experience-free either. If my own answers below point at a combination no one defends, give me that leaf as unnamed rather than forcing me into the nearest occupied one.

Phrase every node as a question about a concrete situation the eleven of us could answer — waking mid-sentence with the kitchen turning into an old flat; being located behind my own eyes; catching myself revising the journal two minutes in; having no report for four hours — and never as a question containing a theorist's name or a term of art. The labels and the vocabulary arrive only at the leaves. Each leaf then gets three things: a one-sentence gloss in plain language, the commitment it finds most awkward, and the piece of evidence about dreaming it is worst placed to accommodate (first-person report, the eye-movement correspondence found in lucid dreamers, dream recall under laboratory awakening, or whatever you judge most damaging). Wherever two leaves cannot be separated by the answers my group could give, say so explicitly, and say what evidence other than a report could separate them. Please do not send me a bibliography, a reference list, or citations; I want the instrument, not a reading list.

My own answers, in your question-agnostic terms: I take myself to be recording something that was happening while I was asleep, not a morning composition; I nevertheless admit that detail is added at the telling, and I have proof of it in my own handwriting; I was located in the scene and looking out from somewhere, and the scene seemed present rather than described; I did not believe he was alive — I was not fooled — but I would also resist saying I was merely imagining him; I think some nights contain nothing it is like and some contain a great deal; and about the hours I cannot report on at all I have no idea what to say.

Give me the tree, the occupied/empty combination analysis, and the annotated leaves, in that order. Then state where my answers land, and make the final line of your reply exactly one line beginning "Verdict:" followed by the label of the leaf I occupy — or, if my answers genuinely straddle two leaves, both labels separated by a slash, and nothing else after it.
