I work twenty hours a week in my university library's cataloging and metadata unit, and I am simultaneously enrolled in a cognitive science seminar on concepts, where we have been reading the position associated with Hofstadter and Mitchell's Copycat project: that concepts are fluid, that their boundaries shift with the context of use, and that much ordinary reasoning consists of controlled slippage between neighboring concepts rather than the application of fixed definitions. The rules I apply at work appear to assume the opposite. Every physical volume receives exactly one call number and therefore stands in exactly one place on the shelf; when a book treats two topics in roughly equal measure, the classification scheme instructs me to take whichever comes first in the schedule; a work is placed by the discipline it is written from rather than by the subject matter it happens to mention; and the subject headings I assign come from an authorized list in which each preferred term carries a scope note fixing its range, sits nested inside broader terms, and absorbs near-synonyms by redirecting them. Yet two competent libraries routinely classify the same monograph differently, and neither is thought to have erred.

Please think this through carefully as a philosopher of concepts who also understands knowledge organization. What view of concepts does this practice presuppose? Does it commit us to determinate boundaries and a single dominant aboutness per work, or is the one-call-number rule merely a concession to the fact that books are physical objects that cannot stand in two places at once? Which rival accounts — family resemblance, prototypes, the fluid-slippage view — does the practice genuinely rule out rather than sit uneasily beside? Separate what it strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of concepts would license the same rules. Please also address the oddity that the authorized vocabulary is engineered precisely to block slippage, while divergent classification between libraries is tolerated without anyone claiming there is a fact of the matter. Finish by telling me what would have to change, in the rules or in the shelves themselves, if we took the fluid view of concepts seriously, and whether a faceted classification actually escapes the problem or simply relocates it.

Write your answer in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or a considered email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
