I write novels, which means I have composed perhaps a dozen memorial speeches and retirement toasts that were never delivered and never will be — practice, in effect, on other people's occasions. Next Saturday I must give a real one, and there is a structural problem in it that I cannot solve by craft alone, so I am asking for help with the composition rather than for advice about how to deliver it.

The occasion. My aunt Ruth is retiring at seventy-one after thirty-eight years in the reference department of a mid-sized university library. She kept a system of three-by-five index cards: books, patrons' recurring questions, half-formed thoughts, cross-references to other cards, some of which outlived the books they referred to. The cards lived in oak drawers she had requisitioned in 1983 and refused to surrender. Her rule was that nothing went on a card until she had written it out in her own hand; when the library migrated to a shared discovery platform, she entered her data like everyone else and kept the drawers anyway. On the telephone with her you could hear drawers. In 1987 she left a box of cards on a regional train and spent six weeks unable to reconstruct what had been in it; she told me afterward, and I want this used exactly, that the loss was not of the paper but of the two months of herself that had been on it. Two years ago she had a small stroke. She recovered her speech and her mobility, and for six months she could still read her cards but no longer believed them without checking, and the checking made them slower than simply thinking — so she stopped going to the drawers. The cards are being boxed next week and donated to the library's own archive, where they will be catalogued by someone else.

The problem. My brother Daniel is a philosopher of mind, and for twenty years he has maintained at family gatherings that our aunt was not a person who used an excellent filing system but a person whose thinking was partly made of paper — that her mind extended into the drawers in something like a literal sense, and that the family's affectionate joke about her was in fact a correct metaphysical claim about her. My uncle Frank, who spent forty years making tool-and-die equipment and has strong opinions about what a tool is, has maintained the opposite with equal conviction: that she was no cleverer than the rest of us, that the cards did the thinking, and that Daniel is paying a philosopher's price for a librarian's habit. Both men consider themselves the winner. The room will be about sixty people: her colleagues, her three children, her brother Frank, my brother Daniel, and a good many people who knew her only as a mother and will be standing at the back with a glass in their hand.

What I want written is the toast itself — a single speech, not options, of roughly seven hundred words, five minutes spoken, ending in the ordinary gesture of raising a glass. It must satisfy the following, and the difficulty is that these pull against one another:

First, it must not refute either man, and neither may be able to leave feeling that the speech took the other's side. What the room is to feel at the end must remain true whether Daniel is right or Frank is right. If the affection of the speech is parasitic on the metaphysics, I have written something that half the room must silently reject.

Second, it must make felt, without ever stating it, the difference between an instrument a person uses superbly and a resource a person's thinking is made of — and it must do so using material the room already has. The 1987 box and the six months after the stroke are the two places where that difference can be shown rather than argued. The speech should be honest about what the archive boxes mean, without either claiming she persists in the drawers or conceding that they were only card stock.

Third, no jargon and no names. The words extended mind, cognition, offloading, second brain, and external memory must not appear; no philosopher may be named; there may be no thesis, no argument signposted as an argument, and no sentence that would survive being read aloud as a claim about minds in general. The room must be able to hear it as an affectionate speech about a woman and her drawers.

Fourth, the room should laugh once, and the laugh must come from something true about the cards — a detail only a person who knew her would offer — and not from a constructed gag.

Fifth, I am the person delivering it, and I am aware of my own vices. It may not be about the difficulty of writing it. It may not enumerate her qualities. It may not contain the word legacy, or a closing image of the kind that could be embroidered, and the final sentence before the glass goes up may not summarize anything.

After the speech, add a short paragraph — no more than four sentences — identifying the two or three lines that are carrying the philosophical weight, so that I know where to slow down and where to let them pass as ordinary warmth.
