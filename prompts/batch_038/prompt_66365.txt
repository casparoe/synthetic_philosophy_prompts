For several years now I have been working through the question of whether it is ever right to bring a person into existence, entirely on my own initiative. I am forty-three, I dispatch trucks for a haulage company, and I have never taken a course in philosophy; what started as late-night reading has hardened into a position I feel able to defend out loud. Our fortnightly discussion circle — a handful of retirees and two university students who meet in the back room of the public library — has asked me to set that position down in writing before our next meeting. One member, a retired structural engineer, treats other people's arguments as a hobby: he reads them the way he used to read bridge inspections, looking for the one member carrying load it should not be. I would rather find the cracks before he does. So I am going to state my commitments as plainly as I can and ask you to inspect them for coherence. I came to the second and third of them through David Benatar's asymmetry argument, which I read about online and then worked through myself, but I am stating everything in my own words so that you are auditing my package rather than somebody else's.

Here is the package:

1. Pain is bad and pleasure is good. I cannot argue for this and I do not think it needs arguing for.
2. The absence of pain is a good thing even in a world where there is nobody to enjoy that absence — an empty universe is, in that one respect, better than a universe full of sufferers. But the absence of pleasure is a bad thing only where there is somebody it deprives. Since there was never anyone to be deprived by the pleasures of a person who is never conceived, no bad attaches to their absence.
3. Therefore coming into existence is always a harm to the one who comes into it. This holds even for the luckiest person imaginable, whose life is nearly all pleasure and unusually little pain: they acquire real bads that they would not otherwise have had, and on (2) the goods they forgo by never existing are not bad for anyone.
4. I want all of this to be about individuals, and only about individuals. Right and wrong, as I understand them, always come down to particular beings who are made better or worse off. I have no use for the idea that a state of affairs can be good or bad over and above how it goes for the creatures inside it, and I honestly would not know what to do with such a claim if someone handed me one. For the same reason, nobody is wronged by never being conceived, since there is no one there to wrong; the reason not to have a child is that child's own prospective suffering, not any obligation owed to the world at large.
5. And yet I think some things are sad in themselves. A couple who long for a child and cannot have one have suffered a genuine loss, not a confusion. Likewise, the eventual extinction of human beings strikes me as a tragedy rather than as a missed opportunity for happiness that no one will miss.
6. I am also glad that I exist. I would not trade away the existence of most of the people I love, and I think most lives are worth continuing even where I think they were never worth starting. Nothing I hold licenses killing anyone, and I do not think antinatalism implies that it does.
7. Finally, a rule I apply to myself: human beings have a strong built-in tendency to rate their own lives better than those lives deserve, a kind of compulsory cheerfulness, and I try to discount any intuition of mine that I suspect was manufactured by that tendency.

I would like this treated as a structural inspection, not as an introduction to a subject I already know something about. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, a reading list, or anyone's biography, and I am not asking to be talked out of the view. What I want is: which of these commitments pull against which others, ordered from the most severe conflict to the least, with the reasoning that demonstrates each pull rather than a bare assertion of it; which pairs merely look as though they conflict and dissolve once you say what each claim actually amounts to, since I am fairly confident at least one of mine does and I would like to know which one and why; and, for every real conflict, the cheapest revision available together with what that revision would cost me in the way of claims I could no longer make. Note that items 5 and 6 contain claims that look structurally alike to me, and I want to know whether they should be handled alike or separately. I deliberately will not tell you which of the seven I would be least willing to abandon, both because I do not know and because I do not want to steer you; but if (7), applied consistently, already does damage to some of the others, I would like that pointed out rather than left aside. And if your verdict is that the package holds and that my engineer friend will come up empty-handed, say so — but say precisely why each of the pairs that first look suspicious does not in fact conflict.
