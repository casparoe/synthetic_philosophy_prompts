Here's the problem I need help planning around: my city reading group — about fifteen adults, no philosophy training, several of them archivists and museum staff with strong feelings about what history is for — is spending two hours on Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I have volunteered to lead it. I don't want the essay explained to me; I want a plan for how to teach it in that room and that time.

My own framing, which I'd like the session built around but also stress-tested: the three modes are best presented as three competing answers to the question *what does the past owe me?* — monumental history as permission, antiquarian as belonging, critical as a verdict — with the essay's real subject being dosage rather than kind. Tell me where that framing earns its keep and where it bends the text, particularly on the critical mode.

On top of that: the order the pieces must go in and what depends on what (I suspect the unhistorical and supra-historical material has to be settled before the three modes mean anything, but I may be wrong); the single carrying case I should build all three modes out of — one concrete historical subject the group can grasp with no background at all — together with what that case must have and must lack, and why I shouldn't simply follow Nietzsche's own illustrations; the misconceptions they will arrive with or form, of which I'm already bracing for "he is attacking my profession," "critical history just means judging the past by today's standards," and "for life means whatever makes us feel better," each with the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off rather than argued down at the end; what I should openly decline to cover at this level and announce as such; and one question I can pose in the last ten minutes that would distinguish people who have grasped the difference between the modes from people who have memorized three labels.
