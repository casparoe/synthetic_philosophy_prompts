Someone objects that it's perfectly easy to imagine trees standing in a park, or books sitting in a closet, with no person around to perceive them. Berkeley answers, basically: go ahead, imagine that — all you've done is produce ideas in your own mind, and those ideas are yours whether or not you happened to also imagine a perceiver. That shows you can have the ideas of trees and books; it doesn't show you can grasp the existence of those things apart from any mind's awareness of them. When you think you're conceiving objects existing unconceived, you're really just conceiving your own ideas and quietly leaving out the fact that conceiving them is itself a mental act. He then dares the reader to actually try it: attempt, by any amount of abstracting and separating in thought, to conceive of a thing's real existence in a way that isn't an existence in some mind, and see whether you can get any grip on what you'd even be asserting. As for the talk of absolute unthinking things existing outside the mind, he's doubtful it amounts to anything with a consistent meaning, and he says he won't chase it down there.
