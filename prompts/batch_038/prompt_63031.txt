Built this for a reading group on imprecise probabilities and I now think it's a disaster, but I can't tell whether it's invalid or just resting on a premise I ought to hate. The example is mine, so you can check the arithmetic rather than trust my memory of anybody else's.

Setup. Two coins, A and B, flipped together. You know one of two things holds but not which: either they always land alike, or they always land opposite. You also know A is fair. Let H_A = A lands heads, H_B = B lands heads. Your credal set is the convex hull of two probability functions over (A,B) outcomes:

- P1: HH = 1/2, TT = 1/2, HT = TH = 0. (the alike world)
- P2: HT = 1/2, TH = 1/2, HH = TT = 0. (the opposite world)

They agree that P(H_A) = 1/2 and P(H_B) = 1/2, so your interval on each is the single number 1/2. Now you watch B land heads. Conditioning each member: P1(H_A | H_B) = 1, P2(H_A | H_B) = 0. So your interval on H_A goes from [1/2, 1/2] to [0, 1]. If B lands tails it's the same thing with the roles swapped: 0 and 1. Looking at B — which you knew you were going to do — leaves you maximally ignorant about H_A whichever way B lands. I think this is the dilation phenomenon.

The argument I built out of it:

(1) Your doxastic state is defective if you can foresee an update that will leave you, on every possible outcome of it, strictly less informed about some question than you are now.
(2) In the coin case you foresee exactly that about H_A.
(3) So the coin credal state is defective.
(4) Nothing here was optional or rigged on my part. The world can just be like that, and the state is the one an imprecise probabilist is supposed to endorse as matching the evidence — you know A is fair, you don't know whether the coins are matched. So the defect is in the state, not in some choice I made.
(5) And nothing turns on H_A. For any question where your interval is narrower than [0,1] you can bolt on a companion event of exactly this shape: two hypotheses that agree about your question but disagree about how the companion correlates with it. So every non-maximal state is exposed to a foreseen dilating update.
(6) The only states that can't dilate are singletons, because a point can't widen, and the set of all probability functions, because it's already as wide as it goes.
(7) "Just widen your prior" doesn't rescue anything, since by (5) you'd have to pre-widen every exposed interval, which lands you on the all-functions set.
(8) That set is ruled out independently: a rational credal state gives probability 1 to everything you know, and you know things.
(9) So the only permissible states are singletons.
(10) But that escape looks like a representational fluke rather than an insight. Sharp agents somehow never get caught by (1), and I can't tell whether that's because being sharp is genuinely better informed or because width happens to be the measure (1) is built around.
(11) Either way, (5) never used anything specific to the coin case, so (9) applies to every question — including ones where your total evidence is silent, in which case nothing fixes which unique number you're required to hold.
(12) So rational credence has to be razor sharp about everything while being fixed by nothing.

I can't accept (12). It isn't a refutation of mushy credence, it's a refutation of the idea that evidence constrains belief at all, which is the thing I wanted to be defending when I walked in. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (8) and I'd rather keep the thought that evidence can leave you with a range.

So: which step do I reject? Walk me through why the step I should reject looked fine in the first place, and price the ones I keep — what do I owe on the coin case in particular? Do I have to say dilation is harmless, or that my prior was badly built, or that (1) isn't a norm at all? And if the answer is "your prior was wrong," tell me what a right prior looks like for someone who genuinely doesn't know whether the coins are matched but does know A is fair, because that seems to be the whole point of the example.
