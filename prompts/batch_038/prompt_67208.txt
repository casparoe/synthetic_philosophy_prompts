So there's this result in decision theory — Good's theorem I think — that says a rational agent should always accept evidence offered to them for free, because acting on more information can only ever raise your expected utility, or something along those lines. I've been chewing on it and I'm not sold. My intuition is the whole thing only works if you quietly assume you'll respond sensibly *afterwards*, and there's plenty of real cases where you wouldn't — my uncle would refuse a free test for an inheritable disease precisely because he knows the result would wreck him whichever way it comes out. And the usual reply, that a future you who acts myopically just doesn't meet the assumptions so the theorem isn't refuted, feels like it's smuggling in the conclusion.
