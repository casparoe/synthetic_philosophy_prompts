I am in the third year of a dissertation in formal epistemology on epistemic utility theory, and I am drafting the chapter that asks whether an accuracy-first framework can make any principled room for practical stakes. My supervisor has suggested that the cleanest way to test my proposal is to stage it as a dialogue between its two natural opponents, and that is what I should like you to write.

The characters are these.

- **Iris** is an accuracy-first epistemologist of the standard sort. She holds that accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good; that probabilism follows from the fact that any non-probabilistic credence function over a finite algebra is accuracy-dominated, relative to any admissible scoring rule, by some probabilistic one; that conditionalisation follows from the fact that it maximises expected accuracy; and that the admissibility constraints on scoring rules — properness, and hence immodesty, together with truth-directedness, additivity across the propositions in a partition, and continuity — are what stop the whole enterprise from collapsing into instrumentalism. On her view epistemic norms are stakes-neutral: costs bear on what one does, never on how one's beliefs fit the world.

- **Owen** is a stakes-sensitive epistemologist in the pragmatic encroachment tradition. He holds that whether a doxastic state amounts to justification can depend on what turns on being right, that belief is by its nature a state deployed in practical reasoning, and that a framework which announces accuracy to be the only epistemic good has not shown that stakes are epistemically irrelevant — it has merely relocated them to a less conspicuous place, namely the choice of scoring rule.

The proposal I want them to attack from both sides is my own, and I should like it stated in the dialogue as I state it here. Call it **Cost-Calibrated Accuracy (CCA)**: for an agent S at time t, a scoring rule s is admissible for S only if (i) s is proper, and (ii) the relative penalties s imposes on the two ways of being wrong about p track, at S's own credence levels, the ratio of the practical costs S faces in those two error-types. CCA is intended as a compromise: it retains properness, and so retains the dominance theorems, while admitting stakes through the back door of admissibility.

My worry, which I have not been able to resolve, is that (i) and (ii) may be jointly unsatisfiable in any non-trivial way. On one natural reading of (ii), the weights attach to the states of the world — being wrong matters more in the world where the bad outcome obtains — and on that reading the rule appears to cease being proper: the report that maximises expected accuracy becomes a distortion of the agent's conditional probability rather than the conditional probability itself. On a second reading, the weights attach to the propositions scored rather than to the states, and a short calculation suggests the recommended report is again a weighted compromise that departs from the conditional probability whenever the weights are unequal. I should like the two of them to determine whether there is any reading on which CCA is simultaneously proper and non-inert, or whether my compromise is empty, and I should like at least one of the two calculations actually carried out on the page rather than asserted.

Here is the case I want them to work through, with the numbers fixed so that nothing turns on my vagueness. Ada is a structural engineer responsible for a retrofitted road bridge. Let p be *the critical weld in the retrofitted truss is defective*. An initial radiograph is ambiguous, and Ada's own conditional probability in p on it is 0.30. Acting on p when it is false closes the bridge for a full reconstruction: a thirty-mile detour for a valley community over six weeks, at roughly £4m. Failing to act when p is true risks catastrophic failure and loss of life. I take the ratio of the costs of the two error-types to be about 1:20. A second radiograph is available at the price of a week's delay; conditional on p it reports a crack with probability 0.8, and conditional on ¬p it does so with probability 0.1. Three months later, and before Ada has learned anything further about the weld, heavy goods traffic is banned from the bridge, and the cost ratio falls to something like 1:3.

I should like them to address, in the course of the argument and not as a checklist read aloud:

1. What CCA tells Ada to believe after the first radiograph, and whether the answer is simply 0.30 because properness has been retained — in which case Owen has bought nothing at the synchronic level and must either concede as much or surrender properness.
2. Whether CCA changes the expected-accuracy value of running the second radiograph relative to a symmetric rule, and in which direction. I have not checked the sign, and I want it settled by computation, including the two posteriors.
3. What happens when the cost ratio falls to 1:3 with no new evidence. If nothing changes in Ada's credences, CCA looks inert; if something ought to change, Owen owes an account of why a shift in what is at stake, with no shift in evidence, is a reason to alter one's confidence that a weld is defective.
4. Whether CCA can generate genuine disagreement. Suppose Ada's high-stakes case sits alongside a colleague who is writing up the same radiograph for a methods paper at a cost ratio of 1:1. If both use proper rules, both expect their own report to be best, and it is not obvious that they disagree about anything at all — unless the rules differ in the partition they score, in which case they are not scoring the same objects. I want to know whether the granularity of the partition is where CCA's only real work can be done.
5. Owen's stronger move: abandoning properness altogether in favour of a modest rule that licenses shading one's confidence away from the costly error. Iris will reply that this forfeits the dominance argument for probabilism, which is the framework's prize. But Iris faces a reciprocal charge, and I want it answered rather than waved away: if her admissibility constraints are motivated by nothing more than the fact that the theorems require them, the choice of constraints is transparently question-begging.
6. Whether CCA extends from practical to moral costs. Suppose the proposition is not about a weld but about whether a junior colleague falsified test data, and the cost of a false positive is the ruination of that person. If CCA extends, the framework licenses believing against one's evidence for moral reasons; if it does not, Iris owes a principled line between two things that are both costs of error and both enter through the same channel.

Requirements. Write between 1,600 and 2,000 words, and set the scene in a sentence or two at most — a whiteboard, an afternoon in my department — because I want the argument rather than the atmosphere. Neither character may be a strawman: each should be as good at the other's job as at their own, each should engage with what the other has actually just said, and each should make at least one substantive concession in the middle of the exchange rather than at the end. Do not conclude with a conversion. Conclude with the crux stated plainly: the single assumption each would have to give up to accommodate the other, and which of the six points above each regards as fatal to CCA. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations — I know the literature, and what I need is the reasoning. Please write the whole thing in British English, in spelling, punctuation and idiom alike.
