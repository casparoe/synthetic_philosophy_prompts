For the past two years I have been convening a monthly reading group on political economy and social epistemology, and one word has become a standing grievance between us. Half the group reaches for Moloch — the name now commonly given to a multipolar trap — whenever an industry settles into a practice that makes everyone in it worse off; the other half insist that the term is largely rhetorical, that in any given case the supposed trap reduces either to straightforward bad incentives, in which case it is a Prisoner's Dilemma with better branding, or to straightforward ignorance, in which case it is not a trap at all but a mistake that better information would correct. I have come to think the dispute can only be settled by construction, and I should like you to build the pair of cases that settles it, to the specification below. Please write your answer in American English; my own habits are British, but the group's reading is almost entirely American, and the handout will be circulated to visiting students from across the Atlantic.

The setting is the same in both cases and should be entirely ordinary: five independent tuition centres in a mid-sized city, competing for a roughly fixed cohort of pupils who are chasing a limited number of places at selective schools — no collusion, no communication between the centres, no regulator, no science fiction. The competitive practice is escalation: more contact hours, more homework, more mock examinations. It is exhausting for the pupils and expensive for the centres, and, because what the parents are buying is rank, it is of no benefit to anyone once all five do it. Case A must satisfy four conditions at once. (i) Give the payoffs in a compact table indexed by a centre's own choice and the number of rivals that escalate. (ii) Universal escalation must not be a Nash equilibrium: a centre that alone held back while the other four escalated must do strictly better than one that matched them. (iii) Neither strategy may be dominant. (iv) Nevertheless the industry must settle into universal escalation and stay there for years. The mechanism that holds it there must be a belief about rivals which the centres' own conduct, and their own reading of their industry's recent history, actively reinforce — not merely a belief that nobody happens to test — so spell out precisely what a centre observes each term, what it infers from that, and why the inference is false yet never gets corrected. You may not achieve this by making escalation dominant, and you may not achieve it by making the experiment of holding back unprofitable once uncertainty is priced in; if you do either, you will have written Case B twice and the exercise will have failed. Case B keeps the same industry, the same observation structure and the same two strategies, and changes nothing but the payoff table, so that universal escalation is the unique Nash equilibrium, is strictly Pareto dominated by universal restraint, and yet involves no dominant strategy — hence is not simply a five-player Prisoner's Dilemma. I should warn you that this last combination may be impossible if a centre's payoff depends only on its own choice and on how many rivals escalate; if you need extra structure (asymmetries between the centres, a third strategy, or a repeated stage game under the same observability), introduce the minimum of it, say plainly that you have done so, and re-run the checks. If it cannot be done at all, show why, and tell me what that impossibility would itself imply about the phrase race to the bottom.

Then run one intervention through both cases: a trade association begins publishing, each term, every centre's contact hours, homework load and pupils' results — information only, with no power to fine, contract, exclude or otherwise enforce anything. In Case A the publication must dissolve the trap; in Case B it must not, and if you can arrange matters so that it accelerates the collapse, so much the better. Explain in each instance exactly what the publication makes common knowledge, and why that does or does not touch the mechanism you have built. After the two cases, give me the honest engineering report: which single feature separates A from B and is doing the work; what becomes of Case A if you remove that feature while leaving the payoffs exactly as they were; a reply to the objector who insists that Case A is Case B once the cost of uncertainty is properly priced, together with your assessment of whether that objection lands; a reply to the objector who insists that Case B is nothing but a positional externality dressed in a mythological name, together with your assessment of whether the label earns its keep; and finally your own verdict on which of the two cases, if either, deserves to be called a multipolar trap. Keep each scenario to roughly two hundred words, keep the tables small enough to be checked by hand, and show the Nash and dominance checks explicitly rather than asserting their results. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list or citations of any kind — the group has read the literature and is heartily tired of it; what we need is the construction and the reasoning. A case that stipulates its verdicts instead of generating them from the table and the observation structure will be sent straight back.
