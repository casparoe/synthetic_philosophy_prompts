I am revising a draft of a short essay for a workshop on prioritization methods, and I would like your comments on it rather than a summary of it. The essay is reproduced below in full, followed by specific questions. Please write in American English. I am not looking for a reading list, citations, or suggestions about sources to consult; I want your own analysis of the reasoning.

**Draft: "Neglectedness Is Not a Third Criterion"**

*1. The framework and its official justification.* The heuristic now standard in effective altruist discussions of cause prioritization says that a cause scores well insofar as it is important (a large amount of value turns on it), tractable (additional effort actually makes progress on it), and neglected (few resources are already devoted to it). The three are usually presented as roughly independent dimensions, to be scored separately and then combined. Neglectedness, however, is almost always defended by an argument from diminishing marginal returns: if a problem has already absorbed a great deal of money and talent, its cheapest solutions have been taken, so the next unit of effort buys less than the first did. On that defense, neglect is evidence about where we sit on a returns curve. It is therefore not a separate dimension of value at all, but a discount factor applied to the other two — a way of saying how much importance and tractability remain unclaimed at the margin.

*2. Four ways a cause can be neglected.* The defense works only when a cause is neglected because prior effort saturated it. But effort is not the only thing that fails to arrive. I distinguish:

(a) **Saturation neglect** — much work has been done; what is left is harder and less rewarding.

(b) **Infeasibility neglect** — little work has been done because there is, so far as anyone can tell, no lever to pull: no technology, no legal vehicle, no delivery channel, no constituency able to organize.

(c) **Salience neglect** — little work has been done because the problem is unappealing: disgusting, frightening, politically radioactive, aesthetically unglamorous, or bearing on people who are socially invisible (the very young, the very old, non-citizens, the incarcerated, nonhuman animals, the dead, the not-yet-born).

(d) **Opacity neglect** — little work has been done because the problem has not been measured, so there is no evidence base around which effort could be organized and no way to demonstrate impact to a funder.

*3. Only one of these supports the criterion; one is a double count; two are misfiled.* Category (a) is what the diminishing-returns defense licenses. Category (b) is tractability wearing a different hat: a cause with no lever is intractable, and scoring it low a second time for the same underlying fact makes the framework look more demanding than it is. Category (c) is a defect in the attention of the community, not a feature of the cause. Disgust, political risk, and social invisibility do not track a problem's magnitude, and if anything they are negatively correlated with the problem's being noticed by the people best positioned to solve it. So (c)-neglect can be a reason to act, but only as a wager against everyone else's calibration — a claim about the reliability of the reference class, not about returns. Category (d) is different again: where neglect is due to opacity, the highest-value marginal unit of effort is usually not an intervention but a measurement, and its product is epistemic — better estimates for everyone downstream, including people who will never work on this cause. The tripartite framework has no slot in which that return can be recorded.

*4. Two pathologies of the undecomposed criterion.* First, error in both directions. Because (c)- and (d)-neglect are lumped in with (a)-neglect, the framework recommends unpopular-for-irrelevant-reasons causes indiscriminately alongside genuinely under-worked ones, and it penalizes well-worked causes that are well-worked for good reasons. A field can be crowded precisely because it is the most important thing in the world and thousands of able people agree about that. Second, non-convergence. Neglect is defined relative to how many people are attending. A community that follows the criterion successfully renders its own recommendations less neglected and hence, by the criterion, less recommended. The framework has no fixed point: its output is always whatever its users have not yet done. That is acceptable in a generator of novelty; it is not acceptable in a criterion of value, and it would explain a familiar pattern in which an area's attractiveness to its own adherents decays on a schedule unrelated to any measurable progress.

*5. The reference-class problem.* Neglect is indexical. Measured against all philanthropic spending, nearly everything of interest is neglected. Measured against the resources controlled by the community applying the framework, almost nothing is. Measured instead against the number of people who could work on the problem if they chose a different career — as distinct from the number of dollars currently flowing to it — the ranking changes again. A heuristic that must silently select a reference class before it can produce a number is not the neutral tie-breaker it is advertised as.

*6. Proposal.* Retain the three-criteria heuristic for triage and for communication, but treat neglectedness as derived rather than primitive. Estimate expected marginal value directly (value at stake, times the probability that further effort moves the relevant quantity, times the slope of the returns curve at the current level of investment), and then handle (c) and (d) separately: the first as an explicit adjustment to one's prior about how well the reference class attends to what matters, the second as its own line item for measurement and model-building. Where the inputs are unavailable, say so and let the ranking stay coarse.

**Comments I would like**

1. On section 2: is the fourfold taxonomy exhaustive, and are its members genuinely distinct? In particular, does (d) collapse into (b) or (c), or does (b) collapse into (c)? If you think there is a fifth kind of neglect that my categories miss, name it and say which of my claims it breaks.

2. On section 3: is the double-counting charge against (b) fair? A defender might reply that tractability asks whether effort would succeed if applied, whereas neglect asks whether effort has been applied, and that these are different questions about the same cause, so no fact is counted twice. Does that reply restore independence between the criteria?

3. On section 4: is the non-convergence worry aimed at a position nobody holds? If practitioners keep importance fixed and use neglect only to rank among causes already judged comparably important, does the treadmill dissolve, or does it survive the restriction?

4. On section 6: does replacing a heuristic with a calculation commit me to pretending the inputs are knowable when they are not? Is the coarse three-criteria version better exactly because it forces its users to admit ignorance? Tell me plainly whether my proposal is the original framework with extra steps.

5. Identify the single strongest objection to the essay that the draft nowhere engages, and state it in its most damaging form.

6. Assess tone as well as substance: is the draft uncharitable to people who have found this heuristic useful, and if so, where does the uncharitability begin to distort the argument rather than merely decorate it?

7. Finally, flag any claim in the essay about how the framework is standardly formulated or standardly justified that you believe is simply wrong or overstated, and say what the more accurate statement would be.
