Stuck on something in the health/disease debate and want to check whether I'm missing part of the machinery. Boorse's biostatistical theory: disease is a statistically subnormal level of functional efficiency for your reference class, health is just the absence of disease, and the whole thing is supposed to be value-free. But "statistical" seems to make health whatever is common. Take BMI in the US — well over half of adults are now above the cutoff for normal weight, so on a literal reading the BST has to say the majority is healthy and the lean minority is the pathological one. Same with age-related hearing loss, myopia in East Asia, prediabetes. And it gets worse for public health: a population that's been stunted by famine or poisioned by lead would count as *healthy*, because that's now what's typical for them, and the naturalist supposedly has no resources to say otherwise. So doesn't the theory collapse into "whatever your environment does to you is your normal"? Is there something in the reference-class setup, or in the disease/illness distinction, that blocks this — or is this basically why the field drifted toward normativism?
