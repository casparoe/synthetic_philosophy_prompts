I need a seven-minute after-dinner address for the fortieth-anniversary dinner of the Halberd County Defender Project, a small nonprofit defender office in a Rust Belt county, and I want it built around an argument rather than around sentiment. The room will be about a hundred and twenty people: current and retired staff attorneys and investigators; two judges; a district attorney who is attending out of courtesy; the donors, several of whom fund the office's fines-and-fees project and would like to think of themselves as doing something quasi-charitable; the staff's spouses and children; and, at the head table, four former clients invited as guests of honour. The ballroom acoustics are poor, so I want short clauses and nothing that depends on a subordinate phrase landing correctly. I worked at the office for three years as a staff attorney before leaving to do a doctorate, and I now teach jurisprudence at a state university; everyone there knows both facts about me, which is part of why I was asked. My mother is driving up for the first time in years and still describes my job, to relatives, as "the law."

The spine I want is David Lewis's question about penal substitution — whether we in fact believe that one person can bear another's punishment — and specifically the contrast his answer turns on: we think nothing whatever of it when a friend pays your parking fine, and we think it not merely impractical but grotesque when someone offers to serve your prison term in your place. As I remember it, he also presses the point that the Christian version of the story is not the clean case of an uninvolved bystander punished for a stranger, because the one who exacts the penalty is, in that telling, also the one who pays it. I want the speech to use that structure honestly, without becoming a lecture, and without naming its theological setting in the first half. It has to be deliverable by me — I am an atheist and will not assert a doctrine I think false — while not betraying the office's founder, the Reverend Alton Pike, who is eighty-one, in a wheelchair, and in the room, and who used to tell new hires that the job was to be the person who shows up and says, put it on my account. Do not let the speech convert Pike's frame into a metaphor so thin that he would notice the swap, and do not let it either confess or conceal my own unbelief. The argument should also settle what the speech is permitted to say to the donors: someone who pays a stranger's fine is doing something we already accept as legitimate, so the address can thank them without flattering them, and can be explicit about why its gratitude stops where the money stops.

Concretely, then: 900 to 1,000 words; one example — a stated sum, a stated ticket — introduced early and returned to at the close, so that the second appearance means something different from the first; the only proper name spoken aloud is Lewis's, once, with no article titles, no recommended reading, no instruction to the audience about what to look up; no "it's an honour to stand before you," no triads of the hope-and-courage-and-commitment variety, no description of the room as a family; one sentence a former client would recognise as true about being represented by someone who cannot fix the thing that actually matters; one dry moment that the lawyers get and the children's parents don't miss either. Above all, the speech may not promise what defenders cannot deliver — nobody in that ballroom can serve anyone else's time — and any line implying otherwise should be cut before you hand it to me. The ending should leave the boundary between transferable and non-transferable costs standing as a live question rather than dissolving it into uplift, while still letting people walk to their cars without feeling ambushed.

Give me one speech, not options, variants, or bracketed alternatives: make the calls yourself and stand behind them. Then append a note of five or six sentences identifying the two or three hardest decisions you made and why — how you handled Pike's register, where you placed the second appearance of the example, whether the final paragraph lands on the question or on the people — together with your candid assessment of where my summary of Lewis takes a liberty, oversimplifies, or rests on a contested reading of the paper. I would much rather hear that from you now than from someone in the car park afterwards.
