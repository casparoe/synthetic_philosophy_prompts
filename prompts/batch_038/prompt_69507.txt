At our graduate reading group in the philosophy of mathematics — a mixed crowd of set theorists, analysts, and two of us from philosophy — a routine session on the Hahn–Banach theorem turned into a forty-minute argument between two members, Dev (a philosopher with constructivist sympathies, though not an intuitionist) and Miriam (an analyst who works with dual spaces). I was taking notes and neither of them would accept my summary afterwards, so I would like an independent adjudication of the exchange as it actually ran.

The occasion: someone had presented the standard construction of a Banach limit, and the group was tracing which steps do the real work.

Move 1 — Dev: the Hahn–Banach extension step is where the functional is produced, and it produces nothing. The proof invokes a principle whose intuitive support is a picture of choosing an element from each of a family of sets, iterated transfinitely; that picture is licensed by finite experience and then extrapolated. Since the principle is independent of ZF, asserting the functional exists adds no content beyond the conditional. The honest statement of the theorem is hypothetical: if you grant the principle, you get the functional.

Move 2 — Miriam: this conflates "cannot be exhibited" with "does not exist." By a diagonal argument, the reals outrun the definitions available in any fixed language, so ZF's own ontology is already crowded with objects no formula picks out — long before choice is invoked. Applied consistently, Dev's standard disqualifies most of ZF. Moreover, choice for a finite family of nonempty sets is provable in ZF, so his complaint about iterating a finite picture must be aimed at the infinite case specifically, where the finite picture was never the justification on offer.

Move 3 — Dev: he grants that, and relocates the objection. The problem is not definability but that the axiom is self-consuming: Banach–Tarski shows that the very geometric intuitions which make the picking picture seem harmless are contradicted by its consequences. An axiom whose motivating intuition is destroyed by its own theorems cannot be resting on intuition; and being independent, it cannot be resting on proof. What remains is convenience.

Move 4 — Miriam: the geometry was already gone without choice. The free group on two generators admits an explicit paradoxical decomposition, and Hausdorff's decomposition of the sphere minus a countable set is choice-free; consequently ZF by itself proves there is no finitely additive, rotation-invariant measure on all subsets of the sphere. The genuinely choice-dependent casualty is Lebesgue measurability on the line, and even the Vitali set needs only countable choice. So Banach–Tarski is not a reductio of the axiom of choice; it is a reductio of the assumption that every bounded set has a volume, an assumption ZF had already refuted for the sphere.

Move 5 — Dev: granted, but then the dispute is about cost, and cost is where the hierarchy of choice principles does the work. He proposes what he calls a proportionality constraint: a set-existence principle is legitimate in a proof only if its strength is proportioned to what the theorem's own statement quantifies over. Full choice, he says, is a "blank-cheque axiom" — one unlimited withdrawal rather than a line of credit drawn per use. On his accounting, Hahn–Banach follows from the ultrafilter lemma, countable unions of countable sets follow from countable choice, Baire category and ordinary analysis follow from dependent choice, and Tychonoff for countable products of compact metric spaces needs nothing stronger. Everything working mathematicians actually use therefore sits at or below dependent choice; full choice is needed only for the basis theorem and for arbitrary products, which are precisely the unbudgeted withdrawals. He adds a test: any theorem whose statement ranges only over countable or separable objects should require at most dependent choice, and a proof that reaches for full choice in that setting is a proof nobody has read carefully.

Move 6 — Miriam: two counters. First, she rejects the ladder picture Dev's constraint presupposes: countable choice is not innocent, since there are models of ZF in which the reals are a countable union of countable sets, and models containing a countable family of pairs with no choice function at all — so even statements about countable families of finite sets outrun ZF. Proportionality between the apparent size of a statement and the strength it requires fails in both directions. Second, his cost list understates the price of dropping choice: Tychonoff's theorem for arbitrary products of compact spaces is equivalent to full choice, and so is the claim that every vector space has a basis; free ultrafilters on the naturals cease to be provable, taking the Stone–Čech compactification, Stone duality, and ultraproducts with them; and the two standard definitions of finiteness come apart. She then presses the symmetry question: if that is the bill, why is an inaccessible cardinal cheaper? Dev wants every set of reals to be Lebesgue measurable, and Shelah's work shows that Solovay's construction cannot be had without assuming the consistency of an inaccessible.

Move 7 — Dev: the asymmetry is the point, not an evasion. The inaccessible is scaffolding: it is posited once, to show that a world exists in which his principles cohere, and no proof inside that world ever cites it. Choice, by contrast, is cited inside proofs, thousands of times a day. He is not claiming the axiom is false — only that its truth is not something a working mathematician is entitled to assume silently, and that presenting these theorems as unconditional conceals a hypothesis that could in principle be tracked. He notes that where the tracking has been done it has produced results: Hahn–Banach turns out to sit strictly below full choice, while Tychonoff sits exactly at it. So the appeal to convenience is an argument for doing the audit, not for skipping it.

They stopped there, each convinced the other had conceded the central point. Please adjudicate move by move rather than globally: assign each of the seven moves a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification, and where a move contains several distinct claims, rate the claims separately. Treat the factual assertions about relative strength in moves 4, 5, and 6 as things to be checked on their merits rather than as rhetorical flourishes, and say explicitly which of them are correct, which are wrong, and which are right for the wrong reason. Then tell me who is right about what, whether Dev's proportionality constraint survives as a normative principle or is merely a description of a research programme, whether Miriam's symmetry challenge about the inaccessible lands, and what both of them overlooked — including anything about the shape of the hierarchy itself that neither noticed. Argue from the mathematics and the structure of the positions, not from authority or from who has said what before. Finally, state where your own adjudication is most likely to be wrong: which rating you would most expect a specialist to overturn, and what consideration would change your verdict on the exchange as a whole.
