So I'm on a working group at a mid-sized public university that has to decide whether to replace the student records system we've been running since the late 90s, and two people on the group keep saying "history matters, this is a path-dependent decision" while using it to argue for opposite things. One says because we're path dependent, ripping out the old system is hopeless — we'd just get locked into a new bad thing, so the rational move is to keep patching what we have. The other says because we're path dependent we must not rush, we should sit tight another five years until the vendor market settles, since whatever we pick now will still be here in 2060. Neither will elaborate; the phrase just gets deployed to end the discussion, and its starting to feel like it means whatever the person saying it needs it to mean.

Can you lay out, as a list I can actually bring to the next meeting, the different precise claims that "history matters" / "this is path dependent" could be making — ranging from ones that are barely more than a truism to ones that are genuinely strong and debatable? I'm thinking along the lines of: does it just mean the past causally constrains the present, or that undoing the original cause doesn't undo the effect, or that whatever advantage the incumbent has gets amplified the longer it holds it, or that small early differences never wash out, or that among several options that were once roughly equally good one won for reasons nobody could have foreseen, or that no amount of later spending can buy you back onto the road not taken. Those may overlap or be wrong, that's part of why I'm asking.

For each reading, say (a) how plausible it is as a description of how enterprise software and data standards actually get stuck, (b) what would have to be true empirically — what would count as evidence for it and against it — and (c) which of my two colleagues it would actually license, if either. And if there's a reading under which both of them are right at the same time, or one under which they're both wrong for the same reason, spell that out explicitly, because I suspect that's where the real disagreement is hiding. Finally, flag any place where the wording is doing work the content can't bear — like if "locked in" is quietly being used to mean switching costs are infinite rather than merely large, or if "we'll still be using it in 2060" is a prediction dressed up as a law.
