Last Thursday our reading group (six of us, meets in someone's kitchen, mostly people who did philosophy as undergrads and never quite got over it) got onto Schlick's essay on the meaning of life -- the one he published in 1927, though you'll sometimes see it dated to the early thirties because he folded the material into his book on ethics. Almost nobody in the room had read it, and two people, Ines and Rafe, ended up going at it pretty hard while the rest of us ate too many biscuits. I'd like you to referee. You don't need to know the essay, so here's the part they were arguing over, in my own paraphrase rather than in quotes. Schlick thinks the question about life's meaning gets asked mainly by people who have taken morality's demands very seriously, and that the answer is not more seriousness. The meaning of life is play -- activity carried on for its own sake, with no further purpose hanging off it -- as against work, which is always a means to something else. Because play is the form of activity that carries its own justification, the qualities of youth matter so much on his account: the young do things out of curiosity and delight, for their own sake, while the old mostly do things for the sake of other things. Even knowing things is a species of play, and the struggle for scientific truth is its own reward, with the usefulness that falls out of it beside the point. There's also a framing device in which an older professor, whom the narrator dubs a new Epicurus, lectures him on the difference between work and play.

Then it went like this. (1) Ines said this is the best answer to the meaning-of-life question she's ever come across, because it puts meaning in the form of an activity rather than in its results, so a life can be full of meaning without achieving anything at all. (2) Rafe said it's arbitrary and, worse, ageist: on that reading meaning peaks in youth and declines after, which would make a 70-year-old's life less meaningful than a 19-year-old's by default regardless of what either is actually doing, and it turns growing up into a loss of meaning rather than, say, a gain of understanding. (3) Ines said he'd flattened the claim -- Schlick isn't ranking people by birth year, he's identifying a quality, doing-things-for-their-own-sake, that happens to be commoner in the young, and the youth talk is just his way of pointing at it; a 70-year-old who paints badly and joyfully every morning is playing, while a 19-year-old grinding through a degree purely for the CV is working. (4) Rafe said fine, but then play has collapsed into whatever-you-enjoy-for-its-own-sake, and the theory stops telling you anything, because it can't explain why a life spent joyfully collecting bottle caps is less meaningful than one spent joyfully doing mathematics -- and Schlick plainly wants to say that, since he treats the joy of knowing as the paradigm case. (5) Ines said he doesn't want to say that at all and Rafe is smuggling in a productivity standard; if the cap collector really is delighted, the view is committed to calling that a meaningful life, and that's a feature, not a bug. (6) Rafe said then it's empty in the other direction too, because it can't handle the case he actually cares about: his aunt, who has had severe depression for years, has raised three kids and kept a very promise-heavy life going, and would say without hesitation that her life has meaning even though almost none of it feels like play or joy -- so does Schlick really have to say her life is meaningless? (7) Ines said the honest answer is yes-in-a-sense, because Schlick keeps apart two things Rafe is fusing: whether a life is admirable and morally serious, and whether it has that luster, that self-justifying joyfulness, that makes you want to call it meaningful; her aunt's life can be good and dutiful and worth living while lacking meaning in Schlick's sense. (8) Rafe said that's exactly the problem -- if meaning-of-life gets defined so as to exclude the thing most people are after when they ask the question, then Schlick hasn't answered it, he's changed it, and Ines's defense amounts to saying the theory is right because it's about something else.

Where do they land? Please go through moves 1 to 8 one at a time and rate each one -- clearly right / mostly right / mixed / mostly wrong / clearly wrong -- with a sentence or two of justification, and be specific about which part of the move earns the rating, since several of them are half-good and I don't want the halves averaged together into mush. Then say who is more right overall, and what both of them seem to be missing: for instance whether meaning in Schlick is doing the same job it does in the ordinary question what-is-the-meaning-of-life, whether the work/play distinction can actually bear the weight Ines puts on it (does it survive cases where you do something both for its own sake and for the outcome, like a surgeon who loves operating and also wants the patient to live?), whether there's a reading of the youth claim that's normative rather than statistical, and whether Rafe's ageism charge has any force left standing after move 3. I'm not on either side here -- I genuinely couldn't tell on the night, and I'll be reading your verdict out loud at the next meeting, so please be honest rather then diplomatic.
