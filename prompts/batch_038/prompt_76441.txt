I've been going around in circles on pain asymbolia and want someone to actually push on it rather than summarize it. Take two representational accounts of what makes a pain hurt: the imperative one, where a pain is roughly a non-conceptual command to stop the disturbance in this body part and protect it, and the unpleasantness just is that commanding force; and the evaluative one, where a pain presents the bodily condition as bad for you, and the unpleasantness is that evaluation. Now bring in the asymbolics — normal thresholds, they'll tell you a pinprick hurts, but they dont withdraw, sometimes laugh at the stimulus, and insist it doesn't bother them, usually after damage around the left insula. How does each account handle that split, and does one of them have to bite a bullet by saying the asymbolic isn't really being commanded, or isn't really in pain at all? Related: how should either account respond to the newer pushback that the clinical evidence doesn't even support the standard reading, i.e. that asymbolics simply aren't having pains and their reports are confabulations? Which view does the syndrome actually favor, and what does that imply about whether pain's badness lives in the felt quality itself or in the subject's standing relation to their own body? Please answer in American English.
