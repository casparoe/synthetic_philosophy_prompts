Quick favor — our department's spring symposium is running a two-speaker panel and I got stuck chairing it. Speaker A is a wavefunction realist of the Everettian stripe: for her the quantum state is just a physical thing, roughly a field living on a high-dimensional configuration space, and branching is what that field does. Speaker B is a QBist: for her the quantum state isn't a description of anything out there at all, it's an agent's own catalogue of betting commitments about what she'll experience if she intervenes, and she'll tell you within two minutes that the whole ψ-ontic / ψ-epistemic axis is a trap because it already grants that there's some underlying state-space the quantum state is or isn't a distribution over. Both are very good. Both are extremely practiced at talking past each other. I need questions that don't let them.

What I want is eight *paired* questions: each pair takes up one issue and puts it to both of them, phrased so the two answers land on genuinely comparable ground — ideally the two halves of a pair differ only in which assumption each speaker has to own. Then after each pair, one follow-up per speaker, keyed to the dodge I'm confident they'll reach for. Build the follow-ups to close the dodge off rather than to reopen the topic.

Issues I definitely want covered:

- The no-go result on the reality of the quantum state — the one whose key premise is that two systems prepared independently have uncorrelated underlying states. Press A on how much her realism actually gains from it (does it show the state is real, or only that *if* you've already bought an ontic-state framework then the state can't sit in the overlap?), and press B on whether refusing the framework is a principled dissolution or just immunity-by-redescription — what would she have to say about a view that accepts the framework and lands on her side of it?
- Then a pair built on a case I made up for this panel, which I'd like you to state crisply in the question itself: two labs, nominally running the same preparation procedure. In run 1 the two apparatuses are genuinely isolated from one another. In run 2 they are secretly slaved to the same random noise source, so their outputs are correlated from the start. Nobody in either building can tell which situation they're in, and the quantum description is identical in both. Ask each speaker whether there is a fact of the matter about which run is which, what their view commits them to about that fact, and — the bite — whether the premise the theorem needs is a claim about the world or a claim about how we're allowed to model it. I want A to say something about whether her realism makes the premise true, and B to say something about whether an agent can coherently *assume* it without believing it.
- Decoherence: for A, whether decoherence can single out a preferred basis without help from anything non-unitary, and what happens to her account if the basis choice looks conventional; for B, what decoherence even *is* on her picture, given there's no physical branching process — is it a consistency constraint on an agent's assignments, and if so whose?
- Friend-in-a-box cases: for A, what the word "outcome" is doing when the friend has one and Wigner doesn't; for B, whether the friend's own state assignment can be wrong about the friend's own experience, and if not, in what sense her probabilities are answerable to anything.
- Probability: for A, how a Born weight is supposed to do any work when every branch happens; for B, if her probabilities are no more objective than tastes, what explains the striking convergence between different agents' assignments, and is that convergence a miracle or a platitude?
- Close with a meta-pair: if no experiment even in principle separates the two views, is this a substantive disagreement or a choice of vocabulary? Force each of them to name one thing they'd give up if the other turned out to be right, and make sure the thing they name is actually load-bearing rather than decorative.

Two housekeeping things. First, after each question give me a one-line gloss of what an evasive answer would sound like, so I can tell in real time when to push — I'll be reading these off a page while moderating, so keep the glosses short and blunt. Second, flag which single pair is the one I should cut if we're down to twenty minutes, and say in a sentence why that one is the most expendable.

Keep the whole thing under about 1200 words. Don't have the questions hinge on any speaker having read a particular paper, and don't ask them to cite anything — the room is mixed and half of it is undergrads who've had one term of quantum mechanics and no formal epistemology, so the physics content should be statable in a sentence or two of plain prose each time.
