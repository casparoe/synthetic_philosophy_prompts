ok so my roommate and i just spent three hours arguing about the fiscal rule going into our constitution next spring — structural deficit cap, entrenched, amendable only by a two-thirds majority. his line, repeated enough times that i can do the impression: it's a Ulysses contract, the demos lashing itself to the mast so it can't steer onto the rocks the next time some gorgeous tax cut or panic-driven bailout looks irresistible, and anyone who opposes it is just saying the electorate should be trusted with the wheel every single moment of every single day. my line was less clean, and somewhere around the second bottle we stopped talking about the policy entirely and started fighting about the analogy, which is what i actually want settled.

because i think the analogy is carrying way more weight than it can hold. odysseus uses two completely different devices in that episode and everyone smashes them into one: the ropes on himself, and the wax in the crew's ears. one is capacity-binding — you keep wanting the thing, you keep hearing the song, you just can't act on it. the other is preference-shaping — the song never reaches you. which one is an entrenched deficit rule? and which one is civics class, or the fact that basically nobody can explain what the rule actually says? he said the ropes, obviously, and then i said something i'm still not sure was right: that the ropes are only ropes because of the standing order — circe tells him that if he begs to be released they should tie him with more bonds, so the real machinery isn't the rope at all, it's a set of instructions to a third party to refuse the future self's explicit, sincere, screaming request to be let go. that has got to be the constitutional court, and it's the bit that makes the whole picture feel kind of authoritarian rather than prudent. also, if i remember the poem right the sirens aren't offering pleasure, they're offering to tell him things — they claim to know everything that happens on earth — so the binding is against the most attractive kind of knowing rather than against something shameful, which sounds much less like overspending and much more like something else entirely.

so: audit it. i want the mapping done properly and then stress-tested, and i want to know whether either of our conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table — his "trust the rope" or my "it's wax wearing a rope costume." be explicit about which disanalogies actually bite for this use, a fiscal rule entrenched against a bare majority, and which are decorative; i genuinely don't care that odysseus is one guy and a polity is millions if that turns out not to do any work. and please no bibliography, i want the reasoning, not a list of who else has said it.
