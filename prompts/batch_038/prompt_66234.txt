You are a philosopher with serious expertise in both classical Jain epistemology and contemporary philosophy of language. Before writing anything, spend a short bulleted passage (about 150 words) sketching the three strongest moves available to each side of the dispute described below, and note which ones you will actually deploy and in what order. Then write the dialogue.

**The case.** A municipal inquiry must publish a single sentence answering the question: *Did human-caused warming cause last year's flood?* Two consultants have been asked to draft it, and they cannot agree, because the science they are relying on answers two different questions. One framing asks how much anthropogenic warming changed the likelihood of a flood of roughly that magnitude in that catchment — on which the answer is a ratio, and comfortably above one. The other asks what would best explain this particular event, its own rainfall, its own antecedent soil moisture, its own failed levee — on which the answer is that warming contributed a modest increment to a storm that would very likely have flooded the town anyway. Both consultants accept all the same numbers. Neither thinks the other is lying.

**The characters.** One is a philosopher trained in the Jain tradition of many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*), who thinks the sevenfold scheme of conditioned predication — "in some way it is," "in some way it is not," "in some way both," and the further combinations that end in "inexpressible" — is exactly the right apparatus for writing that sentence. The other is an analytic semanticist who suspects the apparatus is either empty or incoherent.

Write a dialogue between them, roughly 1,300–1,600 words, in which they try to settle the sentence. The semanticist must press at least three objections and press them well: that the "in some way" operator is just an indexical, so the doctrine makes a trivial claim about language rather than a substantive claim about floods; that conjoining affirmative and negative predications either reintroduces a contradiction the scheme was meant to avoid or commits the Jain to true contradictions, and that the "inexpressible" predication looks like a placeholder for an admission of defeat; and that the doctrine, applied to itself, either loses its force or loses its scope. The Jain must answer with real philosophical resources rather than appeals to tolerance or mystical humility — including the distinction between describing a thing as a persisting substance and describing it as a sequence of modes, and an account of what makes the unqualified question defective rather than merely under-specified. Where the Jain reaches for an analogy from contemporary semantics (context-sensitivity, supervaluationism, the failure of a question to determine a proposition), the semanticist should test whether the analogy actually holds, and the Jain should be honest about where it breaks.

Both characters must be equally competent: no strawman on either side, each responding to what the other just said rather than to a prepared speech, and each conceding at least one real point. Assume your reader has never heard of *anekāntavāda*, so introduce each technical term in passing as it is used. Do not cite, quote, or name-drop primary texts, scholars, or schools, and include no bibliography; the argument should stand entirely on its own reasoning. End without a resolved sentence for the inquiry, but with the disagreement stated sharply enough that a reader can see precisely what would have to be settled to resolve it.
