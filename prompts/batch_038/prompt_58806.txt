I work as a programme officer at a small private foundation, and last winter we produced an internal primer for incoming analysts on how to think about trade-offs between beneficiaries. One section of that primer attempts to summarize Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and it has been read into our grant memos often enough that I now need to know whether it is sound. My own training is in philosophy, but I never took social choice theory, and when I showed the passage to an economist friend she said only that it was "confident about the wrong things" and would not elaborate. Here is the passage as it currently stands:

> "Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is the closest thing we have to a mathematical proof of utilitarianism. He begins from premises nobody seriously disputes: that each person's own preferences over risky prospects satisfy the axioms of expected utility, that society's ranking of those same prospects satisfies those axioms too, and that if every individual is indifferent between two prospects, society is indifferent as well. Everything after that is pure mathematics, and what the mathematics delivers is that social value must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. Moreover, since none of the premises says anything about who anybody is, the weights cannot but come out equal — Harsanyi's impartial observer, given an equal chance of being any member of society, ranks prospects exactly as a utilitarian does. The theorem therefore also disposes of the hoary objection that interpersonal comparisons of utility are impossible: no such comparison is ever required, because the only thing that enters the social calculus is each person's own ranking, and the arithmetic takes care of the rest. It follows that whoever rejects utilitarianism is committed to rejecting either the Pareto condition or the axioms of rational choice, and the latter is hardly a substantive commitment, being close to a definition. The practical upshot for anyone evaluating a policy is straightforward: work out the utility each possible outcome would produce, add these totals together, and choose the prospect with the largest sum."

Could you go through this sentence by sentence and give me an accuracy audit I could act on — that is, tell me what a specialist in social choice theory or its philosophical interpretation would accept without qualification, what is a defensible simplification that a primer could keep with a footnote, and what is simply false? I am especially interested in three things. First, which of the errors, if any, are load-bearing: does the argument the passage is making collapse without them, or would correcting them leave the same conclusion standing on a weaker footing? Second, I have a suspicion, which I cannot defend, that the passage has run together two distinct pieces of reasoning and has also slid from a claim about prospects to a claim about realized outcomes; please tell me whether either suspicion is right, and if so what the corrected claim would be. Third, where the passage states something on which competent specialists genuinely disagree rather than something they would regard as an error, please say so explicitly and sketch the disagreement, because I do not want to rewrite the primer so that it takes a side in a live controversy without flagging that it is doing so. Finish by telling me what the theorem, correctly characterized, actually licenses us to say about how a foundation should weigh competing beneficiaries — whether that is more or less than the passage claims. I will supply the references myself, so please argue from the content of the positions rather than from citations.
