I am a junior at a public high school and the humor editor of our literary magazine. Our spring issue is built around the theme of optimism, and since our AP European History unit gave the Enlightenment about three days of coverage, I have spent the last several months reading Leibniz on the problem of evil on my own, along with some secondary commentary, mostly to understand why Voltaire found the whole position so unbearable. One of my co-editors proposed an "Ask Dr. Pangloss" advice column, and I have vetoed it: that joke has been made so many times that nobody in my class would read past the headline. I want the opposite — a piece that takes Leibniz's actual machinery with complete seriousness and is funny because of how well the machinery works, not because it is mocked from outside.

Please write a satirical document of roughly 900 to 1,100 words, formatted as a completed final project report submitted by a principal investigator to a funding agency: numbered sections, a deliverables table with a performance-metric column, a budget variance explanation, a risk register, and an appendix of auditor queries and responses. The project being reported on is the design, construction, and launch of the world we are living in, and the report's governing claim is that it prevailed in a competitive selection because it scored highest against the agency's published criterion: the greatest possible variety of phenomena obtained through the greatest possible simplicity and order in the laws producing them.

The document should contain the following, and each item should be a real Leibnizian thesis wearing a compliance officer's suit rather than a joke stapled onto one:

- A procurement section explaining components that were individually superb but could not be shipped together, on the grounds that worlds are assembled from sets of mutually compatible substances, and that certain otherwise admirable substances are admissible only in designs that score worse overall.
- A competing-bids section covering at least two rejected proposals: one which argued that there was only a single candidate design and therefore no selection to perform, so that the agency's competition was a formality and the winning investigator deserves no credit; and one which proposed to govern everything by a small number of general rules and to let particular outcomes fall where they may, which the panel scored as under-delivering on particular goods without meaningfully improving the simplicity of the rules. Do not name the historical figures behind either bid; let a reader who knows them recognize them from the description.
- A budget variance section accounting for one very large line item covering widespread malfunction, waste, and harm in the delivered world, entered as "won't fix." The report should insist that this was not introduced by the build but is a limitation inherent in any created component at all, which could not be removed without regressing other deliverables. It must never claim the investigator's hands were tied by necessity, and it must never appeal to mystery: it should hold, without flinching, that other designs were genuinely available and were freely declined, while also holding that any single alteration to this one would make the total worse.
- An auditor's query asking whether the report's own account of the build means every outcome was necessary all along, and a response that distinguishes being certain of an outcome from its being necessary, invoking the idea that some truths can be resolved only by an analysis that never terminates. This answer has to actually answer the auditor.
- An annex on archived candidate designs that were not funded, noting that they remain under version control and retain a standing claim to be instantiated someday, proportionate to their merit.
- One post-launch incident report: several deployed agents behaved contrary to specification, and the change request to patch them was denied because the patch would have removed the very capacity the incident presupposes.

Two further constraints on the craft. The investigator and the investigator's employer are never named anywhere; use agency euphemism throughout — the Commissioning Authority, the Principal, a project code — which should make the whole thing funnier rather than vaguer. And the prose should stay flat, procedural, and faintly aggrieved about the paperwork from the first line to the last: no wink to the reader, no closing paragraph that steps outside the document, no line acknowledging that any of this is a metaphor. Someone who has never read Leibniz should be able to follow the report and laugh at it on the strength of concrete bureaucratic examples; someone who has read him should recognize that nothing in it is invented in substance, only exaggerated in format.

Our print section has a hard cap, so please stay inside the word limit rather than sending me something I have to cut.
