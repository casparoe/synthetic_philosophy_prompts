The problem set question was:

"Parfit (in Reasons and Persons) asks us to consider a fourteen year old girl who decides to have a child now rather than waiting a few years. Because of the time-lag involved in conception, if she waits she will conceive a numerically different child, one whose prospects are much better. The child she does have will have a life well worth living, though a worse life than that other child would have had. (a) Explain why this looks like a moral mistake even though, on a harm-based account of wrongness, the child she conceives cannot have been harmed. (b) Would adopting a person-affecting restriction on wrongness help resolve the difficulty? Explain."

Student's answer:

"For (a), the mechanism is the time-lag. Waiting a few years means a different sperm and egg, so it isn't that the same child gets a worse start in life, it's that a numerically different child exists at all. That's why Parfit calls it a non-identity problem. The child who is born has a life worth living, but she is still worse off, because there is a possible child who would have had a much better life if the girl had waited. So on a harm based account, which says an act is wrong only if it makes things worse for some particular person, there's nobody to point to: the girl isn't harmed by being conceived, since her only alternative was never existing, and never existing isn't worse for her. That's the tension, the choice looks clearly irresponsible but the harm account can't locate a victim.

This is also basically the repugnant conclusion in miniature. The girl's choice brings about a larger amount of life at a lower quality, and Parfit's worry is that the total view can rank such an outcome as better even though it's clearly worse, so the case pushes us to give up the total view as well.

For (b), I think a person-affect restriction is the right move. If wrongness has to be tied to how things go for particular people, then we can say the girl's choice was wrong because it went worse for her child than it needed to. The child can point to the better life she was denied, even if nobody else can. So the restriction resolves the problem rather than creating it."
