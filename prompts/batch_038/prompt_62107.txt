I'm giving a department colloquium in about three weeks and what I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not another defense of the view — im pretty settled on the view, I'm scared of the room.

The thesis: "collapse" is a real category and not just a slur that scholars should stop using, but only if you define it by rate rather than by level. My version is that a society collapses when it loses the capacity to reproduce its own most complex institutions within a single generation — the moai-carving and transporting polity, the palace-administered redistribution of the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the divine-king courts and the habit of raising dated inscriptions in the southern Maya lowlands. Three consequences I actually intend to defend out loud:

1. People aren't the unit. Demographic and linguistic continuity is fully compatible with collapse, so Rapa Nui can be a genuine case even though Rapanui are still there and their ancestors didn't vanish — and that cuts against both the ecocide melodrama and the resilience correction that replaced it, since the resilience people mostly show that the food system kept working, which my definition never said it wouldn't.
2. Only vertically integrated systems can collapse — ones where the periphery depends on a core for redistribution, specialists, and legitimacy. Horizontally networked systems can shrink, fragment, or reorganize without collapsing, which is why I think a lot of the "systems collapse" writing about the Bronze Age is describing robustness and calling it failure.
3. The concept is classificatory, not explanatory. Saying a collapse happened tells you nothing about why; the causes are always local and dull (a drought signature in the Yucatán speleothem and lake records, metal flows rerouting, the palaces' own extractive demands).

I also plan to concede up front that the word has been used badly — the Easter Island story is a colonial morality tale in which islanders are supposed to have done to themselves what was substantially done to them — and say I want to keep the term anyway. I know that concession is where I'm most exposed, since it hands my critics the moral high ground and then asks them to give it back.

The room: maybe a dozen anthropological archaeologists, two of whom work in the Maya area and live and die by the paleoclimate proxies; an economic historian who studies state failure and will want to know what my definition buys her that hers doesn't already; a complexity scientist who builds agent-based models and will say "vertically integrated" isn't an operational variable; and three or four grad students steeped in the collapse-is-a-colonial-narrative literature who think I'm rehabilitating a toxic term in order to keep writing sweeping books. Nobody there is a philosopher, so please don't hand me vagueness or sorites worries about "a generation" — those are the objections I'd get somewhere else, not here.

Give me exactly seven numbered items in the order they'll come, most damaging first. Each item should be three short sentences at most: the objection in the words that particular person would use, their vocabulary not mine; my reply, sized for the sixty seconds I'll actually have; and one line on where that reply is soft. Item seven should be the question I'm praying nobody asks, and it needs to be a real one, not a strawman I can swat. No preamble, no closing advice, no reading list — I'm not asking you what to go read, just the seven.
