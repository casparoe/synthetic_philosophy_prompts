Bit of an unusual ask. I'm chairing a process for a combined authority somewhere in the north of England: they have to decide how to settle a genuinely unpopular road-charging scheme, and the options on the table are (a) a straight public referendum, (b) commissioning a citizens' assembly of about sixty randomly selected residents whose recommendation goes to the mayor, or (c) some sequence of the two. Two local groups have been campaigning on opposite sides for a couple of years and have completely stopped arguing with each other and started arguing past each other, so the council asked me to run an adversarial collaboration between them and produce a single document both will actually put their names to before the decision gets taken. That document is what I need you to write.

Camp A, the referendum side: a mass vote is the only device that can legitimately settle a question that big, because legitimacy comes from the whole electorate acting rather than from a sample of it however well-informed; a mini-public is unelected, tiny, chosen by a contractor, and its agenda and its expert witnesses are set by whoever commissioned it, so it's a gift to whoever writes the terms of reference; and the French climate convention is what happens when you let citizens recommend and politicians filter.

Camp B, the sortition side: referendum campaigns are structurally distorted by money, by question wording, by the fact that most voters are rationally ignorant and take their cues from tribal signals, and by the fact that a yes/no vote doesn't even express a determinate will once you notice majorities can cycle; and the Irish sequence — an assembly deliberating first, then the country voting — is the design that actually works.

The bits I want the document down in the weeds on rather than waving at:

- Camp B leans on something like a jury-theorem argument for deliberation. Camp A's answer is that the competence condition (each voter better than chance at getting it right) makes no sense when the question isn't a matter of fact but a clash of interests, and that deliberation can push a group further from the answer, not closer. Which of these is right, or are they two different claims being run together?
- There's a judgement-aggregation result — the one where a group votes consistently on each of several connected propositions and still ends up holding an inconsistent set of conclusions. Camp A wants to use it against assemblies (your sixty will be collectively incoherent too). Camp B says it's an argument for deliberating over the reasons instead of counting votes. Say plainly if one of them is misusing it.
- The cycling objection cuts against Camp A's own rhetoric about the will of the people. Do they have a real answer, or do they have to give up the phrase?
- Advisory vs binding, and whether an assembly gets any agenda-setting power, strikes me as doing more work in this dispute than either side admits.
- Switzerland and California as the two empirical counterexamples each side will reach for, and what those two cases actually differ in.

Format: a numbered section of claims both camps can sign; then a disagreements table with columns for the issue, the wording Camp B would sign, the wording Camp A would sign, whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal, and whether it's a crux; then the cruxes proper — for each one, the case or evidence both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, before anyone sees a result. If part of the dispute turns out to be about words, say which part and why.

Two house rules. Its British English throughout, please. And no bibliography, no reading lists, no named papers or authors — I can't check any of that and neither can the two groups, so refer to results descriptively.

Finally, and this matters most for my chairing: I want a short closing note that seperates out which of the things you've put in the agreed-claims section are genuinely settled among people who work on this, which are agreed only because these two particular camps happen to agree, and where there's live disagreement in the field that your document is quietly papering over. Don't hand me a contested position dressed up as the consensus — the last thing this process needs is me importing one by accident.
