Our hospital's Pharmacy and Therapeutics committee has spent a year drafting a Clinical Adequacy Protocol, and I sit on the subcommittee that wrote it. The protocol will govern, for the next three years, how standing committees at this 400-bed regional hospital choose among competing treatment regimens and formulary additions. It has four operative provisions.

First, the adequacy threshold. A regimen counts as adequate if its expected performance on the committee's outcome index — a weighted composite the hospital already uses for reporting — reaches at least eighty percent of the expected performance of the best regimen under consideration.

Second, the stopping rule. Once an adequate option has been identified and recorded, deliberation on that item closes. The committee does not continue comparing options, does not rank the adequate ones against each other, and does not solicit further evidence.

Third, the commitment clause. An adopted regimen stays in force for the remainder of its review cycle, ordinarily three years. It is not revised mid-cycle even if a superior option becomes known to the committee in the interim, absent a demonstrated safety hazard. The drafters' stated reason for this clause was to give clinicians a stable target and to protect the credibility of the committee's decisions.

Fourth, the recalibration rule. The eighty-percent figure is itself revisited at an annual audit, where it may be revised in light of the outcomes actually observed and the deliberative costs actually incurred.

The draft is finished and goes to a vote in two weeks. Two members have asked for thirty minutes on the floor beforehand to argue the matter out in front of us: Dr. Ilse Okonjo, a decision theorist who thinks the protocol licenses the hospital to adopt a knowingly inferior treatment, and Dr. Arun Raman, an internist with philosophy training who thinks a maximizing protocol would be unusable and would in fact harm patients.

Write the dialogue those two would have. Requirements as follows. Both of them must argue at full strength; neither may be made to miss an obvious reply, forget what the other just said, or concede in order to be agreeable. They must respond to each other's actual moves, so that the exchange would read differently if the speeches were reordered. I would like the argument to reach, among other things, the question of whether the stopping rule and the commitment clause stand or fall together, given that a defense from the cost of further search seems to reach only one of them; the question of what the phrase "eighty percent of the best" assumes about comparability, since the best is often unidentified precisely because the stopping rule halts before it is found; the question of whether the commitment clause can be defended by appeal to the reasons that the decision itself generates rather than to the quality of its outcome; and the question of whether an adequacy threshold silences the reasons favoring better regimens or merely outweighs them. If you find it useful to bring in the historical origins of satisficing, or the aspiration-level machinery behind it, do so as the characters would naturally, but the dialogue must be fully intelligible to someone who has never read that literature, and the characters may not quote from it.

Let it run about a thousand words. Have the two of them close by each stating, in a sentence, what they now take the real disagreement to be and what would count as settling it. Do not let them reach a tidy reconciliation unless one of them genuinely has a point to concede, and if so, make the concession specific — a named provision, not a mood.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose with no formatting: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold, no italics, no tables, and no separate commentary before or after the dialogue. Speaker attribution should be plain, in the manner of a transcript, with each character's name at the start of their turn and each turn set as an ordinary paragraph, the way one would type it into an email.
