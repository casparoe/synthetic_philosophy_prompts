The exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I can from memory (I have paraphrased; nothing here is a verbatim quotation):

1. **Nadia.** The chapter is a chronicle, not an explanation. It tells you what happened next, in order, and that is all it does. To explain why there was a general European war by August 1914 you would have to show that the outcome was to be expected given the antecedent conditions together with some general laws about how such systems behave — laws about alliances, mobilisation timetables, deterrence, whatever the right ones are. Historians never state such laws, and she took that not as modesty but as the reason their accounts cannot be tested and cannot be used: a narrative that does not subsume its subject under a generalisation cannot tell you whether the same thing is liable to happen again.

2. **Owen.** The demand is either empty or self-defeating. Press a historian for the law and you get either something so vague that anything confirms it ("states act to preserve their security"; "governments under strain defer hard decisions") or something specific enough that a single counterexample destroys it. The real content of the chapter is not general at all: it consists in showing that each decision made sense to the people making it, given what they believed about the others' intentions and what they thought they could get away with. That is a complete explanation. You show the action was the appropriate thing to do in those circumstances, and there is nothing left over to be explained.

3. **Nadia.** "Made sense to them" is not an explanation but a redescription in sympathetic vocabulary. Telling her that a system behaved as its parts were disposed to behave explains nothing until you say what the dispositions are and how widely they hold. She added that if Owen were right, historical claims would be answerable only to the participants' own testimony, and the comparative enterprise — asking whether 1914 resembles some other crisis — would be illegitimate, which she would not accept, because reasoning from precedent is precisely what analysts do for a living.

4. **Owen.** Comparing cases is legitimate and valuable; it simply is not subsumption. You learn from 1914 by understanding it and then noticing resemblances, not by extracting a rule and applying it. He said Nadia was conflating two different jobs: making an event intelligible, and licensing a prediction. History is in the first business, and insisting that it discharge the second is changing the subject.

5. **Nadia.** Explanation and prediction stand or fall together — that is the entire point of the covering-law picture, as she understood it. If you can explain why the war happened, you should have been able to say in advance that it would happen given those conditions. If you could not, you did not have an explanation; you had a good story.

6. **Owen.** Then almost nothing counts as an explanation, including much of the natural sciences, since we explain a great many things nobody could have predicted. Moreover the "law" Nadia wants for human action — that people do what seems best to them given their beliefs — is exceptionless only because it is empty. It cannot tell you which action will follow, so it does no explanatory work; all the work is done by the particular beliefs and perceptions, which are not law-governed.

7. **Nadia.** Very well: then concede that explaining an action always depends on some rough generalisation about how people respond to situations, even one nobody could write down precisely, and the dispute is only about how precise it must be. **Owen's** reply was that on that formulation she has abandoned the requirement altogether, because a generalisation nobody can state is doing no work.
