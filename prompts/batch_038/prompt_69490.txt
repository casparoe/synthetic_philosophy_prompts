I am in eleventh grade and I run the ethics discussion group at my school. Our next meeting is on moral trade: the idea that two people who disagree about what matters can each end up better able to advance their own values by exchanging actions instead of arguing. I stop doing something you object to, you take up something I want done, and both causes gain. Half our group thinks this is obviously sensible; the other half thinks it is obviously repulsive, and the two sides keep talking past each other because nobody can say precisely what they are objecting to. I would like to hand out an instrument that settles who is who before we start.

Please write it as a quiz with a scoring key, to these specifications.

**Four dimensions.** Sort readers along exactly these four questions. I drafted them myself; you may sharpen the wording, but do not add, drop, or merge any of them.

- *Scale*: can the goods at stake in a moral trade be weighed against one another on a single scale, or are they different in kind, with no exchange rate between them?
- *Authorship*: when you pay or arrange for someone else to do something you judge wrong, do you become an author of that act and answerable for it, or merely a cause of it, answerable only for how things turn out?
- *Standing*: does a trade that disposes of the interests of a being that cannot consent fail unless someone properly represents that being?
- *Publicity*: is an act performed as part of an open bargain a morally different act from the same act performed privately, or only a differently motivated one?

**Eight questions.** Write exactly eight case questions, two per dimension. Each must present a concrete situation with a first-person choice and two or three options. No option may name a principle or a view, no question may ask the reader what they believe about exchange rates or complicity in the abstract, and a reader who has never encountered the topic must be able to answer every question on intuition alone. Please include, among the eight, at least: a case where what is traded away is something you judge merely optional and unperformed rather than wrong; a case where the trade requires you to enable an act you judge wrong rather than to perform it; a case that is a mutual truce — both sides agree to abstain in some arena, such as a school board meeting — rather than an exchange of deeds; a case in which the traded-away interest belongs to something that cannot object; and a case in which the bargain is standing, published, and administered by an institution rather than struck once in private.

**Scoring key.** Present the key as a complete table over all sixteen combinations of yes and no on the four dimensions. For each combination, either give the position that occupies it a short descriptive name or mark it "unnamed," and for every unnamed cell say why it is empty: whether the combination cannot be coherently held, whether it is coherent but nobody would want it, or whether it is coherent, defensible, and simply unclaimed. At minimum, these five positions must appear somewhere in the table: the plain gains-from-trade view; a view permitting trades only where neither party is asked to do anything they judge wrong; the no-exchange-rate view; the view that trading damages the trader's own integrity even when it advances their cause; and the view that private trades are acceptable while open, institutionalized ones corrupt the meaning of what is traded. If two of these land in the same cell, say so, and either supply a further question that would separate them or concede that your instrument cannot separate them. Also state what the key does when a reader's two answers within one dimension conflict.

You do not need to cite anything or attach these positions to particular philosophers. We have not read the literature, and I would rather every label be descriptive than borrowed.

**A check that the key works.** Run your finished instrument on this reader. Nadia says the wetland and the stadium can be weighed against each other on one scale. She says that if she pays a contractor to pour concrete over an active nest, she has poured the concrete, not merely caused it to be poured. She says the birds' inability to object does not void the deal, because someone has to decide and she is entitled to decide. She says a bargain struck in the open is no different in kind from the same bargain struck in private, though she prefers the open one because it shows other people what things are worth. Report which cell she falls into and what your table calls it.

End your response with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing only the name your table assigns to Nadia's cell, in six words or fewer.
