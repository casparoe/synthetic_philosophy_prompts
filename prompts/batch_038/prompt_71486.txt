I am putting together the reading packet for the final session of an upper-level seminar on the metaphysics of causation, and I have run into a teaching problem: my students accept the counterfactual account without resistance and then treat causation by omission as a footnote rather than as the place where the account is in the most trouble. I would like a piece of satire to close the term with — specifically, a mock appellate opinion, roughly 900 to 1,200 words, from an invented jurisdiction that has adopted a pure counterfactual theory of causation by statute and is now living with the consequences. Please invent the court, the jurisdiction, the statute (something like a prevention-of-harm ordinance imposing liability on anyone whose failure to act made the difference), and every case and authority the judges cite; nothing real, and no verbatim quotation of any real text. Do not name a single philosopher: the arguments have to be recognisable to a specialist purely from how the judges handle them.

Here are the facts I would like you to build on. A canoeist drowned below a municipal weir. At trial, the estate recovered against nineteen defendants, each of whom was found to have caused the death by failing to prevent it: a passer-by who stood on the bank with a coiled rope and a mobile phone and used neither; a town councillor who three years earlier had voted against funding a warning sign; a marine outfitter who had never donated a lifebuoy to the site; a hydraulic engineer who had once studied weir flows and never wrote a letter to the local paper; a woman who was asleep in another hemisphere at the relevant time; and, joined by the trial judge's own logic, the deceased himself, for failing to wear a buoyancy aid. Damages were apportioned in equal shares. The case is now on appeal. The opinion should include a short syllabus or headnote of about eighty words stating the holding, a majority opinion in numbered paragraphs, a brief concurrence proposing a distinction between the nineteen that it cannot cash out without reaching for a normative notion, and a dissent from a purist who denies that absences cause anything at all and would dismiss against every defendant, visibly unbothered by the fact that this also exonerates the man holding the rope.

Two things I care about most. First, the comedy has to come from the metaphysics being handled correctly rather than from the law being absurd: the majority must at some point try to say which counterfactuals are close enough to count, in order to deal with the sleeping woman, and must discover that every similarity metric it proposes either admits her or throws out the man on the bank; it must confront the fact that the deceased's own omission looks exactly like the others, which would erase the recovery entirely; it must deal with the councillor's vote as a prevention of a prevention; and at least one judge must notice that if omissions are causes on all fours with acts, the distinction the ordinance itself is written in terms of has no work left to do. Second, keep it strictly deadpan — no winking, no footnotes explaining the joke, no judge ever saying that the court is facing a philosophical problem, and no summary of the moral at the end. The points should be made wholly in legal idiom: but-for causation, remoteness, foreseeability, duty, floodgates, surplusage. The target of the satire is the practice of settling a question about causation by appeal to what the law happens to require, and equally the purist refusal to notice what that costs; it is not any real person, court, or system. A reader who has never heard of the omission problem should still find it funny, and a reader who works on causation should find the satire fair. Please end with a disposition that refers the metaphysics back to the legislature.
