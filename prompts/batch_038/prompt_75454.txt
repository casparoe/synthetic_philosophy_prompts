For thirty-six years I worked at a bench — most recently measuring ion transport across epithelial membranes — and I retired four years ago with a good deal of accumulated leisure and no particular plan for it. I have spent that leisure reading the philosophy of mind, and this term the instructor of an advanced seminar at our local university has been kind enough to let me mark essays alongside her. We mark independently and compare notes afterward. Before I commit mine, I should like an assessment from someone more steeped in the literature than I am.

The question set was the following. It carried 40 marks, with a limit of 900 words:

> Explain what Chalmers means by the meta-problem of consciousness, and how it is to be distinguished from both the easy problems and the hard problem. Then consider this claim, made in seminar by a member of the class: "If a complete neurocognitive account of why we judge and report that there is something it is like to see red were available, the hard problem would be left exactly where it started." Say whether you accept the claim, and justify your answer. In the course of your discussion, address at least one position on which an explanation of our problem reports is held to settle the hard problem rather than to step around it.

Our scheme runs: 34–40 excellent, 28–33 strong, 22–27 satisfactory, 16–21 marginal, below 16 inadequate. The mark was to be apportioned as follows: accuracy in the exposition of the positions discussed, 40 per cent; cogency of the student's own reasoning about the seminar claim, 40 per cent; range, and the quality of the engagement with at least one contrasting position, 20 per cent.

Here is the answer submitted. It was within the word limit.

> The easy problems concern the performance of cognitive functions: discrimination among stimuli, categorisation, the focusing of attention, the availability of information for the global control of behaviour, and the deliberate reporting of mental states. The hard problem asks why the discharge of any of these functions is accompanied by experience at all, why there is anything it is like for the organism discharging them. Chalmers' meta-problem sits alongside these rather than beneath them. It asks not why experience exists, but why we say and believe the things we do about it: why we find the hard problem compelling, why we describe our experience as ineffable and unified, why we are drawn to talk of qualia in the first place. The explananda are our problem reports.
>
> The dialectical interest of this is easy to miss. Our reports are very nearly the only third-person evidence we have that there is anything to explain. If those reports can be accounted for in full by mechanisms which carry no information whatever about any further phenomenal fact, then the reports cease to be evidence for such a fact. Consciousness is placed under a kind of epistemic quarantine: it may exist, but nothing we say speaks for it. That is why a complete neurocognitive account of our talk about red would not, as the seminar claim asserts, leave the hard problem where it started. It would strip the hard problem of the evidential footing on which it stands.
>
> This is also, I think, why the meta-problem is best understood as Levine's explanatory gap restated at the level of our access to it. Levine's point is that no physical story makes it intelligible why certain states should feel like anything; Chalmers' point is that no physical story makes it intelligible why we should say that they do. The two are the same concern grasped at opposite ends of one rope — an intelligibility deficit that further mechanism cannot discharge — and treating them as independent problems multiplies difficulties needlessly.
>
> I want to press the consequence with a principle of my own. Call it report-parity: where two systems yield indistinguishable reports of the same experiences by way of the same functional organisation, there is no further fact of the matter as to which of them is conscious. A complete neurocognitive account of the reports would show that systems differing arbitrarily in their physical realisation could satisfy its conditions, so report-parity entails that consciousness adds nothing to the explanation of those reports. But if consciousness adds nothing to the explanation of our reports, then the reports are not about consciousness; they are about the mechanisms producing them. Consciousness, on this account, simply is the report-generating mechanism, apprehended from the inside.
>
> Chalmers draws precisely this moral and accepts the cost. He concludes that the meta-problem shows consciousness to be epiphenomenal with respect to our talk about it, and he treats that result as his principal argument for property dualism, since a property which does no explanatory work of its own is not the sort of thing the physical story was ever going to accommodate.
>
> Illusionism answers the seminar claim in a different register, and it is the position the question asks us to consider. Dennett and Frankish do not think an explanation of the reports leaves the hard problem untouched; they think the hard problem is what remains when a mistaken conception of what is being reported goes unchallenged. On Frankish's account introspection delivers a compressed and distorted model of the states it monitors, and it is in virtue of that distortion that we judge our experience to bear properties no functional description could deliver. The judgement that there is a further problem to solve is then not a datum to be preserved but part of the explanandum. This is a genuine engagement with the meta-problem, not a sidestepping of it, and it is the more economical of the two options available.
