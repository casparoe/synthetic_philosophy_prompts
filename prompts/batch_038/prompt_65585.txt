I host a philosophy-of-mind podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher whose recent book defends the view that what makes a pain bad is not any intrinsic phenomenal property of the sensory episode itself but a separate affective attitude the subject takes toward that episode — an attitude she treats as a genuine mental state with its own functional profile, not merely a disposition to withdraw or to complain. Her principal evidence is pain asymbolia: patients who, following unilateral hemispheric damage that appears to interrupt connections between sensory cortex and the limbic system in the region of the insula, localize and discriminate noxious stimuli normally yet do not withdraw, do not learn avoidance, and report that they feel the pain without minding it.

Please draft exactly five numbered interview questions for her, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each numbered item should run one to two sentences and should contain both the question and a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give; none of the questions should be answerable simply by restating her thesis, and none should concern her biography or her route into philosophy. Between them, the five items must cover: the phenomenological worry that the asymbolic's sensory episode may itself be attenuated rather than fully present and unminded, and how she proposes to tell the difference from the outside; the objection that her account renders the badness of pain merely contingent, so that a creature lesioned or engineered to lack the attitude is not thereby spared anything of value, and what she thinks this implies about the badness pain relief removes and about non-human animals; how her attitude is supposed to differ from the rival representational proposals on which a pain represents bodily damage as bad, or issues a standing command to protect a body part, and what evidence could ever separate those accounts from hers; the possibility that asymbolia leaves the affective component intact but disconnected from action and learning, so that its absence is inferred rather than observed; and finally, what observation or experimental result she would accept as counting against her view.

After the five items, add a single unnumbered line saying how confident you are in the empirical and philosophical claims your questions embed, and naming which of the five you are least sure of and why.
