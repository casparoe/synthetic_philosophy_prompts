I have now spent something like a decade reading rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums on governance, and I have noticed that whenever the word *corruption* appears, the discussion converges almost immediately on the same picture: there is a principal, there is an agent, the agent betrays the principal's interests for private gain, and reform means better monitoring and better incentives. I have come to suspect that this is not a theory of corruption so much as a tic — a default diagram we reach for because it is the one we know how to draw. So before I read anything else, I want the specification written down: the desiderata that any adequate account of corruption has to meet.

Fix the purpose first, since adequacy is relative to it. I want an account good enough to do two jobs: (i) to classify practices that reasonable and well-informed people disagree about, without smuggling in the classifier's own political culture; and (ii) to be usable by someone designing governance reform, including someone operating inside a system where patronage is the ordinary mode of administration rather than a deviation from it.

What I want is a list of requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it — not a list of slogans. To indicate the altitude I am after, here are cases the account has to land somewhere defensible on: the official who accepts a gift his entire community treats as obligatory at a wedding, and whose refusal would be read as an insult; the official who appoints her less-qualified cousin; the badly underpaid clerk who takes small payments for services the state is failing to deliver anyway; the legal lobbyist and the revolving-door appointee; the official who destroys a file in order to damage a rival, gaining nothing for herself; the official who diverts funds to build a school in her own district; the private-sector procurement manager who takes a kickback; and electoral manipulation that never involves anyone acting outside her formal authority. I also want the distinctions the account must not collapse — corruption against rent-seeking, against regulatory capture, against mere illegality, against ordinary self-interest in politics — and the empirical results it cannot contradict, among them that reported and experienced corruption come apart, that petty and grand corruption have different dynamics and different effects on trust, and that some rapidly growing economies have scored badly on corruption measures while some comparatively clean ones have stagnated, so no analytic link between corruption and development is available. Finally: the account must license at least some observable predictions. A definition that makes corruption undetectable in principle is not a theory of anything.

The part I care about most is the sorting. Tell me which of these requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are the parochial commitments of a particular camp — in particular, whether the demand that corruption be defined relative to a public/private distinction is a discovery or a prejudice, and whether the demand that corruption always harm identifiable victims survives the cases above. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say why; where two requirements pull against each other, I would much rather be told the list is unsatisfiable than have one of them quietly weakened. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate accounts.

Then, briefly — a paragraph each, no longer — score four families against the list: the office-and-rules-centered account; the market-centered account, on which an official treats her post as a business from which to maximize revenue; the principal-agent account; and the collective-action account, on which corruption is a self-enforcing equilibrium in which no principal capable of punishing defectors exists at all.

I want a self-contained argument, not a literature review: no citations, no reading list, no names. I would like to be able to check your claims against my own reasoning rather than against your references. A table is fine for the requirements. Please write in American English. And throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this seriously: where a requirement I have listed is itself a live dispute, or where you are endorsing a minority position, say so plainly instead of presenting it as the received view.
