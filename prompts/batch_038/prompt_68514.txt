A sidebar in a popular book on the multiverse frames the Everettian probability problem like this:

'If every outcome of a measurement really occurs, each in its own branch, then nothing in the theory singles out any one of them as the outcome that will happen, and probability looks unintelligible — yet experiment shows outcomes turning up at frequencies given by squared amplitudes. Deutsch and Wallace repaired this with a decision-theoretic argument: any rational agent who knows the universal wavefunction and cares about what happens to her future selves is compelled to bet exactly as the Born rule prescribes. The only assumptions are quantum mechanics itself plus ordinary standards of rationality, so the rule is derived rather than postulated. And since the branches genuinely differ in weight, and weight is what the argument shows a rational agent must bet on, Everettian quantum mechanics turns out to have objective chance after all.'

I found this convincing, but a friend who works on foundations of physics says it is over-enthusiastic in ways I can't locate. Could you audit it sentence by sentence? For each claim, tell me what someone sympathetic to the Deutsch–Wallace programme would accept, what they'd tolerate as a simplification, and what they'd reject outright. Two spots particularly puzzle me: whether 'cares about what happens to her future selves' is doing something quiet, given that I've also read there is no fact about which branch an agent ends up in; and whether 'the only assumptions are quantum mechanics plus ordinary rationality' is really available to them. Then, separately: if everything in the first three sentences were corrected, would the final inference to objective chance still go through, and what does the sidebar leave out that a critic would insist is the actual difficulty? Argue from the structure of the ideas rather than pointing me to the literature — I have no access to it.
