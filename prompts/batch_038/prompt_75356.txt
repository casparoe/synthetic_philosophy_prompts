I am in the second year of a philosophy MA and am putting together a review essay on the aggregation debate for a seminar on moral mathematics. Alongside the primary literature our instructor handed out a trade-press book on "the arithmetic of rescue," and its chapter on John Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" opens with a compressed statement of his argument that I found myself quoting approvingly in my first draft before a classmate told me I had been taken in. The passage reads as follows:

> Taurek's case against counting the numbers runs as follows. If you can save either five strangers or one man you happen to know, the temptation is to say that five deaths are worse than one. Taurek denies this. Badness is always badness for somebody, and there is no one for whom the death of the five is worse: each of them loses his own life, and nobody loses five lives. Five separate losses do not compose a single larger loss, any more than five separate headaches compose one very bad headache. So nothing is worse overall, and once that is granted there is nothing left that could justify saving the five — the numbers simply drop out of morality. What is left is sympathy: Taurek concludes that you should save the person you care about, and that if you care about none of them in particular, the only way to take everyone's loss seriously is to give each an equal chance with a coin toss. In effect he is defending the right to favour your own, a prerogative to give extra weight to the people you love. His critics reply that he cannot escape so easily: Taurek concedes that it is worse for each of the five to die than it is for the one, and if an outcome is worse for each of five people than an alternative is for one person, then it is worse, full stop. What Taurek is rejecting is not a moral principle but addition.

Could you go through this sentence by sentence and tell me how much of it survives scrutiny? For each stretch I want to know whether a careful reader of the paper would accept it, regard it as a harmless popular simplification, or judge it a genuine distortion — and, where it is a distortion, what the accurate formulation is. Three points especially. First, my classmate's objection was to the claim about what is left once the impersonal comparison is refused: he says Taurek explicitly allows that a justification for saving the five can be given which does not appeal to its being in itself a better outcome, and that the book therefore closes off a door Taurek leaves open. Is he right? Second, the summary reports that where the agent has no particular concern for anyone, Taurek's recommendation is a coin toss, but my classmate insists that in that same case Taurek is happy for the numbers to come back in. Both cannot be right, and I have no way to check the text this week; tell me which of them has it right, and how much weight the passage in question will bear. Third, does the book's gloss of Taurek as defending a prerogative to favour your own belong to him at all, or does it quietly substitute a different anti-consequentialist strategy for his? And separately: does the critic's inference — from worse-for-each to worse, period — follow from anything the summary states accurately, or does it simply reassert what Taurek is disputing?

One further request about how you handle this. This literature has a settled core and a great deal that is live dispute, and I need the two kept apart: please mark clearly which of your corrections are matters on which readers of the paper are simply agreed, and which depend on a contested interpretation of what Taurek is doing — whether his argument is chiefly about value or about reasons, whether he is a skeptic about impersonal betterness or only about its action-guiding role, and whether the objection from worse-for-each is thought by those who work on this to be decisive or merely pressing. Do not let one side of a standing disagreement appear to me as the consensus, and where you are reconstructing an argument rather than reporting what Taurek says, say so. I do not want a bibliography or a list of further reading; I want an audit I can act on, together with an honest indication of which parts of it you are less than fully confident about.
