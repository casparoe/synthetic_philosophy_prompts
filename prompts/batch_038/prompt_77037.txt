I'm part of a reading group working through the problem of evil, and we keep sliding past what now strikes me as the prior question. The debate we're having isn't about whether this world is the best of all possible worlds — it's about whether that phrase even picks out anything, independent of whether any deity exists or has creative options. So I'd like your credence, as a number, on the following claim, which I'll state as precisely as I can:

**P:** The class of genuinely possible worlds is structured such that (i) any two of them are comparable in overall value, (ii) the resulting "at least as good as" relation is a total preorder, and (iii) there is exactly one maximal element — a world than which none is better, and no other world equal in value to it.

Note that I'm asking about the axiological and structural preconditions, not about theology. P could be false even if God exists and creates, and P could be true even if nothing has ever chosen anything.

Two arguments are pulling me in opposite directions, and I'd like you to adjudicate between them rather than just report them. The case for P runs roughly as follows: a rational choice among alternatives requires a best option, since otherwise the chooser has no sufficient reason to select this one rather than that; and if possible worlds form a continuum of value with a well-behaved density of goods, a continuum should attain its supremum, so a maximum ought to exist. The case against P runs roughly as follows: for any world W, form W-plus by stipulating that W obtains throughout and that one further unit of value is added somewhere in it; W-plus seems strictly better than W, and the operation is always available, so the ordering has no greatest element but only an endless ascending chain. I can see three places where that argument might fail, and I want you to weigh each: it assumes value is unbounded above; it assumes that adding a unit of value to a world that may already contain an infinite total actually changes that total at all; and it assumes that "one more unit of value" is a determinate operation on a whole world rather than a gesture. There is also a separate route to the falsity of P that doesn't involve chains at all: worlds differing wildly in size, structure, or the number and kinds of valuable beings they contain may simply be incommensurable, so that (i) and (ii) fail before we ever reach (iii). And behind all of it is the suspicion that "the overall value of a world" is not a quantity with a determinate magnitude, in which case P has no truth value and the whole decomposition collapses.

So: give me your credence in P, decomposed. I want the sub-questions you think P actually depends on — comparability across heterogeneous worlds, the absence of unbounded value, the behavior of value under addition in infinite worlds, the well-definedness of world-level value totals, the uniqueness clause ruling out ties at the top, and anything else you consider load-bearing — with a rough probability for each and one line of justification, then an explicit account of how those terms combine into your final number rather than a bare product presented without comment. Tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to, and where you think the gap lives between my own credence of about 0.3 and my study partner's of about 0.75: at one identifiable sub-question, or in some prior disagreement about whether the question is well-posed at all. Then say what argument or discovery would move your number most, and in which direction.

Please don't hand me a survey of positions or a list of names and technical labels I could go look up; I want your own reasoning and your own numbers, argued for.
