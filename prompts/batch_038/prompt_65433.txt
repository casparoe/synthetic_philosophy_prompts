I'm convening a graduate seminar on the epistemology of disagreement in the fall and I want to open it with a piece of short fiction I've comissioned, not another parable about an oracle AI. The setup I have in mind: a near-future institute builds an instrument that reads a person's prior credence function straight off their brain, and the headline finding is that everyone measured so far has, within instrument noise, the same one. Triumph — disagreement is now officially a bug rather than a feature.

The heart of the story should be two co-leads of that project who have each other's complete data streams, who know each other's posteriors exactly and know that the other knows, all the way down, and who nevertheless sit forty points apart on one single question and have for months. I want the reader to come away feeling which assumption is actually breaking, without anybody in the story explaining it: maybe the priors aren't really common, maybe common knowledge of the posteriors is a polite fiction, maybe the machine isn't measuring a prior at all, maybe they're conditioning on different things, or maybe the theorem was just never about creatures like them.

Under 2,000 words, mostly dialogue, and please don't resolve it in the last paragraph — I want the seminar to fight about it for an hour. One more thing: no name-dropping Aumann or the agreement theorem inside the story itself. Let the structure carry it.
