I am a PhD candidate in philosophy and this semester I am the teaching assistant for an upper-level course on the philosophy of life's meaning. Our unit on the Vienna Circle's unexpected contribution to that topic ends with Moritz Schlick's essay "On the Meaning of Life" (Vom Sinn des Lebens), and I have to grade roughly eighty short responses to the same question by Friday. Before I commit to a grade on this one, I would like an independent assessment of it, since the student raised some of these points in section and I want to be sure I am not grading either too generously or too harshly out of that familiarity.

Here is the question we assigned:

In "On the Meaning of Life," Schlick appears to give a substantive answer to the question of life's meaning, and yet much of the essay is devoted to arguing that the question as ordinarily posed rests on a confusion. Explain how both things can be true. In your answer, discuss his hedonism, his contrast between work and play, and his treatment of the claim that life contains too much suffering to have meaning. Around 500 words.

And here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Schlick's answer to the question about the meaning of life is unusually direct: the meaning of life is life itself. Rather than looking for something outside life that would justify it, he says that life already carries its meaning in the living of it, and the search for a further purpose is a mistake produced by religious habits of thought. Once we see this, the question stops being a metaphysical puzzle and becomes a practical one, namely how life should be lived so that its meaning is actually felt.

For Schlick the answer is enjoyment. He is a hedonist, though a reflective one: what we want at every moment is pleasure, and the meaningful life is the one that gets the most of it. This does not mean the life of constant intoxication. Schlick points out that the pleasantest moment is not always the one we would choose, because a painful effort now can be part of a course of action that is, taken as a whole, the most enjoyable one available to us. The wise person therefore asks not which option is pleasantest on its own but which is the most enjoyable overall.

That distinction organizes his contrast between work and play. Work is activity carried out for the sake of something beyond it, while play is activity whose value lies in the doing. Schlick thinks modern life is far too dominated by work, and that we have lost the capacity to treat anything as an end in itself. This is why he attaches such importance to youth. The young are still capable of play; they do things for their own sake and so their lives have meaning in a way that grown-up lives, organized entirely around usefulness, no longer do. The best we can manage later is to preserve as much of that youthful attitude as possible, which makes Schlick's philosophy of meaning in the end a rather sad one, since it locates the full possession of life's meaning in a period that is by definition over.

He is more successful with pessimism. The pessimist argues that life contains so much suffering that it cannot have meaning. Schlick's response is that this does not follow: meaning comes from enjoyment, and the presence of suffering does not prevent a life from containing enjoyment, so a life full of hardship can still be a meaningful one. What would show life to be meaningless is the absence of enjoyment, not the presence of pain.

Finally, Schlick's account is unusual in refusing to treat morality as a rival to the meaning of life. Since morality is basically a set of social constraints on what we may enjoy, he has to explain why the moral person is not thereby losing out, and he does so by arguing that moral action can itself be a form of play and one of the most enjoyable things available to us. Ethics on this view is not a demand imposed on life from outside but another way of getting at the same thing the rest of the essay is about.

The overall effect is to make the question of meaning a private one. There is no answer that applies to everyone, only the answer each person reaches by finding out what is most enjoyable to them."

The rubric I am working from has four components, and I would like the grade to reflect them rather than a general impression. First, whether the student has correctly characterized the deflationary side of Schlick's argument, that is, what exactly he thinks goes wrong with the ordinary way of asking the question. Second, whether the hedonism is stated accurately, including the difference between what is most enjoyable and what is most pleasant in the moment. Third, whether the material on work, play, and youth is used correctly and whether the student's conclusion about the overall temper of the essay follows from the premises he has attributed to Schlick. Fourth, whether the treatment of pessimism is right, both about what the pessimist claims and about what Schlick needs in order to answer him.

Please give me a grade on the standard scale from A+ down through C, with no inflation and no hedging, and then justify it in a point-by-point commentary that a student could act on: which claims are correct and well put, which are wrong or unsupported, and in each of those cases whether the error is one of misattribution, of conflation, or of a conclusion that does not follow from what was said. If a passage is right but for the wrong reason, say so and explain the difference. Where two of my four components overlap in the answer, weight the error once rather than counting it twice.

I would like the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter to a colleague, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables; the student's answer is already set out above, so there is no need to reproduce it. Please do not offer suggestions about how to teach the unit or how to design better questions, and do not recommend further reading for me or for the student.
