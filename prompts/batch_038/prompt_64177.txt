I'm running a 75-minute seminar for 18 second-years, the week we do choice over time — diachronic rationality, money pumps, that whole cluster. I want an in-class activity where they get pumped as a group before anyone says the words "money pump" out loud. I've got a sketch and I can't tell whether it holds up. Help me build it out into something I can actually run.

The sketch: 18 students split into three "committees" of six. Inside each committee they're dealt into three blocs of two by colored role cards, and each bloc gets a different ranking of three options — call them A, B and C — arranged so the committee's majority ranking cycles (bloc 1: A over B over C; bloc 2: B over C over A; bloc 3: C over A over B). The committee "holds" one option at a time, tracked with a token on a laminated card in the middle of the table. Then I run three rounds. Each round the committee votes, majority rules, on whether to pay 4 tokens out of the committee pot to switch to whichever option beats the one they're currently holding. Becuase of the cycle there's always such an option and always a majority for it. Three rounds later they're holding what they started with and the pot is 12 tokens lighter. Crucially nobody is told why the vote structure is what it is and nobody sees the whole sequence in advance — each round they only ever see "you currently hold X, the motion is to pay 4 to move to Y."

Then the part I'm least sure of: before round 1 each committee is offered the chance to spend 6 tokens to adopt a rule — I was thinking either "no switch motion passes without unanimity" or "the whole agenda is revealed and put to a single vote" — and committees that buy the rule don't get pumped.

What I need from you:

- the full rulebook, including the actual text to print on the role cards, and concrete descriptions of A, B and C so the cycle feels like a real choice about something rather than an abstraction with letters on it. Also tell me whether 20-token pot / 4-per-switch is calibrated right for the punchline to land without feeling punitive, or what it should be instead.
- the staging. I'm one person with three tables going at once and I can't narrate all three, so most of this has to live on paper and let the committees self-run.
- what's actually at stake. No real money, and it can't be free either — the pump has to cost them something they care about. Hard constraint: no individual student should lose anything personally as a result of what their committee did, the loss has to land on a group-level pool, otherwise I've got two people resenting me for the rest of term.
- a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75.
- the debrief, mapping specific moments in the game onto specific moves in the argument: the beat where they realize they ended up worse off even though every single vote was rational; the question of whether the committee's ranking was intransitive or the committee was merely myopic and each step was fine on its own; and the moment where they have to say who, exactly — which temporally extended thing — could have foreseen all this and refused at round 1. I want the commitment-rule purchase to come up as the hinge of that discussion, not as a footnote.
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. I already expect at least one committee to refuse a switch out of sheer suspicion, which wrecks the demo, and I don't know how to prevent that without tipping them off.
- an honest note on which feature of the argument the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand. I suspect one already: the bloc rankings are dealt out on cards, so the committee's cycle is stipulated by me rather than emergent from anything the students actually believe, and a sharp one will say the whole demo is rigged. Is that fatal, and if not, how do I defuse it in the room?

Two more things. I'm torn on whether buying the rule should be clearly the right call or only clearly the right call in hindsight — tell me which produces the better debrief and why. And my TA thinks the commitment-rule phase should be cut entirely, his argument being that once you let them buy their way out the lesson collapses into "game theory says buy commitment devices" and the harder question, whether a pump really indicts the preference ordering at all, just evaporates. Tell me straight whether he's right, and if he isn't, how I make both halves pull their weight.
