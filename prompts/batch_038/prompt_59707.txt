I am auditing a working-group argument for a graduate seminar on the temporal structure of experience, and I have become convinced that it establishes far more than anyone in the room is willing to accept. The setup it starts from is the color phi paradigm investigated by Kolers and von Grünau and made famous in Dennett and Kinsbourne's discussion of the timing of consciousness. Two small spots of light of different colors — say red at one location, then green at another, a few degrees away — are flashed in succession, and at certain combinations of separation and interval the subject reports a single spot travelling smoothly between the two locations and changing colour abruptly at the midpoint of its path. The report is thus a report of something that happened before the second spot was physically present, which is what makes the case philosophically useful. Two interpretations are standard. On the Stalinesque interpretation, the discrimination required to produce that report is completed before the content is delivered to consciousness at all, so the experienced sequence contains the colour change from the outset and nothing that was ever experienced gets edited. On the Orwellian interpretation, the subject genuinely experienced the first colour persisting into the empty interval, and the record of that experience is revised before it is reported, so the report misdescribes what was experienced. Dennett and Kinsbourne's own assessment is that in this paradigm the two interpretations are empirically indistinguishable, and that is the engine of the argument I want checked.

Here is the argument as our group reconstructed it. (1) If two hypotheses about the course of a subject's conscious experience imply exactly the same reports, memories, and behaviour on every occasion on which anything could be checked, then there is no fact of the matter which of them is true. (2) In color phi trials, the Stalinesque and the Orwellian hypotheses imply exactly the same reports, memories, and behaviour on every such occasion. (3) So in those trials there is no fact of the matter about when the content of a colour change entered the subject's experience. (4) What makes (2) true is perfectly general: given any hypothesis that places the entry of some content at a time t, one can always construct a rival that places the entry earlier and adds a compensating revision of the record before any possible report, and since the record is the only thing available to be checked, the rival implies everything the original did. (5) So for any subject and any content of experience whatsoever, there is no fact of the matter about when that content entered experience. (6) Claims about the temporal order of experiences are claims about when their contents entered experience. (7) So no claim about the temporal order of experiences is true or false — not the subject's claim about the colour change, and not my own claim, made a second ago, that I heard the tone before I saw the flash.

The conclusion is not something I can live with, since it seems to dissolve exactly the sort of claim the seminar was convened to investigate, yet I cannot find a step I am willing to drop. Please diagnose the argument: identify the step or transition where it goes wrong, if it does, and explain why that step looked innocuous when we first wrote it down. Then price the alternatives — say what is lost by rejecting (1) and conceding that undetectable differences in the history of an experience can be real differences; what is lost by denying (2) and insisting the two interpretations do diverge somewhere, and if so, what kind of trial design would expose the divergence; and what is lost by resisting (4) or (6) instead. I would also like to know whether the argument earns its local conclusion at (3) even if the generalisation at (5) fails, or whether the local case is already contaminated by whatever flaw makes the generalisation unacceptable. If I have to state a preference, I would most like to keep (1): abandoning it looks like admitting a distinction that could never do any explanatory work anywhere, and I do not see how to remain a naturalist while paying that price. But tell me plainly if that attachment is the mistake.
