In my AP European History seminar we were assigned a group presentation on the July Crisis of 1914, and two members of my group got into an argument that went well past the assignment and that I could not settle.

(1) Jonah said the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand by Gavrilo Princip did not really cause the First World War, because a war among the great powers was coming regardless; the assassination only fixed the date, and a cause that could have been replaced by any of a dozen other Balkan sparks is not doing serious explanatory work.

(2) Amara said this proved too much: on that standard no event in a densely caused period counts as a cause, since there is always some background condition such that the outcome would have arrived anyway. For her, the relevant question is not whether war would have come without the shots in Sarajevo but whether the war that actually came ran through them.

(3) Jonah answered that if the counterfactual test is abandoned, causation stops being informative, because the same running-through relation traces back indefinitely — to the Treaty of Berlin of 1878, or to the invention of the rifle — so he proposed a replacement rule: rank candidates by how much later the war would have occurred without them.

(4) Amara objected that this reduces causation to timing, and that it demotes the mobilization timetables and the alliance commitments, which on her view matter not because they determined when war came but because they explain why it came as a general European war rather than as a third Balkan conflict.

(5) Jonah's final move was that explaining why an outcome took a particular form is description rather than causation, and that once description is allowed in, nothing distinguishes causes from the mere context in which causes operated.

Please adjudicate this move by move, saying of each of the five whether it succeeds and why, then say who is more right overall about what a historical explanation of the war should identify as its causes, and whether either of them is relying on an assumption that neither one ever stated.
