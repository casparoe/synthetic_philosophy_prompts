I sit on the grants panel of a foundation that funds projects whose benefits arrive over centuries. Before us is a proposal to standardize, across the whole portfolio, a planning assumption about how much longer the current technological era will last. The applicants derive it by Gott's Copernican method: the era is roughly eighty years old, our position within it is presumably not special, so its remaining duration lies between about 27 and 240 more years at the method's 50 percent confidence level, and even the 95 percent upper bound comes out near three thousand years — which, they argue, is license to score down any project whose payoff falls beyond that window. They add that the alternative, expert judgment about civilizational longevity, is no less arbitrary, except that it cannot be written down as a rule the panel could audit. One panelist wants the proposal rejected as pseudostatistics that the foundation should not dignify; another says that even if the reasoning is flawed, adopting it merely shifts a scoring weight and so costs little; a third observes that the applicants never state whether the era is to be dated from 1945, from the Industrial Revolution, or from the emergence of our species, and that these choices differ by orders of magnitude. Draft the memo I will circulate before the vote: exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no preamble and no closing paragraph, stating plainly what the panel should do with the proposal and why, and including the strongest consideration you can muster for the course you recommend against.
