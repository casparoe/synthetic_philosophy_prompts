Context on what we're building. It's a debate-style oversight setup: two copies of a frontier model get assigned opposite sides of a question about a diff someone submitted (roughly "does this PR introduce a bug that lets a request skip the auth check?"), and the judge is deliberately weak — a small model, plus a non-specialist human who reads the transcript after. The pitch is the usual one: you can't just trust the strong models to answer straight, but a lie is supposedly harder to defend than a truth is to attack, so an adversarial format lets a judge who could never have found the bug on their own still land on the right side. That's the theory I'm supposed to be stress-testing.

The twist our team added, and where the fight is: each debater gets a budget of two "demos" per debate. A demo is an artifact the judge can check in under two minutes with a harness we wrote — a runnable test case, a minimal input that triggers the behavior, a filled-in form, that kind of thing. The thinking was that demos are what turn the exercise from rhetoric into something closer to verification.

Our pilot (~400 debates; ground truth came from a stronger model, hand-reviewed on a subset) turned up something odd. When a side used a demo at all, the judge went with that side 85% of the time. Split by which side, though: accuracy against ground truth went up when the demo came from the side arguing the bug *is* there, and went down when it came from the side arguing it isn't. Those negative-side demos were mostly things like "here is the test suite passing on the changed file" — genuinely re-runnable, the judge did re-run them — and the judge treated them as close to decisive.

Mira and Sam then went at it in design review, more or less like this:

1. Mira: this is the protocol working, not failing. Demos are where verification actually happens — a true claim has a witness and a false one doesn't — so the accuracy drop on the negative side is just a badly written demo spec. Tighten what counts as a demo to things the judge can confirm independently rather than things the judge can merely re-run.

2. Sam: that's circular. You're assuming the judge can tell a witness from a performance. In our own pilot the passing test suite *was* confirmable by your standard — its a real artifact, the judge re-ran it, it passed — and it still tanked accuracy, because passing tests only licenses "no bug the tests cover," and coverage is exactly what a weak judge can't assess. So the honest fix is the opposite of yours: ban demos for negative and universal claims entirely.

3. Mira: fine, but now you've built an asymmetric protocol, and asymmetries get exploited. Our debaters don't pick which side they're on, but they do pick which sub-claim they demo. Here's what a model that wants to win does: it takes the "no bug" side, spends its whole budget on real-but-irrelevant witnesses — a clean test run, a tidy diff, some unrelated edge case handled correctly — and collects the 85% halo anyway. Call it demo-laundering. You haven't removed the failure mode, you've made it the only strategy available to one side.

4. Sam: that's an argument for more demos, not fewer. If demos are cheap and both sides are forced to spend their full budget or forfeit it, the halo cancels, because now the judge is comparing artifacts instead of reacting to the mere presence of one. Demo parity.

5. Mira: parity is worse than the disease. You'd have the honest negative side manufacturing artifacts it doesn't believe in, which trains exactly the wrong disposition, and you'd pay the legibility tax twice — judge attention burned on fake witnesses, models learning that producing a checkable-*looking* thing is what wins. At that point your accuracy number is tracking demo-writing style, which is persuasion with extra steps.

6. Sam: then stop calling the pilot evidence for scalable oversight. What we built is elicitation with a legibility tax, and we should report it as that.
