I am a third-year PhD student in early modern European history, and this semester I am auditing our department's historical methods seminar while drafting my dissertation prospectus. The seminar opens on the ancient end of the problem — whether Herodotus, who tells us he records what he has been told without vouching for any of it, is a credulous collector of tales or the first honest source-critic, and whether Thucydides' insistence on eyewitnesses cross-checked against one another actually delivers the accuracy he promises — before moving to the modern literature on documents, archives, and inference. My own dissertation works almost entirely with criminal trial records from a cluster of small jurisdictional courts, mostly witchcraft and infanticide proceedings, in which the accused's words survive only inside transcripts produced by the authority that was prosecuting them. Two analogies for what we do with such material keep circulating in the seminar, and they point in opposite directions. I would like both audited rather than merely described.

The first is the one my advisor reaches for by default: source criticism is a courtroom. The historian is counsel, the source is a witness; you establish whether the witness was in a position to know, whether they have an interest in the outcome, whether they are consistent with themselves and with other witnesses; you test the account against independent corroboration; and if it survives, you have established something to a standard approximating proof beyond reasonable doubt. My advisor invoked this picture to tell me that my second chapter was too modest: if the transcripts are mutually consistent and the court had no stake in inventing certain details, then I am licensed to assert what happened, not merely to report what the record says. The second analogy came from an anthropologist visiting the seminar, who argued that the courtroom picture has the relation backwards: a trial record is not a witness at all but a crime-scene trace — a footprint, a fiber, a partial print — whose evidential value lies precisely in what its producer could not control or did not intend to disclose, such as the clerk's word choice, the order in which questions and answers are recorded, and the formulae that recur across otherwise unrelated cases. On that view, whatever a source asserts about the events is epistemically second-rate next to what it inadvertently reveals about the apparatus that produced it.

For each analogy, give me an explicit mapping — a table is fine — of what plays the role of counsel, witness, oath, cross-examination, opposing counsel, jury, standard of proof, and the record itself, and then identify where the mapping breaks. I want the disanalogies that actually bite in my case separated from the merely cosmetic ones: is it fatal to the courtroom picture that the witness is dead, that no live cross-examination is possible, that the transcript stands several removes from any speaking event (clerk, interpreter, later copyist, nineteenth-century editor), or that the fact I want to establish was observed by no one who wrote anything down? And is it fatal to the trace picture that traces are supposed to be produced causally and without intent, whereas a clerk's phrasing is deliberately made — so that a leak is involuntary only relative to some level in a hierarchy of the author's intentions, several of which are strategic? Say what each analogy quietly imports about the shape of the past and about what a historian may claim: does the courtroom picture smuggle in an individualist psychology of lying and truth-telling that cannot see genre, formularies, scribal convention, and the archive's own selectivity, and does the trace picture smuggle in a suspicion so total that it cannot explain why we ever believe anything a source says? Finally, tell me whether my advisor's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — that consistency plus absence of interest licenses assertion about the events themselves — and whether the visiting anthropologist's conclusion survives the same treatment, and if both are being asked to carry more weight than they can bear, propose a better analogy for source criticism and specify the work it would have to do that neither of these can: in particular, how it would handle a case where the only surviving evidence was generated by the party with the strongest interest in its content, and where the events in question left no trace in any document at all.
