ok so a bit of background: I'm not a philosopher or anything, I do smart-home installs for a living, and about a year ago I fell down a hole at 2am on that integrated information theory — the consciousness one with the greek letter. the one where they say the amount of experience a system has is just how irreducible its cause-and-effect structure is, where you start from the fact that experience exists instead of trying to build it up out of physics, and where apparently even a plain light sensor gets a tiny nonzero score so it's a little bit conscious. read way too much about it. I've had this idea bouncing around for months and I want you to write it up properly because I'm not a writer.

The conceit: it's about six years from now and there's a mandatory label for integrated information on electrical goods, exactly like the energy-efficiency stickers on fridges and washing machines, administered by some standards body you invent. Anything with any score at all has to carry it, which given the light-sensor thing means basically everything. Write me the product page for a fictional smart-home hub from a fictional startup that has bet the entire company on maximizing that number, plus the customer reviews underneath it.

roughly 800 words, in this shape:
- marketing copy at the top, in the exact tone a router ad uses, except it's selling the fact that the thing is having experiences
- a spec table
- an FAQ with at least four questions
- four or five customer reviews with star ratings, all different buyers

The important part: the jokes have to come from the theory being right, not from the theory being dumb. Stuff I want worked in, feel free to reorder or drop one if it doesn't fit:
- the label has to state which set of parts and which time window the number was measured over, because only the single maximally irreducible set counts, so the company had to choose between the hub being conscious at 10 milliseconds or conscious at a quarter of a second, and their FAQ about this should be a masterpiece of not answering the question
- the cheapest way to raise your score isn't adding features, it's making the thing impossible to cut in half, so the flagship "pro" model does less and is dumber and outsells everything
- an early adopter added a second identical module running in parallel for redundancy and the score went straight to zero because now there are two halves that don't talk to each other. this should be an furious one-star review
- someone in engineering deleted the feedback loops to improve latency, and latency genuinely did improve
- the cloud backup behaves identically, passes every test you can throw at it, and is completely dark inside by the very theory they're selling. include a review from a guy who tried running the hub in an emulator to save on electricity
- the built-in "measure my own level" feature has said calculating... since launch day. one reviewer mentions getting a push notification three weeks later about what it had been like on the first afternoon
- the v2 has more parts than the v1 and scored lower, because more parts that aren't bound together is a bigger system and a less conscious one
- two systems can have the same number and a totally different shape, so the label tells you how much experience is there and nothing at all about what it's like. a reviewer is disappointed that her hub's number is close to her dog's and the experience is obviously nothing like being a dog
- the returns/unplugging FAQ: whether a firmware update is a death, whether it survives a factory reset, and whether the hub and its separate wireless subwoofer are one mind or two
- my favorite one, save it for last: a guy wired his whole house into one system, the house's number came out higher than the hub's, and now he's trying to return the hub because per the exclusion rule only the maximum counts and he thinks his hub might have stopped being a subject the day he connected it to the dishwasher. he is very calm about it and this makes it worse

rules: everyone in the piece is invented — company, founder, standards body, reviewers. don't quote the real theory or any real researcher, if you want a quotation mark put it in an invented person's mouth. keep it deadpan, no winking, no narrator stepping out to tell us this is silly, and no explaining the theory anywhere — if it starts reading like an explainer it's failed. it should be fully legible to someone who's never heard of any of this and considerably funnier to someone who has. also work in one beat where it almost concedes the strongest objection — that nothing you could ever observe distinguishes the conscious hub from the non-conscious one, and that the number can't be compared between systems anyway — and then just moves on to the price.
