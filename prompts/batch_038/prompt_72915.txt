In about three weeks time I'm giving a colloquium talk on Schrödinger's unity-of-consciousness doctrine, and I need a Q&A prep sheet aimed at the actual room, not at a generic philosophy audience.

The thesis: I think the whole one-mind business — that consciousness is a singular and its apparent plurality is a kind of illusion — is not a mystical bolt-on that a great physicist let himself have late in life. It's load-bearing for his account of what makes a scientific description objective. My reconstruction goes in four moves. (1) Everything I know about the world is given in one consciousness, and nothing within experience marks that consciousness off from others as a separate item. (2) So the number of subjects is not an empirically determinable quantity: there's no counting procedure for it, unlike for anything inside the world-model. (3) Therefore an ontology that treats subject-plurality as a settled fact has smuggled in structure that experience can't supply. (4) The one-mind claim is just the refusal to pay that bill, and it's cheap, becuase it leaves the world-model untouched and does no damage to physics. I then argue it deserves to be read as a live competitor to contemporary deflationary views about persons — it does the same job, getting subjects out of nothing but the unity of a field of experience, without needing Parfit's or Dennett's apparatus.

The room: mostly analytic philosophers of mind. A couple of type-B materialists who will already be irritated that I'm treating an anti-physicalist as a serious arguer rather than a case study. One or two panpsychists who'll want to know why I don't just say that's what he was. A big contingent of grad students who turned up because of the cat. Second row, a historian of science who works on interwar Vienna and cares about sources and about whether I'm doing whiggish philosophy with a dead physicist's private reading. And I know at least one student in the department who works on classical Indian philosophy and can be extremely precise about what Advaita Vedanta does and doesn't claim.

So: the eight or so questions I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage each would do, and each phrased in the register of whoever's asking — the physicalist's version of the unfalsifiability worry sounds nothing like the historian's version of it, and neither sounds like the panpsychist's. For each one, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing up, plus a blunt note on where the reply is soft. Mark the one or two I should concede in the first minute so nobody gets to spend a question on them, and mark the one where the honest answer is that my step (3) doesn't follow from (2) and I need a fallback that isn't a shrug. Also tell me which objection I should quietly hope somebody raises, because answering it well is the best part of the talk. Finally, the question I'm hoping nobody asks: I've got a guess already — something about whether his Vedanta was ever more than secondhand German scholarship — but tell me if there's a worse one waiting.
