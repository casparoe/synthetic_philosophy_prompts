I've been teaching myself game theory out of a library book (no math past high school, so go easy on me) and I've built an argument that seems to prove way too much, and I cant find the exit. Here's the general recipe I'm trusting:

(1) If some other strategy does at least as well as strategy s no matter what the other player does, and strictly better in at least one case, then playing s is a mistake — you lose nothing by switching away from it.
(2) If both players are rational and both know it, each can cross off the other's mistake-strategies before deciding.
(3) Crossing those off can turn strategies that used to be fine into fresh mistakes, so you repeat until nothing more crosses off. The order you cross things off in doesn't change what's left at the end.
(4) "Some other strategy" includes a blend: even when no single strategy beats s, a 50/50 mix of two others might beat s against every possible reply, and that counts just as much.

Then I made up a matrix to test the recipe. Row player (me) has A, B, S; column player (my friend) has L, M, R; my payoff is the first number in each cell:

              L           M           R
      A    (4, 6)      (0, 5)      (0, 1)
      B    (0, 7)      (4, 3)      (5, 7)
      S    (1, 0)      (1, 5)      (1, 9)

(5) My S is a mistake. No single row beats it everywhere — A pays 0 where S pays 1 at both M and R, and B pays 0 where S pays 1 at L — but half A half B pays me 2, 2, 2.5 against L, M, R, all better than the 1, 1, 1 that S pays. Cross off S, courtesy of (4).
(6) With S gone, my friend's L beats her M in both remaining rows (6 to 5 against my A, 7 to 3 against my B), so M is a mistake. Worth noting this wasn't true a step ago: against my S, her M paid 5 and her L paid 0. Cross off M.
(7) Now her L does at least as well as her R against each row I have left — 6 beats 1 against A, and 7 ties 7 against B. By (1) she loses nothing by never playing R. Cross off R.
(8) With only L left, my B pays 0 where my A pays 4, so cross off B. Exactly one cell survives: (A, L), worth (4, 6) to us.
(9) And the same recipe should work on any finite game, since mixing hands you fresh firepower at every round and (1) keeps handing you more, so you can always grind down to a single cell — meaning every finite game has exactly one rational answer.

Two things bother me. First, (9) has to be false, because in matching pennies nothing is dominated at all and the grind never even gets started.
