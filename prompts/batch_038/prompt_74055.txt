You are a philosopher of mind working at the intersection of cognitive science and AI safety, and you are helping me prepare a reported piece. I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am trying to write an article that does justice to a dispute I keep running into but cannot find anyone stating cleanly.

The dispute concerns vagueness and the sorites paradox, but not in its familiar seminar-room form. The standard setup is a heap of sand, or a bald man, and the standard menu of responses — epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree theory, contextualism, and so on — is one I already know well and do not need rehearsed. What interests me is a microdomain in which the paradoxical predicate is manufactured rather than found, and in which there is a real, checkable fact about the boundary that we nevertheless cannot state. Here is the case I want you to work from; treat everything in it as stipulated.

A lab has a corpus of exactly one thousand color swatches, each a physical card. Each card differs from its immediate neighbour in the corpus by a step so small that a trained observer under controlled lighting cannot reliably tell the two apart, even on repeated forced-choice trials. Swatch 1 is a clear, undisputed teal; swatch 1000 is a clear, undisputed blue. The series passes through the blue-green region. So the observational premise of a sorites sorites holds locally at every step, while the endpoints are unambiguous.

Now the stipulations that make this case unusual.

First: the corpus was built by a machine that mixed pigments to a recipe. For every card, there is a definite record of its spectral reflectance, and therefore a definite answer to whether it is, physically, more teal or more blue by any fixed metric the lab chooses in advance. The lab in fact chose such a metric before the corpus was printed, and by that metric there is a first card in the series that falls on the blue side. Call that card N. The number exists, is determinate, and is knowable from the recipe.

Second: the lab sealed N. Nobody who is asked about the cards is told which card it is, and nobody is permitted to look it up. The only access anyone has to the series is by looking.

Third: the lab recruits naïve judges. Each judge sees a single card in isolation, is told the corpus runs from a teal to a blue in imperceptible steps, and is asked to classify that card as teal or blue. Judges are consistent with themselves on repeat presentations. Across many judges, the proportion answering "blue" rises smoothly from near zero around card 1 to near one around card 1000, crossing half somewhere in the neighbourhood of card 600 — several hundred cards away from N, which for the sake of the argument sits at card 240.

Fourth: the lab wants a rule for classifying any single card that a judge could follow using only what is available to the judge, and it wants that rule to satisfy three demands at once: (i) it must never deliver a verdict that a judge looking at the card would find flatly wrong, meaning it must respect the local indiscriminability of neighbouring cards; (ii) it must not be hostage to how the series is presented, so it cannot turn on which end the judge is shown first or on which card happens to be labelled the starting point; and (iii) it must land the boundary near N, since N is where the lab's own chosen metric puts it and the lab regards that metric as tracking what the word is supposed to mean.

Fifth, and this is the wrinkle I most want pressed: a graduate student in the lab proposes a fix. Rather than classify a card by looking at it alone, classify it by looking at it together with a designated anchor card, and let the verdict depend on the pair. This makes the rule context-involving in an explicit, engineered way. But the anchor itself has to be chosen by someone looking at cards, and the choice of anchor is not determined by anything the judges can see.

With all of that stipulated, write an essay on the following controversy: in the sealed-boundary corpus, is there a fact of the matter about where teal gives way to blue, and if so, whose fact is it — the metric's, at N, or the classification practice's, somewhere around card 600? Take a position and argue for it. Do not survey the standard responses and declare a draw; I want a verdict, and I want the reasoning that gets you there. In particular, I want you to grapple with whether the three demands the lab makes on a classification rule are jointly satisfiable, and if you think they are not, to say which one should be given up and why that is the cheapest concession rather than merely the most convenient. The graduate student's anchor proposal should be assessed explicitly: does relativizing to an anchor solve the problem, relocate it, or dissolve it?

Before you draft, spend some effort brainstorming: lay out for yourself what turns on the stipulation that N is sealed, what would change if it were not, and where the interesting difference lies between a boundary that is determinate but inaccessible and a boundary that is not determinate at all. Then write.

Two requirements on how you deliver this. I want exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, and no introduction, no conclusion, and no framing text before or after the list. The five points between them should carry the whole argument: the verdict, the reasoning for it, the assessment of the three demands, the treatment of the anchor proposal, and the concession you would make. Because there is no room for hedging prose, each point has to do its work directly. Within that same list, have one of the five points state how confident you are in the position you have taken, and single out the specific claim in your argument that you are least sure of — not a generic caveat about the difficulty of the topic, but the load-bearing step you would most expect a good objector to break.
