I am in the second year of a philosophy PhD and have been convening an informal reading group on the foundations of Bayesian epistemology. Last week we reached the Dutch book arguments — the claim that a set of degrees of belief is defective precisely when it disposes the agent to accept a package of bets that guarantees her a net loss — and one member of the group announced that the whole enterprise is logic in fancy dress: that exploitability is a mere symptom, and that the axioms do the real work. Rather than argue about it in the abstract, I sat down and wrote out, case by case, when I actually take a betting vulnerability to track a genuine defect in someone's doxastic state and when I do not. What follows is my list, and I want to know what it adds up to.

1. A coin-toss punter with 0.45 for heads and 0.45 for tails who accordingly sells a bookie two tickets, one paying £1 on heads and one paying £1 on tails, at 45p each: she banks 90p and pays out a pound whichever way it falls. Clearly defective, no qualification needed.
2. The very same agent, but the two halves of the book are offered on separate days by separate bookies who make no mention of one another, and she would have declined both had they been tabled together as a single package. Still defective, though I think less so, or differently so — this reads to me as a failure of attention rather than of coherence.
3. An author whose degrees of belief form a perfectly coherent probability function: 0.99 on each of a hundred claims in her manuscript and, quite consistently, a low value on their conjunction. She knows at least one claim must be false. No book can be run against her, and yet I want to say something has gone wrong.
4. An agent with strongly diminishing marginal utility of money who declines every bet her linear-utility counterpart would take, and who therefore looks exploitable only if you ignore her utility curve. Not irrational in the slightest.
5. An agent who revises by some rule other than conditionalisation, and who is disposed both to buy bets now at her current odds and to buy further bets later at her post-evidence odds. Exploitable, and irrational.
6. Identical prior, identical posterior, identical non-conditionalising rule — but this agent has resolved never to place a bet after she learns anything, so the second half of the book can never be sold to her. No feasible book exists against her, and I still judge her exactly as irrational as the agent in case 5. This is the pair I cannot get my head around, and I note that cases 2 and 6 pull against each other: in one, fragmentation across time and bookies seems to excuse, and in the other it does not.
7. An agent who conditionalises impeccably but forgets that she has already bought a certain exposure, so that a bookie can sell it to her a second time. Exploitable, but not irrational — she lost her memory, not her coherence.
8. An agent who refuses all bets, or who accepts only bets with a spread wide enough to swallow any book's profit. Invulnerable, and her degrees of belief may be as wild as you please. I think the framework owes me an account here that does not amount to stipulating betting dispositions she simply lacks.
9. An agent who accepts bets at odds that are entirely fair by her own evidence, but whose bookie has privately observed the coin's bias and can therefore extract a sure profit from her. I do not think this vulnerability counts against her at all.

Is there a principle — or a short list of rival candidates — that generates all nine of these verdicts? I would like each candidate stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a case you have never seen, with an explicit note of which item on my list forces each clause into the formulation. Then please run it against three situations I have deliberately left out: an agent whose doxastic state is imprecise, a set of probability functions rather than a single one; an agent who discounts the future hyperbolically and is therefore vulnerable to a book assembled across time; and an agent whose incoherence concerns propositions that no bet could settle within her lifetime. Finally, tell me plainly which of my verdicts the best candidate has to overturn and what it costs to keep them, and say where in the formulation my colleague's charge shows up — if exploitability really is a symptom rather than the substance, the principle should reveal that somewhere in its own structure. Please write the whole answer in British English.
