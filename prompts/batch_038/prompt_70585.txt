I run a close-up magic night at a bar, and because I'm a nerd I film some of the tricks on my phone in slow motion and then ask people afterwards what they saw. Frame by frame I know exactly when things happened. The reports don't line up with the frames, and after a couple of years of this I've started to think the mismatch has a shape to it. I just dont have a word for the shape.

Two cases.

Case one: a card is red, my hand passes over it, now it's blue. On the footage there is no in-between — red up to frame N, blue from frame N+1 — and the blue card is never at the place in space where people claim the change happened. Ask "where was it when it turned?" and they point, dead confident, at a spot where the footage says it was still red, and they describe watching the colour change right there. Ask the same person ten minutes later, differently — "so did you see it turn, or did it look like it just jumped?" — and now it jumped, they never saw a change, and they're sure that's what they would have said all along. Both answers come with the same confidence and the same feel of "that's just what it looked like."

Case two: I tap the table and a beat later the coin drops. The frames say tap, then drop. They report drop, then tap, and they give me a little causal story that only works in that order — "it fell, that's when you tapped." After I show them the slow-mo they don't say "oh, I was wrong," they say "yeah, but that's still not what it looked like," and then five minutes after *that* they describe it in the correct order, just as vividly, with no sense of having changed their mind at any point. The switching doesn't register as switching.

Third thing, smaller, and I don't know if its the same phenomenon. Sometimes there's a leftover. Someone says flatly that nothing happened between two moments, and then unprompted says something like "there's a bit in the middle I can't get to." Not doubt about their answer — a sense that there was a moment there, with nothing in it. The confident report of nothing and the feel of a gap sit right next to each other and don't seem to conflict for them.

I gather there's a standard pair of pictures for this general area: one where the brain does its editing before the experience is ever had, so the person never actually saw the wrong thing, and one where they do see it and then the record gets rewritten afterwards. My cases won't sit on either side. Which of the two pictures you'd have to make true seems to depend on which question I happen to ask, of the same person, minutes later. And the leftover doesn't look like either one is entitled to it — the first says there was never anything there to leave a trace, the second says the trace got overwritten.

So: is there an established concept for a revision of experience that's relative to the probe rather than fixed one way or the other? And separately, is there anything for the trace-without-content bit?

I'm not after a label, I'm after the carving. What exactly is this thing, what isn't it, and where are its edges? In particular, how does it differ from (a) ordinary memory getting rebuilt badly every time it's pulled out, (b) suggestion from my wording — maybe there's no phenomenon in their experience at all, just in my interview technique, and they're answering the question I actually asked rather than the one I think I asked, and (c) cases where there was no experience of the relevant moment to begin with and the person just produces something on demand because a question was posed. If (b) is the honest answer here, say so and tell me how I'd test for it. And if there genuinely isn't a name for the probe-relative version, coin one — give me a definition plus boundary conditions I could apply to a transcript, so that I could tell a real case from someone just being vague or agreeable.

No reading list or references, please, I can't chase those down. Just the concepts.
