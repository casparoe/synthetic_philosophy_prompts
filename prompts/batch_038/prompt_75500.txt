My roommate Dev and his friend Nora spent like two hours on the Allais problem at our kitchen table last night and I only followed part of it, but they both asked me to judge and I chickened out, so I wrote down the actual moves as best I could. Neither of them gave an inch.

They had four options on index cards:

- A: $1 million, guaranteed
- B: 10% chance of $5 million, 89% chance of $1 million, 1% chance of nothing
- C: 11% chance of $1 million, 89% chance of nothing
- D: 10% chance of $5 million, 90% chance of nothing

Both of them picked A over B and D over C, so the fight wasn't about what they'd choose, it was about whether choosing that way is a mistake.

1. Dev said obviously it is, and there's no deep story to tell. He drew 100 tickets: ticket 1 is the 1% slice, tickets 2-11 are the 10% slice, tickets 12-100 are the 89% slice. On tickets 1-11, A and C both pay $1 million and B and D both pay the identical gamble — nothing on ticket 1, $5 million on 2-11. On tickets 12-100, A and B both pay $1 million and C and D both pay nothing. So the two decisions are the same decision on tickets 1-11 with an identical freebie stapled on, and preferring differently in the two cases is like preferring coffee to tea on Monday and tea to coffee on Tuesday. He added that Nora is turning down about $390,000 of average payoff in the first pair to buy that freebie.

2. Nora said the ticket drawing already assumes the thing in dispute. In the first pair A is the option where you cannot walk away empty-handed; in the second pair both options probably leave you empty-handed, so the only live question is which long shot you want. Certainty isn't a side dish you can factor out of the comparison, it's the thing being decided. Independence is just the name of the rule that says you're not allowed to care about that, so Dev citing it to convict her is arguing in a circle.

3. Dev said fine, then state the rule that lets certainty matter in one pair and not the other, and he wrote a variant on the back of a card: exactly the same structure except the 1% slice pays $999,000 instead of nothing. So it's $1 million sure versus 10% of $5 million, 89% of $1 million and 1% of $999,000; and 11% of $1 million versus 10% of $5 million, 1% of $999,000 and 89% of nothing. Nora took the gamble in both. Dev said: so the second you replace the catastrophe with a scratch your choices fall right back in line with the axiom, which means what's driving you isn't a principle about certainty, it's a flinch about zero.

4. Nora said the flinch is the principle and she'd state it: when two options differ in how likely you are to end up with nothing, take the one that's less likely to leave you with nothing; when they don't differ on that, just take the better bet. She said C and D both leave her broke about nine times out of ten so there's nothing to protect there, and she said that's a rule with an answer for every case including his variant, not a mood.

5. Dev said then your rule convicts you in the second pair. C leaves you with nothing 89% of the time and D leaves you with nothing 90% of the time — those differ — so "take the one that's less likely to leave you with nothing" tells you to take C, and you took D. And if you patch it to "only when one of the options rules out total loss," you've got a cliff sitting at exactly zero that you can't defend and that your own second choice shows you don't respect, since you were perfectly happy facing a 90% chance of nothing ten minutes earlier.

6. Nora said okay, the rule isn't about the size of the probability, it's about whether walking away with nothing is on the table at all, and she can live with 9-to-1 odds on a lottery she picked but not with a guaranteed fortune being put at risk for an upgrade. Then she said Dev's real complaint has to be that people like her get exploited over time, and she doesn't see it. Rank her four cards out loud: A first, then B — she'd take B over D for free, since B pays at least as much as D on every ticket and strictly more on 89 of them — then D, then C. One clean ranking, no circle in it anywhere. So: hand her C for free and she'll pay a dollar to swap it for D, take B when he offers it for nothing, pay another dollar to swap B for A, and now she's holding the card she wanted most for two bucks. Her words were "that's shopping, not a pump."

7. Dev said she was only comparing four frozen cards, and that the leak shows up when you get to choose again after some of the uncertainty has resolved, and Nora said that was an IOU and not an argument, and then they started re-litigating move 1.
