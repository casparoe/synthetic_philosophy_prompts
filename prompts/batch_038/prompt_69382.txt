I work on family policy for a municipal labor federation, and we are currently in a fight over a package that would fund a city childcare subsidy and extend the paid-leave top-up for municipal employees. The most effective rhetoric arrayed against us has not been about cost projections or labor-supply effects. It is a single analogy that keeps getting recycled in letters to the editor and by two council members: subsidizing someone's decision to have a child is like subsidizing someone's decision to buy a boat — a private, optional, expensive taste, paid for out of the pockets of people who chose differently, including the child-free who will never draw the benefit. The version I keep seeing adds that nobody proposes a public marina for people who enjoy sailing, and that if parents want the thing, they should save for it the way boat owners do.

I would rather take this seriously than resent it, because part of me suspects it is doing real work on undecided people and I cannot say precisely what that work is. Can you set out the mapping term by term — which element of the boat case is doing duty for the child, for the leave weeks, for the subsidized childcare slot, for the taxpayer who never sails, for the moment of purchase — and then sort the disanalogies into those that actually bear on the inference being drawn and those that are merely decorative? I am most interested in what the analogy quietly imports from boat ownership: that the good is rival and excludable, that it is consumed rather than produced, that the chooser is the only party whose interests are engaged, that the acquisition is a single datable act by an adult who could have refrained at no one else's expense, and whatever else you think is being carried across. Then tell me whether the conclusion the council members draw — that public funding here is a transfer from the child-free to parents on behalf of a private good, and so presumptively unjust — survives once those imports are stripped out, or whether only some weaker and less quotable version survives.

Two further things. Our side usually answers with an analogy of its own: children are a public good, and paying for childcare is like paying for roads or the fire service, things everyone benefits from whether or not they personally use them. Please audit that one as well and be blunt about where it overreaches, because I suspect the public-goods language buys us more than it earns — the non-parent's benefit is diffuse and arrives decades later, not present and direct, and the analogy to roads papers over that gap. And tell me whether either comparison ought to be retired in favor of simply stating the fiscal incidence; if some third framing would carry the intended conclusion more honestly, describe it. Finally, test whatever analogies you keep against a design detail we are actually fighting about: the proposal reserves a portion of the leave that only the second parent can take, and it lapses entirely if he does not use it. The boat analogy appears to have nothing to say about that clause, and I want to know whether the public-good one does any better.
