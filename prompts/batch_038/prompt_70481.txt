I'm giving a paper at a social science history seminar in a few weeks — audience is mostly economic historians and historical demographers, plus one political scientist who works on state capacity and one person who does Chinese county-level mortality reconstruction. No philosophers, and nobody who cares about the ethics side except as a distraction.

My thesis: famines are neither natural disasters with political aggravators nor political crimes. "Crime" is the wrong charge because it smuggles in an intent that almost no famine policy actually had. What I argue instead is that the thing doing the explanatory work is the state's accountability structure — who, if anyone, could make rulers pay for letting people starve — and that the whole food-availability-versus-entitlements fight is a dead end because both sides are arguing about proximate causes while the variance in death tolls tracks accountability instead. Cases: Bengal 1943, Ireland 1845-49, the Great Leap Forward, and a couple of severe droughts that didn't turn into famines.

Give me the Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, in their idiom — identification, what my counterfactual baseline is supposed to be, whether the accountability claim is testable or just Sen's democracy point with new vocabulary, how much of the Chinese case survives the data problems, whether the Irish grain-export argument is doing work I'm claiming it does. Order them by how much damage they'd do. For each one give me a two-minute reply, what I should concede in the first sentence, and a note on where the reply is soft. And end with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
