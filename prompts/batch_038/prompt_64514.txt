I write about physics and its conceptual fallout for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a piece on the thermodynamics of information — on whether the century-old argument over Maxwell's demon has been settled or merely renamed. Before I commit to a thesis, I want my own case verdicts tested for consistency, because I suspect they are not, and I would rather discover that now than in print. Here is what I currently think, case by case.

Szilard's one-molecule engine, in which a partition is inserted, one bit is measured about which side the molecule is on, and roughly kT ln 2 of work is extracted, then the bit is erased: no violation. The erasure cost exactly balances the withdrawal. I am confident here, and this is the verdict my piece takes as settled.

The same engine, but the demon carries a memory of a billion bits and never erases: violation. It draws work from a single heat bath indefinitely, and the reassurance that the bill comes due when the memory fills does not move me, since "fills" can be postponed past any time you like.

A slab of photographic film sealed inside a cavity at temperature T, recording every photon that strikes it, never developed: not a demon, not a threat. Recording is not sorting, and film cannot lift a weight.

Feynman's ratchet and pawl with both ends at one temperature: harmless. The pawl's own thermal fluctuations let it slip backward as often as the ratchet advances, so nothing accumulates.

The identical ratchet with its paddle in a hot bath and its pawl in a cold one: this turns, and I am happy to call it an engine. But the mechanism is unchanged from the previous case, and if the only difference is the reservoirs, then the explanation I gave there — that the pawl jiggles too — was beside the point. These two verdicts I cannot hold in my head at once.

A demon that must illuminate the molecules in order to see them, and so runs down a lamp: harmless, and for an uninteresting reason — the lamp is a store of low entropy being spent. What troubles me is the corollary: a demon operating in the dark that learns about molecules by colliding with them seems equally legitimate, and if it is, then the cost of measurement was never where the difficulty lay, and the popular slogan that information is physical is doing less work in the argument than my colleagues assume.

A cell that sorts correct from incorrect substrates by kinetic proofreading, spending nucleotide triphosphates to do it: obviously no threat. But note that for the cell I say it pays in free energy, while for the demon I say it pays in erasure, and I have no idea whether these are the same debt denominated twice or two different debts.

A demon whose memory is reset not by an explicit erasure but by decoherence into its surroundings: I want to say no violation, since decoherence is a physical process that carries entropy outward. A physicist I interviewed said that decoherence does not destroy the record, it only spreads and hides it, so the debt is relocated rather than paid. I have no reply to him, and I do not want to publish this verdict without one.

A fully reversible demon that uncomputes its measurement rather than erasing it: here I alternate, sometimes from paragraph to paragraph. On one reading the memory returns to blank for free and the sorting was therefore free; on the other, any cycle in which everything ends as it began cannot have delivered net work, and the first reading is simply an accounting error I have not yet located.

Finally, the generalized second law with an information term, of the kind Sagawa and Ueda developed, which permits extraction beyond the free-energy drop in proportion to the mutual information a feedback controller acquires: I accept the formalism, but it makes the law look like a statement about what someone who knows something may extract, and I resist a second law that is relative to a knower. When I try to read the term as a statement about correlations rather than about anyone's knowledge, I lose the sense that the law explains anything at all.

My tentative principle, which I expect you to break, is this: a device threatens the second law only if it converts an entropy-free correlation into work without ever paying to break that correlation. Note that on this principle I must accept the demon in a universe that expands forever against a cosmological horizon, dumping waste heat outward and never erasing — yet I do accept it, while I reject the billion-bit memory that likewise never pays, and I cannot say what distinguishes them.

What I am asking for:

First, state the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that best captures these verdicts. I want it stated precisely enough that a clause-by-clause reading is possible, with each clause tied to the case that forces it.

Second, tell me which verdicts the best principle cannot keep. For each, say whether the retraction is forced by the principle or merely cheaper than the complication required to preserve it, and say what my piece loses if I give it up.

Third, apply your principle to three cases I have not listed: a demon whose memory is periodically reset by a laser pulse fired from outside the sealed box; a semipermeable membrane that passes only fast molecules, with no measurement and no memory anywhere in the story; and one case of your own choosing in which your principle delivers a verdict that would surprise me.

Fourth, settle the currency question. Is there a single ledger in which free-energy expenditure and erasure cost are the same entry, and if so what is the exchange rate and where does the kinetic-proofreading cell appear on it?

Fifth, and this is the sentence my piece will turn on: is the Landauer-Bennett resolution a discovery about nature, or a redescription that moves the difficulty from the demon to the memory? If the two readings make different empirical predictions, describe the experiment that would separate them.

No bibliography and no citations, please — I am after the reasoning, not the references, and I will chase down sources myself. Where you are unsure of a name, a date, or a title, describe the thing instead of guessing at it.
