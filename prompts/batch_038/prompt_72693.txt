I am in the fourth year of a philosophy doctorate working on British value theory around the turn of the twentieth century, and my department is hosting a public lecture this term on what poetry and axiology can do for one another. I have been asked to say something at the head of the session, and rather than a dry preamble I would like a poem that performs a piece of G. E. Moore's argument instead of summarising it. I can supply the philosophy; what I need from you is the craft.

Here is the material, in my own words, so that you do not have to recall it. Moore holds two claims that pull against each other. The first is his doctrine of organic unities, laid down early in Principia Ethica: the worth of a whole may be different from the sum of the worths of its parts taken separately, and one may not assume the two coincide. The second is his conclusion about what is actually worth having. He compares a world of extraordinary natural splendour that no conscious being ever sees with a world as ugly as anything could be, and argues that the splendid world, unseen and on its own, is only marginally preferable; the great goods are states of consciousness, and above all those complex states in which affection for other persons is bound up with the apprehension of something lovely. For Moore the lovely object is nearly inert until somebody is loving it, and the best things on his inventory are conspicuously two-person things.

My own wrinkle — the hinge I want the poem to turn on — is a case I have been calling the unshareable good: a world containing exactly one perceiver, awake and attending to something genuinely lovely, but unable to tell anyone, to be answered, or even to carry the seeing forward in memory. By Moore's own criteria this ought to rank among the highest goods, since the state of consciousness is there, the object is lovely, and the appreciation is real; and yet it lacks every trace of the reciprocity that his favoured examples carry. I do not want the poem to settle that. I want it to make a listener feel the case as both a real good and a diminished one, and to leave the ambivalence standing.

Form: a closed pantoum of five quatrains, twenty lines, with a short title that does not count toward the twenty.

1. Lines 2 and 4 of each quatrain are carried forward verbatim to become lines 1 and 3 of the quatrain that follows. The fifth quatrain's lines 2 and 4 are lines 1 and 3 of the first, so that the poem's last line is its first.
2. Verbatim means verbatim. You may alter capitalisation and end punctuation only — no substitutions, no elisions, no reordering of words.
3. Lines of roughly nine to twelve syllables. Rhyme is optional; I would much rather you kept the metre loose and the images exact than forced a rhyme that damages a line you still have to reuse three quatrains later.
4. Three quatrains are assigned: one must be a landscape with no person in it and no implied viewer; one must be two people together attending to the same thing; one must be the unshareable good. You choose their order and which quatrains remain free.
5. At least one line of the empty-landscape quatrain must reappear, verbatim, inside the two-people quatrain. The point is that the identical line sits in two different wholes and is worth something different in each; anyone who knows the doctrine should be able to put a finger on that line.
6. No line may name the philosophy. Do not use the words good, value, worth, beauty, beautiful, intrinsic, organic, moral, ethical, aesthetic, or consciousness, and do not use any philosopher's name. Concrete nouns and verbs only.
7. The final quatrain must not turn into a thesis. No summarising connective, no couplet that tells the room what the poem meant; its whole leverage should come from where the opening lines land the second time round.

It will be read aloud to a mixed audience of philosophers and poets. Someone who has never read Moore should find it a good poem; someone who has should find it right.

After the poem, please give me a structured account of how it works, as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- `title` — string, or null.
- `lines` — array of twenty strings in order.
- `reuse_map` — array of one object per line position, with `position` (integer, 1–20), `reuses_position` (integer, or null for a line appearing for the first time), `altered` (one of `none`, `capitalisation`, `end punctuation`), and `shift` (one sentence on what the line is doing at this position that it was not doing at its earlier appearance; `n/a` where there is no earlier appearance).
- `shared_part` — object with `first_position`, `second_position`, `line`, and `non_additivity`, the last being three or four sentences on why the two wholes containing that line are not related as sum to parts.
- `moorean_elements` — array of objects, one per element you can claim, each with `element` (one of `two worlds`, `organic unities`, `appreciation as the seat of value`, `the unshareable good`), `positions` (array of integers), and `how_rendered` (one sentence).
- `banned_words_clear` — object mapping each of the eleven excluded terms to true or false, true meaning the term appears nowhere in the title or the twenty lines.
- `weakest_link` — object with `position` (integer) and `why`: the place where the poem comes closest to asserting rather than showing, or where a reused line fails to change value on its return.
- `aloud` — two sentences of guidance for whoever reads it: where to slow down, and exactly where the hinge turns.
