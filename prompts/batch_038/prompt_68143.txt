I am a third-year undergraduate writing a paper for a seminar on ancient biography and moral education, and I have run into a difficulty I would like help thinking through.

The course has been built around the claim that Plutarch's Parallel Lives is ethically purposive writing rather than history. The preface to the Life of Alexander is usually offered as the evidence: Plutarch announces that he is composing lives rather than histories, that the most celebrated deeds do not always disclose virtue or vice, and that some slight thing — a remark, a casual gesture, a joke — often tells us more about a person's character than a battle with thousands killed or the siege of a city. His method is then to select and arrange incidents for what they reveal, to pair a Greek with a Roman, and to append a comparison in which the two lives are graded against one another, including the ways in which each falls short. On this account the reader is meant to learn something about how to live by forming a judgment about the people depicted.

My problem is that this description seems to fit late-antique and medieval saints' lives at least as well, and in some respects better. A text like the Life of Antony was written to be read for edification, presents its subject as a model to be imitated, and selects incidents not because they are the most consequential but because they are the most revealing of holiness; hagiographers reorder chronology and elaborate freely in much the way we reproach Plutarch for doing when he bends a story to make an ethical point. Both genres, in other words, aim at moral formation through an exemplary life, and both license invention in its service. If that is so, the difference between them cannot be a difference of aim or of method. It looks like a difference of degree: Plutarch admits vice into his portraits and hagiography largely does not, and Plutarch's subjects are soldiers and statesmen where the saints are ascetics and martyrs.

So what I would like to know is why the scholarly treatment of these two bodies of writing diverges so sharply. Classicists mine Plutarch for evidence about the classical and Hellenistic world, whereas medievalists tend to treat a saint's life as evidence about the piety of its author and audience rather than about its subject. If both are doing the same kind of work by the same kind of method, on what grounds is one admitted as a witness and the other not? Is the pairing and the appended comparison what earns Plutarch that status, or is the disparity simply a lingering preference for pagan over Christian texts?
