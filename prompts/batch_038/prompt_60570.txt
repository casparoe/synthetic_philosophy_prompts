I keep coming back to the argument that an RL agent's reward signal is the natural locus of valence -- that whatever scalar the policy gradient climbs is doing, functionally, the job pleasure and pain do in us, which makes systems trained that way candidates for suffering. What I cant tell is whether that view has a principled line or just a vibe. I think a designed case would settle more than another round of intuitions, so build me one.

The case I want: a setup where the reward-signal view is committed to there being genuine aversive valence in play, and a rival view denies it. The rival says valence attaches only to signals the agent has itself learned to seek or avoid as ends -- things it represents and can act on -- not to terms that merely shape the gradient from outside.

Use a bog-standard RLHF-style PPO run on a language model: a learned reward-model score at the end of the sequence, plus a per-token KL penalty pulling the policy back toward the frozen reference model, with the coefficient set (and maybe auto-tuned) by the trainer. No sci-fi, no stipulated phenomenology, no "just imagine it's conscious anyway." Every fact the two views disagree over should be a structural fact about the training loop that both sides already accept. The interesting thing about the KL term is that it's scalar, it's part of what the policy is optimized against, it generates real gradients pushing the policy out of whole regions of output space, and it's exactly what stops the agent from getting as much of its reward-model reward as it otherwise could -- a standing frustration term. But the agent doesn't receive it as an observation and has no representation of it. Make view one say something aversive is present and view two say nothing is, and walk through the derivation for each. If you think view two honestly can't deny it in this setup, say so and tell me the minimal change to the case that lets it.
