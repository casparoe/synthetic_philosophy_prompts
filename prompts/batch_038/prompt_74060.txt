I'm a staff software engineer at a company that builds safety interlocks for industrial robot cells, and I sit on our Product Safety Review Board, which has to sign off on a safety case before a product ships. I have no formal training in philosophy — I write embedded C for a living and review other people's test evidence — but the current case has collapsed into an argument about what a probability statement is even supposed to mean, and the chair has asked every member to circulate a written position before we reconvene in a fortnight. Please take on the role of a philosopher of science who specializes in the interpretations of probability and who has actually sat through standards-committee meetings: I need a decision memo I can hand to eight impatient engineers, not a survey of positions.

Here is the case. The product is a vision-based presence monitor: a camera plus a machine-learned detector whose function is to notice that a person has entered the robot cell and command the drive into a safe stop. Our internal standard requires that any function credited with preventing a fatal or permanently disabling injury carry a probability of dangerous failure below 3 × 10⁻⁹ per operating hour. That threshold was inherited from a document drafted with hardware components in mind, and that provenance is part of why the argument got ugly. The team's safety case asserts that the threshold is met, and it supports the assertion in three distinct ways. First, the predecessor function — pressure-sensitive floor mats and light curtains, no machine learning at all — accumulated roughly 4.2 million field operating hours with no recorded dangerous failure, and the reliability engineer treats that as a measured frequency of the same kind of thing. Second, the team built a Bayesian belief network from structured elicitations of eleven senior engineers, and the safety analyst describes its output as a calibrated degree of belief about this product. Third, there is a paragraph in the argument stating that the figure expresses the system's own inherent disposition to fail in service, independently of what anyone believes or has happened to observe. The same number appears in all three places, and nobody has said which reading it is supposed to have.

The board is split accordingly. The hardware reliability engineer holds that probabilities like this are objective facts about the world, no different in kind from a bearing's or a relay's, and that software simply needs more field hours. A former actuary says the only honest reading is the subjective one, that the number should ship with an interval and be presented frankly as an input to a decision rather than a property of the product. Our safety-case auditor says the entire exercise is a category mistake: the code is deterministic, it does not wear out, and a failure rate describes a population of units undergoing random degradation, which is not what we have — so we should replace the number with argument-based assurance, enumerating failure modes and showing each is handled. Meanwhile the product VP points out that three competitors put numbers in their responses, that the customer's procurement template has a mandatory field for one, and that if we leave it blank we lose the bid and the field gets filled in by somebody less careful than we are.

Please draft the memo, roughly 900 to 1,200 words, recommending one of three outcomes: approve the safety case as written, approve it subject to stated conditions, or reject it and require a different form of assurance. It needs to (a) lay out the interpretive disagreement in terms engineers will follow, naming the positions correctly where that helps but without a bibliography, reading list, or citations — I cannot chase references down and would rather you argue from the concepts; (b) actually check the arithmetic, stating what 4.2 million clean operating hours can and cannot license as an upper bound on a failure rate, and whether the predecessor system is even in the right reference class for a learned detector; (c) say plainly what the number should be understood to mean if we keep it, and what we should tell the customer it means; (d) take the auditor's category-mistake objection at its strongest rather than waving it off, and say what would have to be true for that objection to carry the day; and (e) close with an actionable bottom line — conditions the team could realistically implement this quarter, with a suggested owner for each.

Before you write the memo, think through the problem on your own terms: sketch what each interpretation implies about whether our claim is even the sort of thing that could be true or false, and where the evidence genuinely bites versus where it merely sounds impressive. Then produce the memo. If you conclude the board is debating the wrong question — for instance, that the real defect is the inherited threshold rather than the interpretation of the number — say so explicitly, but still give me a document the board can vote on. Please be decisive: I would much rather argue with a definite recommendation than receive a balanced shrug.
