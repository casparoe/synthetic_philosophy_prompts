I'm about sixty percent through a novel set in a pair of river towns on an international border — nothing dramatic, no spies, the whole plot is a bridge and a joint funding agreement — and my protagonist is the engineer on one side who has to decide how to structure the repair money. I've been reading the patriotism material to get inside her head: MacIntyre's old argument that patriotism is a virtue, the cosmopolitan reply that national partiality is just an arbitary bias like any other, and the bad-faith objection that says you can't love your country without believing things about it that you'd never accept about someone else's. My reading group got into a fight about all this last week and it went nowhere, mostly because everybody kept reaching for the same stock examples. So I want to build a better one, and I want it built to spec.

Here's the shape. Two scenarios, a matched pair — same cast, same town, same project — with exactly one variable changed between them. In the first, the nationally partial way of doing it and the impartially best way happen to coincide, so both the particularist and the cosmopolitan say do it, but for reasons that can't both survive scrutiny; I want that invisible disagreement dragged out into the open and stated. Then in the second, change one thing so that the particularist flips to condemnation while the cosmopolitan still approves, and thinks the particularist is now burning real good for the sake of a symbol. Constraints: both cases stay in the workplace-and-paperwork register, no violence, no state secrets, and no evil regime — her country cannot be a villain or the whole case is rigged. The thing she'd be doing in case two has to be costless in every legal and retaliative sense, nobody sanctions her and nobody even finds out for years, so that the only stuff on the scale is her country's own character versus net welfare; and the arithmetic has to genuinely come out in favor of the impartial calculation, not be a rounding error. Keep each scenario to a paragraph, give her a name and a place, and show me her motive in what she does and what she declines to do — if I read a sentence that says she acted because she loved her country, I'll know you've stipulated the verdict instead of generating it.

Then walk me through the thing. What each of the three positions says about each version and why; which single variable is doing the work; what happens to the pair if you take that variable back out; and the best attack each side has on the case as designed. I assume the cosmopolitan will say version two isn't patriotism at all but a fetish about symbols, and the particularist will say version one's agent was never really a patriot, but tell me if either of them has a sharper move available. And be straight with me: if you think these constraints can't all be satisfied at once without cheating, say so, and tell me which one to drop and what the case looks like after. I need it for the book, obviously — but I'd also kind of like to know whether the crossover is a real feature of the debate or just an artifact of thier being too few good examples in circulation.
