I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am reporting a feature on why causal explanation in the historical sciences so often ends in a standoff. Much of the piece will turn on an analogy given to me in an interview by an economic historian, and before I build an argument on it I want to know how much weight it can bear.

His analogy was a firing squad. When five marksmen all shoot a prisoner through the heart at the same instant, he said, the death is overdetermined: any one bullet would have done it, so no method of inquiry — comparison with cases where fewer shots were fired, counterfactual reasoning about what would have happened otherwise, apportionment of responsibility — can single out a cause, and the honest conclusion is that the question which shot killed him does not have an answer. He then applied this to the end-Permian marine extinction, where candidates include Siberian volcanism, a consequent pulse of greenhouse warming, spreading anoxic or sulfidic water, and the collapse of carbonate shell-building, most of which were not independent of one another. Each bullet would have been fatal, he argued, the shots are causally entangled, and so the debate among paleobiologists about which factor did the killing is, in his phrase as I paraphrase it, a fight about a question that has no answer. He said the discipline should stop hunting causes and start measuring degrees of dependence, because the firing squad shows that in genuinely multi-causal settings attribution is a category error rather than an unsolved problem.

A paleobiologist I work with disputes this. She says the analogy only looks apt because nobody states its assumptions, and that once you do, the case it is meant to cover dissolves it. On her telling the historical situation is rather a firing squad in which the shots leave the barrels hours or millennia apart, some of the rifles turn out to have been loaded with blanks, one marksman fires at a prisoner who is already dying of something else, several of the shooters are the same shooter firing twice under different names, and no one ever establishes how many marksmen there were. She argues these are not refinements of his analogy but a different structure altogether, and that once they are on the table his deflationary conclusion no longer follows — indeed, she thinks it is the analogy, and not the evidence, that is doing the work of making attribution look impossible.

Please audit the firing squad as an analogy for multi-causal explanation in the historical sciences. Specifically:

1. Give me the correspondence table. For each element of the source — the marksmen, the bullets, the simultaneity, the prisoner, the fixed moment of death, the independence of the shots, the sufficiency of any single shot, the absence of a further fact about which bullet was the killing one — say what, if anything, it maps onto in the Permian case, and mark the entries as exact, approximate, or empty.

2. Separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his intended use from those that are merely picturesque. I want to be able to say in print why this one breaks his argument and that one does not.

3. Identify what the source domain quietly imports that the target lacks: discreteness of causes, a well-defined victim with a determinate time of death, agents who could in principle have been counted and interviewed, binary sufficiency rather than matters of degree. Say which of these imports his conclusion depends on and which it could survive without.

4. Tell me whether his conclusion survives the audit, in whole or in part. If a deflationary verdict is right for some questions about the extinction and wrong for others, draw that line for me as sharply as the material allows.

5. She also claims the amended firing squad supports a positive conclusion of her own. Test that too: is the repaired analogy now doing real work, or has it been amended until it is a restatement of her thesis rather than evidence for it?

6. If neither version can carry the load, propose a better analogy for jointly sufficient, mutually entangled, asynchronously operating causes in deep time, and state its own known failure points — I would rather publish an analogy whose limits I have specified than one whose limits I discovered from readers.

Please write for an intelligent non-specialist. Name the technical apparatus only where it earns its keep, and do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I am interested in your reasoning, not in sourcing I would have to verify elsewhere.
