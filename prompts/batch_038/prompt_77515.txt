Bit of an unusual request and sorry it's long. I'm a backend dev, no philosophy or history training whatsoever, but me and a few mates have a reading group where we argue about one book every couple of months in the pub. We did Browning's Ordinary Men and then Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners, and the group split straight down the middle: were the killers mainly ordinary blokes ground down by orders, peer pressure and careerism, or were they mostly willing, carrying a view of the victims that was already in place before anyone put them in uniform? We went round in circles for two hours, and I realised the problem — every time one side made a point, the other just reached for a different bit of the actual history. Nobody was arguing about the explanation, they were arguing about which facts they happened to remember.

So I did the dev thing and built a synthetic dataset instead. Below is a completely invented case study. Please write a dialogue between two people arguing over it: one who explains it the situation way (orders, conformity, brutalisation over time, the pull of your mates, the practical impossibility of walking away) and one who explains it the culture/disposition way (a belief system about the target group that predates the uniforms and shows up most clearly in what people do when nobody is making them). Neither of them should have read the real books or mention them — they're arguing about my unit, not about Browning and Goldhagen.

Setting: an unnamed occupying power, mid twentieth century. "Unit 9" is an auxiliary police formation of about 400 men. My facts:

1. Average age 38, reservists found unfit for front-line service, mostly railwaymen, clerks and warehousemen from one city. No political record, none had ever held party office.
2. Before posting they filled in a short attitude questionnaire. Their scores are indistinguishable from a control group of depot clerks who never left the city, on every single item.
3. Before the first operation the commanding officer tells the men anyone who doesn't want to take part can step out and will be given other duties. Eleven of about 400 do. None are punished, none lose pay, all are moved to rail and guard work. Two are later promoted.
4. The eleven are never sanctioned but they are frozen out — none of them is spoken to again by their old mates, and two of them apply to come back within the year and are taken back.
5. After the first operation roughly a third of the men are vomiting or crying, there's heavy drinking, nightmares are reported. By the twelfth operation the same platoons treat it as routine, with jokes and a betting pool.
6. Ideological instruction during training consisted of two lectures, badly attended. Several hundred letters home survive and they almost never use the official vocabulary about the target group; they're about food, weather, parcels, missing the kids.
7. From year two a subgroup of about 30 men volunteers for operations beyond quota, and their records include cruelty nobody ordered. It's selective — aimed at one group only. Other groups in the same territory are left completely alone, and men in that subgroup are separately documented being decent to those others.
8. Also from year two, transfers to a soft rear-area posting are genuinely open and everyone knows it. Four out of five men who have already taken part decline to apply.
9. Post-war interviews with survivors: most express no remorse, many still call it necessary, three say they'd do it again.
10. Three sister units raised in the same city, same training school, same commanders' course, same orders: near-identical records. A fourth unit, raised in a region annexed a generation earlier with a different language and a different church, has markedly lower participation under the same orders and the same NCO cadre, plus three documented refusals, none punished.
11. The confound I can't get past: that fourth unit operated in a district with far more army officers, inspectors and outside observers around, and its operations were much more visible. So you can't cleanly seperate "different people" from "more supervision".
12. The 30-man subgroup overlaps heavily with one pre-war club — a shooting and hillwalking society with a perfectly innocuous stated purpose. Nothing on the questionnaire in fact 2 would have picked up club membership.
13. Awkward one: the most enthusiastic man in that subgroup later turns informer against his own commander and spends the rest of his life campaigning for survivors.
14. Awkward one: participation tracks platoon-level stuff — whether the sergeant drank with the men, how tight the mess group was — much more strongly than it tracks anything measured about the individual man.

The two characters should be my mates. Priya's a data scientist, thinks in base rates, instrument validity and confounds. Tom reads history obsessively as a hobby, thinks in documents and what people said afterwards. Keep those voices genuinely distinct.

What I want out of it:
- Real back and forrth. Each of them responds to what the other actually just said, and at least once someone concedes a point and it visibly changes what they argue next. No alternating prepared speeches.
- Both competent. I don't want a strawman on either side, and I don't want Priya or Tom winning by default.
- Every one of the 14 facts gets picked up by somebody, and each of them names at least two facts their own account handles badly and says what they'd do about it — whether that's a measurement problem, an auxiliary assumption, or a genuine dent.
- "It's obviously a bit of both" is banned unless whoever says it commits to a rough weighting and says what evidence would move the weighting.
- I want each of them to make one risky prediction about records that aren't in my list — something like "if I got hold of the transfer applications, here's what I'd bet I'd find" — and then state what they think their rival would predict about the very same records. That's the part I care about most, because it's how I'll know the two explanations aren't just redescribing the same data.
- Don't end it with a winner. But do end with them agreeing on the single further fact that would actually discriminate between their accounts, and why that one rather than another.
- Please no speechifying about how awful it all is. I'm after the explanation arguement, not the moral one.

About 1,100 to 1,600 words. Cheers.
