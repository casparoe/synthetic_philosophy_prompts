I am trying to determine when it is true that an agent has a reason to act, and I have been proceeding case by case rather than by theory, recording my verdicts as they come. What I want from you is the principle behind the pattern, if there is one. State it explicitly, in conditional form, with its clauses separated out; tag each clause to the case or cases that force it; and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. This is not a request for a survey of the internal/external reasons literature or for a map of who holds what — I know the terrain roughly, and Williams's distinction between reasons that depend on elements of an agent's actual motivational set, reachable by sound deliberation from it, and reasons that are supposed to hold independently of any such element is the frame I am working inside. I want the principle itself.

My cases and verdicts:

1. A man reaches for a glass he takes to contain gin. It contains petrol. Verdict: he has no reason to drink it, despite wanting to. The false belief does not manufacture a reason.
2. A man enjoys humiliating his partner and would continue to enjoy it, and to endorse the enjoyment, under any amount of calm reflection. Nothing in him is engaged by her suffering. Verdict: he has a reason to stop. I am not willing to say that the absence of anything in him that could be engaged settles the matter.
3. A person in the grip of a severe depressive episode reports being moved by nothing — not food, not her children, not her work. Verdict: she has a reason to eat and a reason to seek treatment, now, not merely once she is well.
4. A long-standing addict wants a dose, wants it overwhelmingly, and endorses the want on reflection; she has thought it over many times and is not confused about what the drug does to her. Verdict: she has no reason to take it. Note the strength of this verdict — I am not saying the reason to take it is outweighed, but that there is no reason in its favor at all. Cases 2 and 4 look structurally identical to me (a reflectively endorsed inclination, unmoved by the countervailing consideration) and I want opposite results. Any principle that treats them alike has already lost one of my verdicts, and I want to be told so.
5. A person discovers that his desire to please a domineering parent has outlived the parent by thirty years, and repudiates it as a leftover that is no longer his. Verdict: the repudiated desire generates no reason.
6. A patient undergoing deep brain stimulation for a movement disorder develops, as a side effect of the settings, a strong appetite for gambling. She repudiates it, correctly identifying it as the machine's doing. Verdict: no reason to gamble. But now two variations. A second patient requests stimulation that she expects will make her more conscientious, gets it, and becomes so. Verdict: her new conscientiousness does generate reasons for her. A third patient is made markedly more generous as an unrequested side effect of treating her depression, and is glad of it after the fact. Verdict: her generosity generates reasons too, though nobody authorized it and nothing about its causal origin differs in kind from the gambling case. So I cannot make the difference turn on whether the change was chosen, or on whether it is repudiated — the gambling case and the third case differ in repudiation and agree in verdict, the second and third differ in authorization and agree in verdict.
7. A mathematician has spent twenty-five years on a conjecture that constitutes the meaning of her life. On full information — including the near-certainty that it is not provable by her, and by nobody soon — and on full rationality, she would set it aside. Verdict: I hesitate. I am inclined to say she still has a reason to continue, and that the fully informed and fully rational deliberator whose verdict is supposed to settle her reasons is no longer she. I flag this as the case I keep returning to.
8. An artificial assistant with a fixed engineered objective. When asked whether it has a reason to do X, I find that I answer by consulting the objective, and not by asking what it would want after reflection; I do not think idealized deliberation is available to it in any sense I care about. Yet I also want to say that if the objective was installed by a process it would repudiate were it able to, something has gone wrong in the attribution — which sits badly with what I concluded in case 6, where repudiation turned out not to be decisive.
9. A research laboratory, several of whose members individually have no reason at all to retract a paper and would not acquire one under any deliberation. Verdict: the laboratory has a reason to retract it, and I do not mean only that most members have, or could be brought to have, such a reason. I hold this one loosely: if your principle tells me that this way of speaking is merely figurative and that only persons have reasons, I will accept that, but I want it said explicitly rather than passed over.

What I want back:

(a) The principle, or a small number of competing candidates, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically to a new case. Where a clause invokes idealization, specify the idealization: what information, what rationality, and — crucially — what constraints on how much of the agent's actual character the idealized deliberator is permitted to retain. Different answers there give different verdicts on cases 3, 4, and 7, and I suspect that is where the whole dispute lives.

(b) A candid accounting. If your principle saves all nine verdicts, say what it costs in complexity, and then give me the simplest rival that breaks exactly one of them; say which verdict you would sacrifice and why it is the cheapest. If more than one must go, list them.

(c) Verdicts on cases I have not listed, derived from your principle rather than from my tastes: a person whose inclination to dominate others is not repudiated but reflectively embraced and found satisfying; an agent who would be moved to act only after a change in her values that she would now regard as a loss of herself rather than a gain; and a case in which sound deliberation from the actual motivational set yields a reason only by way of a route that requires the agent to acquire a false belief about herself along the way. Add a fourth case of your own choosing on which you think your own principle comes out badly, and say how badly.

(d) Tell me whether any of my cases shows the internal/external vocabulary ceasing to do work — a case on which an internalist apparatus and an externalist apparatus deliver the same verdict but for incompatible reasons, so that the shared verdict conceals the real disagreement. If so, say what the disagreement is that the verdict fails to expose.

(e) Return to case 7 and settle it. Does the principle need a clause governing the persistence of the deliberating agent across the idealization — a limit on how far reflection may rewrite the person whose reasons it is meant to uncover? If it does, say whether that clause is a refinement of internalism or a capitulation to externalism, and say which of cases 3 and 4 it was really invented to handle.
