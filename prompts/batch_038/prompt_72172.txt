ok, kind of a long one, but I need the detail for the thing to work.

I'm an RA on a comparative politics project that codes episodes of democratic backsliding. Our unit of analysis is the *episode*, not the regime — so a country-year gets flagged if something happened in it, and there's a separate column for whether the episode culminated in an actual regime change. There's a lead coder (call her M) and a four-person committee; M does the first pass and the committee overrules her maybe one time in ten. I've been asked to draft the coding memo for the next batch and I want to predict what M will do with a case we just picked up. Mostly though I want to know whether she's running one rule or several that happen to have agreed so far.

Her track record to date (countries are anonymized A–G because the data's embargoed, you'll probably recognize a few):

- **A:** a party wins a two-thirds legislative majority in an election nobody disputes, then rewrites the constitution, redraws districts, and replaces the heads of the public broadcasters and the judicial appointments council. Elections keep happening; the opposition keeps winning the big cities. M: backsliding, high confidence.
- **B:** government makes foreign-funded NGOs register as "agents" and puts the state media regulator under direct ministerial control — but the constitutional court strikes the media part down and the government complies inside a month. M: not backsliding. Margin note, literally: "constraints still bite."
- **C:** a referendum, narrowly passed on a contested count, moves the country to an executive presidency and lifts the bar on consecutive terms. The campaign was legal, the vote was administered normally, no fraud established. M: backsliding. This one threw me, because procedurally nothing improper happened.
- **D:** an incumbent loses, concedes, transfers power — but during the campaign his administration opened tax and procurement investigations into three opposition donors and the head of an opposition broadcaster. M: not backsliding, coded "episodic abuse, no institutional change."
- **E:** the losing candidate in a presidential election refuses to concede, publicly pressures the officials certifying the vote in two jurisdictions, supporters breach the legislature. Courts reject the challenges, certification happens, transfer happens. Committee split 2–2 and M's own vote was "episode, non-culminating."
- **F:** a supreme court, staffed by the *previous* administration, disqualifies the leading opposition candidate on corruption charges that most observers call politically motivated but that were established through ordinary criminal procedure. Incumbent goes on to win with 52%. M: backsliding. Worth noting the executive isn't the actor here.
- **G:** after mass protests, parliament passes a law stripping the president of decree power and vesting it in the legislature; the president signs it, complies with it, and the next election eighteen months out is expected to be free. M: not backsliding, coded "constitutional rebalancing."

Now the new case, **H**. A president elected with 38% in the first round faces a legislature held by an opposition coalition. Fourteen months before the next election:

1. The legislature passes a law transferring appointment of the electoral commission from the president to a council of judges.
2. The president's constitutional court — whose members were mostly appointed by his predecessor, who was of the president's own party — strikes the law down.
3. The legislature ignores the ruling, seats its own commission anyway, and bars the president's party from state broadcast airtime until it "returns to the constitutional order."
4. The president responds by directing the tax and anti-corruption agencies to open investigations into eleven opposition MPs.
5. No violence, no censorship of independent media, no change to the franchise, and outside observers still think the election's outcome is genuinely uncertain.
6. Each side's foreign backers describes the *other* side as the backslider.

So:

- What does M code for H, and how confident? Assume she has a fourth option available — "uncodable pending further information" — which she's never used.
- Write out the rule that best explains all seven earlier verdicts as an actual codebook entry, the kind you could hand to a different coder and get the same results. If you can't write one that covers A–G without straining, say where it strains.
- Then give me the other rules that fit A–G just as well but diverge on H. I can already see at least three candidates — something about who holds the pen (incumbent vs. challenger), something about which institution is being degraded regardless of who does it, something about whether adverse rulings get complied with — but I want them stated sharply enough that I can see exactly how each one lands on H and why.
- Which of A–G is the closest analog to H, and which single earlier case is doing the most work in your reconstruction? If I could get one more precedent coded, or one more fact about H, what would best discriminate between your top two rules?
- Separate question, and this is what my PI actually cares about: if you handed this codebook to a coder with the opposite partisan priors, would they code A–G the same way? Is there anything in her track record that's evidence she's running a rule rather than a taste that's been dressed up as one? I have to say something defensible about this in the memo, and "she's rigorous" isn't going to cut it.
- If the codebook genuinely underdetermines H, tell me what principle the project would be adopting under each possible coding. Because whichever way we go we'll be accused of picking a side in a fight where both sides are calling the other one the fascist, and I'd rather know which side we're picking before we pick it.

I'm not looking for a lit review of the concept or a survey of how the major indices would score it — I know they'd disagree, thats kind of the point. Just the reconstruction of her rule, the prediction, and the audit of how much of it is rule and how much is her.
