I am in the fourth year of a PhD in modern European history — my dissertation concerns how nineteenth-century industrial towns narrated their own founding — and for the past eighteen months I have been seconded three days a week as research lead at a municipal heritage service. We run a small town museum, a reading-room archive, four satellite interpretation points along the river, and the schools liaison programme, and between them we field somewhere between eight and twelve substantive proposals a year about how some contested piece of the local past should be presented: a gallery reinstallation, the text of a new plaque, a walking-tour script, a Key Stage 3 module, a digitisation project, a request to remove something from a wall. Each one has to become a written recommendation my four colleagues can vote on at the monthly programming meeting, I typically have twenty minutes of preparation per item, and anything that alters a physical installation then goes to the Trust, two of whose nine members sit there representing the local descendants' society. The decisions are not high-stakes in the abstract, but two or three a year go to the regional press, and the ones that do are almost always about who is being honoured and who is being exposed.

Our meetings currently resolve into temperament rather than argument. One colleague believes we owe visiting children figures worth admiring; another resists any evaluative adjective whatsoever and would accession the entire contents of the mill owner's attic rather than choose between them; I default, predictably, to exposure and condemnation. I have been trying to discipline this with the three modes from Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation of 1874 — monumental, antiquarian and critical history — not as a taxonomy of exhibit styles but as a diagnostic, on the grounds that he ties each mode to a distinct need of the living person (one who acts and strives, one who preserves and reveres, one who suffers and wants release) and warns that each has its own characteristic corruption. In staff meetings I have been using shorthand for those three corruptions: costume, for monumental history that slips into invention and into an exemplar so lofty that it paralyses rather than spurs the present; reliquary, for antiquarian history that flattens every distinction, reveres the old merely because it is old, and ends in an institution that can neither judge nor make room; and root-cutting, for critical history that forgets the critic is himself descended from what he condemns and so leaves him with a borrowed, second-hand nature in place of a grown one. I have also been insisting on two further things from the same essay that my colleagues resist: that a healthy relation to the past depends on the capacity to draw a horizon and to leave things outside it, which is why I have taken to calling our 120-word panel limit the horizon test; and that what is actually being measured is something like his plastic power, an individual or community's capacity to convert what happened into nourishment rather than to be crushed or embalmed by it.

What I want is a procedure that a curator, archivist or interpretation officer with no philosophical training can run on a written proposal in about twenty minutes and that produces the recommendation memo. It should be an ordered set of steps, each with the question to ask, what counts as an adequate answer, the observable sign that the step has failed, and where the step exits — and it should say plainly which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume, because I would rather my colleagues knew that than discover it in a public meeting. I need it to identify which of the three needs a proposal is actually serving, which is often not the one its sponsor claims, and I need it to detect the three corruptions at the level of the specific artefact under discussion: a sentence on a draft panel, an object proposed for display, a name proposed for a title. It should include a rule for when two modes must be sequenced in a single installation — one panel doing one thing, the next undercutting it — as opposed to blended into a single voice that is none of the three. It should mark the point at which the procedure stops being about evidence and interpretation and becomes a question of standing, where the decision belongs to the Trust or to the descendants' society rather than to us, and it should say whether the 120-word limit changes which modes are even available on a given panel. Please write the steps so that each is statable without Nietzsche's name anywhere in it, since one of my colleagues considers him irrelevant to the work and the procedure has to survive her reading it aloud. Then run the procedure end to end on three cases and show the memo each produces. First: a proposal to reinstall the interpretation at the mill itself, where the same building produced the town's prosperity and the 1911 fire that killed thirty-nine workers, several of them children, and where the current installation is forty years old and mentions the fire in one sentence on the exit panel. Second: a formal request from the descendants' society to remove the founder's portrait from the entrance hall and commission a replacement. Third: a proposal from my own team to publish the company's payroll ledgers online, which list workers' names and ages and include a ruled column recording injuries and fines. I expect the procedure to land on at least one answer I will not like — I have already guessed that it will tell me my preferred critical framing is the wrong one for the school module — and I would rather it say so and explain why than soften the verdict. Close with an honest statement of the procedure's limits: that it can diagnose and sequence modes but cannot tell a community what it ought to remember, and that there are proposals for which no mode is correct and the right output is a decision to leave a site uninterpreted, if that is in fact a conclusion the procedure can reach.
