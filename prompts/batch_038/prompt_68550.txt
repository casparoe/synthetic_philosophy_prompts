The question as set was this.

> Question. In his 1939 address Proof of an External World, Moore maintains that he can give a rigorous proof of the existence of external objects, while also maintaining that he cannot prove the premises from which that proof proceeds. Two charges are standardly pressed against the proof: that it begs the question against the skeptic, and that it fails to satisfy one or more of the conditions Moore himself lays down for a rigorous proof. (a) Set out the proof and the conditions. (b) Take one of the two charges and assess it. In your assessment, state explicitly whether, if the charge is sound, it shows that Moore does not know the premises of his proof, or only that he cannot deploy them to convince an interlocutor who denies them.

The candidate's script follows in full.

> Moore's proof is deliberately theatrical. He holds up his two hands, announcing as he makes a gesture with the right that here is one hand and, with the left, that here is another; from these two premises he draws the conclusion that at least two external things exist, and hence that external things exist. The conclusion is not a claim about appearances, since Moore glosses the class of external objects as things to be met with in space, and he takes himself to be answering the complaint, which he attributes to Kant, that philosophy has never managed to prove the existence of things outside us. Against Kant's view that only one such proof was possible, Moore insists that there are many, each of them perfectly rigorous.
>
> A proof is rigorous, on Moore's account, if it meets three conditions: its premises must be known to be true rather than merely believed; the premises must be different from the conclusion; and the conclusion must follow from them. Moore holds that all three are met in this case.
>
> The interesting question is which of the two charges gets traction. I shall argue that the first does, and that the second charge collapses into it. The premise of the proof is that here is one hand. That premise is not neutral with respect to the dispute, because to assert the presence of a hand is already to assert the existence of something to be met with in space, which is precisely what the skeptic denies. The proof is therefore circular in the strict sense: the conclusion is contained in the premise, and the argument cannot give anyone a reason to accept the existence of external things who did not already accept it. This is also why, on my reading, the second charge collapses into the first. Moore's condition requires that the premises be different from the conclusion; if the conclusion is contained in the premises, they cannot be genuinely different propositions, and the proof fails that condition as well. The failure is not merely dialectical — it is not simply that a skeptic will refuse to grant the premises — but structural, a feature of the argument itself.
>
> There is a second line of attack, which I take to be more damaging, and which draws on Moore's own concessions. In his later discussion of the argument from dreaming, Moore admits that he cannot prove that he is not dreaming; he cannot, that is, rule out the very possibility the skeptic deploys. The skeptic's argument runs: if you knew that here is a hand, you would know that you are not dreaming; you do not know that you are not dreaming; therefore you do not know that here is a hand. Since Moore concedes the second premise — he cannot prove that he is not dreaming — he cannot claim to know the premises of his own proof, and the first condition goes unmet. The proof thus fails by Moore's own admission, and Moore appears to have recognized the difficulty, which is why he says that his proof will convince only those already disposed to accept the existence of external things.
>
> Two responses are worth noting. Wittgenstein's remarks in On Certainty treat the whole exchange differently: on his view what Moore claims to know functions not as an empirical claim but as part of the framework within which empirical claims are tested, so that the misuse lies in Moore's first-person knowledge claim rather than in the inference. Crispin Wright's diagnosis is closer to my own, in that he holds that warrant fails to transmit from premise to conclusion, so that the proof cannot furnish a first warrant for its conclusion. If Wright is right, the skeptic is entitled to say that Moore's proof gives him nothing new, which is exactly the point I have been pressing.
>
> What survives, then, is not a proof but a diagnosis. The demand that Moore justify his premises before he may claim to know his conclusion is one that no argument addressed to a skeptic could ever satisfy, and the proof is best read as an attempt to meet a demand that was never legitimate. That is a defensible conclusion, but it is not the conclusion Moore thinks he has earned, and the argument he actually gives does not get him there.
