I've just been made secretary of our little electoral-reform group in a south Wales valley town — about a dozen of us, affiliated to the national society but we write our own submissions — purely because I was the only member who'd actually read the council's consultation paper. Our first job is to say something about how the town council's twelve seats should be elected, and I'm terrified of us contradicting ourselves in public, because we've got a bit of a track record and half of it is in the minutes. Here's the record, more or less. In 2011 we campaigned for AV, though our minute said explicitly that we weren't pretending it was proportional, only that it was a foot in the door. When the town council used to elect all twelve at once from a single ward — block vote, twelve crosses each — we objected loudly on the grounds that it's the one system where you can vote for every seat and still not get a single one, and we asked for either single-member wards or STV in three four-seat wards. We've defended tiny upland wards for the villages on the hill, arguing that at-large elections drown them. We've held up the London Assembly as the model — fourteen constituency members, eleven compensating list seats on modified d'Hondt, five per cent threshold — while saying the threshold ought to be nearer three, since obviously some threshold is needed to stop a chamber filling up with single-issue people. We backed d'Hondt over Sainte-Laguë for splitting the chairs of the scrutiny committees between the groups, on the basis that a small tilt to the bigger groups buys workability. We cited the Scottish councils as proof that ordinary voters cope fine with ranking a multi-member ballot, against the people who say STV is too complicated — though we did tell a housing association no to cumulative voting for their nine-seat board, partly because nobody would understand the ballot and partly because it hands everything to whoever can organise a slate. And when the Senedd went over to sixteen six-member closed-list constituencies we welcomed the proportionality publicly but sent in a letter regretting the loss of the personal vote, saying a voter ought to be able to punish a named individual. (My nan's always had one MS she could ring up; from May she'll have six and no idea who any of them are, which is roughly how the branch feels too, though nobody wants to say so.)

Now the actual case. The community governance review has three options on the table for the twelve seats. A: eight single-member wards under first-past-the-post plus four top-up seats allocated from a town-wide party vote by d'Hondt with a ten per cent threshold — the officer's paper describes this as "the same principle as the London Assembly". B: our old demand, STV in three four-seat wards. C: twelve single-member wards and nothing else, which is backed both by the hamlet on the hill (about twenty-eight per cent of one of the proposed four-seat wards, about seven per cent of the town as a whole) and by the people who think a community council is simply about who you can knock on the door of. There's also a loose group of private renters, mostly under thirty, roughly nine per cent of the electorate and spread evenly right across the town, who want the list tier for obvious reasons. One thing I can't work out whether matters: its a community council, so hardly anybody actually stands under a party banner here and there's been no party politics in the place for as long as anyone remembers.

So — which of A, B or C would we back if we wanted to be consistent with ourselves, and which single precedent in our own record is the closest analog? I'm more interested in the rule we're implicitly running than in the answer, because our record seems to fit at least three different principles: proportionality subject to some workability constraint; representation of actual communities of interest whatever the mechanism turns out to be; and something about voters being able to mark a ballot honestly without expertise and still hold a named person to account. I'm fairly sure different members are running different ones and none of us has admitted it. Tell me what each principle predicts about A, B and C, give me your best guess at what the branch will end up saying, and if our record genuinely underdetermines it, say plainly what principle we'd be adopting with each choice — I'd rather we picked one knowingly than drifted into it. Then design me a fourth option, call it D, that would force apart the two rules I most suspect we're each running: a system on which those two rules have to disagree, so I can put it to the meeting and see who flinches. And last thing, is there anything in A as drafted that's actually incoherent, or that we'd be mortified to have endorsed once someone cleverer than us points it out?
