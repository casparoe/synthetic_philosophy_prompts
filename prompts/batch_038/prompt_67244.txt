**The directive as a rebuttable default**

The standard debate about advance directives is a debate about whether the earlier self has authority over the later one. Dworkin's answer is yes: a person has *critical* interests as well as experiential ones, interests not in how their life feels moment to moment but in how it goes as a whole, and it is those interests that give a competent person's earlier decision the right to shape the ending of their story even when the demented person now in the bed has no stake in that shape at all. His Alzheimer's patient who paints, enjoys music, and seems entirely content is supposed to prove the point: her contentment is real but shallow, and honoring her directive respects the person whose life it is. The critics reply that once the psychological connections are gone, the being whose critical interests mattered is not the being who would bear the consequences of the decision, and a directive can condemn a contented patient to death on the strength of values she no longer holds and could not endorse.

My claim is that both sides are arguing about the wrong object. Both treat the directive as a verdict — either binding or not — and then fight about the conditions under which verdicts of that kind are legitimate. But the directive isn't a verdict. It's an instrument that reallocates justificatory burden inside a specific institution: the bedside deliberation of surrogate, clinician, and family that actually produces the decision. On that reading its function is not to settle the question but to structure it — to set the default and to say what would count as a reason to depart from it.

Two things follow, and they're the parts of the paper I care about.

First, an ambiguity in ordinary directive language that I'll call the indexical slide. When people fill these forms out they write something like: if I can no longer recognize my family and there's no reasonable hope of improvement, I don't want artificial means used to prolong my life. That sentence contains two very different propositions. (a) A statement of the earlier self's own evaluative standard: a life lived that way would be, by the lights I now hold, not a life worth continuing. (b) A prediction about the later self: whoever is in that bed will not want to go on. Almost nobody writing the sentence is in a position to assert (b), and nobody reading it later can check it. But (b) is what gives the sentence its force at the bedside, because (b) is about the patient in front of you, while (a) is frankly about someone who is no longer present. I think much of the Dworkin–critics dispute is a dispute about which of these a directive expresses. The integrity argument only gets off the ground on (a); the critics' objection has its full force only against (b). And once you keep them apart, the two have different scopes: an (a)-directive can have authority over the shape of a life without having authority over the interests of the person now in it, which is exactly the concession the debate keeps needing and never makes.

Second, a practical upshot that I think is more than cosmetic. The advice people get is to be specific about conditions and treatments. On my view that's backwards. Specificity about conditions is nearly worthless, because the actual case will be some disjunctive mess no list anticipated; specificity about *reasons and thresholds* is what a surrogate desperately needs. A useful directive would say: here is what I'd count as a loss serious enough to refuse treatment for; here is roughly how much evidence of present contentment it would take to change my mind; here is whose judgment about that evidence I trust and what would make me distrust it. That turns the document from a prediction into standing instructions about how to weigh evidence, and it makes the directive's authority graded rather than all-or-nothing.

The objection I expect and can't yet answer: this looks like a retreat dressed up as a theory. If the directive only sets a default, then in precisely the cases that generated the debate it licenses the outcome Dworkin wanted forbidden. Worse, a default defeasible by "she seems happy now" is a default that won't survive contact with a motivated family, since apparent contentment is cheap evidence and relatives have every reason to see it. So the view may be self-defeating in practice — it hands the decision to whoever is at the bedside with the largest emotional stake. My only reply is that the directive should specify what counts as good evidence of contentment, which starts to look like the condition-specificity I just rejected.
