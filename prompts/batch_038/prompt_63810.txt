So me and my friend Ravi got into it until like 2am last night and I still don't think either of us won. He's a quant, I'm in a philosophy PhD, and the argument was: if a liquid market's price disagrees with something you've thought hard about and would defend in public, what does that actually tell you? His line is basically the no-trade stuff — if we're both rational, know each other to be rational, and started from the same prior, then the fact that I want to bet against the price means either I'm wrong or I think somebody on the other side is dumb, and neither of those is a great epistemic position to be in. I said the common prior is doing all the work and no two humans actually have one, plus there's the Grossman-Stiglitz point that if prices fully revealed information nobody would ever pay to find any out, so they can't. He said that's an argument that prices are noisy, not that they're worse than my armchair reasoning, and honestly that's a fair hit.

I run a little reading group on the epistemology of markets — mix of philosophy people, two quants, one guy who trades prediction markets for fun — and I want to hand out something that makes people locate where they actually stand rather than where they say they stand. Could you write me a decision tree / self-sorting quiz for that? The entry commitment is "a deep, liquid market's price disagrees with an estimate I arrived at myself." Then eight or nine questions, each one a concrete situation rather than a label: e.g. you find out the counterparty on your order is someone who's been right about this stuff before and they sized up; or there's no market on the question at all because the payoff lands two hundred years out; or you'd take the bet but only for pocket money; or you'd take it and are quietly relieved nobody's asking you to. Someone should be able to answer every question cold, without knowing any of the jargon. Each leaf gets its standard name if it has one (strong-form efficiency, Hayekian epistemic humility, the informational-unraveling objection, limits of arbitrage and noise trader risk, the view that the market is answering a different question than the one you asked, whatever else belongs in there), one sentence on it's most awkward commitment, and then — the part I actually care about — tell me which combinations in the logical space come out empty and why: incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's staked it out yet.

Two requests. First, please don't build the thing around the efficient-markets-versus-behavioral-finance binary, that's not where the interesting splits are. The splits I want are things like whether your willingness to trade is itself evidence about you, whether two rational people can genuinely disagree at all, whether you're the marginal trader or a price-taker, whether the price you're looking at is even a belief-aggregate or mostly a liquidity and risk-premium artifact, and whether a market is a knowledge aggregator or just an incentive machine that happens to spit out numbers. Second, flag any pair of leaves your questions can't really separate — if two positions always produce the same answer pattern, I'd rather know that then have you invent a fake question to pry them apart.
