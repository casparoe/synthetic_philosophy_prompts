Me and a friend have been co-running a Stoic practice group for about two years now — ten-ish people, half philosophy grad students, half normal humans who just want to stop spiralling about work email — and we've hit a wall that has basically eaten the last six months of sessions. Half the group are strict-dichotomy people: only assent, desire, aversion and impulse are up to us, full stop, and everything else including our own bodies, our health, our reputations and whether our projects succeed is "not up to us" in the only sense that does any work. The other half are the trichotomy crowd: some things are only *partly* up to us, so the right move is to internalize your goals (play the match to play your best, not to win) and attach a reserve clause to every plan, fate permitting. We actually did the adversarial-collaboration homework already — we have a signed list of agreed claims (both camps accept that "up to us" isn't a claim about causal omnipotence, both accept the archer case where the archer's end is doing everything he can to hit rather than hitting, both accept that preferred indifferents are genuinely choiceworthy without being good) and a disagreements table where each side approved the wording of its own row. What we cannot produce, after three attempts, is the cruxes section.

So: write the cruxes section our joint statement should contain. Exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no summary, no sign-off after item five. Each item a sentence or two, and it has to carry three things: the sub-question stated in wording both camps would sign, the specific case or argument or piece of evidence both camps agree bears on it, and each camp's prediction about how that will come out, written down in advance so nobody can retrofit later. I want the five to be genuinely different cruxes, not five slices of the same one; at least one should be about whether the trichotomy actually collapses back into the dichotomy once you notice that an internalized goal is itself fully up to us, and at least one should come out of the four-week self-experiment the group ran last spring (weeks 1–2 everyone logged one recurring goal in outcome-framed terms, weeks 3–4 half the group re-logged the same goal in internalized terms, with a 0–10 "how much did this event unsettle you" rating stamped before and after, plus a note of whether they actually used the reserve clause that day). And if you judge that one of the five is really a verbal dispute in disguise — my suspicion is it's about what "control" is being used to mean — flag it with a bracket at the end of that item naming the word doing the smuggling, but still give both predictions.
