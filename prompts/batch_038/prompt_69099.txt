I'm putting together a session for a first-year philosophy-and-film seminar on the moral status of non-player characters, and I want to build it around Wreck-It Ralph (2012). The literature I know on video-game ethics is almost entirely player-facing: Kant's indirect-duty move, according to which what's objectionable about cruelty in a game is what it does to the one performing it; the gamer's dilemma about which virtual acts are permissible when no real person is harmed; and Chalmers's argument in Reality+ that virtual worlds contain real digital objects and support genuine experiences. What strikes me about this film is that it takes the player out of the moral scene altogether. The human players are all but absent; the action happens after closing time, in Game Central Station and Diet Cola Mountain. And the wrongs that carry the plot — the Nicelanders shutting Ralph out of their own celebration, Vanellope being barred from the race and barred from leaving her game because she is a glitch, King Candy editing her code so that no one in Sugar Rush remembers who she actually is, Turbo's cabinet-hopping that gets two games unplugged and apparently kills everyone still inside them — all occur between characters, with no one watching.

I'd like a reading of the film on exactly that question: do these characters matter morally, and if so on what grounds — sentience, the fact that they persist and remember across sessions, the fact that they stand in relations of dependence and obligation to one another, or something about the roles they were written into? Please take three complications seriously and say what the film does with each. First, whether the player-facing arguments survive at all once the player is gone, or whether the film is quietly assuming that a moral community of NPCs is self-sufficient. Second, what I'll call the reset problem: the film turns on the fact that finishing a race resets Sugar Rush and erases King Candy's edits, which suggests that wrongs against these characters depend on their effects persisting — tell me whether persistence is doing real work in the story or whether I'm overreading it. Third, the Cy-Bugs, whom every character treats as pure appetite and whom the film exterminates wholesale without a second thought: is that a limit on the film's own moral ontology, or just the genre needing enemies?

King Candy's tampering is the piece I find most interesting, because it is a wrong committed against the facts of someone's nature, in a way that makes an entire community's moral responses track a falsehood. Say whether that is a wrong against Vanellope, against the game, or both, and whether Walton's make-believe apparatus — props, prescriptions to imagine — illuminates what happens when the props start making claims about one another, or actively obscures it.

Be candid where the reading strains. This is a comedy with a plot to get through, and its ending has Ralph back at the apartment block, wrecking it and content, which looks like an endorsement of precisely the functionalist picture — characters as roles — that would deny them any standing of their own. Tell me plainly whether the film takes a position or whether I'm supplying one, and close with what the film contributes to the debate that argument alone does not. Describe the scenes rather than quoting dialogue. Finally, say what would change your mind: which consideration would push you to the verdict that these characters are not moral patients after all, or that the film is neutral between the readings I've sketched, and where in the above your interpretation is most likely to have gone wrong.
