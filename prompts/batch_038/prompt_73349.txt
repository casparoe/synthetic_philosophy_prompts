I'm auditing a first-year political philosophy seminar this term, and our seminar leader circulated a two-page revision handout that a previous lecturer apparently wrote for an online course; it has been passed around the department for years. Several of us are using it to prepare an essay on distributive justice, but a friend of mine insists it is sloppy in a way that would cost us marks, and neither of us has actually read the book, so we cannot settle it. Here is the passage, transcribed as it appears:

"Nozick asks you to start from a distribution that your favourite theory of justice declares perfectly just — call it D1, whether that means strict equality or Rawls's difference principle — and then to watch what happens. A famous basketball player, Wilt Chamberlain, signs a contract under which 25 cents of the price of every ticket goes into a box with his name on it; over a season a very large number of fans happily do this, and he ends up with a fortune no one else has, producing a new distribution, D2. Nozick's reply to anyone who calls D2 unjust is devastating: the fans got the game they paid for, Chamberlain got what they freely handed over, and nobody else had any claim on the money, so D2 is just too. A theory that condemns D2 is really saying that people are entitled to less than what they voluntarily agreed to give. Two lessons follow. First, freedom and patterned justice simply cannot coexist: to keep any pattern in place — equality, need, desert, marginal product, whatever — the authorities must continuously step in and stop adults from making deals with one another, and Nozick's own view is that taxing what people earn is a way of making them work for other people's purposes, so the interference is objectionable twice over. Second, this settles the bigger question in favour of the market: D1 came about through free dealing, D2 came about through free dealing out of D1, and so a society of voluntary exchanges will automatically produce whatever distribution entitlement requires, leaving the state nothing to do but protect people against force, fraud and theft. Patterned principles of distributive justice are thereby refuted, and with them socialism and welfare liberalism — every view that asks what a just distribution looks like instead of asking how it came about."

My instinct is that the description of the basketball case in the middle is broadly right and that the two lessons smuggle in something, but I cannot say what. Could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a Nozick scholar would sign off on, which are defensible simplifications a careful reader would tolerate in a revision sheet, and which would make them object outright? I am especially interested in four things: whether the inference from the fans' choices to the justice of D2 depends on assuming that D1 was itself just, and whether the handout handles that assumption correctly; whether Nozick thinks the example shows that liberty is incompatible with any principle of distributive justice, or with something narrower than the handout suggests — noting that he offers a theory of distributive justice of his own, with acquisition, transfer and rectification, plus a proviso, all of which the handout never mentions; whether the remark about taxing earnings belongs to this argument at all or is a distinct claim that has been welded onto it; and, the point my friend and I argued about for an hour, whether the second lesson is anything Nozick would endorse, given how much of the book is taken up with the actual history of how holdings were acquired and with correcting past injustice. Please do not simply balance the readings against each other and leave it to me: tell me which sentences of that handout you judge to be false rather than merely loose, say plainly how you would rewrite the passage if you were setting it, and give me a straight answer on whether a student who reproduced the handout's reasoning in a marked essay would be penalised for it or forgiven it as standard simplification.
