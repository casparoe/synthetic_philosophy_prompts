I'm the secretary of a tenant union — about 400 of us, one board election a year, currently first-past-the-post, and we've got maybe five people running every time. At last month's meeting the membership voted to put a question on the next agenda: do we switch to ranked-choice (instant runoff, rank them 1-2-3)? Someone stuck me with writing the one-pager that goes out to members beforehand, which I said yes to before realizing I don't actually understand the arguments being made about it.

Here's the problem. Two members invoked the exact same theorem in opposite directions and both acted like it settled the question.

One said ranked choice means you can just honestly rank everybody and stop worrying about wasting your vote or splitting it — apparently the math people proved you never have to think strategically again under RCV. The other one, a guy who does data stuff at work, said that's backwards: the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem proves every voting system is gameable, so RCV buys us nothing over what we already have and we shouldn't bother changing anything.

Can you pull apart what "this system can be manipulated" / "this system is strategy-proof" actually means, as a list of the distinct precise claims somebody could be making? For each one I want to know whether it's trivially true, actually true of instant runoff specifically, false, or true-but-doing-no-work. In particular I'm fuzzy on: what has to be true of a system before that theorem even bites (how many candidates, what kind of ballot, is a coin flip to break a tie going to ruin it?); whether "can be manipulated" has anything to do with "you should manipulate" or "manipulating usually pays"; whether the theorem covers the approval-ballot option we also discussed, where you just tick everyone you'd accept; what changes if in practice only two of our five candidates are ever really in contention; and whether a bloc ranking only its own guy and leaving the rest blank is a real strategy or a folk myth. I also dimly remember reading that for some systems it's computationally hard to even work out what the strategic vote would be — does that matter at all to whether manipulation is a practical worry?

End with which of the two members is closer to right, and a plain-English bottom line I could actually print for members about how to fill in their ballot next year. Don't bother with references, I just need the distinctions stated clearly enough that I can defend the one-pager when that data guy reads it.
