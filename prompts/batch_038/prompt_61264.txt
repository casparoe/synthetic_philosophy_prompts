I'm a hospital social worker. For eleven years I have written capacity evaluations and drafted guardianship petitions, and eighteen months ago I became my sister's plenary guardian after an anoxic brain injury at thirty-four. My working vocabulary is a very particular one: the presumption of capacity, capacity as decision-specific and time-specific rather than global, substituted judgment versus best interests, the least restrictive alternative, limited versus plenary guardianship, undue influence, the dignity-of-risk idea, and the disability-rights push away from substituted decision-making and toward supported decision-making built on a person's own will and preferences. A reading group I joined is working through the first book of the Politics, and I have not been able to shake it — not because I find the institution defensible, but because the argumentative moves sound disturbingly like ones I make for a living.

I would like a translation exercise rather than an evaluation. Restate Aristotle's case for natural slavery — the argument, not the historical practice — in the vocabulary above, as faithfully as you can manage, working through these five components in order:

1. The capacity claim. In Jowett's rendering of Book I, the natural slave is "he who participates in rational principle enough to apprehend, but not to have, such a principle." What is the nearest counterpart in my framework, and does the apprehend-but-not-possess distinction have an expression there at all?

2. The interest claim: that rule over such a person is expedient and right for the ruled, and that master and slave have a common interest rather than opposed ones.

3. The structural analogy: soul rules body despotically, intellect rules appetite constitutionally and royally, and the same pattern of a ruling and a subject element extends to human beings, where equality or the rule of the inferior is harmful.

4. The instrumental and teleological claims: the slave as a piece of property that acts on its own, an instrument subordinate to the ends of the household, and the further claim that such a person has a genuine but subordinate and derivative excellence of his own.

5. The diagnostic claim: that this is a natural kind identifiable in principle by observation, together with Aristotle's own concession that the marks nature intends often come out wrong — some have the souls and others the bodies of free men — and his distinction between natural slavery and slavery by convention or war-capture, the latter of which he treats as contested and possibly unjust.

For each of the five, give me a residue report: the nearest counterpart in my vocabulary; a verdict of clean translation, translation only with distortion, or no counterpart whatever; and a precise statement of what the distortion or the gap consists in.

Then take the residue seriously, which is the part I actually want. For every item you mark distorted or untranslatable, tell me which direction the failure points. Either my vocabulary is missing something — for instance, that because it can represent a deficit only as decision-specific, time-limited, and remedial, it has no way of representing a standing and non-accidental condition in which another person's practical reasoning is genuinely the better instrument of one's own good, and so drives the acknowledgment of exactly that underground into informal family arrangements nobody audits — or Aristotle's claim was less determinate than it looks, and the components that vanish were carrying no weight once the teleology is removed. Say which, item by item, and say why. I have a candidate for the strongest form of the first possibility: that the question Aristotle is raising is whether authority over an adult's decisions can ever be constitutive of that adult's good rather than merely protective of it, and that my framework cannot even formulate that question, as distinct from answering it differently. Test that candidate, tell me whether it survives contact with the actual standards I work under, and propose better candidates if you think it is wrong.

Two disciplines I want you to hold to. Do not smuggle: the presumption of capacity, the individualized-assessment requirement, and the least-restrictive-alternative constraint are presuppositions of my framework, not of his, and a translation that quietly supplies them to Aristotle and then reports an encouraging fit is worthless to me — mark every place you feel the pull to do it. And do not caricature: he distinguishes natural from conventional slavery and concedes the conventional sort may be unjust, and the interest claim does real work for him, so treat him as making a claim about who benefits and not merely about who is stronger.

Since I intend to argue about this with people who read the Greek and I do not, close with an honest account of your own exposure. Tell me where your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong; what would change your mind about the items you mark untranslatable — what feature or claim, if I could show my framework does have a term for it, would force you to revise those verdicts; which residue verdict you hold least confidently and why; and the single place where your translation is most at risk of importing my framework's assumptions into Aristotle and reporting the result as a clean fit.
