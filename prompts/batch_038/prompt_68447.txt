I work in claims at a mid-size insurance company, mostly handling the wierd commercial liability files that don't fit the standard templates. Last month we had a consultant in who spent two hours arguing that our team of eleven should be cut to three, and the load-bearing line of his whole pitch was a slogan he kept repeating: "if a person can do it by following rules, a machine can do it." He insisted this was a mathematical theorem, like Pythagoras, not an opinion — he actually said "this was settled in the 1930s, it's not up for debate." Our VP nodded along the whole time, and the line is now in the slide deck as the justification for the restructure, with no argument behind it at all.

I'm not a philosopher (my degree is in statistics, ten years ago, and I've forgotten most of it), but something about this bugs me and I want to walk into the follow-up meeting with something better than "it just feels wrong to me." So: what are the different precise claims that one sentence could actually be standing for? I can already sense they aren't all equally strong — there's some version that turns on what "following rules" is supposed to mean, some version about whether this was proved or just assumed and nobody's found a counterexample yet, and some version about machines in principle versus machines that actually return an answer before the heat death of the universe. Sort them out for me as a list: for each reading, say exactly what it asserts, whether it's true, false, or an unproven-but-reasonable assumption, and whether it would actually get the consultant his conclusion about our team. And flag wherever the same words are quietly doing two different jobs — like whether "a machine can do it" means a machine can do it on a budget and a deadline, or a machine could do it if you gave it a billion years and unlimited memory. That distinction is basically the whole ballgame for us.

If it's relevant: the work we do involves reading messy documents, deciding whether some clause was actually triggered, and every so often concluding that a policy doesn't say what it very much looks like it says. Nobody has ever written our rules down, not once. The consultant treated that as an implementation detail.
