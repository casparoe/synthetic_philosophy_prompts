I'm the clinical informatics lead at a mid-sized NHS trust, and next Thursday I've got forty-five minutes in front of our Digital Governance Committee. It's twelve people: three consultants, two ward sisters, the trust solicitor, the finance director, IT ops, a patient representative, a lay member who is (bless her) extremely forthright, and two people from the integrated care board who mostly look at spreadsheets. Nobody writes code. Nobody has heard the phrase "human in the loop" used seriously. The screen in that room has been broken since March, so there will be no slides — a whiteboard and a flip chart, that's it. What I have to teach them isn't any particular product. It's the difference between an AI system that's a tool and one that's an agent, and why that difference changes what the committee is actually being asked to approve. Act as someone who is genuinely expert in both AI system design and in teaching technical concepts to non-specialist decision-makers, and think carefully before you write — I want a plan for how I should explain it, not an explanation of it to me.

The concrete hook is two proposals sitting on their agenda. Proposal A reads ward notes and drafts a discharge summary, which a doctor then edits and signs. Proposal B does that too, but it also books the community transport, sends the letter to the GP practice, and, if no care package is available by Friday, cancels the bed and reschedules the follow-up clinic on its own — so the consequential actions happen across a delay, out of anyone's sight, and some of them can't be walked back. My worry is that the committee thinks B is just A with an extra permissions tick-box, and that once they've satisfied themselves the drafting is accurate they've done their job. So first: tell me honestly whether the right thing to teach is that this is a categorical break or a spectrum with a governance-relevant threshold somewhere on it — I lean towards spectrum myself but I don't know where to put the threshold or what to call the thing that changes as you cross it, and I don't want to teach them a false dichotomy just because it's tidier on a whiteboard.

Then give me the teaching plan. I want: the order the components have to go in and why that order (what depends on what — I suspect I have to get "who is holding the goal" in place before "who presses the button" makes any sense, and I suspect I should enter through something they already have, which is accountability); one example that carries the whole thing, and before you settle on it, brainstorm three or four candidates in a few lines and say which you're rejecting and why — my own guesses are the satnav-versus-the-car and the junior doctor who does what you say versus the one who does what they think you'd want, but I suspect both smuggle in a mind the system doesn't have, and I need to know what the winning example must contain (a delay between deciding and reviewing, an irreversible step, no anthropomorphism) and must lack. I also want the misconception map, with the exact point in the sequence where each one gets headed off rather than contradicted later: "if a human signs it off it's a tool" (they will sign off two hundred recommendations a day and check none of them), "agents are riskier because they're cleverer" (conflating capability with autonomy), "we can always switch it off" (true, and beside the point, because the transport is already cancelled), "a tool that gives bad advice is just as dangerous" — which is partly right and I'd rather absorb it than swat it, so tell me how. The lay member will ask, within about ten minutes, "so who do I complain to if it goes wrong", and I think that question is a gift if I sequence for it. The finance director will say the tool is cheaper and we can always add the agent bits later; tell me what to concede and what to refuse to concede. Finish with one question I can put to the room at the end that would show me whether they've grasped the distinction rather than just agreed with me, and with an explicit list of what I should refuse to teach at this level and say so out loud — no consciousness, no timelines, no how-the-model-works, and no word like "agentic", because someone will write it down and put it in a paper where it explains nothing. Write the whole thing in British English, please, and keep it to something I could actually read the night before and run from.
