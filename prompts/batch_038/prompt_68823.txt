You are a decision theorist who has worked extensively on Newcomb-style problems and on translating them into institutional policy. I am head of information security for a regional hospital group — three acute sites, roughly 9,000 staff. We have had two extortion incidents in four years and expect about one a year henceforth. Each time, the board turns to me for a recommendation within hours, and that recommendation has to remain defensible afterwards whether or not it worked. The board chair and the chief medical officer sign off jointly; the procedure itself must be runnable in under an hour by whoever is on duty, who may not be a specialist.

Please write me a standing decision procedure for three linked questions: whether to pay, what we should commit to publicly in advance, and at what point the matter stops being a prediction problem and becomes a values problem the board must own. I want it grounded explicitly in the two structures that seem to me to be lurking in this situation. First, the counterfactual mugging: a predictor has already acted on a model of how you would have responded in a branch that did not obtain, so the payoff to behaving as though that branch mattered lands elsewhere and later. Second, XOR blackmail: the extortionist's claim is that they selected you precisely because of what they predicted you would do, so that complying appears to be evidence against the threat having been set in motion at all.

Begin with a short brainstorm identifying which features of a real incident genuinely have that structure and which merely resemble it — the puzzles stipulate a near-perfect predictor, and no attacker is one; say what survives that weakening and what collapses. Then give the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a refusal, where it hands the decision upward, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. It must address a pre-announced no-pay policy that a sophisticated actor will simply predict we abandon in extremis, and it must address the fact that our response today feeds the targeting model applied to other hospitals next quarter, whose patients have no one at our table.

Finally, run it on three cases: (i) encryption on a Sunday evening, with elective theatres already cancelled and the attacker citing our own published statement that we engage with negotiators; (ii) a demand where the stolen records have already been published and payment buys only an assurance of deletion and non-republication; (iii) an automated campaign hitting dozens of hospitals in a week with small demands, where paying restores full service within a day. At least one case must yield a recommendation I would rather not take to the board, and you should say why the procedure is nonetheless right to give it. Close with what the procedure cannot settle at all. Write in British English; do not include citations, references, or reading lists.
