So, last year history student here, and this term I'm in a seminar on explanation and comparison in history. Our prof has this thing where everyone has to submit one exam question plus a model answer and a marking scheme into a shared revision bank, and he picks a few of them for the mock paper we all sit before finals. I've drawn the Great Divergence week and I really don't want to write another "discuss the causes of Britain's industrial takeoff" question, because half the seminar would just dump everything they remember about coal, wages, empire and enclosures and walk away with a good mark.

What I want the question to actually turn on is whether "why Britain and not China?" is even a well-posed question. The bit our seminar leader kept hammering is that a contrastive question isn't one question, it's a whole family indexed by the contrast you pick, and the set of legitimate difference-makers changes when you change the foil. Coal sitting near navigable water might be a difference-maker if the foil is the Yangzi Delta and not if the foil is China as a whole, since China had plenty of coal, just nowhere near its commercial core. Same worry for the New World land argument, and for the high-wages-plus-cheap-energy story, which arguably isn't a Britain-versus-China answer at all but a northwest-Europe one. And then there's the awkward fact that this happened once, so there's no reference class telling you which conditions were necessary and which were just present.

Can you write:

1. The question stem. Our mock is 3 hours, answer 2 of 4, 25 marks each, so it should be about 45 minutes of work. Put three candidate difference-makers in the stem itself (coal geography, relative factor prices, the ecological relief from New World land) and make students do three things: say for each one which change of foil would break it and which wouldn't; say what the explanandum has to be pinned to before any of it is answerable (industrialisation measured as what, starting when, against what baseline); and then rewrite the question in a version they'd defend as well posed, saying what evidence would settle it.

2. A model answer, roughly 700 words.

3. A marking scheme out of 25, split into must-hit points with the marks each is worth and one line on how people usually fumble it, plus a seperate short paragraph saying what moves divide a first from a 2:1.

Also tell me explicitly which answers should be marked down — I'm thinking reciting causes, declaring the question Eurocentric and stopping there, saying its overdetermined so nothing counts as an answer, treating China as a single agent, and answering why industrialisation happened at all instead of why here rather than there. Students shouldn't need to know the historiography by name to do well, so no reading list or citations anywhere in the deliverable.
