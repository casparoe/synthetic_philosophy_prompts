I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for close to twenty years, and I have finally written down where I actually land on cooperation, reciprocity, and the prisoner's dilemma — partly because a local meetup asked me to present my views, and partly because I suspect the package is less coherent from the outside than it feels from the inside. What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Tell me which of these genuinely conflict, which only look like they conflict, and for each real tension what the cheapest revision is and what that revision costs me elsewhere. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally bad, and close by naming the single change that would clean up the most.

Here is the package.

1. I am a functional or acausal decision theorist, more or less. I one-box in Newcomb's problem, and I think that in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a sufficiently similar agent — a copy of me, a system that predicts me by modeling my decision procedure, another careful reasoner running the same algorithm — cooperation is rational. The similarity that matters is similarity of decision procedure, not causal influence. I do not need my cooperating to cause theirs for it to be decision-relevant.

2. At the same time I reject evidential decision theory as a general account. I think the Smoking Lesion is decisive against it: a correlation that is grounded neither in a common cause nor in the shared structure of the agents' decision procedures is not a reason to act. I am not in the business of managing the news.

3. On the empirical side I take Axelrod's tournaments seriously. The strategies that did well were nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear, and the practical upshot — don't be envious, don't defect first, reciprocate both cooperation and defection, don't be too clever — has genuinely shaped how I behave.

4. I know tournament performance and evolutionary stability are different questions, and I accept that tit-for-tat is not strictly stable in a population where unconditional cooperators can drift in and then admit defectors. I do not think this undercuts 3.

5. The general shape of my view is that cooperation in the world is fundamentally the shadow of the future. Indefinite repetition plus a high enough continuation probability makes cooperation sustainable; where the horizon is short or monitoring is poor, defection is not merely tempting but correct. I accept the backward-induction worry about finitely repeated games with a known endpoint, and I accept the reputation-based resolution: a small probability that the other player is a committed cooperator can sustain cooperation through most of a finitely repeated game.

6. I am a selection-level individualist. I am skeptical of group selection, and I explain apparently selfless human behavior by kin selection, direct and indirect reciprocity, reputation, and costly signaling. I do not believe there are pure other-regarding motives underneath any of it; the machinery is strategic even where the phenomenology is not.

7. I also think punishment is what holds large-scale human cooperation together — the public goods experiments in which people pay real costs to punish defectors convince me — and I think punishment works chiefly because it is visible, which makes it a reputation mechanism. I will admit I do not have a satisfying answer to the second-order problem: if punishing is costly, why doesn't everyone free-ride on other people's punishing?

8. My moral intuitions are, on my view, evolved heuristics calibrated for small-group ancestral conditions; they misfire in novel environments, and when a gut feeling about fairness conflicts with an explicit expected-value calculation in a modern context, the calculation wins. This is why I give the way I give and why I am willing to accept trades that feel slightly wrong.

9. In ordinary life I keep my word, honor deals, and do not cheat when I could get away with it. Partly this is because repeat play and reputation make it payoff-maximizing. Partly it is because I want to be the kind of person whose commitments are reliable: I think the disposition has to be real rather than feigned, since a transparent reasoner's dispositions leak and other people price them in.

10. But in genuinely anonymous, one-shot, no-reputation interactions, I take the money. A one-time transaction with a counterparty I will never meet or hear about again; a billing error in my favor that no one will ever trace; a vendor I will never buy from again. No shadow of the future, no audience, no effect on anyone's future behavior toward me. I think the honest calculation says defect, and I think most people who say otherwise are flinching.

I expect the pressure is somewhere among 1, 2, 6, 8, 9, and 10, but I would rather not tell you where, because I do not actually know. So: which pairs are in real conflict, and how badly? Which pairs look bad but dissolve, and what exactly dissolves them — I want the distinction drawn, not a flat list. For each real tension, what is the least costly revision and what does it cost me downstream? And which single change fixes the most?

One further demand. If you think I can hold 1 and 10 together, I want the criterion of similar enough that licenses one-shot cooperation against a modeled copy while refusing it to an anonymous stranger, and I want to know whether that criterion is something I could actually apply to a real counterparty or merely a way of restating a preference. If it cannot be supplied, say which of the two has to go, and whether losing it forces me to revise 2 or 6 along with it.

Two house rules. Assume I know the material: do not explain the folk theorem, tit-for-tat, or one-boxing back to me, and do not spend words on the standard textbook framing. And no reading list or citations — I am not going to chase them, and I would rather you reason from the structure of my commitments than appeal to authority.
