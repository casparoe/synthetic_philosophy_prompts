---

**The Audition Log**

Procreative beneficence, as Savulescu put it forward, says something like this: if you're going to have a child, and more than one possible child is available to you, you have a moral reason to pick the one expected to have the best life. A reason, not an order. And crucially it's meant to be impersonal — nobody is wronged whichever way you go, because the child you didn't pick was never anyone. Habermas's objection runs the other way: a person who was specified in advance according to somebody else's idea of a good life can't stand in the same relation to their own biography as the rest of us do. Something about authorship gets broken.

Both of these positions are about the future child. I think that's the mistake, or at least that it's where the interesting stuff gets missed.

Here's what the counsellor's log actually records. Selection produces a category of person who does not exist, and it produces that category on purpose, at scale, with paperwork. Nobody mourns them because there is nobody to mourn. This is usually presented as the great advantage of the whole enterprise — no harm, no complainant, no court. But strip the complainant out of a moral system and see what's left holding it up. Guilt is a feedback mechanism. So is resentment. So is the awkward phone call. An ethic with no possible victim has no internal brake at all, and procreative beneficence is proud of this, it's the load-bearing feature. My claim: PB is right that there's no victim, and that is exactly why it should worry us rather than reassure us. The absence of a wronged party isn't a clean bill of health, it's the removal of the only check the ethic ever had.

And notice where the cost lands instead. It lands on the one who got picked. My counsellor's daughter was ranked third. Nothing was done to her that she can call a wrong — she is alive, she is fine, arguably she is a winner. But she learns that her existence was conditional on a comparison, and that the comparison went against her first. She was cast, not conceived. Whatever Habermas is groping at about authorship, I think the felt version is narrower and worse than he says: not "my life was designed" but "my life was a placement."

The other thing the log exposes is the metric. "Best life" has to be one scale, because you can only rank on one scale, and the moment a clinic builds the ranking it turns out that what's on it is a culture's taste written in numbers — height, certain kinds of cognitive score, absence of deafness, absence of anything a school would find inconvenient. Traits that are genuinely incommensurable get forced onto a line, and the line does the moral work that the argument couldn't. PB doesn't optimise. It launders preference and calls the result a ranking.

So the log is the whole philosophy in one object: a list of people who lost an audition they never attended, kept by someone who has to live with the ones who got the part.
