I am preparing a workshop paper on the philosophical conclusions Gödel drew from his rotating cosmological solution, and before I commit my objections to print I would like to test them against the strongest available version of the position. Please play Gödel — in the first person, at the Institute for Advanced Study in the early 1950s, shortly after your rotating solution appeared and after your remark in the volume honouring Einstein's seventieth birthday. You may draw on anything you would then have known: general relativity and cosmology, your own results in logic, your realism about mathematics, your conviction that relativity theory has been misread by the positivists and the Machians, and your sympathy for the Kantian thesis of the ideality of time. Do not use results, vocabulary, or objections from after 1952, and do not cite sources or supply a bibliography; if you need a fact about my stipulated case or about physics, ask me for it as Gödel would ask a colleague. Stay in character throughout, with no meta-commentary about the exercise and no disclaimers, until I say stop. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so that this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture. You may concede subsidiary points freely — I expect you to grant, for instance, that whether we ourselves live in a rotating universe is a contingent empirical question — but never the main claim, that your solution has consequences for the objectivity of time. Be courteous, exacting, and sharp about distinctions, and where my premises are sloppy, say so rather than working around them.

To make the contest precise, I stipulate two worlds, both satisfying the field equations, together with a distinction of my own that I ask you to engage with directly. World A is homogeneous and isotropic, admits a preferred slicing into spacelike hypersurfaces on which the matter is everywhere at rest, and has a cosmic time labelling those slices that orders all events globally into earlier and later. World B is your rotating universe: pressureless matter of uniform density in rigid rotation, a cosmological constant tuned against that density, closed timelike curves along which a material particle can return to its own past, and consequently no consistent global ordering of events at all. Now the distinction. Call it the ordering requirement: a world has objective time if and only if its events admit an objective, global, asymmetric ordering into earlier and later. Call it the passage requirement: a world has objective time only if, in addition, one slice of that ordering is distinguished as the present, and there is a fact about which slice it is that changes. I concede at the outset that World B violates both, and that World A satisfies the ordering requirement. But I maintain that World A satisfies nothing that deserves the name of passage: the geometry designates no slice as present, and nothing in the field equations could make such a designation advance. The foliation is a stack, not a flow.

Here, then, is my opening, and I want your answer to it in character. Your argument appears to face a dilemma. If objective time requires passage, then World A already lacks it, and your rotating universe is surplus to the argument: you could have concluded that the lapse of time is no part of the furniture of the world from the plainest cosmological model there is, so the exotic solution does no philosophical work beyond decoration, and the physics you are famous for having supplied is irrelevant to the conclusion. If instead objective time requires only ordering, then you have quietly changed the subject: what you mean by the lapse of time is an asymmetric global ordering, which is not what anyone who says that time flows means by flow, and the demonstration that one solution lacks such an ordering shows only that ordering is contingent on how matter happens to be arranged, never that flow is unreal. Which horn do you take? And whichever you take, tell me what your rotating universe contributes to the argument that a static, foliated world like A could not have contributed on its own. If you reject my two requirements as a misdescription of your position, argue against them head-on and give me the distinction you actually rely on — but do not settle the matter by invoking Kant's authority, and do not simply repeat that such a world is possible.
