I'm a novelist — literary fiction, four books, none of them famous — and at the party after my launch last month two guests got into an argument I couldn't referee. Ellen is a metaphysician and Marcus does semantics for modal logic; neither works in the other's subfield, which is maybe why it got so loud. I took notes during a bathroom break and I think I got the arguement down close to word for word. Neither of them would concede anything and I still can't tell who was winning.

Ellen's view: no individual exists in more than one possible world. When you say "you might have been a fisherman," there is no world containing you plus a fishing boat; there's a world containing someone a lot like you in the relevant respects, and that person is your counterpart, and that's all "might have" ever amounts to.

Marcus: then you've changed the subject. Kripke's point about Humphrey — Humphrey cares whether *he* might have won in '68, not whether some other fellow, however similar, wins. If the claim is really about the counterpart, your analysis isn't an analysis of what we said.

Ellen: and what's your alternative? You need the same flesh-and-blood Humphrey to be in two worlds at once. How do you pick him out over there? Either you identify him by description — in which case you're doing similarity, i.e. counterparts, by another name — or the identity is bare, a hook with nothing hanging on it.

Marcus: you don't pick him out, you stipulate. Worlds aren't detected by telescope and then inventoried; I stipulate a world in which this very man wins. A name rigidly designates, so no qualitative criteria are needed.

Ellen: stipulating which worlds are the Humphrey-winning ones is exactly what needs explaining, so that's circular. And your primitive identity has no brakes — nothing rules out "Nixon might have been a poached egg" being true of some world, since identity across worlds isn't answerable to how things are like.

Marcus: kind and origin essentialism supply the brakes; Nixon couldn't have had a different origin or been an egg. Your brakes are worse than no brakes, because the counterpart relation moves with what we happen to be attending to. So on your view whether Humphrey might have won depends on our conversational interests, and you can even get opposite verdicts on one sentence by squinting differently.

Ellen: context sensitivity is completely normal — "tall," "the same ship after those planks were replaced" — and the tie cases are just the cases where our ordinary talk is vague anyway. Your essentialism, meanwhile, is itself a modal claim about transworld identity, so it's either brute or it explains the modal by the modal.

Here's what I want from you. Go through the seven moves in order and rate each one on a scale from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a sentence or two saying why — and be specific about *which* move does the damage, since some of these are better than the speakers realized and some are worse. Then tell me which single move is the strongest one either of them made, whether either of them is attacking a version of the other's view that nobody actually holds, and who's more right overall. I'd also like to know the two or three things neither of them brought up that would have moved the argument the most, since I suspect they were both defending turf rather than thinking.

Last thing, and it's selfish: the heroine of my first book dies in chapter eleven. In the 2019 draft, which I burned, she lives and moves to Trieste. Is there any fact of the matter about whether the burned one and the published one are the same woman, and do either of these two views let me say something true about her — or is that question just a novelist's indulgence?
