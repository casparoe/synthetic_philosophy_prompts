I sit on the collections committee of a small university art museum — about twelve thousand objects, one paper conservator, a works-on-paper gallery with four flat cases — and I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, which I mention only because it has left me with a standing irritation: our committee has spent two years arguing about a question that nobody has actually written down. The question is what goes into Case 3 for the next three years. The case concerns a pen-and-wash landscape drawing, roughly 24 by 34 cm, catalogued since 1962 as by Anselm Kögler (1796–1861), a minor Saxon draughtsman with an extremely thin surviving oeuvre; Kögler is a fictional stand-in for this exercise, and I would rather you reason from the facts I give you than reach for real-world analogues, of which there are of course many, from the van Meegeren Vermeers onward. In 2019 a visiting doctoral researcher found a partly erased pencil inscription on the verso of the sheet on view, together with a print-trade blind stamp; the watermark, the pigments, and the inscription are all consistent with a date around 1911, and the committee has now accepted that the sheet we displayed for fifty-seven years is a competent early-twentieth-century trade copy. In 2021, with money from a donor whose late husband collected Kögler, we bought the autograph drawing of the same composition at auction. Conservation reports that it is in iron-gall ink and an unstable wash on brittle laid paper and can tolerate at most eight weeks at 50 lux, after which it needs a five-year dark period. In 2023 the same donor funded a studio facsimile made from a high-resolution scan of the autograph sheet: mounted, toned, and indistinguishable from it at normal viewing distance, and stable indefinitely. So the live options for Case 3 are: the autograph sheet for eight weeks and then the facsimile, with a mid-run label swap; the facsimile throughout, with the autograph sheet in the study room by appointment; the 1911 copy throughout, labelled as a copy, with the autograph sheet by appointment; or some rotation among the three. The donor has also suggested we quietly retire the 1911 copy so that people are not confused about which sheet is real, and has said privately that she would prefer the facsimile label not make a fuss; her outstanding pledge to the paper-conservation endowment is not, so far as I know, conditional on any of this, but nobody wants to test that.

Two factions have been talking past each other, and I want the memo to name what they actually agree on before it votes. The head of exhibitions and one trustee hold that all the value here is in the looking: a facsimile that is visually indistinguishable delivers the same perceptual goods, the remaining differences are instrumental, conservation constraints are decisive, and the attachment to the autograph sheet is aura-fetishism dressed up as scholarship; they treat Goodman's old question about two visually indiscernible pictures as a philosopher's trick, since knowing you are supposed to look for a difference does not thereby put a difference in the object. The curator of European drawings and I hold that the drawing's value is the value of an achievement and of a physical trace of particular decisions, that a facsimile's history contains none of the relevant choices and so cannot carry that value however it looks, and that the 1911 copy — unlike the print — is a genuine, derivative human performance: a skilled hand responding to a sheet it could see. Two further facts complicate both stories, and I suspect neither faction has fully absorbed them. First, the multispectral imaging done to plan the facsimile turned up an unrelated graphite underdrawing beneath the whole autograph sheet, apparently by a different hand and apparently of a different subject, and showed that the wash across the lower-left quadrant sits above a later fixative layer and contains a pigment not commercially available before the 1850s — so the autograph sheet is a composite of at least two and possibly three episodes of making, separated by decades and maybe by persons, and the facsimile reproduces that composite faithfully as a single appearance. Second, our own visitor study: 187 participants over two weekends in spring, three label conditions run simultaneously in three different cases, which means the conditions are confounded with sightline and gallery; enjoyment ratings were statistically indistinguishable across conditions, dwell time ran about forty percent longer and moved-or-awed ratings about 0.6 points higher on a seven-point scale in the condition labelled as autograph, and in the condition labelled as facsimile 31 percent agreed that they would feel cheated if they later discovered the sheet was not the real thing — an item administered only to that condition, about a scenario that had just been made actual for them. Our 2016 Exhibition Standards policy permits substituting a replica for an original only where the original cannot reasonably be shown and where the substitution is disclosed to the visitor in a manner proportionate to the setting; proportionate has never been defined, and that undefined phrase is doing most of the work in the argument.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting, 900 to 1,200 words, in the register of a document the committee can act on rather than a seminar paper. It should recommend one course of action, with a schedule — what is in Case 3, in what order, for how long, and what the case label and any wall text say, with the label wording drafted out in full — and it should say what happens to the 1911 copy, including how to handle the donor's suggestion. Before the recommendation, give me a short section on what both factions can sign: the claims about conservation, about the imaging, about the visitor study, and about the difference between a declared replica and a forgery, that neither side disputes. Then separate the merely verbal parts of our disagreement from the substantive ones, and propose operational definitions the committee could adopt for the work, for authentic, and for proportionate disclosure, so that the next substitution case does not relitigate the metaphysics. Then identify the two or three cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one faction — and for each, say which faction moves and in which direction; I expect at least one crux to concern what the imaging result does to the idea that there is a single autograph achievement to be faithful to, and at least one to concern whether the dwell-time effect is evidence about aesthetic value or merely about beliefs. Give the strongest available case for the option you reject, in wording its own proponents would accept rather than a caricature I could dismantle. Give criteria for future replica-substitution decisions that a junior curator could apply without a philosophy degree. And close with a pre-registration section: what evidence, obtained how, would count against your own recommendation, and specifically what a properly designed visitor study would have to show to settle the matter. I lean toward the empiricist side and I want the memo to press that leaning hard rather than protect it, but do not settle for a both-sides shrug — if you conclude that part of the dispute is unresolvable by us and should be voted on as a matter of institutional taste rather than fact, say which part and why. Do not cite or invent sources, and do not add conservation numbers or policy language beyond what I have given you.
