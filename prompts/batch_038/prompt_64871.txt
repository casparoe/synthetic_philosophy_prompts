I run a small breed rescue and I do all the behavioral assessments myself, and on Sunday I have to sit in my kitchen and tell a family whether a particular dog is safe around their six year old. My sister is finishing a philosophy PhD. She watched me do an eval last week and then dismantled my entire job over dinner. Here's her argument, cleaned up:

1. When I write "reactive" on an eval form, what I'm claiming is: this dog would lunge and snap if another dog got close.
2. Buddy didn't, on Tuesday, with a strange dog four feet away. Because he was muzzled, behind a baby gate, and I had a fistful of cheese at his nose.
3. So that "would" is false of Buddy, at least as I stated it.
4. But the muzzle and the gate and the cheese didn't change Buddy. He's still reactive. That's just the packing foam point — styrofoam stops a fragile glass from breaking when it's dropped, and the glass doesn't stop being fragile. Nothing was taken out of the glass, the breaking just got blocked.
5. So whatever makes the ascription true can't be the counterfactuals about behavior. It has to be something intrinsic — Buddy's wiring, his arousal threshold, his neurology.
6. Fine. But generalize. For any behavior and any dog whatsoever, I can always cook up a setup where the behavior doesn't happen: more distance, a longer line, a full belly, a tired dog, a quieter street, a handler he likes, a sedative.
7. So there are no true unconditional "would" claims about any dog's behavior. Every one is true only relative to a choice of conditions.
8. And the conditions are mine to choose. So "Buddy is reactive" and "Buddy is the gentlest dog alive" are both compatible with everything Buddy has ever actually done.
9. Therefore reactivity isn't a property of dogs. It's a property of dog-plus-situation. There are no reactive dogs, only reactive setups.
10. Same for people, obviously — which is why she thinks character traits are a myth and I should stop writing "high arousal, needs experienced home" and start writing "needs a fence."

She's right that (4) is solid. That distinction is the whole difference between managing a dog and rehabilitating one, and every trainer I know trades on it daily. And (6) is just true practically speaking. But if (9) holds then what I do on Sunday is either a lie or a shrug, and I've been doing it for eleven years.

Which step do you reject, and what do you actually pay for rejecting it? I want the cost, not just the name of the step. Two things I'm already nervous about, address them either way:

- Rejecting (4) means the muzzle and the gate genuinely make Buddy a non-reactive dog, and then I can't distinguish managing a dog from curing one, which isnt a thing I can accept.
- Rejecting (7) seems to commit me to there being true claims about what Buddy would do that no eval I could ever run would confirm, and I have no idea how I'd defend that to someone who thinks I'm a quack with a clipboard.

Also tell me where she'd push back hardest on whatever fix you give me, because she will.
