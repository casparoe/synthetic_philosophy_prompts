I have a philosophy background and am in the second year of a law degree, and I am drafting a seminar paper around a tension I keep running into but cannot yet name. A classmate of mine, who came to law school from litigation practice and considers himself a realist, is confident I am inventing the problem, which is partly why I want it stated carefully rather than waved at. Here is the practice I want analyzed. In common-law systems courts treat earlier decisions of their own court and of courts above them as binding, and they do so even where the earlier decision is widely thought mistaken; the ordinary route of escape is not overruling but distinguishing — locating the present facts outside the earlier holding — with the result that a line of distinctions can narrow a precedent almost past recognition while leaving it formally intact and still cited. When courts do overrule, the traditional self-description was that the overruled decision was never really the law at all but an error now exposed and corrected, which carries the implication that the corrected rule governs transactions closed years earlier under the discredited understanding. In the twentieth century, though, American courts developed the technique of prospective overruling, announcing that a new rule would govern only conduct occurring after the decision and leaving past transactions settled under the old one, with the justification usually framed in terms of the parties' reliance on the law as it stood when they acted. Meanwhile, in at least one jurisdiction a highest court that had for generations regarded itself as absolutely bound by its own decisions released itself by means of a brief announcement that it would depart from them when it appeared right to do so.

What I want is not a defense or a critique of precedent but a reconstruction of what this cluster of practices presupposes. Start with the most basic question: does the binding force of precedent make sense only if judges make law, so that a later court is constrained by an earlier court's exercise of creative power — or only if judges find it, so that no one is ever bound by anything but the pre-existing law and the earlier decision was simply a misreport of it? Both descriptions are alive in the same body of doctrine, sometimes in the same opinion, and I want to know whether one of them is doing all the work or whether there is a coherent position that occupies the space between them. Then separate the components. Does treating a decision as binding presuppose the same thing as preferring distinction to overruling, and does prospective overruling presuppose the same thing as either? I suspect that a court which overrules prospectively has quietly conceded that it legislates, since only a lawmaker can choose the effective date of a rule, and that this concession is in tension with the declaratory self-description that the rest of the machinery still runs on — but I would rather you test that suspicion than simply elaborate it.

I would also like the following addressed explicitly. First, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with: must binding precedent commit one to a positivist account on which judicial decisions are themselves sources of law, or could a view on which there is always a single right answer, recoverable by constructing the best account of the legal materials as a whole, license exactly the same behaviour? Could a prediction-oriented realist, for whom law is just what courts will do, make sense of a court feeling bound? Second, the point my paper is really about: does this practice presuppose that legal outcomes are contingent yet locked in — that a particular decision reached by particular people at a particular moment permanently narrows what later courts may say — or does its official rhetoric presuppose the opposite, that the answer was there all along and history decided nothing? And if the conjecture were correct that common law tends to drift toward workable or efficient rules regardless of its starting points, so that most early accidents are eventually absorbed or corrected, would the practice still presuppose the same thing, and what would it look like if the people inside it actually believed the conjecture? Third, whether the two appeals to reliance can be reconciled: refusing to overrule a bad decision because people have relied on it, and refusing to let an overruling disturb people who relied on the bad decision, invoke the same value while pulling in opposite temporal directions — tell me what a single principle would have to say to justify both, or show that they cannot both be justified. Finally, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories of law would license it equally well, and tell me what would concretely have to change — in the reasoning opinions give, in the vocabulary available to judges, in who wins the cases — if the presupposed view were replaced by its main rival. You do not need to cite cases or supply any bibliography; describe the doctrinal moves generically, and where a hypothetical would sharpen the point, invent one.
