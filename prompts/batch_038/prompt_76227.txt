My friend and I argued until two in the morning over three cases: a visitor who dines in a city where waitstaff live on their tips and leaves nothing; a pedestrian who crosses a four-lane road at three in the morning with no traffic and no one in sight; and a resident who shovels out a parking space, claims it with a folding chair, and returns for it twelve hours later. I said all three are moral matters. She said only the chair case is, because there one person's expectations have been deliberately invited and then ignored, whereas the other two are at worst bad manners — and that I was inflating etiquette into obligation. We were disagreeing about criteria, not cases, so I want the criteria laid out.

Write a decision tree sorting a reader into a named position on two questions: whether breaching an entrenched local convention can be a moral wrong, and whether it binds someone who has merely passed through the practice. Eight to twelve questions, each a concrete vignette with two or three answers, phrased so that a reader can answer without knowing which leaf they are walking toward. Track at least these distinctions: what grounds the wrong, if anything — coordination failure, thwarted reliance, expressive disrespect, fair play for benefits accepted, or nothing at all; whether the obligation survives when nobody is watching and nobody else complies, the test the social-norms literature uses to separate moral from merely social norms; and whether it reaches a non-participant who holds none of the relevant expectations and has contributed nothing to the scheme.

Name each leaf, or mark it unnamed, and give one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Note every answer-combination your tree cannot reach, and say whether nobody occupies it because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; flag any pair of leaves your questions fail to separate. Close by diagnosing which leaf each of us stands on and which single node our argument really turned on.
