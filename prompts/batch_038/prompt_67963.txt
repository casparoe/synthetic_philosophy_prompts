Write a joke set at a Copenhagen lecture hall in the 1840s, in which Kierkegaard takes the stage to deliver a talk whose announced theme is that the crowd is untruth and the single individual is the category of truth. The humor must be generated by the machinery of his own critique of the press rather than by his reputation as a melancholy fiancé-dumping Dane: the fact that he is addressing an audience at all, the anonymous journalist who will review him tomorrow under no name and therefore bear no responsibility, the abstraction he calls the public — a phantom no one has ever met and yet whose verdict everyone fears — and the leveling of genuine distinctions by a readership that has opinions on everything and stands nowhere. I want the punchline to be unavailable to someone who has only heard of Kierkegaard in passing; ideally the joke should quietly implicate the audience, and the teller, in the very thing being mocked. Please answer in exactly this order and under these headings: (1) The Joke — the piece itself, in whatever shape it works best (anecdote, dialogue, or setup and punchline), and no longer than about 200 words; (2) Why It Lands — roughly 120 to 150 words identifying which specific concepts do the work, where each came from in his writings on the press and the age of reflection, and what you had to set up earlier in the piece for the payoff to be possible.
