write a dialogue between two philosophers arguing about the necessary a posteriori. One is a straight Kripkean: an identity between rigid designators is necessary if true at all, we discover it empirically, and that's a real metaphysical result, not a trick. The other is a two-dimensionalist in the Chalmers line: the necessity is just the secondary intension keeping the books, the substantive content lives in the primary intension, and that one's contingent.

here's the case they have to fight over — please don't let either of them fall back on Hesperus/Phosphorus or water:

in 1872 a geologist working the quarry outside the village of Orsbeck coins the name "orsbeckite" for the pale green mineral she keeps pulling out of it, and it's treated as a single species for a hundred years. Then somebody finally does the crystallography properly and it turns out the quarry was yielding two different minerals with distinct structures all along — one of them abundant on site, the other much rarer and also found at three locations nobody in 1872 had heard of. So "orsbeckite is the abundant one" looks like an a posteriori identity, except it isn't obvious the name was ever latched onto one thing in the first place.

have each of them say what was actually discovered and what the modal status of the eventual identity claim is. the Kripkean owes a story about whether this is reference failure, or ambiguity, or the community quietly fixing reference after the fact — and whether the necessity survives in the last option. the two-dimensionalist owes a reason her primary intension picks out the abundant mineral rather than the rare one, or an admission that it doesn't and what that costs her. both should be sharp, they should actually respond to each other's moves rather than trade speeches, and nobody gets a clean win. around 900 words.
