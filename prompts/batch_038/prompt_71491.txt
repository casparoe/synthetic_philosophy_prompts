I don't work in AI research, but I've been reading the mechanistic interpretability material on my own, and one framing keeps coming up that I can't decide whether to trust. The claim is that a mechanism inside a trained network is like a function in a software codebase, and that catching something anomalous — a deceptive plan, a reward hack, a hidden objective — is then like running an instrumented build: you watch the call graph, and if some routine nobody wrote shows up, or a familiar one gets called with inputs it should never receive, you have your flag. What people draw from this is that the detection problem becomes cheap: you don't have to enumerate failure modes in advance, because anything out of line will stand apart from the trace of a normal run the way a typo stands apart on a page.

Could you set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the source code, the function boundaries, the call graph, the regression tests, the version history — and then say where the mapping fails? I'm particularly stuck on what makes two mechanisms the same mechanism, because the analogy assumes functions have crisp identities, whereas in a network one computation seems able to smear across several attention heads, to be quietly re-implemented in different weights after fine-tuning, or to be duplicated by two internal pathways that share no structure at all. Please separate the disanalogies that actually threaten the conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic, say what the framing smuggles in about there being a fixed, inspectable specification behind the behavior, and tell me whether the idea that you needn't know what you're looking for survives the audit — or whether it was the codebase picture carrying it all along. If there's a better analogy for the same job, I'd like to hear it too.
