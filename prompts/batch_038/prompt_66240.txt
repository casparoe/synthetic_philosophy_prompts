I am co-teaching a two-week module on strategic ambiguity to a mixed group of philosophy and international relations graduate students, and its centerpiece is the meeting in Baghdad on July 25, 1990, between the American ambassador April Glaspie and Saddam Hussein, eight days before Iraq invaded Kuwait. The history is familiar to the class, so I will state only the features that matter for the analysis: Glaspie had asked for the meeting in order to find out why Iraqi forces were massed on the Kuwaiti border; Saddam answered that he was giving negotiations one last chance and that if they failed Iraq would not accept death, and then asked her directly what the American position would be; her reply was, in substance, that the United States had no opinion on Arab–Arab conflicts including the Iraq–Kuwait border disagreement, and that the Secretary of State had instructed her to stress that the Kuwait issue was not associated with America. Saddam is reported to have smiled at that point. The Iraqis released their own transcript in September 1990, it does not match the American memorandum of conversation line for line, and the two records have been argued over ever since — including over whether the same meeting also contained explicit warnings that the use of force would not be tolerated. Half my class describes the episode as a signaling failure; the other half wants to describe it as a piece of Gricean communication that worked exactly as designed and simply had catastrophic uptake. I want your analysis to adjudicate between those two descriptions, and I want it to be a genuine test of the Gricean framework rather than a competent recitation of it.

Begin with a disciplined mapping. Separate what Glaspie said, in Grice's technical sense, from what she conversationally implicated, and tell me which maxim is doing the work — since the sentence about having no opinion on a category of disputes becomes a claim about American non-intervention mainly by virtue of its position as a complete answer to a direct question about intentions, and I want to know whether the resulting implicature is generalized or particularized, and whether it is detachable from that question-and-answer placement. Then tell me how to classify the move within Grice's taxonomy: flouting requires the hearer to notice the flouting and reason past it, violating requires him not to notice, opting out is what she was professionally unable to do, and it is not obvious that any of these fits a speaker who is being fully explicit while arranging that her explicitness will not be heard as explicitness. If the taxonomy has no slot for this, say so and describe the slot it would need. Next, take up the standard Gricean loophole — that a speaker can be scrupulously truthful at the level of what is said while generating a false implicature, and thereby mislead without lying — and say whether that loophole describes what happened here or whether it is doing unearned work, given that Glaspie's later insistence is that she also warned him; if the warning was present, the implicature analysis changes, and I want both readings worked through rather than one assumed. I also want two structural problems pressed hard. First, deniability: Gricean speaker meaning requires the speaker to intend that the hearer recognize her intention, whereas plausible deniability requires the speaker to intend that the hearer be unable to attribute that intention to her on the public record — so the episode seems to demand a split between private recognition and public attributability that the account of meaning-nn does not obviously contain. Second, the institutional speaker: the Cooperative Principle is built out of the beliefs and intentions of an individual who expresses them, but here the speaker disclaims an opinion on behalf of a diffuse principal whose opinion is formed by many agents none of whom may hold it, so it is unclear whose belief the maxim of Quality is even tracking. Finally, consider the audience problem — Saddam was the addressee, but Kuwait, the Gulf states, Congress, and the press were co-audiences of an utterance designed to be reported, and a theory keyed to the intention that this hearer recognize that intention may not be built for speech aimed at several audiences with divergent inference procedures.

Then give me the verdict, running in the other direction. Which features of the episode does Grice's framework explain better than the signaling-and-deterrence vocabulary the IR students bring, which does it misdescribe, and which is it simply silent about — and where the framework is silent, stop rather than conscripting it. In particular, tell me whether the Cooperative Principle can function as a normative warrant in an adversarial exchange where the parties' purposes conflict, or whether in such contexts it degenerates into a descriptive default that hearers extend to their own detriment, in which case the theory predicts implicature success but has no account of licensed versus unlicensed inference and therefore cannot tell us whether Saddam's inference was a reasonable misreading or a warranted one. Address whether the cancellation test survives contact with this case: the United States responded militarily within days, which looks like the implicature being cancelled by subsequent conduct rather than by a clause inside the same utterance, and if cancellation-by-action is available to any speaker who behaves otherwise afterward, the test may cease to be a mark of conversational implicature at all. Close with a judgment about the status of the episode for the theory — confirmation, anomaly, or a case the framework never anticipated because it was assembled out of ordinary conversation among people with broadly aligned interests — and state plainly how much of your conclusion depends on which of the two conflicting records is true, and what would have to be settled to remove that dependence. I want argument throughout, not a survey of the secondary literature and not a reading list.
