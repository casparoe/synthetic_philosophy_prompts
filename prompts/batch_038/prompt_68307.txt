I have forty minutes plus a twenty-minute question period at an interdisciplinary philosophy of education colloquium in three weeks, and I would like a realistic Q&A prep sheet rather than encouragement. The audience will be roughly twenty people: mostly analytic philosophers of education, two committed Deweyans who run the department's pragmatist reading group, one historian of process philosophy who has read Whitehead's cosmology far more closely than I have and who I expect will be sceptical of anyone importing it into pedagogy, and a curriculum-assessment researcher from the school of education whose work is entirely about measuring learning outcomes and who has said publicly that most talk of stages in education is unfalsifiable nostalgia. The talk defends a reading of Whitehead's educational essays that I have been developing for two years, and its central claim is deliberately provocative: the three-phase rhythm he describes — romance, precision, generalization — is not a developmental-psychological stage theory about children of different ages, which is how it is usually read and then discarded once Piagetian stage research fell out of favour, but a claim about the unit of analysis. On what I call the scale-invariant reading, the rhythm applies at every level of educational organisation simultaneously: a single forty-minute lesson, a teaching unit, a school year, a degree programme, and even the institutional life of a discipline. Nested cycles must be completed in order, and the pathology Whitehead complains about — knowledge acquired but never used, never tested against fresh experience, which he characterises as inert — arises precisely when a cycle at one scale is interrupted by a demand from the scale above it, typically an examination that rewards only the precision phase.

Two further claims are where I expect trouble, so please aim your objections at them rather than at the framing. First, I argue that this reading lets Whitehead diagnose the examination problem without needing an empirical psychology at all: testing precision in isolation is not bad measurement but a category error, because precision is only legitimate as a phase when it is answerable to a preceding romance and a succeeding generalization. Second, I argue that Whitehead is not a warmed-over Dewey, and that the difference is doing real work rather than decoration: for Dewey the rhythm of inquiry is a social-pragmatist account of how problems get resolved in communities, whereas for Whitehead it is a metaphysical claim about how actual occasions of experience achieve their own satisfaction, and only the metaphysical version, I contend, can support the normative judgement that skipping a phase is not merely inefficient but a distortion of the learner. I know this is the soft underbelly of the talk: the moment I start gesturing at his cosmology — prehension, concrescence, subjective aim — I risk sounding like I am doing mysticism with technical vocabulary, and the process historian in the room will know whether I am.

Please give me the eight to ten objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument if it landed, and phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup would use — the Deweyans will not object the way the assessment researcher objects, and neither will object the way the historian of process philosophy will. For each, give me a reply I can deliver aloud in about two minutes, say explicitly what I should concede in the first sentence to avoid looking defensive, and mark where the reply is weakest so I know what not to lean on. I specifically want the textual objection: does Whitehead anywhere support the nesting claim, or is it my construction, and if it is my construction, how do I say so without collapsing into admitting the whole reading is projection? I also want the unfalsifiability objection, since if any sequence of instruction can be redescribed after the fact as romance, precision, and generalization, my account predicts nothing. And include the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it concerns Whitehead's own class and institutional commitments and whether his defence of a certain kind of cultivated elite is compatible with the emancipatory use I am making of him. Two housekeeping constraints: do not send me to secondary literature or hand me a reading list, since I will be preparing offline and cannot verify it, and if you think any of my three claims simply misreads Whitehead, tell me bluntly and tell me what the more defensible version of the claim would be, because I would rather restructure the talk than defend a position that cannot survive the room.
