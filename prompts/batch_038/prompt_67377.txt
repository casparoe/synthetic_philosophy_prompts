I'm teaching a unit on perception and I've paired Bierce's An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge with the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist response to it. Quick recap of what I'm relying on: Peyton Farquhar is dropped off a railroad bridge with a noose on, and in the instant before his neck breaks he lives a whole escape — the rope parts, he dives away from rifle and cannon fire, drifts downstream, walks a day and a night through forest with his senses uncannably sharpened, hallucinating unfamiliar constellations and voices in a language he doesn't know, and finally gets to his own gate and his wife, at which point there's a blow on the back of his neck, a white flash, and the story tells us his body is swinging under the planks of the bridge.

The obvious reading is that this is a gift to the highest common factor view: from the inside, Farquhar's escape was exactly what a real escape would have been like, so whatever perception and hallucination share is the thing that matters. But here's what I cant make work, and it's the thing I want your piece to work on. Bierce gives the hallucination vastly more phenomenal richness than he gives Farquhar's actual perceptions — back on the plank, the gold-touched water, some mist under the banks and a piece of drifting wood is about all he really sees. Disjunctivists tend to treat a mere seeming as derivative and content-thin, borrowing its character from the veridical perceptions it mimics. This story does the reverse: the fake experience is the lush one and the real one is spare. Does that count against disjunctivism, or can a disjunctivist say the lushness is itself a symptom — a tell that the state isn't answerable to the world? And then there's motivated non-noticing. The escape is full of things Farquhar never questions: shrugging off cannon shot, covering thirty miles on a half-strangled body, stars he's never seen. Is that a resource — hallucination is distinguishable in principle, we just don't check — or does invoking it concede the whole game, since the story's power depends on him not checking?

One more thing I keep circling. Who is actually the deceived subject? The reader is fooled by a narrative, not by her own perceptual contact with anything. Does reader-level indistinguishability put any pressure on disjunctivism at all, or is it a completely different problem about testimony and point of view that people sloppily import into philosophy of perception? I lean the second way but I can't argue it cleanly and I expect a student to push on it.

Tell me which stretches of the story you'd point the class to, say where Bierce resists being used as an illustration of a theory — I'm pretty sure he resists somewhere and I'd rather you found it than smoothed it over — and end with what the story does for this debate that an argument can't. Don't bother with quotations, I have the text in front of me; I need the thinking.
