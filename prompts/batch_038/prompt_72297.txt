**Base rates for the longue durée: why the outside view misfires on empires**

§1. The standard advice, when you want to know how much longer your own polity has, is to drop the inside view — the story you tell about your institutions and your own exceptionalism — and take up the outside view: find a reference class of comparable polities, get the distribution of their lifespans, locate yourself in it. Reference class forecasting, in other words. Gott's Copernicus method is the extreme version: absent other information, a 95% interval for a thing's total duration is just its elapsed duration scaled up and down by a fixed factor. Point it at states and you get bracing numbers, and people find the numbers persuasive.

§2. My claim is that in this domain the outside view isn't a debiasing device so much as a metaphysics smuggled in under cover of arithmetic. Before any base rate exists you need two things: (i) a token-identity criterion — which events count as stages of *the same* empire, such that the empire has a duration at all; and (ii) a type — the reference class the token belongs to. For a bridge, (i) is settled by physical continuity and (ii) by engineering kind, and neither is up for grabs. For a polity, neither is settled by anything observationally available to the forecaster.

§3. The distinction I want to defend is between *indexical* ignorance and *taxonomic* ignorance. Gott-style reasoning treats indexical ignorance: I don't know where I sit along a duration whose endpoints and length are already fixed. Taxonomic ignorance is ignorance about whether there is one such duration and where its bookends fall. Copernican reasoning is licensed only where the second kind is absent. Long-lived polities are precisely the case where taxonomic ignorance is at its maximum, so the apparent precision of the method here is an artifact.

§4. Case: whether the eastern Roman polity is a later stage of Rome or a different empire that inherited some of its apparatus; whether what ruled from Aachen belongs in the same reference class as what ruled from the Palatine. These aren't merely verbal. Your base rate moves by close to an order of magnitude on the answer. But there's no fact of the matter independent of the narrative practices of the people doing the classifying — and those practices are themselves shaped by who survived long enough to write the chronology.

§5. Reflexive twist: the classification practice isn't neutral with respect to the quantity being measured. Successor regimes assert continuity with a prestigious predecessor, or repudiate it, partly in response to the stories on offer about how long such things last. So publishing a base rate can, over a long enough horizon, perturb the reference class it was drawn from. Base rates for empires are in this weak sense fixed points rather than inputs.

§6. Upshot: inside/outside is the wrong contrast here. What we actually have is a contest between two inside views, one of which has learned to speak statistically.
