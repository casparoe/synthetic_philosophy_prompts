Our health system's behavioral health ethics panel meets Thursday, and I have been assigned to write the opinion the panel will deliberate from. The membership is two inpatient psychiatrists, a nurse manager, a chaplain, a patient-rights advocate, and me.

The case: a woman in her mid-thirties with bipolar I disorder was admitted last week in a manic episode. During a stable interval two years ago — at the prompting of her treatment team, as part of discharge planning after a third admission — she executed a psychiatric advance directive. It instructs that if she presents with a specified cluster of features (three days without sleep, escalating spending, a stated conviction that her family is colluding against her), she is to receive a long-acting injectable antipsychotic, and that it may be administered over her contemporaneous objection. It further contains a clause in her own drafting: any refusal voiced while that cluster is present is to be read as an expression of the episode rather than as a change of mind.

The complication is that the formal capacity evaluation completed on admission scored her as retaining decisional capacity. She can state the risks of refusal, and she reasons coherently within her own framework; two psychiatrists on the panel think the evaluation was too generous, but as it stands it is the record. Her brother, whom she named as health care agent, objects to treating her under the directive and says she is a different person now. Counsel tells me the governing statute is unhelpful in both directions and that we should decide on ethical grounds; where your argument needs a legal premise, state it as an assumption and flag it.

I need a written opinion of roughly 1,200 words to circulate before the meeting. It must reach a recommendation — authorize treatment under the directive, decline to, or authorize under explicit conditions — together with a time frame for acting, and it should not settle into "seek further consultation" as its principal conclusion. State the question precisely enough that it is evident we are not merely asking whether she has capacity now: if a directive binds a patient whom the evaluating psychiatrist has scored as capacitous, the panel is committed to some account of why an earlier self may govern a later one, and the opinion should name that account and say what it would commit us to in other contexts. Take up the provenance problem directly — the directive was solicited by the institution during a prior admission — and say whether that makes the earlier endorsement less than hers. Present the strongest case for the losing side in the terms the advocate and the two dissenting psychiatrists would actually use, and explain why it does not carry. Then give the panel a decision rule it can apply to the next directive that arrives with a comparable no-revocation clause. Close with what evidence would change your recommendation. No citations, case names, or statutory references; I will handle those.
