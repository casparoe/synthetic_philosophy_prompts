I help run the risk function at a small proprietary betting operation — mostly sports markets and short-dated financial instruments — and I have been asked to review the firm's written staking policy before it goes to the partners. Three features of the policy are relevant.

First, every opportunity is reduced to two numbers: an estimated probability for the event and the decimal odds on offer. The stake is then set by the Kelly rule, the fraction of bankroll that maximizes expected log bankroll, and the firm actually lays a fixed half of that fraction on every opportunity, however large or small the apparent edge.

Second, the policy's stated justification is a coherence argument. It says that a bettor whose prices are not probabilities can be made to accept a combination of wagers that loses money come what may, so the firm's estimates are held to a standard of internal consistency; and it adds that anyone who maximizes anything other than expected utility is sooner or later exploitable by a sequence of trades that leaves her worse off by her own lights.

Third, the policy says two further things that seem to sit badly with that justification. It says the firm will decline a single large favorable wager that it would readily accept if the same total stake and payoff were divided into twenty small wagers settled one after another. And it says the half-Kelly haircut is not there because the partners believe the firm's utility for money is more concave than logarithmic; it is there because the firm's probability estimates are themselves uncertain, and the haircut buys protection against a bad estimate.

What I want is an account of what this practice actually commits the firm to, and whether the commitments hang together.

1. Reconstruct the theory of rational choice the staking policy presupposes. What must be true of the firm's beliefs, of its utility, and of the structure of the situations it faces for the policy to be correct rather than merely convenient? Where the practice is silent, say so, and say what it leaves open.

2. Distinguish what the policy strictly requires from what merely fits it well. In particular: is maximizing expected log bankroll a special case of expected utility maximization or a rival to it, and does the answer turn on whether the firm is reasoning about a single wager, about a long but finite run of them, or about an unbounded one? What does the practice presuppose about whether the firm ever stops?

3. Adjudicate the two apparent inconsistencies. On the lump-versus-split point, is anything actually being violated, given that the two arrangements generate different distributions over final bankroll and that the sequential version permits decisions to be revisited? On the half-Kelly point, is the haircut a claim about the curvature of the firm's utility or a claim about the epistemic status of its estimates? I suspect this distinction does most of the work, because the coherence argument the policy invokes constrains estimates rather than utilities, and if the haircut is really about uncertain estimates then the numbers the firm reports to itself are not the numbers it acts on. Tell me whether that suspicion survives scrutiny, and what would follow for the firm's exposure to a bookmaker who knows how it actually forms its estimates.

4. Say what the policy would have to become if the firm abandoned point estimates in favor of a set of probabilities it declines to sharpen into one, and whether the coherence argument it currently leans on would still be available as a justification in that setting or would have to be replaced by something else.

5. Sort the stated justification into load-bearing and decorative. Which conclusions about how much to stake genuinely follow from premises about coherence and exploitability, and which are smuggled in from elsewhere — assumptions about the repeatability of the opportunities, the absence of transaction costs and minimum stakes, the firm's inability to walk away from an offered wager, or its permission to precommit and then hold to the precommitment rather than reassess each round?

I do not want references or a survey of the literature; I will chase down sources myself if I decide I need them. What I want is the analysis, and I would rather you be blunt about the places where the policy is underdetermined — where several mutually inconsistent theories would license exactly the same stakes — than have you select one theory and argue for it.
