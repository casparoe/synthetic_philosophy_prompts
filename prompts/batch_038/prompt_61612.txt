I'm working through Book VIII of Montesquieu's The Spirit of Laws (1748), and I've got stuck on chapter 2, on the corruption of the principle of democracy. The passage that has me, in an English translation, with some intervening text elided:

"The people fall into this misfortune when those in whom they confide, desirous of concealing their own corruption, endeavour to corrupt them. To disguise their own ambition, they speak to them only of the grandeur of the state; to conceal their own avarice, they incessantly flatter theirs. [...] We must not be surprised to see their suffrages given for money. It is impossible to make great largesses to the people without great extortion: and to compass this, the state must be subverted. The greater the advantages they seem to derive from their liberty, the nearer they approach towards the critical moment of losing it. Petty tyrants arise who have all the vices of a single tyrant. The small remains of liberty soon become insupportable; a single tyrant starts up, and the people are stripped of everything, even of the profits of their corruption."

The following chapter opens: "As distant as heaven is from earth, so is the true spirit of equality from that of extreme equality. The former does not imply that everybody should command, or that no one should be commanded, but that we obey or command our equals. It endeavours not to shake off the authority of a master, but that its masters should be none but its equals."

Could you help me with four things?

First, what exactly is supposed to be corrupted here — the citizens' characters, the laws, or the "principle" of a government in the sense of this book's title? I follow that for Montesquieu a republic is set in motion by virtue as a monarchy is by honour, but I don't see how that framework gets a purchase on vote-selling, which looks like ordinary private vice rather than a change in whatever moves the regime.

Second, what is the mechanism by which flattery corrupts? He says the people are corrupted by leaders bent on concealing their own corruption, and that the instrument is talk of the state's grandeur and of the people's own avarice. How does that produce the loss of a principle rather than a run of bad decisions — and where does the heaven-and-earth contrast between true and extreme equality come in?

Third, how much work is the fiscal claim doing? That great largesses to the people require great extortion reads as an empirical proposition about revenue, yet it is deployed as a conclusion about the subversion of the state. Is that an argument or an assumption dressed as one, and does it commit him to counting all tax-funded distribution as corrupting?

Fourth, and this is what I most need: if popular appetites are manufactured by the people who exploit them, does the people's consent carry any weight for Montesquieu at all? I want to bring him to bear on a live question — whether a political machine that reliably delivers jobs, heating fuel and legal help to poor constituents in return for their votes is corrupt. On the standard definition, the use of public office for private gain, much of that looks uncorrupt and even public-spirited, and its defenders add that the exchange is voluntary and leaves clients better off than the formal state left them. Montesquieu seems to block both replies. I want to know whether he blocks them for a reason that could be stated independently of his theory of principles, or only because he has already assumed that clients want what they want through manipulation. Is there an argument in this passage, or a stipulation?
