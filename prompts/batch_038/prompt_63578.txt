I run a fortnightly interview series for our philosophy society — half live audience, half podcast, recorded in a church hall with appalling reverb, so about 55 minutes of usable conversation once you strip out the faff. Next month's guest is a philosopher whose new book tries to rescue the free will debate from what she calls the sphexishness trap. Her claim is that everyone since Hofstadter coined the term has been reading the digger wasp story as a picture of what determinism does to us, when what the wasp actually shows is something much less metaphysical: a control system that holds up beautifully inside the range of perturbations it was tuned for and resets to square one outside it. On her account rigidity isn't a failure of freedom, it's a budget constraint — generality costs something, every agent sits somewhere on a trade-off curve, and the drag-the-prey-back-then-go-and-inspect-the-nest-again loop is a cheap routine doing exactly what it was selected to do in its own niche. Freedom, for her, is not about being able to do otherwise but about whether the error-correction and the setpoints belong to the agent, whether the system tunes itself rather than being tuned from outside. She's also on record saying that large language models are the cleanest test case we've ever had for all of this, which is exactly why I want to hold her to it.

What I need is ten or eleven questions I can actually read aloud — each one short enough to say in a single breath, no two-part essays, no jargon that assumes the audience has read the book, but please don't sand off the technical content either. Under each, give me one line on why it's a pressure point, and then the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, because she has two reliable deflections ('flexibility is a spectrum, so your question has no bite' and 'it depends what you mean by freedom') and I want them progressively harder to reach for. I'd like at least one question on each of these: whether the trade-off framing leaves any real notion of a free agent or just turns free will into engineering economics, and on her view what separates a very well-tuned heating controller from a person; the old charge that this whole line begs the question, since the libertarian will say she has simply described the very capacity they insist we lack — I want her pushed on whether 'robustness margin' is an explanation or a relabelling; the empirical standing of the wasp story itself, which I've always suspected is shakier than the philosophy literature lets on, given that Dennett seems to have got the behaviour from a popular engineering book rather than from the ethology, and given that at least one ethologist (Keijzer, I think) has argued that repeated inspection of a disturbed nest is plausibly adaptive and that wasps do adapt after enough iterations — how much of her argument survives if the wasp turns out not to be sphexish after all; and the LLM question, where I want a specfic observation she'd accept as showing a model is genuinely non-sphexish rather than merely broadly tuned. Two more if you can fit them in: a case where an agent's setpoints were installed by somebody else — covert manipulation, say — in which the agent is perfectly robust and self-correcting and by her lights ought to count as free, so what work is 'the system belongs to the agent' doing and how does she spell out 'belongs' without going in a circle; and something on the emotional side, since for a lot of people the attachment to this debate isn't an argument but a dread of being a remote-controlled animal, and I suspect her reframing leaves that dread untouched. Then close with what would change her mind, ideally turned back on her: what would count as discovering that she is herself sphexish in a way her own framework can't accommodate.

Finally, please order them as a plausible running order for the 55 minutes rather than by topic, tell me which two I should cut if we're over-running, and write the whole thing in American English — the feed goes out mostly to US listeners and I don't want 'behaviour' and 'programme' reading like typos. No warm-up softballs about what drew her to philosophy, and don't invent quotations from her book; I'd much rather paraphrase her position in my own words than have her correct me on air.
