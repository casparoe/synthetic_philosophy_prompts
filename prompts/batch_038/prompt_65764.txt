**DRAFT: "Pivotal Competence: What the Jury Theorem Actually Assumes"**

**1. Setup and the received objection.** Condorcet's jury theorem, in the form I will use, says this. There is a binary question with a fact of the matter. Each of n voters independently hits on the truth with probability p, where p > 1/2. Then the probability that the majority is right increases with n and tends to 1. (The theorem goes back to Condorcet's *Essai sur l'application de l'analyse à la probabilité des décisions rendues à la pluralité des voix* of 1785; nothing below turns on the history.)

The standard objection is that independence is a fantasy. Real jurors deliberate, read the same papers, and defer to the same authorities; once their votes are positively correlated the asymptotic guarantee collapses, and enlarging the group can make it worse rather than better. This objection is the common currency of work on deliberation, informational cascades, and polarization, and it is usually taken to show that the theorem has little to say about real institutions.

I want to argue that this is too quick. The hypothesis the theorem really needs is considerably weaker than global independence, and once it is stated correctly, a great deal of what looks like epistemic corruption turns out to be strictly costless.

**2. A decomposition.** Let n be odd, n = m + 1 with m even, and suppose the first m ballots are already in the box. Under simple majority rule the last ballot can change the outcome only if the first m are split evenly. Write

- T = P(the first m ballots are tied);
- C = P(a strict majority of the first m ballots is for the truth);
- q = P(the last ballot is for the truth | the first m are tied).

Then the accuracy of the (m + 1)-voter body is exactly

  A = C + T·q.  (*)

No probabilistic hypotheses enter (*) at all. It records only the fact that off the tie-set the marginal ballot is inert. What independence buys you is a value for q: if the marginal voter reports her own signal, and that signal is independent of the first m, then q = p. That is the only place p enters.

Two things follow. First, the marginal voter's unconditional competence is irrelevant to collective accuracy; what matters is her competence restricted to those states in which she is pivotal. Call this her *pivotal competence*. Second, T → 0 as m grows — exponentially fast once p is bounded away from 1/2 — so the marginal voter's entire contribution to accuracy, namely T(q − 1/2), vanishes in the limit. The theorem's asymptotic infallibility is purchased by making every individual ballot negligible. That is the content of the familiar complaint that the theorem gives no voter a reason to vote well; but the complaint is usually advanced as a paradox when it is just (*) read off.

**3. The conformist.** Now consider a voter who is a pure conformist. She inspects the running tally and votes with it; only when the tally is tied does she fall back on her own signal, which is independent of everyone else's and correct with probability p.

Her pivotal competence is exactly p, because the tie-set is precisely the set of states in which she consults her signal. By (*) she therefore contributes exactly what a sincere voter would have contributed. Copying costs nothing at the margin.

Two worked cases. With two sincere voters, C = p² and T = 2p(1 − p), so A = p² + 2p(1 − p)·p = 3p² − 2p³ — which is also the accuracy of three sincere voters, p³ + 3p²(1 − p). With four sincere voters, C = 4p³(1 − p) + p⁴ and T = 6p²(1 − p)², so A = 4p³(1 − p) + p⁴ + 6p³(1 − p)² = 10p³ − 15p⁴ + 6p⁵ — again exactly the all-sincere figure for five voters.

**4. The generalization.** The argument iterates. Pad the body with as many conformists as you like, provided each one falls back on her own signal whenever the tally she faces is tied, and collective accuracy is exactly what it would have been had they all voted sincerely. On this picture the size of the sincere core is the only thing that matters, and the conformists are free riders who do no damage. The theorem's promise therefore survives mass conformity untouched, and the literature attacking it has been aiming at the wrong target: the enemy is not copying but whatever destroys the tie-set — whatever removes the states in which fresh information can still enter the tally.

**5. Upshot.** If that is right, institutional design should be oriented around the tie-set rather than around independence. T is largest when the sincere core is small and the question is hard, that is, when p is near 1/2, and it collapses as the body grows. So there is a tension inside the theorem's own recommendation: enlarging the jury makes the jury better and simultaneously makes each member's judgment worth less, and the limit in which the group is infallible is a limit in which no member matters. An institution that cared about members mattering — for responsibility, for the educative effects of participation, for the sense in which the verdict is *theirs* — would want small committees facing genuinely close questions, which is very nearly the opposite of what the theorem is usually invoked to recommend.

**6. What I have not touched.** I have said nothing about whether correctness is available for the questions democracies actually decide, nor about the preference-aggregation settings in which the theorem is often misapplied. My claim is conditional: to the extent that the theorem is worth appealing to at all, it is worth appealing to on weaker hypotheses than people suppose.
