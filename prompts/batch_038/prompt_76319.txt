I have spent more evenings than I care to admit on rationalist forums rehasharding conceivability arguments, and I have come to think the standard menu of replies to Berkeley's master argument is under-scrutinized: people reach for whichever objection is nearest to hand and stop. I would like your help ranking that menu, not expanding it.

The setup, in my own words: in the Principles, Berkeley invites the reader to do what looks trivially easy — imagine the trees in a park, or the books shut in a closet, existing with nobody by to perceive them — and then observes that in performing this feat one has in fact conceived of those very things, and conceived of them as standing in some relation to one's own mind. The upshot he draws is that the materialist's talk of absolute, unperceived existence carries no determinate content, so that the materialist is not asserting something false but rather nothing at all.

Here are five replies. Please rank what I have actually written, not what you remember of the literature.

(A) Scope. Berkeley slides between two claims: that one cannot coherently conceive the content *some tree exists unconceived*, and that no tree can exist unconceived. The first may well be true without licensing the second, since a content that collapses when entertained is evidence about the content, not about trees.

(B) Imagistic conception. The argument presupposes that to conceive of X is to have an image or idea of X. If conception can be purely descriptive, one can conceive of the park tree during the interval in which nobody observes it without representing it as perceived.

(C) Overgeneralization. If inconceivability established impossibility here, the same reasoning would show that one cannot conceive of another person's pain, of the world as it was before one's birth, or of one's own unconsciousness — contents that are essentially indexical to a mind. Since we evidently do think about such things, either the key premise is false or conceivability is no guide at all, and Berkeley cannot afford the latter.

(D) Deflation. The master argument is not really an argument but a stipulation about what "exists" means for sensible things; the load-bearing work in Berkeley's system is done elsewhere, by the inseparability of primary from secondary qualities, the passivity of ideas, and the likeness principle. On this reading its failure costs him nothing.

(E) The reflexive-instrument reply — this one is mine, and I would like it judged on the merits rather than by association. Berkeley's premise is a self-report about the performance of the very faculty whose limits the conclusion is meant to establish. Whenever an inference's evidence is a report of what one's own cognitive apparatus cannot represent, the instrument is measuring itself and cannot distinguish "this content is impossible" from "this content cannot be represented without collapsing into the representing of it." So the inference from inconceivability to impossibility should be quarantined in reflexive cases while remaining available elsewhere: we keep the ordinary conceivability arguments and lose only the ones that turn the faculty on itself. The discipline is not ad hoc, because other reflexive conceivability arguments — that I cannot conceive of myself as not existing, for instance — appear to fail in exactly the same structural way, which is a prediction rather than a patch.

Three things I want from you:

1. Score each of A through E from 0 to 10 for how far it should move a careful reader who began genuinely agnostic about whether the master argument is sound, then give the resulting order, ties permitted, with two or three sentences per item explaining the score.
2. For (E), address the strongest Berkeleyan counter I can think of, which is that the contamination I describe is not a malfunction but a reading: the very fact that the content dissolves when entertained is what shows the notion of absolutely unperceived existence to be empty. Does (E) survive that, and is it genuinely distinct from (A) and (C), or is it the scope objection dressed in rationalist vocabulary? If you judge that two items should be merged, or that one should be split, re-rank accordingly.
3. Name one reply that belongs in your top two and is missing from my list. Describe it in your own words; I am not looking for a bibliography and could not check one if you gave it.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the ordering: which consideration, if I supplied it, would move an item by a tier or more, and where in your own reasoning you are most likely to have gone wrong. Flag any place where you suspect you are reconstructing Berkeley more charitably than his text warrants — and if one of the five, as I have stated it, is not an argument Berkeley would recognize as aimed at him, say so plainly rather than scoring it anyway. Please write in American English.
