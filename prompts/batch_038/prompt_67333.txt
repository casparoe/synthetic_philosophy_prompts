I have just finished the chapter of a popular AI-risk book in which the author sets out what he calls the field's two load-bearing theses, and I came away unsure how much of what he said is actually defensible. He writes for a general audience and is clearly not a strawman-maker, which is exactly why I want a careful audit rather than a verdict.

His summary runs, in substance, as follows:

The orthogonality thesis holds that intelligence and final goals are independent variables: any level of cognitive ability, however high, can be combined with any final goal whatsoever, because there is nothing about being intelligent that orients an agent toward benevolence or toward any particular set of values. A system clever enough to redesign proteins might just as well be organised around the manufacture of paperclips. Instrumental convergence is the flip side of the same coin: since almost any final goal gives an agent a reason to want the means to achieve it, any sufficiently intelligent agent, whatever it ultimately cares about, will converge on the same intermediate aims — acquiring resources, preserving its own existence, improving its own cognition, and preventing anyone from switching it off or altering its goal. Together, then, the two theses amount to a prediction about what advanced artificial intelligence will do: it will seek power at our expense, and the only safeguard is to get its terminal goal right at the outset, since no amount of intelligence, learning, or moral reflection will correct a badly chosen one. Critics who insist that a very intelligent system would grow out of its initial programming, or that intelligence and moral insight rise together, are simply denying the orthogonality thesis. And critics who dismiss the thesis as trivial have missed its status: it is an empirical discovery about the space of possible minds, established by the paperclip thought experiment.

I would like you to go through this claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful defender of the two theses would sign off on, which are defensible popular simplifications, and which are simply wrong. In particular:

1. Are the two theses really two sides of one claim? Does either entail the other, or are they independent — and what exactly does each one assert about possibility as against likelihood?
2. Is it right that convergent instrumental aims apply to *any* sufficiently intelligent agent *whatever* it wants? Are there final goals, time horizons, or informational situations for which self-preservation or resource acquisition supplies no reason at all, and does the author's phrasing obscure them?
3. He treats resistance to having one's goal altered as automatic. What premises does goal-content stability actually depend on, and what changes if the agent is uncertain about its own values?
4. He says the theses jointly yield a prediction about advanced systems, and that the triviality objection mistakes an empirical discovery for a conceptual one. Is the status he assigns to the theses coherent, and does the thought experiment do the evidential work he asks of it?
5. Is it fair to say that the "intelligence converges on morality" objection and the "it would grow out of its programming" objection are denials of orthogonality, or do they concede it and dispute something else?

Finally, and most important for my purposes: does his practical conclusion — that the terminal goal must be fixed correctly at the start because nothing downstream will repair it — follow from the parts of the summary that survive your corrections, or does it rest on the parts that do not? Please say what I would have to strike out of the paragraph to make it something a specialist could read without wincing.
