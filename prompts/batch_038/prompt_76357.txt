I commission long-form pieces on physics and the philosophy of mind for a broadsheet's ideas desk, and each autumn I run a residential fellowship for six early-career science writers. The fellowship closes with a written exercise that I set and mark myself. Its purpose is narrow and deliberate: to establish whether a writer can distinguish a substantive argument from a decorative quotation attached to a famous physicist's name. This year the exercise concerns Erwin Schrödinger's late metaphysics — his insistence that consciousness is never given in experience as a plural, and the monism of mind he took up from Vedantic thought, reaching him largely by way of Schopenhauer, and set out in the lectures published as Mind and Matter and in the short late book in which he gave his view of the world, alongside the coda to What Is Life? (1944).

Fellows sit the exercise for ninety minutes, in about 900 words, with no library and no internet access. They are given the following paragraph, which is raw copy from a junior reporter and is my own invention, not Schrödinger's:

> Because Schrödinger coined the term "entanglement", he knew better than most that the physics itself refuses to treat observers as separate. Two systems that have interacted remain, strictly, a single system; and so, he argued, do the minds that observe them. That is why he concluded that there is only one mind in the universe, and why our feeling of being many is an illusion the Vedantic tradition called maya. The doctrine also settles a tiresome worry: if we are all one consciousness, solipsism cannot even get off the ground, since there is no private mind left to be alone in.

Please draft the whole exercise for me, in British English throughout, comprising:

1. **The essay question.** One question only, calibrated to ninety minutes and roughly 900 words. It must be unanswerable by paraphrasing Schrödinger's position or by rehearsing the paragraph's slogans. It should oblige the writer to locate and then assess a specific inferential step, and it should make clear that a verdict is required, not a survey.

2. **A model answer of 750 to 900 words.** It should take a definite line, argue for it, and be written at a standard I would actually publish. Because the fellows have no sources to hand, the model answer must contain no citations, no bibliography and no reading list; and any wording presented as Schrödinger's must be a close paraphrase explicitly flagged as paraphrase. Do not put sentences in quotation marks as though lifted from his texts.

3. **A marking scheme out of 25 marks**, allocated to named moves rather than to topics covered. It should state plainly what separates a distinction from a bare pass — for instance, which moves a pass answer may make in any order and which a distinction answer must make in the right order.

In designing both question and scheme, please build in the following cruxes, and make the marking scheme show where each is rewarded or punished:

- The slide from "no plurality is ever given in experience" to "there is numerically one mind". These are different claims, and the second does not follow from the first without a further premise. A good answer should say what that premise would have to be, and whether Schrödinger is entitled to it.
- The conflation of two distinct problems: the unity of a single consciousness (why my experiences hang together as one field rather than a scatter) and the numerical plurality of minds. The junior reporter's paragraph treats them as one issue; they are not.
- Whether anything in his quantum mechanics actually does work here. Entanglement is a claim about the non-separability of physical systems, not about how many subjects there are; the answer should say whether this leaves Schrödinger's monism unsupported, and whether unsupported-by-physics means unsound. His related thesis that natural science constructs its world picture by withdrawing the experiencing subject from it — leaving a world without a spectator — is more relevant than entanglement is, and a distinction answer should notice that.
- The solipsism point, which the paragraph gets backwards. The Vedantic identification of the self in me with the self in you is not the claim that only my mind exists; the answer should distinguish them and say why the distinction is doing real work rather than being a matter of tone.
- At least one objection about the appearance of plurality — most obviously, why separate centres of experience have non-overlapping memories and different sensory data if the experiencer is one — together with the shape of Schrödinger's own reply (the many-viewpoints or many-mirrors style of answer), in the writer's own words. A distinction answer should assess whether that reply explains the illusion or merely redescribes it.
- Whether the second-handness of his Vedanta, filtered through a nineteenth-century German pessimist, bears on the argument at all. I want the writer to confront the genetic-fallacy question directly rather than use provenance as a substitute for evaluation.

Finally, list four or five stock responses that should cost marks — the moves I see every year, such as invoking the cat thought experiment as though it were about consciousness, treating the monism as an interesting biographical eccentricity to be excused, applauding it for being ahead of its time, or reproducing the entanglement inference with nicer prose — and say for each what deduction it attracts and why.
