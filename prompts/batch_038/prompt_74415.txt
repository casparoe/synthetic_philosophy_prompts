I'm a staff writer at a general-interest magazine; my beat is neuroscience and medicine. For the past several months I've been reporting a long piece on pain asymbolia — the condition, typically following damage to the insular and opercular cortex, in which people report that they feel a pain perfectly well, can localize it and grade its intensity, but say it doesn't bother them; they don't flinch, don't withdraw, and in some documented cases laugh at the stimulus. The older neurosurgical literature supplies a parallel: patients given cingulotomies or lobotomies for intractable pain who said the pain was still there and simply no longer mattered, along with the observation that high-dose opioid analgesia can produce a mild version of the same dissociation. My reported piece works through what this does to the claim that pain is bad in virtue of how it feels.

For the same issue we're running a piece of speculative fiction opposite it, and I'd like you to write that. Approach it as a literary novelist who also has graduate-level training in philosophy of mind: someone who can make an abstraction felt in a scene rather than explained in a paragraph. Think carefully before drafting, and do the conceptual work in the open first.

**Step one, before any prose:** give me a short brainstorm — four or five candidate framings, each with (a) whose point of view the story takes, (b) the one philosophical fault line that framing is able to dramatize and the ones it forecloses, and (c) the specific image or scene the framing would hang on. Then say which you would pick and why, and write that one.

**The premise I want you to build on** — invent everything else: a company has developed a targeted, reversible intervention that leaves the sensory side of pain completely intact while removing what I'll call, for want of a better word, its grip. Location, quality, duration, and intensity on a zero-to-ten scale are all preserved and all reportable, which the marketing treats as the selling point rather than as a limitation — you keep the information, you lose the suffering. It is offered in tiers, and it is being taken up first by people with ordinary, manageable, chronic pain rather than by the desperate.

Three things the story has to do:

1. It must never use the vocabulary of the literature. No asymbolia, no unpleasantness, no hedonic tone, no affective dimension, no naming of philosophers or theories. If a character needs to articulate the distinction, she has to do it in the flat, embarrassed language of an ordinary person.

2. It must make both answers to the central question genuinely available to the reader — that these people are better off, and that they have lost something no measure of suffering would register. Don't resolve it with a twist, a death, or a reveal. The loss, when it appears, should appear somewhere other than nociception: in the treated person's relation to her own body, to her reasons for doing things, or to the pain of people she loves. Give me one quiet, physical, undramatic detail that carries it — something like the way a person stops shifting her weight in a chair.

3. Somewhere past the midpoint, someone has to ask the treated character whether she wants the pain to stop. Her answer is the hinge of the piece. Write it so that it can be read two ways at once, and so that finishing the story changes which reading is correct.

Length: 1,700 to 2,000 words. Setting and period are yours; keep it contemporary and ordinary — the intervention should feel like a consumer medical product, not like science-fiction furniture. Do not cite anything, do not name real studies, patients, researchers, or institutions, and do not append a bibliography; wherever you need clinical texture, invent it.

When you're finished, add a separate note to me as editor, about 150 words: state in plain terms the philosophical question the story is arguing about, say what you take the story's implicit answer to be, and flag the one place where you think a philosopher would say the fiction has cheated.
