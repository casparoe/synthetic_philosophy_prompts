So I've been going down a rabbit hole on Maxwell's demon and Landauer's principle, and somewhere along the way I accumulated a pile of intuitions about which operations have to dissipate energy and which don't. I can't tell whether they add up to one coherent principle or whether some of them are flat wrong, and I'd rather find out from you than from another pop-sci chapter. Assume everything below is idealized: perfect devices, arbitrarily slow operation, a single heat bath at temperature T, no friction, no leakage, no dumb engineering losses.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Resetting a bit to 0 when it was equally likely to be 0 or 1 before: must dump at least kT ln 2 as heat. Very confident about this one.
2. Resetting a bit to 0 when I've just measured it and written the result in my notebook, so I know it's a 1: costs nothing in principle, because I can use the notebook as a control and flip the bit conditionally, and that whole operation looks reversible to me.
3. Exact same reset, but the notebook page got shredded and the result now exists only in my lab mate's head, where I can't get at it: back to kT ln 2. So the price tag seems to depend on who happens to be holding the information, and that makes me uneasy.
4. Copying an unknown bit onto a fresh blank: no minimum cost.
5. Preparing that fresh blank in the first place: kT ln 2 per cell, since preparing is just resetting by another name. Which I think means reversible computing isn't free, it just amortizes — you need a fixed pool of blanks you recycle rather than a new one per step.
6. An AND gate: two inputs, one output, logically irreversible, so I'd expect at least kT ln 2 per gate. Except if the inputs are always 0 and always 1, the output is always 0 and nothing has been destroyed. So does the bound depend on the input distribution? If it does, then irreversibility isn't a property of the gate at all, which undercuts how I've been talking.
7. A measurement that produces a durable record of an outcome that was genuinely unpredictable beforehand: I'm inclined to say this costs at least kT ln 2 right there, at the moment of measurement. I gather this is the older view and that Bennett argued against it, but I never really followed the argument, and I'd rather keep the cost at the measurement, because otherwise the demon seems to get its information for free and the whole accounting starts to look like a shell game.
8. The Szilard one-molecule engine: extracting kT ln 2 of work per cycle is legitimate, because erasing the demon's record at the end of the cycle costs exactly that much. Net zero over the cycle.
9. A demon with an enormous memory that simply never erases, sorting molecules for centuries: doesn't violate the second law, it's spending its memory the way a battery spends charge. I think this is right but it feels like a lawyer's answer.
10. Erasing the record into a colder reservoir instead: cheaper, kT_cold ln 2, and still fine, because the temperature difference was itself a resource I could have fed to an ordinary engine.
11. Storing a bit and just holding it for a decade: no cost whatsoever. Only changes in information content cost anything.
12. Deleting a file on a real machine: dissipates maybe a billion times kT ln 2, and that's not a violation, just bad engineering. So the principle is a floor and never a prediction.

The pair I cannot reconcile is 2 and 3. Physically it is the same bit in the same state being driven to the same state, and my verdicts differ only because of what I know, or rather because of what a system I can physically reach into knows. A principle that saves both has to make thermodynamic cost depend on the agent's access to correlations, and I don't want to accept that if I can avoid it. Also 6 and 7 both feel shaky to me in a way 1 and 8 don't.

So: is there a principle behind all of this, and can you state it precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a new case? Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and just say so plainly rather than trying to rescue all twelve. I'd especially like to know whether the right statement is about logical irreversibility, about entropy decrease of the memory, or about mutual information between the memory and everything else I can touch, and whether the generalized second law with an information term is doing the real work here or is a separate thing.

Then run it on cases I haven't given a verdict on: resetting a bit that's correlated with something I can measure but only imperfectly, so I have partial rather than full side information; a device whose whole job is to output a fresh random bit, where I honestly can't tell whether producing randomness costs anything, is free, or somehow earns work; measuring the same bit twice and then erasing only one of the two copies; and erasing a quantum bit I prepared myself versus one somebody handed me in an unknown state. Relatedly, should I even be reading my "at least kT ln 2" claims as per-cycle guarantees, or as averages over many runs, given that I've heard small systems sometimes come out ahead by luck?

And if there's some case you think I would get badly wrong that isn't on my list at all, give me that one too, with your verdict and the reasoning.
