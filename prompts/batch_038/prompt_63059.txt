I'm putting together a long-form piece for a small politics blog — basically an argument with myself that I want to stage as a made-up dialogue rather than lecture at people. Setting: a county commission hearing in a fictional high-desert county (call it Larkspur County) over whether a private company gets to site an interim dry-cask storage facility for spent nuclear fuel on county land. Two commissioners, both smart, both honest, no strawmen, and they should be responding to what the other actually said rather than to a cached version of it.

Commissioner A is a self-described public-reason liberal, ex–state legislature staffer, who thinks the whole point of justifying coercion to people who reasonably disagree is that you hand them reasons they can check for themselves; he's voting yes on the strength of the national regulator's probabilistic risk assessment — per-cask containment failure rates, groundwater transport models, the works. Commissioner B is a Mennonite farmer, her late father drilled wells for forty years, and she's voting no. She holds that you may not saddle people who can't consent with a burden that outlasts them, she understands that as an obligation running out of her sense of creation and of covenant stretching across generations, and she also has very concrete hydrogeological claims about the local basalt and the shallow aquifer that she picked up at her father's elbow.

Here's the part I actually care about, so don't drift off it: I don't want the standard "religious reasons have to be translated into secular ones before they're admissible" script, because I think the twist in this room is that her case is far more *checkable* by the people being coerced than his is. She can point at core samples and at wells that went sour; he's asking twelve laypeople to take an expert institution's word for a ten-thousand-year model they can't open. So A should start by trying to show that B's religious premise is doing no work — strip it out, he says, and you're just making a secular precautionary argument in a funny hat — and B has to show that stripping it changes the verdict, because the secular version gets discounted at three percent across a century and the covenant version doesn't, and that discounting is what makes her a no and him a yes. Then A should notice what he's committed to. I want the real question to surface by the end rather than be announced: whether "public" means the premises are accessible in principle, or assessable by the actual audience in the room, or something about the mode of justification being open to revision on shared evidence — and whether A can hold onto any of those three without disqualifying his own vote.

Constraints: 900–1200 words, two speakers only, no narrator, no closing summary of the moral. Both of them land real hits and both take at least one. Don't quote any actual philosopher verbatim and don't invent quotations from real books, real hearings, or real court cases — if a character gestures at an author or an agency document, gesture, don't cite. Afterwards, in your own voice and separately from the dialogue, give me a short note: how confident are you that you gave A the strongest version of his position rather than the version that makes the twist land neatly, which single exchange would you bet least on, and can the assessability refinement be made precise at all — or does it quietly collapse into "the reasons I happen to find convincing count as public"?
