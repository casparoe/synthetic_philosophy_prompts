I'm auditing a graduate seminar on Buddhist ethics this term, and the running dispute has been whether any recognizably Buddhist theory can count as consequentialist at all, or whether the tradition's preoccupation with the agent's own mental states makes it something closer to a virtue ethics. For my final paper I want to pressure-test the consequentialist reading with a hard case of my own construction, and I'd like your help designing it. Here is the specification. Build me a thought experiment — one paragraph of scenario, no more — in which a Mahāyāna Buddhist consequentialist, meaning someone who holds that the right act is the one that best promotes the welfare of all sentient beings across the whole future course of their rebirths, must judge an act impermissible that a secular act utilitarian is obliged to judge obligatory, and in which the utilitarian is not a straw man: he is fully informed, purely benevolent, counts the welfare of every sentient being including animals, discounts the future only as much as you like, and has no deontological squeamishness of any kind. I'm not after citations, a reading list, or a survey of what any scholar has said about Buddhist consequentialism; I want the case and the analysis.

The divergence has to be generated rather than stipulated, and I want three cheap routes closed off in advance. First, the Buddhist may not be leaning on any precept as an absolute side-constraint; if a prohibition on killing or lying is what does the work, the case has failed, since the whole point is that he is a genuine maximizer. Second, the difference may not come from his assigning less weight to ordinary happiness or from ranking some beings' happiness below others'; his welfare function must be perfectly impartial and must fully include the very same pleasures and pains that the utilitarian counts. Third, it may not come from karmic costs the utilitarian has overlooked: stipulate that the act is done from a wholly compassionate and unselfish motive, that it wrongs no one, and that the total balance of pleasant over unpleasant experience summed across all future lives of all affected beings is higher if the act is performed than if it is not. So the utilitarian's number is bigger, and the Buddhist still refuses. As for setting, keep it mundane and institutional rather than science-fictional — a monastery's landed estate, a famine-relief operation, a public health campaign, the terms of an endowment — with no magic, no consciousness-altering technology, and nothing resembling a trolley. Since the case is meant to compare the theories on their own terms, you may grant that the Buddhist metaphysics is true and that both parties know it, so that the utilitarian cannot simply be dismissed as ignorant of karma and rebirth. My own guess is that the turn of the case will have to involve something like the traditional claim that even pleasant conditioned existence remains a species of duḥkha, and the corresponding loss of saṃvega — that urgent dismay at the futility of a comfortable ordinary life which drives beings toward renunciation — or else the loss of awakening as a good that is not reducible to any experiential state. But I would rather you work out the mechanism yourself than take my word for it, and if you judge that this mechanism cannot bear the weight I've put on it, substitute another that can and say why.

After the scenario, walk me through it from both sides. State what the utilitarian's calculus yields and why he cannot escape that verdict without giving up act-utilitarianism itself; then state what the Buddhist consequentialist says and show explicitly that his refusal follows from his own impartial maximizing commitment once a particular good sits in the value function, rather than from a smuggled constraint or a quiet discount rate on other beings' happiness. Then be honest about the engineering: identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed — in particular, whether the two theories then coincide, which would itself be a finding worth reporting about the consequentialist reading of Buddhist ethics. Finally, give the utilitarian's best reply to the case, and say whether that reply amounts to charging the thought experiment with begging the question, or whether it concedes something. Please write the entire response as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italicized terms, and no tables, even for the side-by-side comparison of the two theories.
