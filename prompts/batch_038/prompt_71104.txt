I teach research methods to graduate students in the social sciences, and across three cohorts I keep meeting the same pattern, for which I have no term.

A student hands a well-specified task to a language model — recoding a survey variable, drafting a methods section, debugging an analysis script — and receives an output that looks finished. Asked directly whether it has been checked, and made to sit with it, the student finds the defect within a minute: the recoding silently collapses a response category, the paragraph describes a test that was never run, the axis labels do not match the data. The capacity to evaluate the artifact is fully intact. What has gone missing is the spontaneous arrival of the evaluative question. Previously the suspicion that something was off surfaced unbidden, in the middle of doing the work; now nothing surfaces unless another person voices it, so the artifact is accepted by default rather than by inspection. The same students, working unaided, catch such errors unprompted.

This is not deskilling, since the skill answers readily to explicit invocation, and it is not what the human-factors literature calls automation complacency, since there is no inflated trust — asked, they are appropriately sceptical. The deficit seems to sit in whatever normally initiates the check.

Does this have a name, in work on AI-assisted cognition or epistemic dependence? If a near neighbour exists, tell me how near, and where my description parts company with it. If nothing fits, propose a term, but I care mainly about its edges: what would be a clear instance, what would merely look like one (deadline pressure, or a student who never had the habit to lose), and what evidence would settle which is happening in a given case.
