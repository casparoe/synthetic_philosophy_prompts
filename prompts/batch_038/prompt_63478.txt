So — context. I'm about two hundred pages into a novel whose protagonist is a copy-editor, mid-fifties, famous at her little press for catching the things nobody else catches, and the book is built on the idea that the very faculty making her good is the one that traps her. I keep coming back to Dennett's digger-wasp example: the wasp hauls its paralyzed grasshopper to the lip of the burrow, goes inside to check the tunnel's clear, comes back out and drags it down — and if an ethologist nudges the grasshopper a few inches while she's underground, she emerges, hauls it back to the lip, goes in to check again, and you can keep doing this forever. That's the shape I want for one central scene. Not "she has a blind spot about her own life," which I can get from any number of literary novels and find boring. My beta reader pushed back on the draft and said basically "she's way too smart for this, she'd notice by the third time," and my brother, who's an EMT, made the same argument over dinner — nobody loops like that without clocking it. Defeating that objection is the whole job, so I need the case designed rather than gestured at.

Here's the spec. Contemporary and ordinary — her desk, a manuscript, a kitchen, a school run, whatever you like, but no sci-fi, no AI, no amnesia, no drugs, no lying antagonist feeding her false facts. Two paragraphs maximum for the scenario itself, then a numbered walkthrough. The requirements: (1) she has to be visibly, undeniably smart *inside* the loop — put her solving something genuinely hard in the middle of a pass, so no reader can write the loop off as dumbness. (2) She has to be carrying every relevant belief — no false belief, no missing fact, nothing she'd want to check if it occurred to her to check; list what she believes on each pass. (3) There has to be a real analog of moving the grasshopper: some small perturbation, applied by another person or just by the world, that can be repeated indefinitely and reliably resets her to the top of the routine — tell me what it is and why it never accumulates into evidence for her. (4) Name the two moves precisely — which sub-step is "drag it back to the lip" and which is "go in and check the burrow," i.e. which derivation she redoes from scratch every time and which one she skips because it was settled years ago and she can't see that the settling has come lose.

Then, after the scenario: the frame-problem reading of her failure, in the sense that she has all the facts but not the ability to tell which of the consequences of those facts are relevant right now; the non-sphexish version of the same woman with the same facts and one small difference, where I want you to say whether that difference is more raw intelligence or a cheaper cognitive arrangement — one of Dennetts wheels, some habit or artifact or heuristic that does the noticing on her behalf; which single feature of the case is doing all the work, and what happens to it if I pull that feature out; and how a skeptic would attack the whole thing — someone who says sphexishness applied to humans is just sunk cost and motivated reasoning in a fancy costume — with the best reply you can give and an honest word on whether that reply holds. And be straight with me on this last part: say where you think the design is most likely to fail, what would change your mind about whether this is genuine sphexishness or ordinary stubbornness wearing a wasp suit, and if you think one of my constraints can't be satisfied alongside the others, tell me which one should go and why rather than quietly softening it. Assume I've read Elbow Room and the frame problem essay — I don't need the wasp explained back to me, I want the machinery, not the moral.
