I'm running a small grad seminar on updating and I've decided the useful thing to hand my students is a document, not another lecture from me. So here's the thing I want you to draft.

Two people have been arguing about uncertain evidence for years and they've mostly been arguing past each other:

- **Strict** holds that all real learning is becoming certain of *something*. Maybe not certain of a claim about the world — but at least certain of a claim about how things look to you right now, the "I'm being appeared-to in such-and-such a way" style of proposition. On her view probability kinematics is a derived bookkeeping device at best, and if you think you've learned without becoming certain of anything you've misdescribed your own situation.
- **Kinetic** holds that some learning is irreducibly uncertain: there are updates where nothing in your algebra goes to 1, and where shifting the probabilities across a partition while holding your conditional credences off that partition fixed is simply what rationality requires. He thinks Strict's phenomenal propositions are either too coarse to do the work or too fine for the agent to have.

Write the joint statement the two of them could actually sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes.

In the disagreements table, each row needs the disagreement stated twice — once in wording Strict would accept as a fair statement of her own view, once in wording Kinetic would accept for his. Not neutral mush, not straw. Where you think a putative disagreement is verbal or a difference in modeling bookkeeping, say so and say which one, and don't just quietly drop it from the table.

For the cruxes, I want the sub-questions where settling it would actually move at least one of them, and for each crux a case or argument both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down *before* the answer. Work at least these three cases in somewhere:

1. **The dial.** A tech reads a gauge in bad light. The needle is somewhere in a band spanning three marked regions, R1, R2, R3. Her credences go from (.2, .5, .3) to (.1, .6, .3), and she'll tell you flat out that nothing about the needle became certain and that she couldn't characterize her own experience in enough detail to pin down where those numbers came from. Both sides have to answer: what does she now have that she didn't have before, and what fixes the new numbers?
2. **Conditional-form news.** The report you recieve is itself conditional: if the shipment went north, then the probability it's in the blue warehouse is three-quarters. You had no view at all about whether it went north. Both have to say what happens to your unconditional credences, and whether the answer is even determined. (This is in the neighborhood of that Judy Benjamin case, but don't fuss about the attribution.)
3. **Non-rigid news.** Same dial, but the news also disturbs your credences *within* each region — you find out the gauge was lit by a red lamp, which changes how you'd bet inside R2. The kinematic rule doesn't apply anymore. Both have to say what, if anything, still constrains the update, and whether this is a refutation of the framework or just a sign it's incomplete.

Two more things I want pressed on them explicitly. First: state the conditions under which Strict's phenomenal-certainty route and Kinetic's rule provably give the same numbers, and the conditions under which they provably can't. If they only coincide when the update is rigid, that's a finding and it belongs in the document. Second: I'm pretty sure there's a line of work suggesting that a rigid kinematic update can always be reproduced by ordinary conditionalization on some newly introduced proposition — call it virtual evidence, add it to the algebra, done. If that's right, does the dispute collapse, and if not, what survives it? Make each of them answer that in their own voice.

Also give me a short closing section on what each would give up: the cheapest concession available to each side, and what evidence or argument would make them give up the whole position rather than a piece of it.

One last request, and it matters for how I use this in class. Keep straight what's genuinely settled among people who work on this and what's still live. When something goes in the agreed-claims list, tag it in brackets as either real consensus or a local truce these two struck for the sake of the document — a lot of what they can sign will be the second kind, and I don't want my students walking away thinking the field is more agreed-upon than it is. If you think one of these positions is close to dead, say that too instead of staging a fake balance. Just don't dress a contested view up as the consensus.
