I am a fifth-year economics doctoral student teaching my own section of the introductory game theory course for the first time this term — until now I have only graded problem sets — and the course coordinator is sitting in on my week on solution concepts. For years I have opened the discussion of iterated elimination of dominated strategies with the battle-of-wits scene from The Princess Bride: Westley announces that he has poisoned exactly one of two goblets with iocane powder, Vizzini reasons aloud at length about what sort of man Westley is, therefore which goblet Westley would have poisoned, and whether Westley anticipated that inference and anticipated its anticipation; Vizzini then diverts Westley's attention, switches the goblets, and both men drink, and the reveal is that Westley poisoned both and has spent years building an immunity. My students love the clip, and I have always glossed it as a picture of iterated reasoning about reasoning. Last week a student who had taken decision theory as an undergraduate told me, politely but firmly, that nothing in that scene is a dominance argument at all, and that I have been teaching the wrong lesson for years. I suspect she is right, and I would rather find out now than in front of the coordinator.

Here is what I want the scene read against, in the vocabulary I teach. Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies can run through several rounds and still leave a large surviving set, and it is order-independent only for strict dominance; what genuinely adds eliminative power is admitting mixed strategies, because a pure strategy that no pure strategy dominates may still be strictly dominated by a mixture, and being so dominated is exactly equivalent to never being a best response to any belief about the opponent's play. My own claim for the handout is this: the announced goblet game is a two-person zero-sum matching game in which nothing is dominated, pure or mixed, so elimination buys nothing and Vizzini's regress of he-knows-that-I-know is not a failed dominance argument but a correct intuition that there is nothing there to eliminate; and Westley wins not inside that game but by replacing it, since poisoning both goblets was never in the announced action set and his acquired immunity is a change in payoffs rather than a strategy.

Please give me exactly five numbered notes for the handout, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing remarks. First: the formal structure of the announced game and whether anything in it is dominated, pure or mixed. Second: what admitting mixtures does or does not add in this case, and whether Vizzini's chain is better described as iterated best response, as level-k reasoning, or as something this vocabulary has no clean name for. Third: Westley's actual move translated into the framework — what has shifted, the action set, the payoffs, or the information structure — and whether any dominance argument survives the translation. Fourth: what the scene dramatizes about solution concepts that a textbook example with a payoff matrix cannot. Fifth: where I should drop the lens altogether and concede that Goldman is doing something else, being funny and drawing a portrait of an intelligence that mistakes cleverness for knowledge.

Do not quote the dialogue; I will rewatch the scene myself, and I need only the beats described. And I would rather you tell me plainly that my student is right and that my reading is a category error than confirm a tidy story that will not survive twenty minutes of questions from an economist.
