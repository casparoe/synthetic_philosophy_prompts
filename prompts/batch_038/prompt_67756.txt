ok, kind of an unusual ask but I need a prep sheet, not an essay.

I'm a senior adviser in the premier's office — I'm not the minister, I'm the person who actually drafted the thing — and in about three weeks I go in front of a legislative committee to present our motion of official apology for the state residential school system: the one that ran from the 1890s into the seventies, took kids out of Indigenous-language communities, and contracted the day-to-day running of the schools to churches. I get ten minutes, then it's open questions for maybe half an hour.

Where the draft currently stands: it uses the word sorry, not regret or sorrow. It calls the removal policy wrong but stops short of calling it unlawful. It names no individual and no past government. It's addressed to survivors, to their descendants, and — there's a line I know is going to get torn into — to the children who died in the schools, as though an apology can reach them. There's a clause saying the apology creates no legal entitlement to anything. No money attached; compensation is a seperate bill that may or may not survive the year. It gets read in the chamber on a fixed symbolic date, in English only, no translation into the languages the schools existed to eliminate, with the survivors' delegation in the gallery.

My own view, which is what I'm actually defending: the state is one continuing agent, so it can owe an apology that nobody currently sitting in that room personally earned — and an apology only means anything if it costs the apologizer something, except right now the only things I can make it cost are the naming, the date and the chamber floor.

Assume everyone in the room knows the well-known precedents cold (the Australian apology, the Canadian one, the congressional resolutions with the liability disclaimers) and will be comparing us to them. Committee is nine: three backbenchers who think this is history-war stuff, a constitutional lawyer who doubts the no-entitlement clause holds, a former crown prosecutor, a historian of the period who has published on it and does not like my wording, and two members with big survivor communities in thier ridings who want money now. Survivors and advocates speak after us.

Give me the eight or nine questions I'll really get, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the motion, in each person's own idiom — the historian is not going to put it in philosopher-speak. For each: what I should give away in the first sentence, what I have to hold, and a 60–90 second answer I can actually deliver standing up. Flag the ones where my answer is honestly thin. Then tell me which single line of the draft is the most exposed, and the one question I'm praying nobody asks. No reading list or bibliography, I won't have time to read anything before then.
