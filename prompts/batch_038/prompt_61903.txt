I'm doing my MPH practicum at a city health department, and one of the programs I've been sitting in on is a managed alcohol program — nurses dispensing measured doses of wine, several times a day, to a handful of people with severe treatment-refractory alcohol dependence, in a supervised setting, mostly because they'd otherwise be drinking hand sanitizer or mouthwash on the street. It sounds insane until you look at the outcomes, and it sent me down a rabbit hole I now want to turn into a ~2500 word piece for a public-health-humans blog I contribute to occasionally. The framing I landed on: what would Plato make of how we handle alcohol now? My roommate, who bartends, insists Plato would definately just be a straight-up prohibitionist — "the guy exiled the poets, obviously he'd exile the bottle" — and I think that's lazy, because the Laws is full of him regulating wine rather than banning it: nothing at all until eighteen, moderate drinking with no intoxication until thirty, and then at forty you get invited to the elders' symposion, where Dionysus' gift is supposed to soften the crabbedness of age; he even describes wine mellowing the soul the way iron softens in fire, and there's that puppet image in the first book with the golden cord of calculation getting pulled against the iron cords of appetite. But I don't want to just cheerlead my own side, so I'd rather stress-test the roommate's take properly.

Here's the ask: play the role of a classicist who also happens to know the addiction-policy literature cold, and predict issue by issue where Plato would actually land. The issues, roughly in the order I want to hit them — (1) managed alcohol programs, i.e. the state itself administering measured doses of wine to people whose drinking has destroyed them; (2) Nordic-style retail monopolies, the Systembolaget model where the government is the only seller, hours are limited and there's no advertising at all; (3) minimum unit pricing like Scotland and Wales introduced, which is deliberately aimed at cheap strong booze and lands hardest on poor dependent drinkers; (4) naltrexone, and specifically the Sinclair Method version where you take it about an hour before drinking instead of every day, so the aim is to extinguish the craving rather than block a binge; (5) GLP-1 agonists, which seem to blunt cravings for alcohol as a side effect — basically chemically supplied temperance; (6) Dry January, sober-curious culture, mocktail menus, and the generational drop in consumption among under-25s; (7) extending the same reasoning beyond alcohol — Portugal's 2001 decriminalization with its administrative dissuasion commissions, Canada legalizing cannabis in 2018, Germany's partial legalization in 2024; (8) dram-shop liability, mandatory responsible-service training for bartenders, happy-hour and all-you-can-drink bans, and whether people in safety-critical jobs or public office should be barred from drinking at all.

For the deliverable: start with a short brainstorm before you apply anything — five to seven "wine principles" you'd extract from the corpus, a sentence or two each, so I can see the machinery you're running on. Then one block per issue with a predicted verdict (endorse / tolerate but restructure / abhor), a confidence number out of 100 plus one line explaining why it's that number and not higher, the specific Platonic commitments driving it, and the strongest counterargument available from inside Plato's own writings — I'm thinking especially of the tension between the near-teetotal ideal city of the Republic, the pragmatic wine legislation of the Laws, and Socrates in the Symposium, who drinks everyone under the table and is fine at dawn. Then tell me which of these eight he'd find most congenial and which most horrifying, and whether that pair matches my gut (I suspect he'd like the managed alcohol programs and be contemptuous of sober-curious culture, which is close to the reverse of where most of my classmates sit). Flag honestly any issue where the corpus gives you no real grip and you'd be extrapolating from thin air — I'd rather have "no traction here" than a confident invention. And finish with the one question in the comments that would blow up the whole framing. No bibliography or reading list, and please don't fabricate Stephanus page numbers — if you're unsure of a citation, describe the passage and who's speaking instead. Think it through carefully and don't rush to a flattering answer: where Plato is repellent to modern readers, let him be repellent rather than sanitizing him.
