I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, and one argument has recurred there often enough that I could recite both sides of it in my sleep. The question is whether the Church–Turing thesis is an empirical claim that physics could in principle overturn. One side says obviously yes: the physical version is a hypothesis about the world, and if someone built a device that settled the halting problem we would have our refutation; the exotic scenarios are just engineering difficulties dressed up as philosophy. The other side says the thesis is closer to a stipulation or a conceptual truth about what counts as an effective procedure, so nothing assembled in a lab could bear on it either way.

I want to defend a third position, and I would like you to make the case for it as forcefully as it can be made. The position is this: identifying a physical system *as a device* — as something with inputs, outputs, and a stable input–output relation that survives repetition and re-description — is an achievement that can only be accomplished by finite procedures. The thesis that no physical process outruns a Turing machine therefore functions as a presupposition of measurement rather than as a hypothesis about measurement's results. It follows that we are never in a position to report that some system has transcended the thesis; the only reports available to us are that our description of the experimental setup was mistaken.

To keep this from drifting into generalities, here is the concrete case I have in mind, which I invented for the purpose. A research group announces a sealed device, call it D. They cannot open it, and the builders will not explain it. The protocol is simple: the group selects an index n from a standard enumeration of Turing machines, enters it, and D emits a single bit. For some values of n the group can independently determine the correct answer — the machine halts in a few seconds, or someone has settled the relevant question by other means — and D agrees without exception. For other values they cannot, and are reduced to trusting the device. The most interesting cases are the long ones: an n where the machine is known to halt but only after a computation of astronomical length, so that D's answer is verifiable in principle but not in practice, and the group waits years for a distributed search to catch up with it. D has been right every time so far.

Notice now what the evidence actually consists of. Every item in it is a case where the halting status was settled by a finite procedure — the group's own run of the machine, a proof, a search — and D's contribution is agreement with that procedure. There is no item in the evidence set of the form "D answered correctly where the answer could not have been obtained by finite means," because no such item could ever be entered into the record. So what is the likelihood ratio of the observed sequence of agreements? A merely finite machine that guesses shrewdly and a genuine oracle are indistinguishable precisely on the inputs where checking is possible, and the inputs where they would diverge are, by construction, the ones where no checking exists. Worse for the group: before any of this could begin, they had to establish that D is a device at all — that entering n is well-defined, that the emitted bit is a bit *about* n rather than about something correlated with n, that the relation is stable across repetitions, and that reading the output does not parasitically depend on computations they had already performed.

A few requirements. Argue; do not survey. I am already familiar with the standard inventory of putative counterexamples — machines that accelerate so as to complete an infinite task in finite proper time, general-relativistic spacetimes in which an observer situated outside a certain region is handed the answer to an undecidable question, and their relatives — and I know the standard replies to each. What I want is the case that these scenarios fail not merely as engineering projects but as descriptions of an *observation*: that in every such case, whatever we would end up reporting is a report about our own finite interaction with a system, and no report of that kind can express the content "the halting function was computed here." Take seriously the objection that this renders the thesis unfalsifiable and hence empty, and the objection that it quietly equivocates between the mathematical and the physical formulations. Do not hedge the conclusion into a balanced digest of the debate — I want an argument, with its costs acknowledged where they genuinely bite.
