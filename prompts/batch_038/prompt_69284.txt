Ok so this is embarrassing, but I spent most of the weekend on it and I still cant tell whether I found a bug in my own reasoning or refuted Zorn's lemma, and I'd rather not walk into section on monday having said the second thing out loud.

Setup: we just did the proof that every nonzero commutative ring with 1 has a maximal ideal. You take P = the proper ideals, ordered by inclusion; the union of a chain is an ideal and is still proper, because 1 isn't in any link of the chain; Zorn hands you a maximal element. Beautiful. I noticed the proof didn't use commutativity, and barely used anything ring-specific except that 1 exists, so I tried to port it to groups.

(1) Zorn's lemma: if P is a nonempty poset in which every chain has an upper bound that is itself a member of P, then P has a maximal element.
(2) Let G be any nontrivial group, and let P = the proper subgroups of G, ordered by inclusion. {e} is proper when G is nontrivial, so P is nonempty.
(3) If C is a chain in P, then ⋃C is a subgroup of G: any two elements of the union lie in a common member of C, so closure and inverses are inherited.
(4) ⋃C is also proper, so ⋃C ∈ P, and it is an upper bound for C.
(5) So by (1) every nontrivial group has a maximal subgroup M.
(6) In the abelian case M is automatically normal, so G/M is a group, maximality makes it simple, and the only simple abelian groups are the Z/p. Hence G/M ≅ Z/p for some prime p.
(7) Take G = Q, the additive rationals. Then Q has a maximal subgroup M with Q/M ≅ Z/p.
(8) But Q is divisible, quotients of divisible abelian groups are divisible, and Z/p is not divisible, since p·x = 0 for every x.
(9) Contradiction. So Zorn's lemma is false, which is terrible news for me because I'm using it in three other proofs this week.
