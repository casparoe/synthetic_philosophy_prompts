So here's my situation. I'm a city councilmember in a mid-sized city — not going to name it because I don't want this to turn into a thing about my state — and last month our legislature passed one of those omnibus "uniformity" bills that voided a dozen of our ordinances retroactively and then barred us from legislating at all in about eight policy areas going forward, with a clause letting the state AG sue us personally if we try, fees on the city either way. Nothing about how we were elected. No gerrymander, no packed court, no touched ballots, no journalist arrested. Our elections were fine and everyone knows they'll stay fine.

Half the council says this is just Dillon's Rule working as intended, states have always had plenary power over municipalities, and both parties have done preemption forever, so anyone calling it "democratic backsliding" is just crying wolf for their own side. Honestly, on the days I'm tired, I almost agree with them. But something about this one is sitting wrong and I can't articulate what.

I've got a hunch it has to do with a distinction I can't find anyone stating cleanly anywhere, so I made up labels for it: call it *procedural* backsliding when a government degrades the machinery for winning power, versus *jurisdictional* backsliding when the machinery stays spotless but the amount of power there is to win quietly shrinks. Second one seems invisible to every checklist I know, including the famous ones about mutual toleration and forbearance and the four indicators of authoritarian behavior — those all look at how leaders treat opponents, and my legislature isn't treating anyone badly, they're just deleting the office.

Please make the strongest argument you can that jurisdictional backsliding is real backsliding, not ordinary politics with a different flag on it. Argue it, don't survey it. I don't want a reading list or a bibliography, I can't check any of that and I'd rather you just reason it through.

Structure it like this, roughly:

**Why my colleagues have a point** — about 150 words. Genuinely steelman them, including the history of state preemption and the fact that municipalities are creatures of the state. If this section is a strawman the rest collapses.

**The distinction** — about 200 words. Sharpen procedural vs jurisdictional. Tell me if you think it's a bad distinction or if it collapses into something people already have a name for.

**The argument** — around 600 words, this is the main event. Why should a voter care that the prize shrank if the race is still fair? What exactly is lost that elections can't restore?

**The reversal test and its big problem** — about 250 words. My proposed criterion is: a change counts as jurisdictional backsliding only if the losing side can't undo it by winning a normal election — if reversal takes more than a majority. Obvious objection is that tons of legitimate democratic stuff is entrenched this way (rights, constitutions, independent central banks), so "can't be undone by a simple majority" can't be the criterion on its own. Fix it or tell me to throw it out.

**Where my city actually lands** — 150 words or so. Apply whatever survives to what happened here, and say plainly whether my colleagues are right that I'm just being partisan.
