I am drafting a chapter for an edited collection on metaphysics and the nineteenth-century novel. My assignment is Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, read through the Special Composition Question: under what conditions, if any, do some things compose a further thing? I want literary-philosophical criticism answerable to both the novel and the metaphysics, not a plot summary with a technical vocabulary draped over it. The volume's style sheet forbids footnotes and the editors are supplying the scholarly apparatus, so please give me the argument and the readings rather than a bibliography, reading list or citations; likewise, paraphrase the novel rather than quoting from it.

Begin by mapping the space of answers compactly and in your own words: nihilism about composite objects; universalism, together with the argument from vagueness usually associated with Lewis and Sider that is taken to motivate it; the contact-and-cohesion family of restricted answers; van Inwagen's view that composition occurs only where the activity of the parts constitutes a life; and the view defended by Merricks that composition occurs only where a plurality has causal powers not already had by its parts acting in concert. Two or three paragraphs at most, and framed so that each answer makes a different prediction about Victor's creature. Then do the reading.

I am not interested merely in the observation that the creature is assembled. Please take up at least the following.

Provenance. The parts Victor gathers have already been parts of other living beings, and the novel is insistent about where they come from. Is what results an organism, an artefact, or something whose unity is of a third kind altogether — closer to a colony, in the way that a siphonophore or a coral head is one animal only in a loose and contested sense? Does the book anywhere treat achieved unity as metaphysically inferior to grown unity, or is that our imposition upon it? Note why this matters for van Inwagen: if the creature is alive, his account makes it a genuine object in spite of its history, which forces the question whether being alive is an intrinsic feature of how a thing is organised at a time, or partly a fact about where its parts came from.

Thresholds. The animation is a threshold crossed, and Victor's destruction of the half-finished female is a threshold crossed in reverse: at which removed part would she have ceased to be a candidate for existence, if she ever was one? Does Shelley locate these boundaries, or does she exploit their unsharpness for effect? If the latter, does that exploitation support the thought that any restricted answer to the question will be intolerably vague, or does it suggest instead that vagueness about composition is a price worth paying — one that a novelist can make us feel rather than merely concede?

Narrative unity. The creature's embedded first-person account is the novel's most sustained argument for his standing. Does the book in effect offer the telling of a continuous self-narrative as the ground of his being one thing? If so, be blunt about whether that is an answer to the Special Composition Question at all, or a Lockean criterion of personal identity over time recruited to do synchronic work it was never meant to do — and whether the novel can be credited with noticing the difference or is simply trading on it.

Victor's implicit mereology. Consider what Victor does: flight, refusal to finish, then pursuit across the ice. Does he regard the creature as one being with a claim upon him, or as an arrangement of stolen parts to be dispersed? Is his horror horror at a composite that should never have been composed, or at something the novel leaves deliberately unclear? And is the Walton frame — a nest of recounted narratives, each contained within the next — a structural image of the same problem, or a coincidence I should not build a chapter on? Tell me which.

Where the reading breaks. Devote a real section to what this lens cannot handle: features of the novel that are not about unity and plurality at all, places where forcing the terminology yields a strained result, and the honest case for saying that Shelley is not addressing our question and that importing it distorts both the book and the metaphysics. I would far rather be told this now than by a referee.

Close with what the novel contributes that argument does not. Abandonment presupposes an answer to the composition question: there must be someone there to abandon. Say what a reader who has felt the force of that presupposition knows afterwards that a reader of the journal articles does not, and whether the novel has thereby changed the question, the answer, or only the stakes.

Assume your reader knows the plot, including the Walton frame, the De Lacey household and the creature's education there, and the Arctic conclusion, but do not depend upon their recalling minor characters' names. Around 2,000 words, under headings of your choosing. If any point you make would turn on differences between the 1818 and the 1831 texts, flag it as contingent upon the edition rather than asserting what the variants say.
