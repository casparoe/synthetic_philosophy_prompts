I run the incident investigation group at a mid-size chemical plant — six of us, and between us we put out maybe 25 or 30 reports a year. Every report goes through our legal department before it goes to the state regulator, and sometimes it ends up read aloud in a deposition. One section of the report is called causal statements, and legal kicks that section back more than any other, always for the same reason. We write sentences with "if" in them that don't hold up, and we write them with a confidence they haven't earned.

The problem, as I understand it, is that we're using "if" for two completely different jobs and can't tell which is which. One job is a general claim about how the equipment or the crew behaves — if the interlock is bypassed, the pump keeps running — the kind of sentence you can back with a test, a rate, a maintenance record. The other job is a claim about a day that already happened, in a past that isn't real: if the alarm had sounded, the operator would have tripped the unit inside twenty seconds. There's no test for that one and no rate. It all rests on which details of that Tuesday you're allowed to hold fixed and which ones you're allowed to change in order to make the alarm sound. I know this is a thing people have worked on seriously — indicative versus counterfactual conditionals, something about conditional probability on one side and closest possible worlds on the other — but I don't know it well enough to teach it, and my people certainly don't.

So what I want is a procedure an investigator can run on a draft causal statement before it goes to legal. Not an explainer on the semantics. An ordered thing: the questions you ask, in what order, what counts as passing each one, where the procedure stops you cold and tells you to strike the sentence, and where it can't decide and has to hand the call to a human because the question left is about salience or blame rather than about the plant.

Some constraints on your end. The person running it is a process engineer with no philosophy and about thirty minutes for this section of the report. Half the time there's no usable data — no historian trend, no repeat of the failure, nothing — and the procedure still has to tell them what to do rather than shrug. We already have three tags legal makes us put on every causal statement: WOULD, meaning a specific counterfactual about the day in question; GENERAL, meaning a disposition or a rate; and UNKNOWN. Your procedure should end at a tag for each sentence, and it should say how to repair a sentence that doesn't earn any tag rather than just killing it. And it has to work with prose, since our reports are read by lawyers who pounce on anything evasive.

There are four failure modes I want it to catch explicitly, because we commit all four. First, sentences that look fine until you flip them around — we write "if the relief valve had lifted, the vessel would not have ruptured," and then somebody in the meeting contraposes it and we suddenly discover we've been asserting that nothing else on that unit could have prevented the rupture. Second, conditionals that are only true relative to whoever is saying them: one of my investigators has the operator's statement that she closed the valve at 14:02, another has trend data showing flow continuing past 14:10, and each of them can say something perfectly reasonable of the form "if the valve was open, then…" that the other has to reject. Both sentences end up in the same draft. Third, the ones where making the antecedent true quietly destroys the reason the consequent would have followed — if the pump had run, but the only way the pump runs is by erasing the trip that also erased the flow we needed. Fourth, chains: three counterfactuals stitched into one sentence so that the last clause is doing work none of the earlier ones licensed.

Then run the procedure on three real ones from our files. One: "If the second operator had been on shift, the bypass would have been caught before startup" — the seat was vacant, the bypass had been left in by maintenance two days earlier, and the pre-startup checklist doesn't include the bypass. I'll tell you now that I want this sentence to survive, we've used it to argue for a headcount change, and I expect your procedure is going to make me rewrite it or drop it; if so, say so plainly and show me what the honest replacement sentence looks like. Two: the two conflicting valve conditionals above, in one draft. Three: "If the trip had been armed, the pump would have stopped" — deterministic logic, documented, bench-tested every month, and the trip had no other defeat on that day. That one should pass, because a procedure that vetoes everything is worse than no procedure.

Finally, tell me what it can't do. Which steps are judgment wearing a procedure's clothes — how narrow you're allowed to describe the antecedent event, which background conditions count as held fixed, when a claim is really about the day and not about the machine. And where it will misfire in practice: the sentences it will downgrade that were actually right, and the way a regulator will read a careful WOULD versus UNKNOWN split as hedging.

Please don't give me a bibliography or a list of who argued what — I can't look any of it up and it wouldn't help my team if I could. Name a distinction if naming it makes the step clearer, but the procedure has to stand on the distinction, not on the source. And write the whole thing as continuous prose, like a long email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. If you need to point at the questions in order just call them step one and step two inside the sentence.
