I am a novelist, and I have spent the last two years reading philosophy of language in an amateur and unsystematic way in order to settle a practical question about my third book — a family saga set across four decades in a Louisiana river parish, in which certain characters speak in the idiom of their place and time, including one racial epithet that I will not spell out here, for reasons that turn out to be part of what I am asking about. My editor has proposed a global substitution: replace the word everywhere with the neutral descriptor, or elide it, on the ground that the term on the page does its damage whoever is nominally speaking it. I have resisted, but I now discover that the views I have assembled in support of my resistance do not obviously cohere, and I would rather find that out from an audit than from a hostile panel at a literary festival in the autumn, where I am to speak on quotation and responsibility. What follows is my position, stated as plainly as I can manage. I do not know which parts of it are load-bearing, and I am deliberately not telling you which one I would prefer to keep.

1. The word and its neutral counterpart make the same contribution to truth conditions. An utterance of the form "he is a [word]" is true exactly when the corresponding neutral predication is true, and nothing of the derogation enters into what is asserted.
2. Nevertheless the two are not interchangeable in my prose. Substituting the neutral term would leave every sentence of the novel equally true and would make the book worse — not merely less vivid, but less accurate, since it would understate what was actually said to and about people in the world I am depicting.
3. The derogatory dimension behaves like a conventional implicature on the usual tests: it is not at issue, it is not removed by negating the sentence, it survives inside the antecedent of a conditional and inside a question, and it projects out of embedded contexts — including, as I read those tests, contexts of reported speech.
4. What the word does on an occasion is not a function of the speaker's intentions. Its force belongs to the practice rather than to the psychology of whoever deploys it: the ironic use, the use in order to expose, and the use in order to wound are the same act, and I resist any account that treats the last as the paradigm and the others as exceptions.
5. Reclamation is real and not a mere truce. When members of the targeted group use the word among themselves, the derogatory content is absent — not waived, not forgiven, not tactically reversed — so that some tokens of the type simply are not slurs.
6. Quotation exempts. In my dialogue the words belong to my characters; I do not assert what they assert, and a reader who meets the word in a character's mouth encounters that character's act and not mine. I have drafted two hundred pages on this assumption.
7. Praise and blame remain individual. Ignorance, upbringing, or good faith can excuse a speaker's use of the word, and I can be held responsible for what I write, or cleared of it, in a way that turns on my own reasons and my own knowledge.

There is also one case on which I have no verdict at all, and I include it because I suspect my principles owe me one: a character who says, of a member of the group the word targets, that she is certainly not a [word] but the best of her neighbours — intending to defend her. I do not want to say that this speaker has slurred, yet I cannot see how to get that result from (1), (3) and (4) without ad hoc machinery.

Please treat this as an audit rather than a lecture. Tell me which pairs of these commitments are genuinely incompatible and which merely look that way, and be explicit about the latter, since I expect a superficial reading would flag most of the set and I want to know where the real fault lines run. Order the genuine conflicts by severity, and for each say what has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs — including what it costs me as a working novelist, since I suspect the fix will land on the manuscript as often as on the theory. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the most, and whether adopting it would leave my editor's proposed substitution looking right after all. I would rather have your reasoning than a list of places to look; I am not in a position to chase references.
