I am drafting comments on a colleague's monograph in the philosophy of biology. She sets out to defend an account of biological function against both selected-effect etiologism of the Millikan–Wright sort and Cummins-style causal-role analysis, yet she never states her positive view in general terms; what she gives instead is a run of verdicts on particular traits, scattered through the chapters. Before I write, I should like to establish what she is in fact committed to.

Her verdicts, as I have collated them:

(i) The healthy human heart has the function of pumping blood, and a neonate's heart with a severe septal defect — which never pumped blood effectively, and whose bearer died within hours — had that same function; it is this that makes it a *malfunctioning* heart rather than a heart with a different function. What such a heart actually does, shunting blood and failing to oxygenate it, is not its function.

(ii) The human appendix has no function. She grants that it does appear to assist in repopulating the gut flora after infection, but she calls this a *service* rather than a function; and she holds that the fermentative work of our ancestors' caeca is a *past function* which did not survive the organ's reduction.

(iii) The non-shattering rachis of domesticated maize has the function of retaining seed on the cob. Selection by early farmers counts as selection for her purposes, and the trait's being lethal to the plant in the wild, together with the crop's persisting only through human intervention, she considers beside the point.

(iv) A genetic toggle switch engineered into E. coli, which reliably does precisely what its designers intended and is fitness-neutral, has a causal role and a *design function*, but not a biological function in the sense in which the heart has one.

(v) The feathers of a flying passerine have two functions, flight and thermoregulation. She sees no principled reason to privilege the more recent of the selection episodes responsible for them.

(vi) A worker honeybee's foraging has a function, notwithstanding that the effect for which it was selected accrues to the queen's reproduction rather than to the worker's own; the bearer's individual fitness is not the required currency.

(vii) On an island that has had no bats for some two hundred thousand years, a moth species' dive response has degraded to the point where it no longer fires even when the moth is played bat ultrasound. She says this trait has no function, and her only gloss is that nothing is now being done by it.

(viii) She attributes to the pupillary light reflex the function of regulating retinal illuminance rather than that of contracting the iris, and argues at length that settling the right description of the trait and of its effect is part of the substance of a function claim, not a preliminary to it.

Two further cases arise in the final chapter, and on neither does she pronounce:

N1. In a vertebrate lineage, a gene encoding a hepatic enzyme duplicated some forty million years ago. Both paralogues are expressed and catalytically competent, and both catalyse the same step; subsequent regulatory change has shifted nearly all the flux through the younger copy, so that deleting the older one — the copy whose ancestral tokens were fixed by selection precisely for catalysing that step — leaves the animal phenotypically normal in every environment the species now occupies.

N2. On a different bat-free island, a moth species' dive response remains fully intact. It fires reliably in the laboratory when bat ultrasound is played, has not been triggered in the wild for perhaps two hundred thousand years, costs the animal very little, and shows no sign of degradation.

What I should like from you:

1. Which of the standard candidate accounts — permissive selected-effect etiologism, its recent-history variant, causal-role analysis, the dispositional account, and goal-contribution or organisational hybrids — survive all eight verdicts, and which single account do you judge her to be running? If you think no one account can accommodate all eight, say which verdict you take me to have misreported, and why.

2. For each surviving account, what does it predict about N1 and N2? Which recorded verdict is the closest precedent for each case, and what principle would she be adopting, wittingly or not, whichever way she went?

3. Your own best guess at her two verdicts, with the considerations that decide it; and the single further case that would most sharply separate the two leading accounts, together with an account of precisely what turns on it.

4. Finally, what does (viii) demand of a theory that the other seven verdicts do not, and which of the survivors can meet that demand?
