I hold the faculty seat on my university's Committee on Governance, which has been charged with recommending a rule for electing the chair of the Senate. The chair is presently chosen by plurality from a single ballot listing all nominees. Four members stood this spring. Because our review was already underway, a graduate assistant in my department persuaded the Senate to collect, alongside the official ballot, an optional ranked-preference ballot; 400 of the 412 voting members completed both. The official count on the full ballot elected A, who had stood as the front-runner and drew nearly all of his first-choice support from the largest of the Senate's four divisions. The assistant's file of the 400 complete orderings reads as follows:

- 160 members: A > B > C > D
- 100 members: B > C > D > A
- 80 members: C > B > D > A
- 60 members: D > C > B > A

Three motions are now before the committee: (i) replace plurality with instant-runoff counting of a ranked ballot; (ii) replace it with a rule that elects whichever candidate defeats every other candidate head to head, with a stated tie-break for the case in which no such candidate exists; (iii) retain plurality unchanged. A fourth member has proposed an approval ballot instead. A fifth has raised instant-runoff's non-monotonicity and its sensitivity to two similar candidates splitting one faction's support; a sixth has asked whether any of the rules on offer is immune to strategic misrepresentation of one's own preferences.

The committee defers on formal matters to a colleague who argues against all three motions on Arrowian grounds. His position, roughly: Arrow's theorem shows that no aggregation procedure can satisfy simultaneously a set of conditions any reasonable person would want; the choice among plurality, instant runoff, Borda, and pairwise rules is therefore a choice among conventions, none uniquely correct; and since these 400 ballots plainly deliver different winners under different rules, our decision is political rather than rational. He proposes instead that the chair be named by a small nominating committee able to deliberate with full information, or, failing that, that we keep plurality, whose defects are at least transparent and familiar.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should do four things. First, set out what these 400 orderings yield under each of the three counting rules on the table; members will contest the arithmetic, so I want it worked through and shown, not asserted. Second, give the committee something it can actually vote on: a definite recommendation — adopt one motion, adopt one with amendments, or reject all three — together with the implementation date you would attach to it relative to the November election, and reasoning capable of moving members who are not yet persuaded by my colleague. Third, engage his argument at its strongest rather than waving it away: whether Arrow's theorem licenses the step from no procedure satisfies all the conditions to the choice among procedures is arbitrary; what the theorem's assumptions and its conclusion actually cover, and how that bears on a problem whose output is a single officeholder rather than a ranking; whether anything in the shape of the Senate's preferences as these ballots reveal it might place us outside those assumptions; and whether there is merit in his nominating-committee fallback. Fourth, if any part of your case depends on a claim about this electorate that 400 optional ranked ballots cannot support — its size, its divisional structure, the composition of future fields of candidates, or the difference between those who ranked and those who did not — flag it as such instead of papering over it.

I am not asking for references or a reading list; I am asking for the argument. Then, in a separate note to me that will not be circulated, state how confident you are in the recommendation you have reached and single out the one point in your reasoning about which you are least sure.
