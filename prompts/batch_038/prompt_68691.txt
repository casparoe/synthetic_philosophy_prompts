I run the science and ideas desk of a British national newspaper. Roughly once a month a story reaches me in which conduct that would be called corruption in London is described by the people involved as ordinary practice, and in which my reporters want the word and I am not certain they are entitled to it. The cases are almost never money in an envelope. They are a professor who sits on the panels that distribute his discipline's public research money and has now funded four of his own former doctoral students in succession, and who says openly that keeping a research school intact is a virtue rather than a racket; a patient charity whose delegates' flights, hotels and conference dinners are paid for by the manufacturer of the drug the charity campaigns for, and whose director maintains that nothing was ever asked in return and that declining such hospitality would simply have been rude; a research network in South Asia that this desk has championed for four years and profiled twice, which allocates equipment, travel grants and co-authorship through personal loyalty to its founder, and against which the complaints now reaching me come from junior members who were passed over.

We have a house rule that I want to keep. We may not print corruption, corrupt, cronyism, gravy train or anything else in that family about an identifiable person or institution unless the copy can name two things: the specific duty or entrusted role that was breached, and the specific party who was not at the table when it was breached and is worse off for it. Note that this points in a different direction from the definition most people who campaign against corruption work from, which asks whether entrusted power has been used for private gain — that is a question about the person doing it, whereas ours is a question about somebody absent. If either of our two things cannot be named, the copy describes the arrangement plainly and lets readers draw their own conclusions. My reporters are not philosophers and have about twenty minutes a case; the final call is mine, but any use of the word about a named living person must be countersigned by our lawyer, whose first question is always what the duty was.

Please write the procedure my reporters could run on their own, containing:

(a) the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and which questions must be settled before any of the others are worth asking;

(b) the point at which the procedure halts and returns "describe, do not name", and the point at which it ceases to be a procedure and hands the decision to me because what remains is a question of values rather than fact — naming, in those places, which values are in conflict;

(c) its treatment of the defence that the practice is normal locally and our standard is not universal. I want a symmetry check built in: before we name a practice elsewhere, the reporter must be able to name the closest equivalent at a British or American institution that we would not call corrupt, and then say what makes the difference matter, or concede that it does not. Tell me honestly whether that check is a real constraint or a rhetorical flourish that any determined reporter can satisfy.

(d) its treatment of gifts carrying no stated expectation. My working assumption is that a recipient's inability to say what was expected of them is not evidence of innocence but the mechanism of the thing, since an unspecified obligation is harder to refuse than a priced one. If you disagree, say so, and build the procedure around your view rather than mine;

(e) whether disclosure cures anything. I suspect that declaring a financial tie can embolden the person who declares it and transfer the work of discounting to a reader who will not do it. If you share that suspicion, the procedure should say what we require beyond disclosure before the word becomes available;

(f) which distinctions have to survive being turned into newsroom instructions and which I can safely drop, and where the procedure will misfire — including the verdicts that are technically correct but will be read as an accusation, and the accusations that will be read as nothing at all.

Then run it on the professor, the charity and the network, and — because I want to see it bite in both directions — on a scientist we have profiled admiringly three times, who sits on a manufacturer's advisory board, who is remunerated partly in share options, and who told us about all of it cheerfully and on the record. I expect the procedure to protect him, and I want to be shown the step that does it. In the network case I expect an answer I do not want: either nameable, which my reporters will say hands a weapon to everyone who has always wished that network gone, or not nameable, which tells the junior members who came to us that our standards apply only to other people's institutions. Do not soften it whichever way it falls.

Two constraints on your answer. I want no bibliography, no reading list, no citations and no invented studies: wherever you are relying on a general claim about how gifts, disclosure or institutions behave, put it as a claim of your own and mark it as a judgment call rather than presenting it as settled evidence, and flag every step that looks mechanical but is not. Write in British English, for someone with twenty minutes, no training in philosophy, and an editor and a lawyer waiting to ask what the duty was.
