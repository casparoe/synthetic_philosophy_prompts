Every year when I teach the time-and-causation unit in my metaphysics seminar somebody reaches for the same line — entropy always increases, so that's the direction time flows in — and the room goes quiet because it sounds like a settled result rather than a thesis. It came up again last week in a different setting: I sit in on an informal reading group with two people from the physics department, and a postdoc used it to shut down a question about whether the second law could ever explain why we remember the past but not the future. His point, roughly, was that if you accept the slogan there is nothing left to explain, and that the grad student who asked the question was just playing with words, since her worry (you need to already know which way time runs before you can say which way entropy goes) is not a real arguement. He's a nice guy and he's better at this stuff than I am, so I don't want to assume he's the one confused. But I've never actually sat down and seperated out what the sentence is claiming, and I have a suspicion that the two halves of it — the "always increases" and the "that's the direction of time" — are doing very different kinds of work.

So here's what I want: a menu of the distinct theses that one sentence could be standing for, sorted by how much work each does. Go at both halves. On the first, I'm thinking about things like which entropy is meant (the textbook thermodynamic one, Boltzmann's, coarse-grained Gibbs versus fine-grained, something informational), which systems the claim is even supposed to cover (isolated only? subsystems that dump entropy elsewhere? the universe as a whole, and is that last one a system with a well-defined entropy at all, especially once gravity is in the picture), and whether "always" means exceptionless or merely so overwhelming that exceptions are irrelevant in practice. On the second half, spell out the different ways entropy increase could be hooked to temporal direction — as the thing that fixes which end of time is the future, as a mere reliable marker of a direction settled some other way, or as a claim about which of two time-symmetric descriptions is the one we should call "the way things happen."

For each reading tell me straight whether it's a platitude nobody disputes, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's false or at least unsupported, and — the part I actually care about — whether it would license the postdoc's dismissal. I'd also like to know which reading the student's circularity objection really bites against, and whether there's some reading that survives it while still being interesting enough to be worth saying. I'm on a search committee all semester so I have about forty minutes a day for anything not on my syllabus: give me a numbered list I can turn into a handout, keep the physics to what's needed for the distinctions, and skip the primer on what entropy is. I know what entropy is.
