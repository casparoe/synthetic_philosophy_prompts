I direct research computing for a consortium of fourteen public universities and institutes that jointly fund a shared accelerator cluster and a federated storage system. Allocation is currently handled twice a year by an administrative review panel: investigators write proposals, the panel scores them, hours are awarded, unused allocations lapse. The predictable pathologies have duly appeared — hoarding in the weeks before each review window, systematically padded requests, a queue measured in months, and an untracked black market in informal trades between labs that has effectively become our real allocation mechanism. Two member institutions now want to move to an internal market with transferable credits and periodic auctions. I am skeptical: for most of what we allocate, I suspect a market would manufacture a spurious price signal out of numbers we could compute directly from capacity data, and that what we actually need is rigorous accounting in physical units (device-hours, joules, gigabyte-months) combined with explicit, publicly stated priorities.

The board has asked my office to produce a decision procedure, to be applied resource-class by resource-class, that determines which allocation regime a given class should operate under. I would like you to write that procedure.

Fix the menu of regimes in advance; the procedure selects one per resource class, or specifies a hybrid of exactly two and justifies the split:

1. Continued administrative review with quantitative scoring.
2. Physical-unit accounting with centrally set priorities and no prices of any kind.
3. Centrally computed shadow prices — valuations derived from the constraint structure of the whole system (bottlenecks, capacity limits, complementary inputs) and revised periodically.
4. An internal market with transferable credits, bidding, and secondary trade.
5. Iteratively negotiated indicative prices, in which members propose adjustments and a coordinator revises until the accounts balance.

The procedure must be grounded in an explicit account of what the socialist calculation debate — Mises and Hayek against Lange and the market-socialist replies — actually establishes, rather than in a summary of who said what. In particular, it must distinguish and test separately for four failure modes that the debate tends to blur together: failure of computation (the allocation is not tractable from the data), failure of information (the relevant knowledge is dispersed and never reaches whoever computes), failure of incentive (the knowledge exists and could be reported, but reporting it is against the reporter's interest), and failure of discovery (the relevant facts do not exist until rival bids, exits, and refusals have been made, so no amount of prior data collection substitutes for the process). Every question in your procedure should say which of these it is probing for. It must also confront the strongest version of the Hayekian objection to regime 3 — that a valuation solved out of technical coefficients is not the same object as a price discovered through actual exchange and actual exit — and state when that difference matters for us and when it is merely rhetorical. And it must handle the case, which is common for us, where the good has near-zero marginal cost of use once it exists, so that scarcity lives in the capacity rather than in the use; explain what a price could or could not convey under those conditions.

Format the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions. Each must be phrased so that a member of my staff can answer it from data we already collect or could collect within a week, and you must say what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence settles it, and who is positioned to supply that evidence. Mark the points at which the procedure terminates in "leave the current regime in place" and the points at which it hands the decision up to the board because the issue is one of values or of fairness among member institutions rather than of fact. For each question, also note whether answering it wrongly is self-correcting or self-reinforcing — I am especially worried about regimes that generate the appearance of the scarcity that justifies them, so hoarding that reads as demand should not license more of the same mechanism.

Then run the procedure end to end on three resource classes, giving a verdict and a one-sentence rationale for each:

- Training hours on our largest nodes: lumpy, rival in use, with plausible substitutes at other national sites, and requested in quantities that depend heavily on how the request is scored.
- Long-term archival storage: near-zero marginal cost of use, very high migration and refresh cost, demand that grows monotonically and never releases, and value that is realized years after allocation.
- Time on a single cryo-electron microscope: extremely heterogeneous urgency of use that only the user can assess, no substitute within the consortium, and a waiting list measured in months.

At least one of these three must come out against my stated preference for physical-unit accounting plus central priorities. Say so plainly and defend it rather than softening the verdict or hedging it into a hybrid that flatters me.

Practical constraints: my office is three people, none of us economists; the procedure must be runnable on one resource class in a single working day; and whatever it produces must be explainable to skeptical faculty in one meeting without invoking economic theory they will not accept. Keep the total deliverable to a few thousand words, with a summary card at the top that a staff member could follow without reading the rest.

No bibliography, no citations, and no quotations from any source. You may name a thinker in order to point at a position, but every step must be justified on its substance, as though your reader had never encountered the literature. Do not invent references, figures, or historical claims; where you need a number from us, describe the measurement rather than supplying one. Close with a short section stating what this procedure cannot settle at all, and the two or three ways you expect it to misfire most badly in an organization like ours.
