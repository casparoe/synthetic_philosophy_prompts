I'm auditing a graduate seminar on language and social cognition, and for the last two sessions we've been working through Hofstadter's satirical essay "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" from *Metamagical Themas* — the piece written in the voice of an exasperated defender of tradition, imagining an English in which race rather than sex is the grammatically marked category. In that counterfactual the third-person singular pronouns are "whe" and "ble", the unmarked generic word for a human being is *white* (hence chairwhite, mailwhite, forewhite, middlewhite, oneupuwhiteship), the reformers derided as "black libbers" and "negrists" want to substitute *person* for it, and their leading spokeswoman is one Niss Moses, author of a *Handbook of Nonracist Writing*. The rhetorical strategy is easy enough to state at a slogan level: transplant the whole apparatus of defenses routinely offered for generic masculine usage onto race, and let the reader's revulsion do the argumentative work. What I can't articulate cleanly, and what our discussion never got past, is precisely what the transplant is licensed to prove — and one structural feature of the counterfactual seems to me to complicate the answer.

The complication is this. The narrator treats the reformers' vocabulary as a fad: he dismisses "racism" as an "awkward neologism, constructed by analogy with the well-established term" sexism, one that does not sit well in the ear. But in the actual world of the mid-1980s the situation was roughly inverted — *sexism* was itself only a couple of decades old and had been consolidated by the women's movement, whereas *racism* and its cognates had been part of ordinary political vocabulary since the 1930s. So part of what explains the fictional audience's obliviousness is that they lack a settled concept-word under which to gather the instances of the harm; they can't see the pattern because they have no name for it. Hofstadter's real readers had the name available in the race case and were still, he thinks, blind in the gender case. That makes me suspect the thought experiment is asymmetrically loaded: if concept availability is doing explanatory work in the counterfactual, then the race version is not the gender version with the nouns swapped, and the revulsion it elicits may be tracking that extra ingredient rather than grammatical marking as such. Hofstadter seems aware of the crossing — his narrator mocks the suggestion "to abandon the natural distinction along racial lines, and to replace it with a highly unnatural one along sexual lines" — but I can't tell whether that awareness rescues the analogy or concedes my point. Please explain whether the objection has any force, and in doing so disentangle two mechanisms I keep running together: the claim that an inclusive generic carries no trace of the group it names (the narrator insists that, used inclusively, the word *white* "has no connotations whatsoever of race"), and the claim that a community cannot notice a regularity for which it has no term. Are these two independent ways a default assumption can be sustained, so that removing one leaves the other intact, or is the second parasitic on the first?

A second and related request. Much of the essay's bite comes from importing the standard rebuttals to nonsexist-language reform almost verbatim: the appeal to the beauty and poetry of traditional phrasing; the redundancy point that in most contexts it is self-evident when the word is being used inclusively; the phonological deflection that the marked suffix is not even pronounced the way the standalone word is; the assurance that obviously the chairwhite of the League of Black Voters will be black, so no one need think twice; and the ridicule of proposed replacements — pe, tey, they, whe-or-ble, bl/whe — as ungainly monstrosities. Please sort these imported defenses into (i) those the race/gender analogy genuinely defeats, where accepting the defense in the gender case while rejecting it in the race case is flatly inconsistent, and (ii) those the analogy merely renders socially uncomfortable while leaving logically intact, because nothing about the transplant gives a reason to think them false. For each, say what the operative difference is. Then tell me where you would locate the essay's real target — a semantic claim about what generic terms mean, a pragmatic claim about what choosing a marked term implicates over an available neutral one, or a psychological claim about which exemplars hearers actually summon — since my impression is that the narrator deliberately blurs all three, and I can't work out whether the satire is stronger or weaker for having done so.
