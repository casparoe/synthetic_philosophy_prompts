Treat yourself as a specialist in American pragmatism and in just-war/military-obligation debates, not a general explainer. No bibliography at the end please, I'm not going to chase secondary literature and you don't have the web anyway — I want your reading, argued for.

Here's the first chunk, from fairly early in the essay:

"Modern war is so expensive that we feel trade to be a better avenue to plunder; but modern man inherits all the innate pugnacity and all the love of glory of his ancestors. Showing war's irrationality and horror is of no effect upon him. The horrors make the fascination. War is the strong life; it is life in extremis; war-taxes are the only ones men never hesitate to pay; as the budgets of all nations show us."

And here's the second, a few pages on, after his tour through the Iliad, Melos, Alexander and Rome:

"Such was the gory nurse that trained soldiers to cohesiveness. We inherit the warlike type; and for most of the capacities of heroism that the human race is full of we have to thank this cruel history. Dead men tell no tales, and if there were any tribes of other type than this they have left no survivors. Our ancestors have bred pugnacity into our bone and marrow, and thousands of years of peace won't breed it out of us."

What I want out of you: (1) the line "The horrors make the fascination" — is that a claim about human nature or a deliberate inversion, and does it contradict or complete the sentence before it? My hunch is that James is saying pacifist propaganda is self-defeating, that the horror-catalogue is what supplies the draw it means to dispel, but I want you to test that against the text rather than take my word for it. (2) The "gory nurse" passage is a just-so evolutionary story, and it announces its own unfalsifiability ("Dead men tell no tales, and if there were any tribes of other type than this they have left no survivors"). Does James need it to be true, or is it a licensing device for the real thesis — that peace, by itself, cannot disarm anyone? (3) Here's the crux I actually care about, and I think it's usually skipped over: call the two options substitution and transposition. On substitution, James wants to keep the martial temperament intact and merely point it at civilian objects, so his proposed conscription is a militia in all but uniform. On transposition, he wants the strenuous discipline to slowly atrophy the pugnacity it's exploiting. The essay's own rhetoric seems to want both, and I want you to say which is more defensible and what specifically breaks if you commit to only one. (4) Then bring it down to the policy cases: my colleague insists the Finnish civilian-service track for objectors is deliberately longer than the military one in order to deter them — treat that as his claim, not established fact — and ask whether such a track is a "moral equivalent" in James's sense or a penalty dressed up as one, and whether James's scheme looks anything like the Swiss militia habit (citizen keeps his rifle at home, comes back for refresher courses for years) or is really a separate institution meant to replace it. (5) Small one: "war-taxes are the only ones men never hesitate to pay" — what does that imply about the difference between a money tax and a blood tax for the legitimacy of a volunteer force?
