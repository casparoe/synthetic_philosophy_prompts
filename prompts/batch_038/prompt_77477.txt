I am the outgoing president of a national professional association of surgeons, and in October I will give the address at the ceremony marking the hundredth year of our code of conduct. The occasion has an awkward centerpiece. For eighty-nine years, Article 9 of that code forbade a member to give testimony against a fellow member concerning the treatment of a patient without the prior consent of the association's ethics committee; in practice it was understood as a rule of silence, and it was enforced socially for decades after it ceased to be enforced formally. Our council struck Article 9 last spring and replaced it with a mandatory reporting requirement backed by referral to the state licensing board. I voted for the change. I also spent thirty-one years keeping the silence, and on two occasions that I can name I knew of a colleague's error and said nothing.

Our own historians will tell you that the article did not fall because anyone was persuaded. Patient-safety data accumulated for thirty years without moving the membership; what moved it was that our two largest malpractice insurers began writing substantial premium discounts for institutions with disclosure programs, and a council elected by members who pay those premiums followed the money. The insurers changed the interests. The arguments that were actually made — by the bereaved families we have invited to speak at our annual meeting for six years running — did not change a single vote, though they changed how people talked afterward.

My own view, which you should treat as a hypothesis to test rather than a premise, is that Appiah is broadly right that a norm of this kind dies when it ceases to be honorable rather than when it is shown to be wrong, and that there are two distinct ways for that to happen: displacement, in which the same loyalty that sustained the silence is reattached to a new object, so that speaking about a colleague becomes what a loyal colleague does; and reclassification, in which the practice is moved out of the register of honor entirely and into the register of compliance, where it is judged by outcomes and audited by outsiders. Reclassification is faster and looks more durable, but it yields conformity without conviction and tends to snap back once the auditors leave. What our council has chosen, deliberately and with the insurers' encouragement, is reclassification. October is the moment at which I would like to attempt displacement instead, knowing that the institution has already committed to the other route and that a good part of the room will hear me as dressing a compliance mandate in the language of honor.

The room: about three hundred surgeons, perhaps a fifth of them over seventy, who remember Article 9 as a real obligation rather than a cover-up and will resent being told they were wrong; a dozen residents and students who regard the old code as a scandal; the six families who have addressed our meetings, seated in the front row at the incoming president's request, who are owed something better than a mechanism; and a scattering of hospital administrators and journalists.

Please write the address. It should run about eight minutes when spoken. It must satisfy the following, which are in some tension with one another:

1. It may not tell the room that the old silence was immoral, since that is precisely what reactivates the defense of honor; nor may it exonerate them. Find a way to hold both, and hold it for the families as well as for the men who kept the silence.

2. Take a position on Williams without naming him: on whether a person had a reason to act only if there was some route from what actually moved him to the act in question, and on whether blame reaches across a distance in which the agent's situation was structurally unavailable to us. Let that position determine what I say about my own two silences. I want it clear whether I am blaming myself, excusing myself, or doing something that is neither.

3. Displacement rather than reclassification: the speech should offer a new object of loyalty, not a new rule. Reporting should be made to sound like something one does for a colleague rather than for a record.

4. Do not conceal the insurers' role, and do not sneer at it. Decide how to say that a change in interests can carry a change in norms without reducing the whole affair to accounting.

5. No name-dropping of any kind. Appiah and Williams must not appear; their content should be load-bearing and invisible. Everything said must be followable by the six people in the front row.

6. End on something a surgeon could plausibly repeat to a colleague in a corridor the next morning — one sentence, and not a slogan.

After the address, add a note of no more than two hundred and fifty words stating which of the two mechanisms you relied on at the three hardest points in the text, and identifying where you judged that the speech had to fail one of my requirements in order to meet another.
