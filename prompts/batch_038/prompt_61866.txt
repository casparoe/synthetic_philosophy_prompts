Two colleagues in my behavioral economics group have asked me to referee an argument about a pricing experiment we ran in our own building's café, and I have not been able to settle it.

Setup: for six weeks the café sold two coffee sizes and logged roughly 9,000 purchases, each matched to a staff badge, so we can follow individuals rather than aggregates. On half the days the large cost 60 cents more than the small; on the other half the two were the same price. Everyone could afford either size on either kind of day. We also ran a two-question exit survey at the register.

(1) Mira's position is that the log is the whole of the evidence: utility is nothing over and above whatever ranking of options is consistent with the records, so a badge-holder who took the large on 68 percent of her days simply prefers the large, and anyone who says those customers were really happier with the small is describing nothing at all. (2) Ted says our own log refutes her: about 300 badge-holders took the small when the large was the cheaper option and the large when the prices were equal, and since both were affordable on both kinds of day, no single ranking fits those people — so the records do not define a utility function, and the content must be coming from somewhere other than choice. (3) Mira replies that this is mis-description, not inconsistency: on discount days the large was chalked up as 'today's special' and handed over in a different cup, and a number of customers told us they avoid deals on principle, so the thing chosen was not the same object as the large on the other days. (4) Ted says that move saves the theory only by making it unbreakable — any log whatever can be rendered consistent by re-describing what was chosen, and a framework compatible with every dataset is compatible with nothing in particular. (5) Mira answers that it does break, since for any fixed description some logs (a customer alternating between two identical options at identical prices) cannot be rationalized, which is precisely why the consistency axioms are worth testing; and Ted is welcome to his private satisfaction, but he owes an account of how he would ever detect it. (6) Ted's rejoinder is that his reading earns its keep: the badge-holders who cycle are concentrated among those who marked the survey item about being torn, and a purely choice-based account can note the cycles after the fact but never predict who will produce them.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — where each of them is right, where they are merely talking past one another, and what technical point one or both of them has gotten wrong or glossed over. Then commit: in a dataset like ours, does utility mean anything beyond the options people chose? Take a definite position and argue for it, rather than laying out the available readings for me to choose among.
