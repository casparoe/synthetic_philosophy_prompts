So I'm in a philosophy-of-cosmology reading group and last week the whole multiverse discussion — string landscape, eternal inflation, that stuff — got killed in about ten seconds when one of the physics postdocs said some version of "if it can't be tested, it's not science." Everyone nodded, me included, and we moved on to the next paper. I've been stewing becouse I'm now pretty sure that one sentence did at least three different jobs in that room and I can't tell them apart.

Here's what he actually argued, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) no possible observation could rule out the multiverse, since any anomaly can be absorbed by saying some other pocket has different constants; (2) therefore the multiverse isn't a scientific hypothesis at all; (3) therefore people spending their careers on it are wasting department resources. Worth noting that the same guy defends inflation, which as far as I can tell is accepted mostly because it handles the horizon and flatness problems and because nobody's got a competitor that works as well — not because anyone has ever detected another pocket universe.

What I want is a menu of the distinct precise claims that slogan could be standing for, laid out numbered so I could print it and bring it next week. For each reading, spell out: what kind of testability is being demanded (direct detection? indirect observable consequences? falsifiable in principle vs. in practice? testable with today's instruments vs. testable by some civilization in five hundred years?), what exactly is being excluded from the club (a single hypothesis? a whole research program? a person's claim to a job?), and what work the word "science" is doing (a line between meaningful and meaningless? a claim about whether there's any epistemic warrant at all? a methodological rule of thumb about how to spend effort? a sociological label for what people in science departments happen to do?). Then give each one a verdict: platitude nobody would deny, substantive and defensible, substantive and false, or just incoherent. And tell me which readings actually license step (2) and which license step (3) — I suspect they're not the same readings — plus which one he was probably leaning on, given how fast the room agreed.

Two extra things I care about. First, a self-exoneration test: for each reading, say whether it also damns inflation, or dark matter, or many-worlds, or anything else this guy clearly accepts. I want to know if any version of his principle is actually consistent with his own commitments rather than just convenient. Second, I've started privately calling the move the falsifiability shuffle — testability treated as a bright metaphysical line when someone proposes something you dislike, and as a mere preference about research priorities when someone proposes something you like. Tell me whether that's a real pattern with a describable structure or just me being annoyed, and whether there's a reading of the slogan that's immune to it.

Please no reading list and no history of who said it first — I can't chase references right now, I just want the distinctions. Labels for positions are fine if they're standard, but don't build the answer around who-said-what.
