Right, so I've been asked to put my views on what disease actually *is* down in writing before next week's journal club, so people can take a proper run at them, and I'd rather find out from you first whether the package hangs together at all. Here's where I've landed:

1. I'm a Boorsean naturalist. Disease is a descriptive notion: a statistically subnormal part-function relative to a reference class, where functions are fixed by what parts have been selected to do — their contribution to survival and reproduction. Whether something is a disease is settled by the biology, not by how anyone feels about it.

2. But I do think a big slice of what gets called overdiagnosis is a genuine conceptual mistake rather than just a policy worry. Screen-detected cancers that would never have caused anyone any trouble, prediabetes, subclinical thyroid numbers — we've been letting statistical deviation from a norm masquerade as something being wrong with the person. My view is that a condition shouldn't count as a disease unless it's actually bad for the person who has it, in some fairly robust sense.

3. On disability I'm a social model person: impairment is the bodily fact, disability is what a badly designed society does to people with impairments. I know that sounds like it ought to be at war with (1), and I've always assumed it isn't, but I couldn't really defend that if pushed.

4. I think Kingma's point about environments is right and I want to embrace it: you can't state a part's function without fixing an environment, so myopia counts against a reading-and-screens environment in a way it wouldn't against a hunter-gatherer one, and the relevant haemoglobin threshold isnt the same at sea level and at altitude. I take that as strengthening naturalism rather than threatening it, because which environment is the relevant one is still an empirical question.

5. For anything that allocates resources — insurance, disability benefits, occupational health, fit notes — I want a single uniform list, applied identically to everybody. Letting disease judgements track someone's job, home or lifestyle is a recipe for unfairness, and for the better-off getting their own lives classed as the normal case.

6. I think the history of psychiatry has to be taken seriously here: drapetomania, and homosexuality as a disorder in mid-century American psychiatry, show that diagnostic categories can carry social values. But I'd say the right lesson is that those rested on bad functional claims — nothing was actually malfunctioning — not that the whole enterprise is value-saturated.

7. Methodologically I'm a systematiser: I think you settle this kind of dispute by looking at how the terms are actually used by clinicians and patients and finding the account that makes best sense of that usage, rather than stipulating a tidy concept and imposing it.

So — does that cohere? I want an audit, not a lecture. Tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do, rank the real ones by how serious they are, and for each say what I'd have to give up and what giving it up costs me. I'm particularly interested in whether any of my fixes borrow something the rest of the package rules out. Then tell me the single cheapest revision that would clean up the most damage. Please don't hand me a reading list or citations, I just want your reasoning, and write the whole thing in British English.
