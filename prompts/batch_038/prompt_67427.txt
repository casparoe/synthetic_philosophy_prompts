I spent thirty-eight years running a small experimental laboratory, chiefly in protein biophysics, and I have been retired for four years. Since retiring I have taken up moral philosophy more seriously than I expected, and I have been working my way through the long-running dispute between those who hold that morality can be captured in a finite stock of principles and those who hold that it cannot. I also still referee the occasional manuscript in my old field. Over the years I have come to regard the referee report as a wonderfully honest genre: it is the one place where a reader, under institutional cover, states precisely what a piece of work does and does not establish.

I would like you to write me a parody referee report on a moral-philosophy manuscript. The comedy must come entirely from getting the philosophy right. A moral philosopher who reads it should recognize the actual dispute and find the report fair to both sides of it; a non-specialist should find it funny as a document.

Please adopt the role of an experienced journal referee who is also a professional philosopher thoroughly at home in the contemporary debate over whether moral thought requires principles. Take a moment to think the piece through before drafting: decide which horn of the dilemma you believe the authors are impaled on, and make every major comment serve that reading. What you hand me should be the report alone, with no prefatory explanation of the debate.

Treat the following particulars as given, and invent everything else. The report is a second-round assessment of a revised manuscript submitted to a double-blind journal; you may name both the journal and the manuscript, but they must be fictitious. The authors claim to have formulated a single, finite, exceptionless principle of right action together with a complete inventory of the conditions under which ordinary reasons lose their force; they have implemented the principle as a decision procedure and report that it classifies correctly all 4,112 cases in their test bank. At the first round the inventory was short. In the revision it has grown enormously, and the authors now include, as an appendix, the same inventory re-sorted by a different criterion.

The report should include, in this order: a summary of the manuscript as the referee understands it; a general assessment; numbered major comments; a handful of minor comments; confidential comments to the editor; and a final recommendation. Somewhere in the general assessment there must be a paragraph of genuine, unironic praise identifying what principles do accomplish even if exceptionless codification fails, so that the report is not a hatchet job.

Among the major comments I would like to see these pressures brought to bear, developed as a referee would develop them rather than announced as theses. First, the growth of the inventory across revisions, and what the referee takes that trajectory to mean about whether the principle is doing any work. Second, the status of the test bank: the referee should notice how it was constructed, observe that the authors report no cases the principle had not already seen, and ask for something like a held-out set. Third, a demand that the authors state in advance what result would have obliged them to abandon the principle. Fourth, at least two counterexamples of the referee's own devising, and these should not be mere exceptions: in at least one, a consideration that counts in favor of an act in one situation must count against it in another, and the referee should explain why this is a more damaging phenomenon than the principle's having outliers. Please invent your counterexamples rather than reaching for the most familiar ones in the literature. Fifth, the possibility that the principle, once fully qualified, collapses into an instruction to do whatever is right.

The minor comments should be recognizably trivial and technical in the way real ones are: an undefined term introduced three sections before it is defined, a table whose caption does not match its contents, a supplementary file that compromises anonymity.

The confidential comments to the editor are where the piece should quietly turn: the referee should be seen relying, in his own reasoning about the manuscript, on general norms of assessment that he has just spent four pages denying the authors the right to appeal to. This must be left for the reader to notice. The referee should never comment on it, never name it, and never step outside the genre. No winking anywhere: the report is to be entirely straight-faced, in the flat, courteous, mildly irritated register of a real one.

Two hard constraints. Do not cite, name, or allude to any real philosopher, book, article, or school of thought by name, and include no references or reading list of any kind; the philosophy has to be carried by the argument, not by attribution. And keep the whole thing between 900 and 1,100 words.

End the report with a single line in the exact form "Recommendation: " followed by precisely one of Accept, Minor revisions, Major revisions, or Reject, chosen so that the report's own reasoning supports it.
