I'm building a taxonomy of why certain markets strike us as repugnant, and I want to stress-test it. Here are seven candidate mechanisms:

1. Degradation — pricing the good changes what the good is, so the market transaction delivers something other than what the non-market relation delivered.
2. Hollow consent — the seller's background options are so poor that their agreement carries little or no justificatory weight.
3. Exploitation — the buyer gains precisely from the seller's poor options, and prices the transaction accordingly.
4. Corruption of the parties — transacting alters the participants' motives or character for the worse, independently of the outcome.
5. Expressive harm — the transaction says something demeaning about the good or about the parties, whether or not anyone ends up worse off.
6. Misallocation — the market assigns the good by willingness to pay rather than by need or desert.
7. Erosion — today's market changes social conditions so that tomorrow's transactions are less free, or the good becomes harder to obtain outside the market.

Cases: (A) selling a kidney to a licensed broker; (B) paying a scalper three times face value for concert tickets; (C) selling your vote for five hundred dollars; (D) a hardware store quintupling the price of bottled water and plywood after a hurricane; (E) commercial surrogacy; (F) paying for a private cell in a public prison.

Three things, in this order. First, score each mechanism from 0 to 1 for how strongly it is present in each case, as a six-by-seven grid, with no commentary yet. Second, for case (A) alone, decompose the felt repugnance into fractions across the seven mechanisms that sum to 1, and state which single entry in that decomposition you are most confident about and which one you suspect is doing borrowed work — that is, is really a proxy for another mechanism on the list. Third, say which one mechanism you would keep if you could retain only one as a general explanation of market repugnance, and then name which of the six cases that choice gets wrong and in which direction.

Treat mechanisms 2 and 3 as genuinely distinct even though they sound alike; the point of the exercise is to find where they come apart rather than where they overlap.
