Working on a draft of an essay about the old argument over whether Black art should be propaganda — Du Bois insisting that all art is propaganda and always will be, Locke pushing back that the real choice is art or propaganda, on the grounds that pleading is a posture of deference. I think both of them are arguing about the wrong thing and I want someone to tell me if my replacement actually holds up. Here's the draft, rough, please be blunt:

**1. The received framing.** The debate is usually set up as a disagreement about cost. Du Bois accepts the cost (aesthetic compromise) because the political work is urgent and nothing gets made otherwise. Locke refuses it, because a work that pleads has already conceded something about the standing of the person doing the pleading. Both sides are treated as accepting the same schema: a work has a political job, the job degrades the work to some degree, and the question is whether the degradation is worth it.

**2. My claim.** The schema assumes one audience whose beliefs the work is supposed to move. Unpack that audience and the single demand becomes two demands that pull in opposite directions on the same textual features. Call it the double-audience constraint: a work of this kind is always facing at least an audience that already accepts what it asserts and an audience that denies it, and those two audiences are not differently-weighted instances of one target. They're targets with incompatible requirements.

**3. Why the requirements conflict.** Take the features that reliably move a denying audience: explicit moral legibility, dignified protagonists, an ending where the wrong is visibly a wrong. Those same features, received by an audience that already knows the wrong is a wrong, don't land as knowledge. They land as the work performing its own defensibility — which is exactly the pleading posture Locke objects to. So Locke's objection isn't wrong, it's just misfiled: the problem isn't that propaganda corrupts art, it's that propaganda aimed at two audiences at once can satisfy at most one of them. Conversely the features that avoid pleading — irony, unresolved endings, dense interiority, formal difficulty — presuppose the uptake they're supposed to produce. A denying audience doesn't experience ambiguity as ambiguity; it experiences it as absence of a claim, and files the work under entertainment.

**4. What that does to Du Bois's slogan.** On this reading, all-art-is-propaganda isn't a prescription to instrumentalize. It's a claim about reception conditions: there is no neutral setting, because which audience you're propagandizing is fixed by who's looking and what they already believe. The art-for-art's-sake position is itself propaganda addressed to a particular audience. So the live question isn't whether a work should be propaganda but for whom it is one.

**5. A taxonomy of failures.** (a) Pleading works: aimed at the deniers, cost absorbed by the in-group audience. (b) Sanctuary works: aimed at the in-group, politically inert outside it, and arguably worse than inert, since from outside they read as confirmation of the separatism charge. (c) Double-coded works, the interesting class: one feature doing opposite work for the two audiences without either being told which it's getting. My candidate mechanism: depict an ordinary act done with unremarkable total competence. For the denying audience that's a counterexample to a stereotype, i.e. belief-change. For the in-group audience it isn't an argument at all, it's just a picture of a person; the fact that it functions as an argument for someone else is invisible from inside. Recognition-work and belief-work from the same sentence, no compromise required.

**6. The part I don't believe.** (c) seems to need the in-group audience to not notice the counterexample function. But any reader with the relevant history will notice — that's practically the definition of having the history. Once noticed, the work is a pleading work again, just a self-conscious one. So maybe (c) is empty and the taxonomy collapses to two categories, which would gut the essay, since (c) is where all the interesting cases live. My patch is that noticeability comes in degrees and (c) is populated by works low on it, but I think that's a dodge and I can't make it anything better.

What I want comments on, in this order: is §3 a real constraint or am I just re-describing an ordinary tradeoff in audience-theory vocabulary? Does my Locke in §1 and §3 survive contact with the actual Locke, or have I turned him into a theorist of reception he wasn't? Is the §4 reading of Du Bois's slogan defensible or is it a stretch that only works because I ignore the explicitly programmatic stuff he says in the same place? And on §6, commit — either tell me the patch can be made to work and how, or tell me (c) is empty and I should restructure around two categories. I'd rather get one definite verdict with reasons than a list of ways it could go, and I'm not looking for places to go read up on this, just your own judgment on whether the machinery works.
