I run a small forecasting unit inside a commodity trading house — eight analysts — and for two years we have operated an internal prediction market on operational questions (will this vessel be delayed, will this unit trip, will the regulator publish before quarter end), settled in cash at modest stakes. On Thursday night I had a three-hour argument about it with a friend of mine who runs our model validation team, and I want an independent assessment before I put anything in writing.

My proposal is a coherence audit. Each week, every analyst is offered a fixed menu of binary bets drawn from questions already live in the market, one at a time on screen, stakes capped at £25 each, with their running net position displayed throughout. For each question they state the odds at which they would take either side. The audit then does what I take the Dutch book argument to license in both directions: if the system can assemble from those stated odds a combination of bets that guarantees the analyst a net loss, the analyst is flagged as incoherent, since their numbers cannot be probabilities; and if no such combination exists, their numbers are probabilities, and — the part I lean on hardest — the same result tells us they are demonstrably expected utility maximisers, so the desk is entitled to feed their stated odds directly into our own models.

I also argued that running the menu one bet at a time is strictly stronger than presenting the portfolio all at once, on the grounds that every bet in a sure-loss combination must have been individually acceptable to the agent, so individual acceptances compose. She said this was the weakest link in my case, and I never understood why.

Her objections, as fairly as I can put them:

1. The result says nothing about real money, because nobody is obliged to accept any bet; the flagged analyst loses nothing and the clean analyst gains nothing, and the theorem merely describes a hypothetical bookie holding a free option on my analysts' dispositions.
2. Even if it were practical, credences cannot be read off betting quotients, because what an odds quotation reveals is belief and attitude to money mixed together and not separately identifiable.
3. Escaping a Dutch book is not the same as being rational: an analyst who declines the entire menu cannot be booked and would pass my audit trivially.

I think the first misreads what the theorem is for and the third is cheap, but I could not dislodge the second, and she kept returning to some distinction between a package of bets and a sequence of bets that I now suspect matters and cannot reconstruct.

Please write me an assessment, in British English, in the following sections and roughly these lengths:

**What each of us was claiming** (~300 words). A neutral reconstruction of my proposal and of her three objections, making explicit the premises that neither of us actually stated.

**Where my case fails** (~450 words). Every point at which my argument rests on something these results do not deliver. Say which premise is doing which piece of work, and do not soften it for my benefit.

**Which of her objections land** (~350 words). Take them in turn and say for each whether it is a genuine refutation, a real limitation of my design, or a misunderstanding of what a coherence argument is in the business of showing. I expect at least one of the two I dismissed to survive.

**The package and the sequence** (~300 words). Reconstruct what she was getting at; say whether the one-at-a-time protocol is stronger than, weaker than, or not comparable to presenting the portfolio; and explain what the running-total display does to the argument, given that the analyst's position is not the same at bet eleven as at bet one.

**What an audit could honestly deliver** (~300 words). If coherence is worth checking at all in a setting with capped stakes and a finite weekly menu, what a defensible version would look like, what it would certify, and what it would have to stop short of claiming.

**Bottom line** (~100 words). The single sentence I should have said on Thursday night, and the one claim I must not write into the proposal document.
