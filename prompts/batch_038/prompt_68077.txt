I am a third-year graduate student in political philosophy, and in a few weeks I am giving a talk to an interdisciplinary seminar on space governance whose members are mostly aerospace engineers and lawyers rather than philosophers. My chapter concerns the ethics of space colonization, and the episode I have chosen to anchor it is the roughly forty-year sequence running from the 1979 Moon Agreement through the 2015 U.S. Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act to the Artemis Accords of October 2020. The bare facts, as I will state them to my audience and as I would ask you to take them as given: the Moon Agreement, which entered into force in 1984, would have declared the Moon and its natural resources the common heritage of mankind and required the parties to establish an "international regime" governing exploitation once exploitation became feasible; it has seventeen parties and has never been ratified by any state capable of launching humans into orbit on its own. The 2015 American statute went the other way, providing that a U.S. citizen engaged in commercial recovery of an asteroid resource or space resource is entitled to possess, own, transport, use, and sell what is obtained, while disclaiming, as a sense of Congress, any assertion of sovereignty, jurisdiction, or ownership over any celestial body. Luxembourg enacted an analogous statute in 2017 and the United Arab Emirates shortly afterward. The Artemis Accords, first signed by eight states on October 13, 2020 and by dozens more since, are non-binding principles that affirm resource extraction as compatible with the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, commit signatories to publish the nature and location of their activities, and introduce the device of safety zones around those activities; Russia and China have objected that this is appropriation by other means and have pressed instead for a negotiated multilateral regime. I am not asking you to check any of this, and I do not want a bibliography or citations — I would much rather you reason from the concepts than guess at references.

What I want is a disciplined case analysis of this episode through the lens of Lockean initial acquisition: the idea that mixing one's labor with unowned resources generates a property right in them, constrained by the proviso that enough and as good be left in common for others, and then modified by the thought that money and consent can lift the proviso's bite. Set that against the common-heritage conception, on which the resource is held jointly and cannot be unilaterally withdrawn from the common stock without a collective decision about sharing the proceeds. Please do the mapping carefully and explicitly before evaluating anything: who is the Lockean appropriator in this episode (a private firm, a state acting through authorization and continuing supervision of its nationals, or both at once); what is the state of nature here, given that the domain is already chartered by treaty and the non-appropriation article of the Outer Space Treaty forecloses sovereignty but arguably not extraction; whether labor-mixing describes what actually happens when the overwhelming majority of the value-added is terrestrial capital, engineering, and risk-bearing rather than anything done to the asteroid; and what the theory makes of safety zones, which look like de facto exclusion without formal appropriation.

I also want you to engage two hypotheses of mine, which I would like tested rather than flattered. First, I suspect the whole dispute about whether the proviso is satisfied turns on the wrong stock. It is routinely said that a single metallic near-Earth asteroid contains more platinum-group material than humanity has ever mined, so that taking a thousand of them leaves enough and as good for everyone; but the physically abundant good is the ore, and the genuinely scarce good is what I have been calling the entry bundle — launch capacity, patient capital, favorable transfer trajectories, orbital slots, spectrum, and the survey data that tells you which body is worth visiting. If the proviso should be evaluated against the entry bundle rather than the ore, then appropriation might be perfectly permissible with respect to the first and deeply problematic with respect to the second, and the two questions have been run together by both camps. Second, and more radically: because the entry bundle is itself already appropriated terrestrial property, what happens in space may not be initial acquisition at all but the downstream projection of an existing private-property regime into a domain that was chartered as a common — in which case Locke's chapter on property is the wrong chapter to reach for, and the episode is better analyzed as a question about the extraterritorial extension of institutions. Say whether either hypothesis survives contact with the actual sequence of events, and revise or reject them if they do not.

Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory? Is the near-total non-ratification of the Moon Agreement a confirmation of the Lockean picture (commons charters fail because nobody can exclude anyone from defecting), an anomaly for it, or a case Locke simply never anticipated — for instance because his proviso does its real work in conditions of subsistence scarcity, where taking from the common threatens others' survival, whereas space resources are, for now, luxuries and inputs to further industry rather than the means of life? Identify at least one feature of the episode that no appropriation-based theory handles well, and say plainly where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to speak.

Please write as a political philosopher who reads space law closely enough not to be bullied by it, and think carefully rather than reaching for the standard talking points. Begin with a short brainstorming section — half a page or so — listing the candidate ways of mapping Locke onto the episode and noting where each mapping strains, and only then write the analysis; use plain-text headings and write between 1,500 and 2,200 words. Write in American English. Finally, close with an explicit note on your own confidence: tell me how sure you are of the main claims you have made, and single out the one point in the whole analysis that you are least certain of and why.
