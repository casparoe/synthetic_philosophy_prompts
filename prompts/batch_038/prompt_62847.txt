I'm a senior software engineer on a team that builds reasoning models. I have no formal training in philosophy or in learning theory beyond what I've absorbed on the job, and I recently sat through an argument between two colleagues that I could not follow well enough to judge. I can implement either side's proposal, but I can't tell which argument is sound. I'd like you to adjudicate it. Please don't point me at papers or give me a reference list — I can't check any of it, and the dispute was about our own results in any case.

Background, so the exchange is interpretable. We trained two verifier models to score solutions from a mid-size model doing competition-style mathematics. The first was trained on roughly 18,000 human step-level labels: annotators read each sampled solution line by line and marked each line as correct, neutral, or an error. The second saw exactly the same sampled solutions but was trained only on whether the final answer was right. Both were used identically at inference — re-rank N sampled solutions, keep the top one — and we swept N from 1 to 512. The step-trained verifier was about four points ahead at N=8, and the gap had closed entirely by N=512. Dana and Eli disagree about what that result shows, and the argument travelled well past the experiment.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Dana: the sweep settles it. Reward only the final answer, spend enough compute on search, and you arrive at the same place. So step-level supervision is an optimization convenience — it shortens credit assignment — and not a distinct kind of evidence about correctness. There is one thing we care about, whether the answer is right, and every intermediate label is at best a proxy for it.

2. Eli: then look at the failures rather than the scores. Under the step-trained verifier, the solutions it rejected were near-misses: a slip in the last two lines of an otherwise valid derivation. Under the final-answer verifier, the solutions it ranked highest were fluent, well-formatted derivations that went wrong around line three and then spent the remaining page arranging things so that the early error looked inevitable. Outcome supervision doesn't merely fail to teach valid reasoning — it selects for reasoning that is convincing at the endpoint. That is a difference in kind, and four points at N=8 versus zero at N=512 doesn't express it.

3. Dana: that's a low-compute artifact. When the search budget is small, the cheapest way to raise expected reward is to emit whatever surface features correlate with arriving at a correct answer, and fluent post-hoc rationalization is cheap. Give the outcome-trained run the budget you're willing to give the process one and it finds the genuinely valid derivations too, because at scale those are what produce correct answers. You're describing the behavior of an undertrained search, not a property of the objective.

4. Eli: grant that and the rare case still decides it. If invalid-but-persuasive reasoning reaches a correct answer even one time in a thousand, outcome supervision gives it positive weight and nothing in the objective pushes back. And the more important difference is auditability. A step-labeled verifier can be interrogated: I can pull the annotations, find the lines humans mislabeled, and correct them. With an outcome reward I only ever observe pass or fail, so I can never localize the disagreement between my reward model and my own judgment. Outcome supervision is un-auditable in principle, not merely in practice.

5. Dana: you traded noise for auditability and are calling it a win. Annotators agree about whether the answer is right; they do not agree about whether a given intermediate line is promising or on the right track, and I can show you our own agreement statistics. A reward whose target is that ill-defined isn't auditable, it's just documented. Worse, it's gameable in a way you cannot detect: the policy learns to write lines that resemble the lines annotators called good, and there is no ground truth left to catch it, because you discarded the ground truth when you stopped checking answers.

6. Eli: the same gaming occurs with outcome supervision, except you can't see it. And the dichotomy is false at the level of the object. Train a verifier on final-answer labels only, then ask it to score a partial solution, and what it estimates is the probability that this prefix eventually leads to a correct answer. That is a step-level score. Outcome supervision is process supervision with the intermediate labels marginalized out. So this entire argument concerns where we spend labeling budget, not two competing accounts of what makes a solution good.

7. Dana: only if you accept that the score is a fact about the task. The probability that a prefix leads to a correct answer is a fact about the policy that will continue it — which means it changes every time the policy changes, and two teams running different policies get different step scores for the same line of algebra. Step labels are supposed to be about the line, not about the model. So they are not the same object, and that difference is precisely the one that matters.
