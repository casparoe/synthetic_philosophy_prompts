Discussions of prison reform almost invariably stage the contrast between a facility such as Norway's Halden and a typical American maximum-security prison as a contest over severity: one model is gentle, the other brutal, and the reformist lesson is that prisons ought to be made less bad. Please argue against that framing, and in favor of a more specific and less comfortable thesis.

Define a custodial regime as *reciprocal* just in case its terms — the daily routine; the measure of control inmates retain over their own time, associations, and possessions; the extent and rationale of surveillance; and above all the procedures by which decisions affecting an inmate's conditions, privileges, transfers, and release are made, explained, reviewed, and revocable only for stated reasons — are such that an inmate could endorse them as a fair price for the harm they caused without thereby renouncing their standing as someone owed reasons. Note that this is not a consent standard. Nothing turns on whether anyone actually agrees; the question is whether endorsement would be intelligible rather than self-abasing.

Your thesis is that reciprocity, not severity, does the real explanatory work in our comparative judgments about these two systems. Two component claims require defense. First, a comfortable but non-reciprocal prison — one offering good food, private rooms, and meaningful work, while subjecting every significant feature of an inmate's life to administrative decisions that are unexplained, unreviewable, and revocable at will — would be more objectionable than a spartan but reciprocal one. Second, the amenities that dominate public discussion of Halden are at best instruments of reciprocity rather than constituents of it, with the consequence that a jurisdiction which reproduced the architecture and the interior design while leaving the decision-making structure untouched would have reproduced nothing essential.

The argument should engage seriously with at least two of the following objections rather than gesturing at them:

- Reciprocity may be proportionality under a new name, in which case retributivists already possess the resources to condemn administrative arbitrariness and the purported distinction collapses.
- For certain harms, no custodial terms whatever could be endorsed by the person who caused them without self-abasement; if reciprocity is unattainable in precisely the cases that populate maximum-security institutions, it cannot be the property that separates good prisons from bad ones.
- The standard may demand a vantage point no actual inmate can occupy, since the characterization of the harm is itself contested and the sentence is imposed; a criterion whose satisfaction cannot be assessed from inside the institution may be doing no work at all.

Where you judge a stipulated case to be pulling illegitimate weight, you may revise the case — but you may not abandon the thesis. Write a reasoned argument rather than a balanced survey, and state your conclusion explicitly.
