In my metaethics reading group we turned to moral particularism, and one member argued that an analogy with forces disposes of the holism of reasons. Reasons, he said, are like forces acting on a body: each has its own magnitude and direction, and what an agent ought to do is the resultant. That an act would break a promise always pulls one way and that it would ease someone's suffering always pulls the other, just as a book's weight pulls down whether or not the table is holding it up; all that varies from case to case is the resultant. It follows, he claimed, that moral principles can be stated as ceteris paribus generalizations and stay exceptionless in what they contribute, and that the particularist's talk of a reason flipping its polarity is simply a confusion about being outweighed — a balanced force does not cease to be a force.

I find this persuasive and suspect it shouldn't be. Could you audit the analogy? I would like the correspondence set out explicitly — what plays the part of magnitude, of direction, of the resultant, of the other forces present, of the body acted upon — and then the disanalogies, sorted into those that genuinely threaten the use he is making of it and those that are merely incidental (reasons are not continuous and cannot be read off a dial, but I doubt that does any work here). Two things especially: what the source domain presupposes about how contributions combine that reasons may not share, and whether his central move — a defeated reason retains its polarity, so holism is false — can be made at all without leaning on the analogy. Close by saying whether the ceteris paribus principles survive the audit, and whether a better image is available to the generalist or whether the particularist has the better imagery.
