**Question 3** (short essay, ~600 words; 25% of the exam)

(a) State the knowledge norm of assertion, and explain what its proponents mean by saying the norm is *constitutive* of assertion rather than merely one moral rule among others governing speech. [10 marks]

(b) Consider this case. Priya runs a bakery. A customer asks whether the croissants are safe for her son, who has a severe nut allergy. Priya has a written certification from her flour supplier stating that the current shipment contains no nut traces; she has read the certification herself and has no reason whatever to doubt it. She says, "These croissants contain no nuts." The certification is in fact wrong — there are traces — and the boy has a reaction.

Many people have the intuition that Priya's assertion was not improper: she was, we might say, a maximally responsible asserter, and yet she did not know what she said. Explain how this case is meant to put pressure on the knowledge norm. Does it succeed? In your answer you must compare Priya's case with a lottery case of the kind standardly used to argue against weaker norms. [15 marks]

---

Williamson's knowledge norm says you must assert p only if you know p. He formulates it with a "must", as a rule of the practice rather than as advice, and it states a necessary condition only: knowing p isn't supposed to be sufficient for an assertion being all right, since there are seperate norms about relevance, timing and manners that can still be broken. That matters, because the knowledge norm isn't a theory of when an assertion is all-things-considered fine, and it shouldn't be attacked as though it were one.

The sense in which it's constitutive is that the rule partly defines what asserting is. If you assert p without knowing p you haven't really made an assertion, you've made something shaped like one — the way that sliding your bishop straight across the board isn't a bad chess move, it isn't a move at all. This is also why criticizing someone for asserting what they don't know isn't moral criticism: they've broken a rule of the activity, not a moral rule, so there is nothing morally wrong with doing it when it happens to be useful.

The evidence for knowledge over weaker norms is the lottery. If I tell you your ticket will lose on the strength of one-in-fourteen-million odds, my assertion is improper, even though my evidence is as good as evidence ever gets and I am overwhelmingly likely to be right. So a justification norm is out. And a truth norm is out too, since it only forbids assertions that turn out false and mine almost certainly won't. What's missing in the case is knowledge, which is exactly what the norm demands.

The knowledge norm also does something weaker norms can't: it explains the badness of "p, but I don't know that p." In asserting the first conjunct the speaker presents herself as knowing, and the second conjunct withdraws that presentation, so the utterance undercuts itself. On a justification norm that conjunction should be unremarkable whenever your evidence is good enough for p and you fall short of knowledge only through some unlucky twist. So assertion is essentially a way of conveying your epistemic position and not just your content.

On Priya: she is the lottery case wearing a disguise. She has excellent evidence — a written certification she checked herself — and she does not know, because the certification is false. The two cases are structurally identical: high evidence, no knowledge, and an audience that ends up misled. Consistency therefore requires that if the lottery assertion is improper, so was Priya's. But plainly it wasn't; there is nothing to reproach her for, she did everything a responsible asserter could do. So the knowledge norm delivers a verdict of impropriety on an assertion that any reasonable person would call proper, and it has to be weakened — presumably to something like "assert only what your evidence makes reasonable." That handles Priya, and I take it the lottery case could be dealt with along the way, though I won't develop that here. A related case is the well-known one of the teacher who teaches evolution to her class: she knowingly asserts what she knows to be false, because the curriculum and the evidence require it, which again shows that a proper assertion doesn't need knowledge.
