I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar and we just spent three weeks on whether DNA literally carries information or whether "code" is a metaphor, and I wrote up an argument for my problem set that my TA said proves too much. I still can't see where. Roughly as I turned it in:

(1) For a physical state X to carry information about some feature Y, X has to depend on Y in a reliable way — X's being in the state it's in can't be equally well correlated with a pile of other things. Call this the reliability requirement.

(2) Whether a correlation is reliable isn't a property of X and Y alone; it's a function of the conditions X actually gets used under. The same sequence gets read out differently in a different cellular context, at a different temperature, with different splicing machinery, and the codon assignments themselves look arbitrary — a frozen accident that could easily have come out otherwise — so the reliability of the sequence-to-trait correlation depends entirely on the developmental setup.

(3) Therefore DNA carries information about a trait only relative to a specification of the context; there's no such thing as the information a sequence carries, simpliciter.

(4) But every other putative information bearer is in the same boat. A V1 cell's firing carries information about orientation only relative to adaptation state, anaesthetic, and the range of stimuli actually shown; a bee's waggle dance carries information about a food source only relative to the hive's conventions and the terrain the scouts really fly over.

(5) So for anything whatsoever, what it carries information about is only relative to a context — and once you write out the full context you've just listed the causes of X's state, at which point the information isn't adding anything over the causal facts.

(6) So there's no such property as carrying information, in genes or anywhere else; it's a façon de parler for correlation getting complicated.

(7) If that's right, every claim of the form "these cells signal the location of the food" in neuroscience and ethology is loose talk at best — and the same people who insist DNA is a code only by courtesy are the loudest defenders of those claims.

(8) So at least one of (1)–(4) is false.

His comment was that I've argued to a conclusion nobody accepts out of premises everybody in the room did accept, which is fair but not much help. I'd really like to keep (2) and (4) — the context-dependence point was the one part of the three weeks that felt like it was telling me something — and I defintely don't want to be committed to (6).

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent going in? And if the cheapest exit is through something I'd rather keep, price it for me. What does it cost to reject (1) instead — does some selectional or etiological account of what fixes content rescue the whole thing, and what does that account have to say about sequences or signals that never had a selection history? What does it cost to reject (4) and insist there's a real asymmetry between genes and neurons, i.e. that one of them is context-bound in a way the other isn't? Is there a version of (5) that doesn't collapse into (6), or is the slide from "relative to context" to "nothing over and above the causes" the actual problem? Also, does the diagnosis change depending on which sense of "code" you're working with — the Shannon sense of a reliable channel, versus the sense of an arbitrary symbol system, which seems to be the sense in which the codon assignments are code-like at all? I have a feeling the arguement is fine on one of those and broken on the other but I can't tell which.
