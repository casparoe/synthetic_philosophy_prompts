I've been going back and forth with my roommate, who runs a permadeath mod for a survival game and takes the loss of a character way more seriously than I take losing a ranked match, and it's pushed me into a corner in the third chapter of my dissertation. I've basically landed on virtual realism — the Chalmers line that the Matrix world is a genuine world, that virtual objects are real objects made of computational stuff rather than pretend ones, and that a virtual killing or theft is a real killing or theft. But the part I've added myself is the part that's most exposed, so please attack that specifically rather than the general metaphysics.

My addition is an asymmetry claim. Call it the cost-tracking constraint: a virtual state counts as a real harm only insofar as it destroys or constrains something that took real, finite resources to build and that the system can't regenerate on demand — invested time, a genuinely unique item, a relationship, a ranking that's scarce by construction. On that view griefing a permadeath character or dissolving a ten-year-old guild is a real harm; getting blown up in a lobby that respawns you five seconds later with identical gear is not, even though it stings and even though someone loses. The respawn case is a bad experience but there's no loss of anything real, because nothing scarce was destroyed. So — what would count against the cost-tracking constraint? I want falsifiers, not a vibe check. Specifically: (a) which cases the view is committed to calling not-a-harm that plainly look like harms, and which it calls harms that plainly aren't; (b) what findings about actual players — attachment, grief, the way people talk about a lost character — would genuinely undermine the constraint rather than just being compatible with it, and be honest about whether such findings could ever be decisive given how easy self-report is to explain away; (c) whether the constraint collapses into a rival once you push on it, i.e. into welfarism about felt experience, or into the deflationary claim that a virtual item is just a licence in someone's database, which would gut the realism I was trying to keep; and (d) whether "can't be regenerated on demand" is even a principled line, since scarcity in these systems is set by a developer who can patch it away overnight. That last one worries me most: if a hotfix can undo a loss, it looks like my constraint has to say the loss was never real, which seems crazy.

Also tell me what a defender would concede. Which of these would a committed cost-tracker admit is a real defeater, and which would they wave off as a misdescription of their view, and why. Rank everything from fatal-to-the-chapter down to merely awkward, and finish with your honest verdict on whether I've insulated the position into unfalsifiability by tying it to "resources" and "scarcity" — and if I have, whether that's actually a problem for a view in this neighbourhood or just business as usual for metaphysics.

Bottom line up front, then the ranked defeaters, then the verdict. Hard cap of 700 words. No bibliography or reading list please, I know the literature, I want the arguments — and made-up examples are fine, just flag when you're inventing a case rather than describing a real one.
