I am auditing an undergraduate seminar on the ethics of speech this term, and the discussion has settled into a groove I would like to break. The working assumption in the room is that lying and merely misleading differ in moral kind rather than only in degree: a liar asserts something she believes to be false, whereas a misleader asserts something she believes to be true and only permits a false inference to arise in her audience, so the liar has committed herself to a falsehood in a way the misleader has not. On that picture the liar is the more culpable of the two whenever the cases are otherwise comparable, and the misleader's residual fault is a lesser, derivative one. Two students pushed back with the intuition that deniability makes the misleader worse, but when asked to produce a case they could not; their examples all involved either a word used equivocally or a lie that was quickly discovered, and the instructor dismissed both as changing the subject. I would like to hand the seminar a properly engineered counterexample.

Design a paired thought experiment — two scenarios differing in exactly one respect — in which a speaker who says only what she believes to be true, and who merely invites a false inference, comes out as more culpable than a speaker who flatly asserts a falsehood, with everything else held as fixed as the case allows. The specifications:

- Both speakers must have the same information, the same motive, and the same relationship to the same hearer; both must bring about the same false belief with the same practical consequences for the same third party.
- The truthful speaker's utterance must be literally true in its context, not true only on a strained or technical reading, and the falsity she induces must arise from what is implicated, suggested, or conspicuously omitted — not from lexical ambiguity, syntactic amphiboly, indexical shift, or vagueness. No equivocation on a word with two senses, no exploiting the ambiguity of "is," and nothing resembling mental reservation or a privately intended qualification.
- Neither speaker may occupy a role that already carries a heightened duty of full disclosure, so no doctor, lawyer, fiduciary, or sworn witness, and no courtroom or perjury setting. I want the reversal to hold for ordinary speakers in ordinary circumstances.
- The hearer must be an ordinarily competent adult — neither unusually credulous nor unusually suspicious — who reaches the false belief by a natural inference that most people in her position would reach.
- Realistic, contemporary, and mundane: a workplace, a family, a sale between private parties, a neighbourhood dispute. Each scenario should be a single paragraph, and the pair should be usable with students who know the saying/implicating distinction but have read nothing else on the topic.
- Both deceptions must eventually be discovered in full, by the same person, with discovery equally complete and equally costly to the deceiver's reputation in each case. This is the constraint I care about most: I want any reversal to survive the removal of the advantage the misleader gets from never being caught.

Two levers have been suggested to me, and you may use either, both, or neither: that the misleader makes her hearer a co-author of the false belief, so that discovery does not restore the hearer's confidence in her own reasoning; and that the misleader must first calculate what the hearer will infer, which makes the deception more deliberately aimed at that particular mind. If you think a different lever works better, use it and tell me why you preferred it.

Then do the demonstration, and be explicit throughout about what is doing the work. First, state for each scenario what an assertion-based account of lying, an intention-based account, and a normative account on which lying involves warranting the truth or inviting trust each say, and confirm that the truthful speaker genuinely does not lie on any of the three; if one of them classifies her as a liar after all, say so and tell me whether the case still does its job. Second, identify the single feature of the case that generates the reversal in culpability and explain why that feature belongs to misleading as such rather than being an artifact of the setting I have chosen. Third, tell me what happens when that feature is removed: does the case collapse back into the standard asymmetry, or does a weaker reversal survive? Fourth, give me the strongest reply available to someone who wants to keep the asymmetry thesis — including whether she should contest the literal truth of the utterance, contest the intuition, or redescribe the pair so that the variables I fixed are not really fixed — and say which of these moves you find most promising and why.

Finally, tell me whether the case, if it works, shows anything about the moral difference between lying and misleading in general, or only that culpability can come apart from the saying/implicating distinction in specially constructed circumstances. I would rather be told that the exercise succeeds but proves less than it appears to than be handed a scenario that quietly builds its verdict into the setup.
