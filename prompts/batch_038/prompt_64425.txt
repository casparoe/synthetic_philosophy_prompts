I attend a philosophy-of-physics reading group, and our recurring discussions of whether the wavefunction is real have become unproductive for one specific reason: the sentence "the wavefunction isn't a thing" is now deployed by people on opposite sides of the debate, and nobody notices that they are saying different things. Two instances from last session. A Bohmian among us, resisting a proposal that treats the wavefunction as a field on a 3N-dimensional configuration space, insisted that it is not a thing at all but something closer to a law, and therefore cannot meaningfully be said to inhabit any space whatsoever. A colleague of QBist sympathies, arguing that the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph result tells us nothing about reality, used the same sentence to the same dismissive effect, glossing the wavefunction as bookkeeping for an agent's expectations. Both took themselves to have shown that a realist worried about configuration space has committed a category mistake. I suspect at least five distinct theses are in circulation here, that the two speakers are asserting different ones, and that at most one of them would actually dispose of the configuration-space worry.

Please lay the readings out as a menu rather than defending one of them. For each reading: state it precisely enough that it could be false; classify it as a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or an error; and say what it commits one to regarding (a) whether distinct pure quantum states can correspond to overlapping probability distributions over an underlying physical state, (b) whether the quantum state belongs to the ontology of the theory or to its dynamical law, and (c) whether the resulting picture is separable and locally supervenient.

Then address three questions directly. First, does the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph argument rule any of these readings out, and which does it leave untouched — and does that answer change depending on whether one accepts the ontological-models framework that the argument presupposes? Second, which reading, if any, dissolves the objection that a fundamental ontology on configuration space is not the space we inhabit, as opposed to merely relocating the objection? Third, what exactly is being equivocated when a nomologically inclined Bohmian and a QBist both say the wavefunction is not a thing and take themselves to be allies?

One further request. My working guess is that the ambiguity turns on two separable questions — whether the quantum state is an ingredient of the world rather than a way of describing it, and whether it is a state of a system rather than a rule governing systems — and that the slogan collapses them. Test that conjecture; if it is wrong or merely incomplete, say so and replace it with a better taxonomy. No bibliography needed, as I will supply references myself.
