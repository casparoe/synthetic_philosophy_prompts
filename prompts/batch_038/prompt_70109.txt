I am a novelist, and for the better part of two years I have been building a book whose middle section follows a government statistician in a small, cold, prosperous country that has decided, for reasons both sincere and promotional, to become the happiest nation on earth. To get the technical texture right I arranged to sit in on a closed two-day workshop at a research institute that advises several such governments on well-being measurement; I was permitted to attend on condition that I record nothing verbatim and identify no one. What I did not anticipate is that the workshop would end in a genuine and unresolved quarrel between two of the participants, and that I would leave unable to say which of them had won. Since the climax of my book is precisely such a quarrel, I would rather not write it on the basis of a misapprehension.

The two antagonists were, on one side, a survey methodologist who spent a decade inside a national statistics office, and on the other, a moral philosopher who advises ministries on well-being policy. I reconstruct their exchange from my notes as follows, in seven moves.

1. The methodologist began by observing that the ranking everyone argues about rests on a single survey item: respondents are asked to imagine a ladder whose top rung is the best possible life for them and whose bottom rung the worst, and to place their own life as it currently stands somewhere on that zero-to-ten scale. That, she said, is a life evaluation, not a report of happiness. It asks people to render a considered verdict against a private standard of comparison, and it is therefore driven mainly by income, standing, health and employment, whereas what people actually feel from hour to hour is a different quantity altogether; the two are known to come apart, most famously in the finding that beyond a certain level of income the evaluation of one's life keeps climbing while reported day-to-day emotional well-being flattens out. Her conclusion: the league table is very largely a re-badged ranking of GDP per capita plus a handful of Nordic institutions, and the annual coronation of a single country as the happiest in the world is a claim the instrument cannot support.

2. The philosopher answered with three replies. First, transparency: the report has never claimed the ladder captures affect; it states plainly that the national rank rests on life evaluation alone, and it publishes the affect measures alongside, so accusing it of a conflation it explicitly declines to make is a straw man. Second, comparability: a single anchored item is one of the very few instruments that survives translation into more than a hundred languages and both telephone and face-to-face administration without serious distortion, whereas multi-item affect batteries degrade badly across cultures; a measure that cannot be administered comparably is not a measure at all. Third, and substantively: if a population judges that its life is going well, that judgment is itself a fact about its welfare and not mere noise, and any theory of well-being on which people's considered verdicts about their own lives are systematically irrelevant owes us an account of why.

3. She returned to the charge on three fronts. The correlation between national life evaluation and national positive affect cannot validate the ladder, because both series are produced by the same response cultures, the same interviewer effects and the same survey apparatus, so agreement is exactly what one should expect whether or not either tracks anything. And where they fail to agree, the failure is not marginal: the top of the daily-affect tables is not Nordic at all but dominated by Latin American countries, several of which sit in the middle of the ladder table, so the divergence is greatest precisely at the summit, which is the only part anyone reads. Finally, adaptation: respondents in deprived or unfree conditions recalibrate their standards downward, which means the ladder systematically understates deprivation, a difficulty the capability literature has pressed for decades, and the reverse inflation may occur wherever a national narrative insists that life is good.

4. He replied that the adaptation objection makes the critique unfalsifiable, since any high score reported under adversity can be dismissed as an adapted preference while any low score reported under prosperity is taken at face value, and a test that cannot fail is not a test. He then shifted the ground: the real problem, he said, is not measurement but use, because publishing a league table with a single winner invites exactly the targeting that destroys the informational value of the number; the honest advice to a client government is therefore not "climb" but "use the decomposition behind the score."

5. She said the decomposition is worse than the ranking. Each country's distance from a hypothetical lowest-scoring benchmark is explained by six variables — income per head, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity and perceptions of corruption — plus a residual, and every newspaper and every minister then discusses those contributions as though they were levers. But generosity is operationalised as donations to charity and social support as having someone to count on in trouble, and both are substantially downstream of the affluence they are recruited to explain; by the report's own account the exercise is correlational. Retracting the table, she added, is unenforceable in any case: drop it and the press will reconstruct the ordering from the same underlying poll data within a day.

6. He conceded that the decomposition is not causal and said it has never been presented as such, then argued that the point is institutional rather than statistical: the ranking has compelled ministries to produce well-being statistics they would otherwise never have collected, and the repeated first place of a country like Finland has made happiness a legitimate object of budgeting rather than a private matter. A useful fiction knowingly held, he said, beats having no measure at all. He added that her alternative is not better but worse, since daily-affect reports are hostage to the hour of the interview, the weather and the respondent's momentary mood; if evaluation is contaminated, affect is hardly the cure.

7. Her last word was that he should then say so in public and stop crowning a winner. If the number is a useful fiction, the fiction ought to be labelled as such, and the institute's proposal to keep publishing the table while appending a paragraph of caveats is precisely the compromise that lets the headline do the work and the caveat do the absolving.

Neither of them budged, and the rest of us were left to write our recommendations around the hole in the room. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the seven, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right but overstated, or right, and justify the rating in a sentence or two, attending to the specific sub-claims rather than to the general drift. Where a move contains one sound step and one bad step, separate them. Then tell me which of the two is more right overall, and do so separately for the two questions they kept sliding between — whether the ranking measures happiness, and whether a country should try to top it — since I suspect those came apart somewhere in the middle and neither party noticed. Finally, say what both of them missed: what consideration, objection or distinction ought to have entered the argument and did not. I am not asking you to tell me which of them would make the more sympathetic character, and I do not need pointers to the literature; I want to know exactly where the argument breaks, because I intend to have my statistician break it in public.
