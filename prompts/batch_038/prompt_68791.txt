I am a novelist three-quarters of the way through a book set in a near-future American jurisdiction that has adopted the extended mind thesis as settled constitutional doctrine — not as a theory that experts allude to in footnotes, but as black-letter law that trial judges apply to routine motions. The set piece of the third act is an appellate opinion, and I would like you to write that opinion in full, as a self-contained document I can argue with. I will not lift your prose wholesale, so please do not hedge or summarize in place of writing it.

**Form.** A decision of a fictional intermediate appellate court, 1,200 to 1,500 words, in the ordinary apparatus: a caption, a one-line statement of the panel, a majority opinion in numbered paragraphs, and a dissent of roughly 400 words that is genuinely persuasive rather than a foil. The register is the flat, courteous, precedent-bound voice of a judge who is not trying to be funny. Nothing winks, nothing is italicized for effect, and no character remarks on how strange the doctrine is. The comedy must come entirely from the court taking its own premises seriously and following them where they lead. Do not use real case names, real statutes, real courts, or citations to academic papers; invent the court's own line of precedents. The target of the satire is neither the thesis nor its critics but the transposition of a metaphysical claim into a bright-line rule that somebody must apply on a Tuesday afternoon — so both opinions should feel competent, and the reader should not be told which one to prefer.

**Facts,** to be stated crisply in the opening paragraphs and relied on thereafter. The appellant was convicted after the trial court ordered him to unlock his telephone and produce the contents of a notes application in which, for nine years, he had recorded the things he did not trust himself to remember: his medication schedule, the combination to a storage unit, the name of a man he owed money to, and a standing reminder to ask his wife about a matter he described only as "the thing." Police obtained the device under a warrant directed at his person and effects. Eighteen months later, in the ordinary course of a data-center consolidation, the vendor deleted the account and its backups. He now argues three things: that the compelled production was a testimonial act drawn from his own mind rather than from a container; that the deletion was not the destruction of evidence but the infliction of a permanent cognitive injury for which the state is answerable; and that the trial court erred in finding that he "believed" the contents of a note he concedes he had not opened in eleven months.

**What I need the piece to get right.** The majority reasons from parity between the artifact and the skull: if the same process occurring in the head would be cognition without argument, its occurring in the device is cognition too, and so the device is not a container searched but a portion of the appellant's mind examined, and its deletion is an injury rather than a loss of evidence. It converts the defenders' own four conditions — constant availability, automatic endorsement on retrieval, speed and ease of access, and settled reliance such that the contents are not re-checked — into a four-factor test, and then actually applies that test to these facts instead of reciting it. It must confront the bloat objection head on: what, on this test, keeps a public library, a search engine, or a spouse out of the appellant's mind? Its answer should be recognizably the answer the thesis's defenders give — that it is functional integration, endorsement, and reliance that do the work, not proximity or biology — and it should concede that the line is drawn by degree, which must cost the court something in at least one holding. The dissent runs the internalist line properly: that however reliable and reciprocal the coupling between an organism and an artifact, causation is not constitution, and that there must be some mark of the cognitive, a property mental states have that ink and silicon lack. The dissent must grant the intuitive force of the parity argument — that it does seem arbitrary to let skin and skull do metaphysical work — and locate the failure in the step from coupling to constitution, then press the reductio deadpan: on the majority's rule the state commits battery whenever a site fails to resolve, a municipal library is a public organ whose defunding is a mass lobotomy, and a widower's grief is actionable. Let the majority's reply be better than the reader expects.

**The note he had not opened in eleven months** is the hinge. The court must decide whether that is still his belief, must not blur the difference between a belief one holds and a belief one is currently entertaining, and must notice — this is the best joke available — that its own four-factor test cannot tell whether the application functions more like memory or more like a library, and that the answer changes who owns the contents.

**Two further beats I would like included.** A footnote on whether the appellant and his wife, who co-maintain a shared calendar and complete each other's sentences in ways neither manages alone, constitute a single cognitive system, and if so whose privacy the warrant invaded. And a passage in which appellant's counsel, emboldened by the holding, offers up a fitness tracker, a refrigerator that has learned his routines, and a barista who remembers his order as further portions of his mind, whereupon the court must admit one and exclude another, or admit that it cannot.

**One quotation, and only one.** The court's doctrine derives from a single sentence in the 1998 Clark and Chalmers article, which the majority should set out as an epigraph and then gloss at length: "If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process." Do not put any further words in quotation marks as if from that article or from any other real text; paraphrase everything else. End the majority on something cold rather than funny.
