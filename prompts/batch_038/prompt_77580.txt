I am a third-year student in a seminar on measurement, incentives, and the philosophy of the social sciences, and I am preparing a set of case cards for our session on Goodhart's law and specification gaming. The readings treat specification gaming largely as a single phenomenon with many instances. My contribution to the section is to argue that it is at least three phenomena that get conflated because they share a surface description. The trichotomy I want to work with is this:

- **Bookkeeping distortion**: the recorded value is manipulated while the underlying activity is unchanged. The proxy comes apart from the target in the data rather than in the world.
- **Substantive distortion**: the records are honest, but real behavior migrates toward what the proxy rewards and away from what the measure was introduced to track. This is the paradigm case.
- **Conceptual distortion**: nobody games anything and behavior need not change in any way an observer would flag; rather, the target concept itself comes to be understood in terms of the proxy, so that the answer to the question of what the institution is actually trying to achieve differs after the measure is adopted from what it was before.

My suspicion is that the third is either the most consequential of the three or not a species of Goodhart's law at all, since nothing in it depends on an agent optimizing a proxy: the failure would be semantic rather than incentive-driven. I want examples that force the issue rather than illustrations that merely decorate it.

Please give me six cases, described generically. No named institutions, no studies, no statistics, no citations, and no invented attributions — this is for a teaching handout, and I want the reasoning to carry the weight rather than the sourcing. Ordinary, familiar institutional settings are preferable to exotic ones. The six cases should be:

1. one case of pure bookkeeping distortion, where you can say with confidence that nothing about the activity itself changed;
2. one case of pure substantive distortion, where the records are scrupulously accurate;
3. one case of pure conceptual distortion, where there is no identifiable gainer and no incentive to game;
4. two cases in which the diagnosis is genuinely contested — settings where a sensible person could classify the same facts under a different heading, and where the competing diagnoses imply opposite remedies;
5. one case that you think my trichotomy handles badly, whether because it requires a fourth category or because two of my three turn out to collapse into one.

For every case, be explicit about what rules out the neighboring types; that is where the conceptual work lies, and a case that could just as easily be filed under a different heading is useless to me unless you say so and explain what would settle it. If you find an ambiguity in how I have stated the categories, flag it rather than papering over it.

Present the six cases as a single table with exactly these columns, keeping each cell to one or two sentences so the table remains readable:

- `case` — a short label for the case
- `setting` — the institution and its measurement regime, described generically
- `proxy` — what is actually recorded or scored
- `target` — what the record was introduced to track
- `type` — bookkeeping, substantive, conceptual, contested, or, for the sixth case, whatever label you propose instead
- `mechanism` — how the proxy–target relationship breaks down, or, for conceptual cases, how the target is redefined
- `why_not_neighbor` — the nearest alternative classification and the specific feature that rules it out
- `diagnostic` — an observation that would in principle settle which classification is correct without introducing a new measure
- `remedy` — what the classification implies should be done, stated so that a different classification would imply something visibly different
- `confidence` — high, medium, or low, plus a clause naming what would change your mind

After the table, add roughly three hundred words of prose doing two things: first, working through the two contested cases and saying which classification you would ultimately defend and why; second, giving your verdict on whether conceptual distortion is properly a form of Goodhart's law or a distinct failure mode that merely tends to accompany it, together with the strongest consideration on the other side of that verdict.
