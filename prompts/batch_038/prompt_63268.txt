I serve on the records-governance committee of a regional health authority. Our master patient index applies an automatic rule: two records agreeing on every captured field — name, date of birth, address, insurer, blood type, allergy list, medications, problem list, registration date — are treated as one patient and merged. The rule eliminates roughly four hundred duplicate charts a year and is credited with preventing several wrong-record medication errors.

In March it merged two records belonging to different people: fraternal twins, same surname, same date of birth, same household, same blood type, no distinguishing diagnoses, who registered through the same clinic portal in the same week. For eleven days they shared a chart. One twin's documented penicillin allergy was applied to the other, who was then given penicillin at an urgent-care visit. He recovered, and the case is now before us.

Two proposals go to a vote Thursday. Proposal A keeps the merge rule and widens the field set — a government-identifier check plus a household-relationship field — so that distinct people are no longer qualitatively identical in our data. Proposal B abandons the qualitative criterion entirely: every record carries a locally issued, content-independent identifier, and numerical distinctness is never inferred from what a record says. Our informatics chair opposes B, and forcefully: the twins case shows only that our field set was too thin, since if two records agree on everything there is nothing left to make them two, and B would tell staff that matching fields never settle identity, licensing a proliferation of phantom patients.

Draft the committee's decision memo — adopt A, adopt B, or adopt a specified combination — with a concrete revision to the rule and an implementation window before the next fiscal quarter. Take the informatics chair's objection at its strongest rather than answering it with the twins case, which cuts both ways. In particular, address the philosophers' thought experiment of a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres: if qualitative agreement on everything could still leave open whether there are one or two, say what our policy should do about that possibility, and say what it should do if that possibility is not real but merely apparent to us.
