Had a three hour argument with my friend Dan on a hike yesterday and we're both still irritated, so I want a third opinion that isn't either of us. It started because I brought up Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary, the ant colony in Gödel, Escher, Bach that has beliefs and problem-solving abilities no single ant has, the one where the colony is treated as something like a mind and the ants are the dumb little components in it. Dan does swarm robotics for a living and his response was that this is just sloppy talk: the colony is a distributed control loop, and saying it knows where the food is has exactly the same content as saying a thermostat thinks the room is cold, which is to say none. His test, which he repeated about six times: if you can write the whole system down as feedback with no internal model anywhere in it, there is no mind there, and ants are all reflex so the colony is all reflex. I said fine, I'll take your test, but then explain why my verdicts on different collectives come apart so sharply, because they do, and I can't find the line. I wrote them down afterwards and honestly I'm not sure they're consistent.

The list. An ant colony that reroutes around a trail I've blocked and gets to the food anyway: the colony worked that out, not any ant, and I'm happy to say it knows roughly where the food is. The same colony after I flood the nest five summers running, now building on higher ground with a different entrance layout: it learned. Something learned, and it wasn't an ant. A slime mold in a dish finding the short path through a maze, or growing a network that looks like a rail map: I'll grant it optimizes, but I refuse to say it has a belief about the maze, and I can't tell you why, since it has no neurons either and neurons clearly weren't what I was demanding. A bee swarm sending out scouts that dance about several candidate nest sites until the swarm commits to one: yes, the hive decided, and decided for a reason, and it preferred the better site. This feels like the strongest case on my list and I don't know what makes it strong. A termite mound holding a steady temperature and humidity all year: the mound regulates, but I won't say the colony wants to be warm or would mind being cold, even though the machinery is the same sort of machinery as the bees'. A colony marching off toward a synthetic pheromone strip and away from real food: I want to say it was deceived, that it got something wrong about the world, not merely that it broke down — but when a slime mold grows the wrong way because I shone a light at it I say the mechanism malfunctioned and stop there. Those two should get the same verdict and they don't. A company where every relevant document exists and nobody has read them all, but the board acts on the aggregate: no belief, records aren't beliefs, even though the colony in case one has no records at all and I give it beliefs freely. Me stepping on half an anthill: nobody is in agony, there's no what-it's-like for the colony however clever its routing is — I'm sure of this one, and it feels like it ought to constrain all the others, but I can't see how to get no-pain out of anything that also hands beliefs to the bees. And the one I keep flip flopping on: cut a colony in half, both halves carry on, and I'm tempted to say one mind became two, which is something I would never say so cheerfully about a person, though split brain patients do make me squirm.

So — is there a principle behind all this, or a small set of clauses where each one is forced by a specific case above? I'd like it stated tightly, with which case motivates each clause, and I want you to be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't deliver, because I'd rather drop a verdict than bolt on an epicycle. Then tell me what it predicts for things I didn't list: a flock of starlings getting out of a falcon's way, an immune system that recognizes a pathogen it met years ago, a market, one of those big trained language networks, and a brain organoid in a dish that will never be a part of anything larger. And finally deal with Dan directly — is the no-internal-model test any good? If it is, does it kill the bee case along with the thermostat, and am I just an instrumentalist who hasn't admitted it? If it isn't, what exactly is wrong with it, given that it feels right when I apply it to the thermostat and the termite mound and wrong when I apply it to the swarm? Bonus points if you can tell me whether the two of us are even arguing about the same thing, because I suspect he thinks mind is a metaphysical fact about a system and I think it's a question about which descriptions earn their keep.
