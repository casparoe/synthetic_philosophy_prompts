Three weeks from now I am presenting a position paper at a workshop co-hosted by a political theory research group and a social policy institute, and I would like a question-period prep sheet rather than a further defense of the paper itself.

**The thesis.** I argue for replacing pooled, transferable parental leave entitlements with individually earmarked, non-transferable ones: each parent receives a block of well-paid, use-it-or-lose-it leave that cannot be handed over to the other. But I argue for it on grounds that are not the usual ones. I explicitly disclaim two familiar justifications — that the design improves child developmental outcomes, and that an equal division of paid and unpaid work between parents is intrinsically valuable and worth promoting. My case is distributive. The care that produces the next generation of citizens and workers has the structure of a public good: its benefits are socialized, while its costs are borne privately and, under a pooled entitlement, are allocated by the household to whichever partner has the lower market wage. A design that lets the household choose therefore ratifies the existing earnings gap and converts it into a lifetime allocation of care work. Neutrality of mechanism is not neutrality of effect. The Becker-style specialization bargain looks like private choice only because the wage gap that drives it is itself partly generated by anticipated motherhood penalties; earmarking works less by changing household preferences than by changing the coordination problem each household faces and the expectations employers hold about which workers will disappear. On this account the state is not reaching into the private sphere to impose a conception of the good family, because the apparent naturalness of that sphere is already an artifact of public rules about who absorbs the cost of dependency. I invoke Okin on the public/private split only as background, and I have tried to strip her conclusion of its perfectionist content — which I suspect is my largest exposure, since it leaves the argument with no principled way to say how big each quota ought to be.

**The audience.** Roughly fourteen people. Four labor economists, two of whom have run difference-in-differences designs on Nordic fathers' quotas and think in terms of take-up rates, benefit replacement levels, and firm-side costs. Three feminist political philosophers, at least one of whom believes I have traded away the normative ambition of the position to make it palatable. One political theorist committed to liberal neutrality in a general rather than technical sense, who thinks the state has no business shaping the internal division of labour. Two sociologists of gender and work, one of whom thinks the entire framing is fixated on continuously employed parents and misses informal, extended-family, and precarious work. One official from a national social insurance agency who cares about administration, single-parent households, and self-employed claimants. One attendee who writes policy briefs defending cash-for-care allowances and views earmarking as a penalty on the parent who actually provides care.

**What I want.** Eight to ten objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do to my argument if they landed — not ordered by how interesting they are, and not the objections I would get from a general feminist audience or from a libertarian think tank. For each: name which of the people above raises it; state the objection in two or three sentences in that person's own register and vocabulary, using the concepts they would actually reach for; give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes of speech; add one line identifying where that reply is softest; and mark whether I should concede it up front in the talk rather than wait to be pushed onto it. Include at least one objection each from the labor economists, the neutrality theorist, the cash-for-care advocate, the agency official, and the sociologist who thinks my framing excludes the un- and under-employed.

Include, separately and clearly marked, the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I have a suspicion about what it is, but I want your independent judgment, and I want the answer I would give if I had to give one in the room.

**Constraints.** Do not invent or guess at citations, study names, dates, or take-up statistics. Where an objection turns on an empirical claim, state the claim and say in which direction the evidence is genuinely unsettled, and note what evidence would settle it. No objection of the form "you have not considered X" unless it names the specific mechanism by which X defeats the argument.

**Format.** Numbered entries, each with the five components above labeled consistently. Finish with a line beginning `Verdict:` that gives the number and a short label of the single objection you judge most damaging, followed by one sentence stating the concession I would have to make for my position to survive it.
