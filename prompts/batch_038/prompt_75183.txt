I'm in eleventh grade and I'm on my school's history-and-economics debate team. This weekend we're arguing the resolution that the collapse of Soviet-style central planning was ultimately a failure of technology rather than of economic theory, and I've been assigned the negative side even though I find the affirmative case intuitively appealing. Our team captain's whole argument runs on a single analogy that he keeps returning to: an economy is essentially an enormous system of simultaneous equations describing what should be produced, by whom, and at what cost; a market is just a very slow, physical machine that stumbles toward the solution by trial and error; and the Soviet planners failed not because the problem was unsolvable in principle but because they lacked the processing power to solve it. On his telling the debate was settled by arithmetic and hardware, and now that we have machine learning and enormous datacenters the old calculation objection is simply obsolete, since a sufficiently advanced AI planner could match or beat the market. To support this he cited our teacher, who told us that an Italian economist had already written down the equations a socialist ministry would have to solve before the Russian Revolution happened, and that Lange himself supposedly remarked decades later that once you could hand those equations to a computer you would get the answer almost immediately, which would make the market look like a clumsy calculating device from before the electronic age.

The other side in our club uses a completely different picture, and I cannot tell whether the two are genuinely in conflict or just talking past each other. Their analogy is that prices are not the output of a computation at all but a communication system, something like a telephone network or a shared language, in which the relevant facts about scarcity, local conditions, and what particular people can do with particular resources are not sitting around waiting to be collected and entered as inputs, but come into existence and get articulated only through the act of exchanging. On their version the market is less like a solver grinding toward a known answer and more like a conversation, or a discovery procedure, in which the questions themselves keep changing. One of my teammates then said that both sides are merely re-describing the same thing with different machinery metaphors, and that whichever analogy you pick decides the debate before any argument gets made.

Could you audit both analogies for me rather than tell me which economist was right? For each one I'd like an explicit correspondence table: in the computer picture, what exactly plays the role of the equation system, the variables, the input data, the objective function, the solver, and the error signal; in the communication picture, the sender, the receiver, the message, the code, and the noise. Then I'd like the places where each mapping breaks, with the disanalogies that actually matter for my captain's conclusion separated from the ones that are merely decorative. For instance, I can see that solving millions of equations is computationally hard, but I don't know whether that is a load-bearing objection or one that better hardware genuinely dissolves. I'd especially like to know what each analogy quietly imports, since the computer picture seems to assume that the data are given, static, articulable, and available in one place before the computation begins, and I suspect that assumption is where all the work is being done. Finally, please tell me whether the two classic objections survive once the analogies are on the table: the one saying there is nothing to compute with in the first place, because meaningful prices for capital goods require actual ownership and exchange, and the one saying the knowledge in question is dispersed, partly tacit, and known only to the person on the spot. Does modern machine learning change anything about either? And if both analogies are bad in the same way, what would a better one look like for each side, and is there any neutral picture that does not already presuppose the answer?
