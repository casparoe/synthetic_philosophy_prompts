Been going back and forth on the Boltzmann brain thing and I want an essay that actually settles something instead of surveying the landscape.

Setup, as I understand it. Certain cosmologies — anything with a de Sitter horizon and a nonzero temperature running for a very long time, which I think includes most versions of eternal inflation — imply that thermal fluctuations spontaneously assemble momentary observers, and that these outnumber the observers who grew up on planets by an insane factor. And the fluctuated ones would have wildly misleading apparent memories and apparent data: for one instant they'd seem to be reading a lab notebook, seem to see a smooth CMB, seem to have a past.

Here are three claims about what that means, and I don't think they're compatible with each other:

1. **It's ordinary confirmation theory.** A theory that predicts you're probably a Boltzmann brain gets badly disconfirmed by your evidence that you're a temporally extended embodied observer with a coherent history. Self-locating updating does all the work. Nothing epistemically exotic is happening; the BB "problem" is just a vivid case of a theory being refuted by data.

2. **It's a different kind of defect entirely.** On this line the theory isn't merely false-to-the-evidence, it's cognitively unstable in the sense that it can't be both true and justifiably believed — because under it, the reasoning process that produced your evidence is itself a fluctuation. So you're not licensed to weigh such a theory against evidence at all. Ruling it out is a precondition of doing cosmology, not a conclusion inside cosmology.

3. **Both of those accept a smuggling move, and that's the real problem.** The typicality measure tells you what a theory's ontology contains and in roughly what proportions. It doesn't tell you who you are. Getting from "most observers in this spacetime are fluctuations" to "I am probably a fluctuation" needs a bridge principle that converts content-counts into first-person probabilities, and I've never seen anyone argue for that bridge independently of the cases where they want it. Deny the bridge and the BB problem evaporates — but so does a lot of normal cosmology: you can no longer say a theory is attractive because it puts you in a typical pocket universe, you can't dismiss a surprising observation by noting that most observers would see something else, and a chunk of the fine-tuning motivation for inflation loses its typicality backbone.

I want the essay to take a side among these three and defend it. But please actually do the following, because this is where I think the interesting stuff is:

- Show whether view 3 can draw a principled line between the typicality inferences cosmologists desperately want to keep and the one that generates Boltzmann brains. If it can't, say so and pick between 1 and 2 instead — that's a legitimate outcome, but it has to be argued, not assumed.
- Put real pressure on view 1: if the fluctuations outnumber normal observers by a large but finite factor and the theory has a strong prior, the Bayesian update shifts credence without refuting anything, which looks like it strands us in a BB world. Can view 1 escape that without quietly borrowing view 2's machinery? This is the move I most want to see handled carefully.
- Say what happens in the literally infinite case, where the ratios are undefined until you pick a measure and different measures give different answers. Does that change which view you should hold, or does it just relocate the problem?
- Address the single strongest objection to your verdict, and give it a real reply rather than a nod.
- End concretely: given your verdict, what should someone actually do with a landscape vacuum that produces Boltzmann brains — discard it, reweight it, or decline to ask the question in the first place?

Around 1500 to 1800 words. Don't cite anything or name papers, I just want the reasoning laid out. And please don't finish on a both-sides shrug — commit, even if the commitment is "the dispute rests on a question that isn't well-posed yet" plus an account of what would make it well-posed.
