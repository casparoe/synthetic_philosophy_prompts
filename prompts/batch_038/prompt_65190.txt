You are a historian of early modern philosophy who works on Leibniz and on the metaphysics of individuation. Please think carefully and lay out your reasoning step by step rather than giving me a summary.

I am working through Leibniz's fourth paper in the exchange with Clarke (as printed in the 1717 *Collection of Papers*), and I am stuck on three consecutive sections:

> 4. There is no such thing as Two Individuals indiscernible from each other. An Ingenious Gentleman of my Acquaintance, discoursing with me, in the presence of Her Electoral Highness the Princess Sophia, in the Garden of Herrenhausen; thought he could find two Leaves perfectly alike. The Princess defied him to do it, and he ran all over the Garden a long time to look for some; but it was to no purpose. Two Drops of Water, or Milk, viewed with a Microscope, will appear distinguishable from each other. This is an Argument against Atoms; which are confuted, as well as a Vacuum, by the Principles of true Metaphysicks.
>
> 5. Those great Principles of a sufficient Reason, and of the Identity of Indiscernibles, change the State of Metaphysicks. That Science becomes real and demonstrative by means of these Principles; whereas before, it did generally consist in empty Words.
>
> 6. To suppose two things indiscernible, is to suppose the same thing under two Names. And therefore to suppose that the Universe could have had at first another position of Time and Place, than that which it actually had; and yet that all the Parts of the Universe should have had the same Situation among themselves, as that which they actually had; such a Supposition, I say, is an impossible Fiction.

Please help me understand these, on four points:

(a) Section 5 says the principle makes metaphysics "real and demonstrative." What work, then, is the microscope remark in section 4 doing? Is it offered as inductive evidence for the principle itself, as evidence that the principle's antecedent is never actually satisfied in nature, or as mere illustration that carries no argumentative weight? Set out the candidate readings and say which the text best supports.

(b) Make the inference in "This is an Argument against Atoms" explicit. Why should the impossibility of two indiscernible individuals bear on atomism in particular, and what must Leibniz be assuming about which differences between two atoms do and do not count as genuine?

(c) Is section 6's claim that to suppose two indiscernible things is to suppose "the same thing under two Names" a restatement of the principle, a further premise, or a conclusion drawn from it? And does it settle the later two-spheres case — a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres a mile apart — by ruling it out as incoherent, or does it merely express Leibniz's confidence that no such case is possible?

(d) Do these sections depend on Leibniz's denial, elsewhere in the same paper, that space is anything absolute? I want to know whether his argument against the two spheres stands or falls with his relationalism, or whether he also has a line that would survive granting the Newtonian view of space.
