I'm about a year into a novel with three strands — Adana, Oxford Mississippi, and a small town in Baden-Württemberg — all of them told by grandchildren who are digging in ground their grandparents won't walk on. A historian friend read sixty pages and told me the spine is too tidy. The spine is this: what decides whether a place ever really looks at what it did isn't schooling or repentance or having a brave chancellor, it's whether the people who did the thing kept the keys afterward. The courthouse, the school board, the police rolls, the archive, the thing that decides what counts as officially true.

Germany had its state smashed from outside and rebuilt with different people holding the keys, and it looked. That's the whole trick behind Vergangenheitsbewältigung, which is a word that costs you half a page every time you use it. The American South lost the war, didn't have the keys for about a decade, got them back in 1877, and spent the next forty years pouring bronze. Turkey's republic was built by men out of the old regime's machinery and the founding story couldn't afford the event, so the event doesn't exist. Japan I'm shakiest on: the occupation looked like a total break, but they kept the emperor on purpose and a lot of the bureaucracy with him, and the country spent the postwar decades as the bombed city rather than the bomber.

So — argue my case. As strongly as its possible to argue it, which means the version that takes the awkward material head on instead of stepping around it: that West Germany spent the fifties cheerfully rehiring former party members and only really turned in the sixties, twenty years after the keys supposedly changed hands; that the Occupation in Japan made the continuity deals knowingly, immunity for some of the medical researchers included; that the American South produced a real Black-led reckoning during the brief window when it didn't hold the keys and then unproduced it, thoroughly, down to the textbooks. If the keys claim survives all three, show me the mechanism and show me why it survives. If it only survives by being thinned into something vaguer — continuity among the people who decide what's true, rather than continuity among the people who did the thing — then argue that thinner version instead and tell me precisely what got lost in the thinning, because that's a structural decision I have to make about whose mouth each strand is told in.

Two house rules. Don't send me reading; I can't take on six books this month, and I want your reasoning, not your shelf. And at the end, tell me whether the claim actually predicts anything about a fourth case I'm not writing about — pick whichever one you think is genuinely diagnostic, I don't care which — or whether it's just an elegant way of arranging four stories I'd already fallen for. If it's the latter, say so. I'd rather know now then in a year.
