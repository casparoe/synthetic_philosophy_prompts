I'm in the fourth year of a philosophy of biology PhD, and this term I'm co-teaching a graduate seminar on the units-of-selection debate with a microbial ecologist who runs a synthetic evolution group two floors below us. Week six is where things usually go wrong: the students arrive having read the standard set pieces, and within ten minutes they've sorted themselves into a "selfish gene" camp and a "group selection" camp, apparently without noticing that the two camps are often answering different questions — who replicates, who interacts, who bears the adaptation, who benefits. To break the habit, my co-instructor and I want to open the session with a scripted argument between two members of his group about a system that does not actually exist, but whose stipulated properties I want treated throughout as fixed experimental data rather than as a toy model to be waved away.

The system: a population of engineered bacteria that secrete a sticky adhesin and thereby form multicellular aggregates. Cooperators carry an intact adhesin locus; cheaters carry a deletion that abolishes production while leaving them fully able to exploit the adhesin their neighbours make. The stipulations that matter are these. (i) Aggregates grow and reproduce by fragmenting, and fragmentation rate — which the lab treats as the aggregate's fitness — is measurably higher for aggregates with more adhesin, so there is a genuine aggregate-level phenotype, roughly the adhesin fraction, with aggregate-level consequences. (ii) Within an aggregate, cheaters outgrow cooperators because they do not pay the production cost. (iii) Most daughter aggregates arise by fragmentation of a parent aggregate and inherit its composition modulo drift, but a minority nucleate de novo from clumps of about eight free cells drawn from the well-mixed medium, so within-aggregate relatedness varies from high to moderate and the between-aggregate covariance partly reflects who happened to be adjacent at nucleation. (iv) The cheater deletion sits on a conjugative element that transfers between cells inside a single aggregate, and its transfer rate rises with local cell density and with the aggregate's adhesin content; consequently an aggregate can go from cheater-free to thoroughly mixed within one growth cycle without fragmenting at all, and an aggregate-level character causally modulates an element-level transmission rate.

The two characters — please invent names for them and treat them as fictional, since I don't want anyone's actual views paraphrased — occupy the following positions. One is a replicator-first theorist who holds that the only entities in this system satisfying a full reproduction-with-heredity criterion are the mobile element lineages, that the aggregate is at best an interactor whose "fitness" is a derived bookkeeping quantity, and that everything the other side calls aggregate-level selection is either assortment plus within-aggregate selection or an artefact of how the covariance is partitioned. The other is a multilevel-selection theorist who holds that aggregates are Darwinian individuals with a life cycle, heritable variation in aggregate-level characters, and aggregate-level descendants, and that the between-aggregate term picks out real aggregate-level causation precisely because stipulation (iv) gives the group character an effect on transmission that no cell-level or element-level description supplies.

Please write the exchange at roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words, as a lab-meeting conversation that drifts into the corridor on the way to the incubator room. Both parties must be fully competent: no strawmen, no one conceding the argument for the sake of a tidy ending, and each must give the other's strongest case enough traction to be worth answering. I want real back-and-forth in which each response addresses the point just made rather than restating a position. Concretely, the dialogue should contain at least the following moves. First, stipulation (iv) is the crux and must be argued over from both sides: the replicator theorist has to explain why an element that spreads by transfer into cells that never divide counts as reproducing rather than merely diffusing, and the multilevel theorist has to say what survives of group heredity when a group can acquire an entirely new composition without reproducing — including whether they are willing to retreat to a regression-style, statistical notion of group heritability, and what that retreat costs them. Second, one of them should press the question whether the between-aggregate term is evidence of aggregate-level causation or only a record of assortment at nucleation, and should propose a specific controlled measurement that would discriminate; the other should explain either why that measurement cannot be run in this system or why running it would not settle anything, so that it becomes clear the residual dispute is not merely empirical. Third, the replicator theorist must say why their view is not simply kin selection relabelled, and the multilevel theorist must say how their view differs from a trait-group model in which only cells have fitness and groups merely structure it. Fourth, include at least two moments where one character concedes a genuine sub-point to the other, and at least one moment where one of them proposes redefining a term and the other objects that the redefinition begs the question.

End without reconciliation, but not without diagnosis: have them state plainly the one assumption each cannot discharge, and say whether they think the surviving disagreement is substantive or partly verbal — and if verbal, which word is doing the equivocation, among reproduction, fitness, heritability, individual, and unit. Two formatting constraints. Do not cite, quote, or name any author, paper, or book anywhere in the dialogue; the students have the reading list already and what I need from you is the reasoning. Do feel free to use the standard conceptual vocabulary of the debate — the Price-style partition into within- and between-group components, contextual analysis, the neighbour approach, the distinction between group-level fitness in the sense of groups as parents of groups and trait-group models in which only individuals have fitness, cross-level byproducts, bottlenecks, relatedness, heritability construed as regression — as long as the terms are explained in the characters' own words by how they use them.
