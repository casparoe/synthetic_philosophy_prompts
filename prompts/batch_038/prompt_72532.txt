I'm writing the methods annex for a national wellbeing framework at my ministry and a colleague told me to just write down what I actually believe before I start arguing about instruments. So here's the list. Tell me whether it hangs together.

1. Wellbeing is what policy is for, and the only way to find out about it is to ask people. Objective dashboards — income, housing square metres, healthy life expectancy — can't tell you whether lives are going well, so I don't want one in the headline.

2. If you're only going to headline one question it should be the life-evaluation ladder, not the affect items. Affect questions just pick up whatever mood someone is in when the interviewer happens to call. And I take the focusing illusion seriously — people massively overweight whatever is salient when you ask them about one slice of their life — so I'd rather make them weigh the whole thing.

3. Take the answers at face value. If a woman in a genuinely bad situation says her life is going alright, that's data, not false consciousness. A framework that quietly overrides what people tell us about their own lives is not one I want to sign my name to.

4. All the same: an intervention whose only effect is to help people adjust to circumstances that haven't changed — resilience courses, reframing, social comparison — has not improved wellbeing, even when the average score goes up.

5. Cross-country comparisons of average levels are shaky. Translation, scale use, response styles. Gaps of a couple of tenths between neighbouring ranks in the league table are noise and shouldn't be presented as an ordering, and I'd fight anyone who publishes one that way.

6. But distribution within a country is not just a taste thing. At equal averages I'd take the society where everyone scores about 6 over the one where the bottom fifth scores 4 and the top fifth scores 8 — and I mean that as a claim about who is actually worse off, not as a preference for equality as such.

7. And I still want one headline number in the annual report, because that is the only thing the political process will engage with at all.

Go through these and tell me which ones are genuinely pulling against each other and which only look like they are — I have no idea where the cracks are and no view on what I'd give up. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each say what would actually have to go and what it costs me to drop it. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
