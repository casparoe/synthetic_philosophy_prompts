**DRAFT: "The Price of Self-Location: Truncated Memory and the Absent-Minded Agent"**

**1. The driver.** The standard version runs as follows. A driver is planning a journey along a motorway with two exits. Taking the first exit is worth 1 to him; taking the second is worth 4; driving past both is worth nothing. He is absent-minded in a precise sense: when he arrives at an exit he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second, so the two decision nodes sit in a single information set. Viewed from the kerb before he sets off, the problem looks trivial — pass the first exit, take the second, collect 4. But that plan cannot be executed, because executing it requires distinguishing nodes the driver cannot distinguish. What he can execute is a single probability *r* of exiting whenever he finds himself at an exit. That policy is worth U(*r*) = *r*·1 + (1 − *r*)·*r*·4 = 5*r* − 4*r*², which is maximised at *r* = 5/8 and worth 25/16, a little under 1.56. In order to be able to act at all, the driver surrenders rather more than sixty per cent of the value that was available to him in principle.

**2. The instability.** The interesting feature is not the loss but the circularity. Suppose the driver adopts the plan "continue at the first exit, exit at the second". Arriving at an exit, he knows his own plan. Under that plan both nodes are reached with probability one, so the natural self-locating credence is one half on each. Exiting then has expected value 2.5 against 2 for continuing: the plan prescribes an action that is worse than its alternative in the light of the very credences the plan generates. Now suppose instead he adopts "exit at every exit". Under that policy the second node is reached with probability zero, so on arriving at an exit he ought to be certain that it is the first; continuing is then worth 4 against 1 for exiting, and once again the plan undercuts itself. No pure plan survives contact with the evidence it produces.

Here, I think, is something worth stating explicitly, because it does not seem to be part of the usual telling. Let the policy be to exit with probability *r* at whichever exit he reaches. The first node is then reached with probability 1 and the second with probability 1 − *r*, so credences apportioned by reach probability give him 1/(2 − *r*) on the first exit and (1 − *r*)/(2 − *r*) on the second. Exiting at the junction is worth (5 − 4*r*)/(2 − *r*); continuing is worth 4*r*/(2 − *r*), since continuing from the first exit returns him to a junction where he exits with probability *r*, and continuing from the second returns him nothing. The two are equal exactly when *r* = 5/8. So the value-maximising policy is also the only policy that is self-verifying: it generates credences relative to which the driver is precisely indifferent, and his local deliberation therefore gives him no purchase on it. Every other policy is locally defeatable. The coincidence is striking, and I want to lean on it.

**3. Transfer.** Consider an autonomous inspection agent working a long pipeline on a remote installation. Its working context is compressed every *k* steps, and the compression scheme is tuned to task relevance, so two checkpoints in the pipeline — call them C1 and C2 — are represented identically once the intervening steps have been discarded. At a checkpoint the agent either aborts and vents, or continues. Aborting at C1 secures a modest saving, worth *a*; continuing past C1 and aborting at C2 completes the inspection properly, worth *b*; continuing past both destroys the installation, worth nothing. The agent could carry a persistent counter recording how many checkpoints it has passed, but the counter is expensive: it must survive compression, and the memory and bandwidth it consumes are drawn from the same budget as the inspection itself.

Note the asymmetry that makes the case philosophically interesting. The driver's absent-mindedness is a cognitive limitation imposed on him; the agent's is a design choice made by someone else, who is never herself absent-minded. For engineered agents, then, the information set is a parameter rather than a fate, and the question is not "what should the agent believe about where it is?" but "what compression scheme and what policy should the designer select?".

**4. Policy-relative individuation.** This is where I want to make a claim I do not think is standard, and I coin a term for it. Which node an agent occupies is not a fact antecedent to the policy it adopts; the policy fixes the reach probabilities, and the reach probabilities fix the self-locating credences. It follows, I suggest, that the familiar objection to evaluating such agents by their policies rather than by their plans — namely, that policy-based evaluation ignores the agent's actual evidence about where it is — misdescribes the situation. There is no policy-independent evidence to ignore. Call this *policy-relative individuation of decision nodes*. On this view, evaluating the absent-minded agent by what it does at a node rather than by what it plans to do at a pair of nodes it cannot tell apart is not a second-best concession to a cognitive defect. It is the correct normative standard for any agent whose memory is a designed object.

**5. The price of self-location.** The general calculation is short. With payoffs *a* at the first checkpoint and *b* at the second, *b* > *a* > 0, and nothing for passing both, a policy of acting with probability *r* at every checkpoint is worth U(*r*) = *ar* + *br*(1 − *r*), maximised at *r*\* = (*a* + *b*)/2*b* and worth (*a* + *b*)²/4*b*. An agent with a persistent counter has two distinct nodes and simply takes the second checkpoint, collecting *b*. The most a designer should therefore pay for one bit of durable self-location — the *price of self-location* — is

*b* − (*a* + *b*)²/4*b* = (*b* − *a*)(3*b* + *a*)/4*b*.

For the driver's 1 and 4 this is 39/16, about 2.44. The formula has the right shape: the price vanishes as *a* approaches *b*, since there is then nothing to gain from knowing where one is, and rises towards three quarters of *b* as *a* falls towards nothing, since the more lopsided the payoffs the more is lost by not being able to tell the checkpoints apart. What began as a paradox about a forgetful motorist thus yields a procurement rule: buy the marker whenever it costs less than that.

**6. Residual worries.** Three things I have not resolved. First, the whole of section 2 depends on apportioning self-locating credence by reach probability, and I do not know whether the coincidence at *r* = 5/8 is a discovery about the driver or an artefact of that rule. Second, the engineered case is not really a case about one agent: the designer has all the evidence the agent lacks, so perhaps the analogy relocates the puzzle rather than solving it. Third, I cannot tell whether policy-relative individuation says anything that a careful redescription of the action-averaged reading of such problems does not already say.
