Since March, the two of us who run a small quantitative consultancy have been deadlocked over whether to keep funding a portfolio company's core technology. We have shared every document, sat through the same site visits, and put our numbers in writing at every partnership meeting: I put the probability of technical success at about 0.2, he puts it at about 0.7. Last week our third partner, who holds an economics doctorate, circulated a memo arguing that this deadlock is not a substantive disagreement at all but a demonstration that one of us has lapsed somewhere, and that the only rational course is to suspend. I would like to find the flaw before Thursday's meeting, and I cannot. Here is his reasoning, numbered by me; I have tried to state each step as strongly and as charitably as I can.

1. Aumann's agreement theorem: if two agents are Bayesian and share a common prior, then their posterior probabilities for the same event cannot be common knowledge between them unless those posteriors are equal. Rational agents cannot agree to disagree.
2. This is a logical rather than a practical limitation: no state of affairs is describable in which a common prior, Bayesian updating, and common knowledge of unequal posteriors all obtain together.
3. We satisfy the anteced. We have a common prior (4), we both update by conditionalization (5), and our posteriors are common knowledge between us (6).
4. On the prior: nothing about the world made one of us an optimist and the other a pessimist. A difference in priors would itself have to be justified by some difference in evidence, and we have had no such difference — we have read the same documents. He adds that Aumann himself holds that people with similar backgrounds cannot justifiably carry different priors in the first place.
5. On updating: we are both trained in this, both careful, neither of us has ever been caught misweighting evidence, and each would correct himself on being shown a mistake.
6. On common knowledge: our numbers have been stated aloud, in writing, before the whole partnership, monthly, for eight months. Everyone knows everyone's number, everyone knows that everyone knows, and there is no depth at which the iteration stops.
7. Therefore the situation we are in is impossible.
8. Since it is nonetheless the situation we are in, at least one of the conditions in (3) must be merely apparent — and we are inside the case, so we cannot tell which. It might be your rationality, my rationality, our priors, or the common-knowledge claim.
9. Where several necessary conditions are known to have failed jointly and you cannot identify which one, continuing to rely on the output they were supposed to guarantee is arbitrary. So neither of us may keep his number.
10. Nothing in (1) through (9) mentions our technology, our temperaments, or our evidence. Hence: no two mutually transparent, honest, and competent people can rationally disagree about anything, and every standing disagreement among such people — in a laboratory, a boardroom, a courtroom, a philosophy department — is evidence that someone has lapsed. As no participant can tell who, every such disagreement obliges all parties to suspend.
11. Therefore our deadlock is not to be settled by further argument. We should stop funding the company and hold no view until we no longer differ.

I cannot accept (11), and (10) strikes me as flatly incredible: long-running disputes between people who share every scrap of evidence are common in my field, and if (10) were true, dissent would be a pathology and the whole apparatus of adversarial review, minority opinions, and red-teaming would be incoherent. Yet I cannot point to a step that is simply false, and I would rather not have to reject (1) or (2) — I believe the theorem, and I do not want to give up conditionalization, which is how I earn my living. I am also attached to (4), for what that is worth.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable as it stands? Where exactly does the memo lose something it needs — is it an equivocation, a scope shift, a move from an impossibility result to a normative conclusion, or something else — and what does the honest repair cost me? If some conclusion weaker than (10) survives the repair, tell me what the strongest survivable version says about our deadlock. And tell me whether (9) has any independent plausibility at all, or whether it is doing most of the damage while looking like bookkeeping. Please also say how confident you are in your diagnosis and in the repair, and single out the one point in your answer that you are least sure of.
