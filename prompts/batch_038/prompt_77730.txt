im in a philosophy of psychiatry seminar and I've gotten a bit too attached to a strong version of Hacking's looping-effects picture. Roughly what I think: psychiatric categories are interactive kinds, not indifferent ones, so publishing a diagnosis changes the people who get sorted under it, which changes the category, which changes the people again -- and my further claim, the one I actually want pressure-tested, is that this means psychiatric epidemiology can't accumulate. Every prevalence figure is more like a self-portrait of a classified population at a moment than a measurement of something that was sitting there waiting to be counted.

So: what would count against that? I don't want "Hacking's case studies are old news," I want the real defeaters -- kinds that stabilized instead of looping, evidence that big cross-national or cross-decade swings in prevalence are better explained by ascertainment, screening changes or criterion shifts than by anyone's self-understanding moving, and the worry that looping isn't special to the human kinds at all (does a BRCA-carrier identity or a marketing label loop in exactly the same way?). Also tell me which of these a committed looping-effects defender would themselves concede as damaging rather than explain away, and be blunt about whether my epidemiology claim has been insulated past the point of risk -- since if a kind stops looping I can say the loop ran its course, and if it keeps looping that's a confirmation, which looks bad.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, ranked from most to least damaging, with the fifth being your verdict on whether the view still risks anything. No introduction and no conclusion.
