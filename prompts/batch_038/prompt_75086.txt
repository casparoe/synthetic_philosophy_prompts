I'm in year three of a PhD that's officially about testimony in epistemology but has drifted pretty far into aesthetics, and I'm increasingly worried that the aesthetic testimony debate is the kind of thing that can never get settled. Rough sketch of the landscape as I see it. There are the pessimists: testimony about a work's aesthetic qualities can't give you what you need, you have to hear the piece or stand in front of the painting yourself, because aesthetic judgment is governed by something like a first-hand acquaintance requirement. There are the optimists, who say that's special pleading — the claim that the concerto is moving is just a claim about how the world is, testimony is testimony, and if it comes from someone reliable there's no principled reason the resulting belief can't amount to knowledge. And there's a third group who think the whole thing is a category error, because aesthetic judgments aren't really truth-apt beliefs at all but expressions of a response, so there was never a knowledge question on the table.

What bugs me is that all three accept basically the same cases: the friend who won't shut up about a novel you haven't read, the reviewer's savage pan, the docent who says the piece really rewards slow looking. Nobody disputes anything about testimony in general — these are people who happily grant that say-so can transmit knowledge in pretty much every other domain. And still nobody moves, and the papers mostly consist of restating the same intuition from a new angle.

So what kind of disagreement is this actually? My hunch, offered as a hunch and not as something I want you to just endorse: it's a fight about which mental state is the target, and the camps are answering different questions under one heading — whether a first-order proposition about a work can be known on someone's say-so, versus whether an aesthetic response can be had on someone's say-so, versus what the word judgment is even doing in this context. Related worry: I ran a small vignette study as part of my quals work, and people's verdicts about whether you should trust the critic flip hard depending on whether you ask them if the film is good or whether they'll like it, which makes me suspect there isn't a single stable folk notion of aesthetic belief for the debate to be about in the first place.

I want a diagnosis, not a tour of the positions and not a ruling on who's right. Tell me what you think the disagreement really is — verbal, substantive but resting on a hidden conceptual or empirical crux, two different questions wearing one label, or bedrock starting intuitions that neither side can argue for — and say which features of what each side actually says support your reading. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction, a reframing, a change of question, some piece of empirical work, whatever. And if your honest answer is that no progress should be expected, make that argument instead. Please write the whole thing in American English.
