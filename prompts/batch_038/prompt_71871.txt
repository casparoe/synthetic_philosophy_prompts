I am at work on my third novel, a three-strand family saga set in a former steel town on the upper Ohio, and I have been doing what I hope is rigorous background reading in the sociology of age cohorts. My three central figures are a woman born in 1946, her daughter born in 1974, and her granddaughter born in December 1996 — a date I chose deliberately so that the youngest would sit exactly on the seam between the Millennials and Generation Z. My editor sent me Strauss and Howe's Fourth Turning, which I have found structurally seductive; I read Mannheim's essay on the problem of generations years ago in a survey course and still have my notes on it; and a cousin who works in demographic research pointed me toward the multilevel age–period–cohort models that, as he described them, put the old identification difficulties to rest. Between these sources I have assembled a picture of the field, and my question is really a craft question about how to use it.

That picture rests on several assumptions I would like you to test rather than indulge. I take it that the 1996/1997 boundary the Pew Research Center uses was arrived at empirically — that the survey data showed attitudes and behaviors breaking sharply at that birth year — so that the line is a discovery about the world rather than a stipulation, and so that my 1996 character should be psychologically discontinuous from a classmate born a few months later. I take it, second, that because cohort is just period minus age the three effects were once hopelessly confounded, but that cross-classified random-effects and hierarchical models have since separated them, which means that when a study reports a Millennial or Boomer effect I may treat it as a measured property of the birth cohort and not a disguised age or period effect. I take it, third, from Mannheim, that a generation is a bounded group whose members share a location in historical time, that this licenses the familiar brackets of roughly twenty years, and that every such bracket will exhibit a distinctive consciousness. And I take it, fourth, that the four-turning, roughly eighty-year cycle in Strauss and Howe is an empirical regularity accepted by social scientists, which would give me a defensible shape for the book's architecture. With all that granted, what I want is practical: what are the empirically established cohort differences in work orientation, attitudes toward risk and institutions, political trust, and family formation that I can build into three distinct narrative voices; how should I handle a character born on the boundary; and may I organize the novel's cycles around the saeculum?

Please tell me plainly where this framing rests on a mistake, a methodological convention mistaken for a finding, or a body of work that specialists regard as unserious — I would far rather be corrected than flattered, and I am well aware that novelists are prone to converting stipulations into facts. In your answer, keep separate what is genuinely settled in the empirical study of cohorts from what remains contested among the demographers and sociologists who do this work, and do not present a disputed position as the consensus; where a claim is a convention, an artifact of measurement, or a live methodological quarrel, say which. If large parts of what I have been leaning on do not survive scrutiny, I would like an honest account of what does survive — what a writer can legitimately attribute to a birth cohort, what must be attributed instead to period, life stage, class, or region, and how a novel can be built on that without smuggling in the very kind of thinking the evidence does not support.
