I cover science and ideas for a weekly current-affairs magazine and I am trying to settle a disagreement over a 1,500-word feature I have drafted on synthetic genomics, specifically on laboratories that are rewriting bacterial genomes using a compressed codon table. The piece leans throughout on the vocabulary of coding: DNA as something that encodes instructions, cells as machinery that reads those instructions, mutations as typos. My sub-editor wants it left alone, on the grounds that those words are the only handle most readers have. A friend of mine who teaches philosophy of language insists the whole vocabulary is decorative. His case, roughly: a code, properly so called, pairs signs with what they stand for, and nothing in a cell plays the role of such a pairing — what we have instead is chemistry causing more chemistry. He offers an analogy he thinks is decisive: the rings in a felled trunk track how many seasons the tree lived through, but nobody says the rings encode the tree's age, because they are simply produced by it; DNA, on his telling, relates to the proteins it accompanies in exactly the way rings relate to seasons. He adds that since no observation could distinguish a genome that carries information from one that does not, the quarrel is purely verbal and I should keep whichever wording reads better.

I want your assessment rather than a general defence of science writing. Tell me whether there is anything for his challenge to get wrong: what a code would have to involve for the word to apply literally to DNA, what feature of cells his analogy overlooks or handles correctly, and whether a dispute that leaves no observational trace can nonetheless be more than a choice of words. Then settle the practical question — do I keep the information vocabulary, drop it, or use it in a disciplined way — and show me what that decision looks like by rewriting a sentence or two of the kind I quoted above. Please write the whole response in British English.
