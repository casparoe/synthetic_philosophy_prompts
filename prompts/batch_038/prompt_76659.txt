At a seminar session on luminosity last week, my advisor cut off an exchange about Williamson's anti-luminosity argument with the remark that "the mind is transparent to itself — that is just what it is to be in a mental state," and used it to suggest that the argument only goes through because it treats knowing as an external relation like any other. I suspect the remark is doing several distinct jobs at once, and that which job it is doing decides whether it is a platitude or a substantive thesis capable of defeating the argument.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the different precise claims that slogan might be expressing — ranging from restricted claims about occurrent conscious states, through self-intimation (that being in the state involves believing that one is in it), through incorrigibility, through the Evans-style point that one settles a question about one's own belief by settling the corresponding outward-looking question about the world, to any constitutive or interpretivist claim about what it is for a state to be mental at all — and for each say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false? I am especially interested in which of these readings, if any, would support a luminosity principle for knowledge, which would go all the way to the KK principle, and which are perfectly consistent with the anti-luminosity result.

One further worry to address: whether "transparent" is sliding between two incompatible senses — a medium one can see through without distortion, and a medium one does not need to look through at all — and whether the readings built on each sense fare differently against the argument. I would also welcome a brief verdict on which reading my advisor most likely had in mind, and why that one cannot bear the weight he put on it.
