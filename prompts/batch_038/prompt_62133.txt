ok so some context, because this has become weirdly personal. Last spring I got denied an apartment by an automated tenant-screening service — I still don't know what was in the report — and since then I've been volunteering with a small group pushing our city to regulate those tools. There's a council hearing in about three weeks and the property-management side has already signalled they'll argue regulation is pointless. Their best weapon turns out to be an op-ed one of thier guys wrote in a local business paper, and a friend scanned me the key paragraph. It reads:

"Mathematicians have proven that algorithmic fairness is impossible. A landmark result showed that a risk score which is calibrated — meaning that among the applicants given a score of 70%, 70% actually go on to default, no matter who they are — must violate equal error rates, so it will always flag more innocent people from one group than another. No algorithm escapes this, and no amount of auditing or regulation can fix it, because the conflict lives in the mathematics itself rather than in how any particular tool is built. Since base rates of the relevant behavior differ between groups in every real population, every screening tool faces the same dilemma. The only honest conclusion is to stop pretending a fairer algorithm is achievable and focus instead on what these tools actually deliver: faster, cheaper decisions."

I'm pretty sure this is a distortion, but I don't want to walk into that hearing and deny a real theorem, because that would go badly. So can you take it apart for me? I'd like it line by line: which sentences would a researcher who works on this nod at, which are the kind of simplification youd tolerate in a newspaper, and which are just wrong. And specifically:

- is calibration really incompatible with equal error rates in general, or only under some condition the op-ed quietly drops? If there's a condition, what happens in the cases it excludes, and are those cases fantasy or do they show up in practice?
- does the result say anything at all about whether a city can regulate, or is he smuggling in a policy conclusion the theorem never touches?
- "flag more innocent people from one group" — is that a fair description of what an error-rate imbalance under calibration actually looks like, or is he borrowing an intuition from the criminal-justice setting where it does something different?
- is this a theorem about machine learning, or would it bite just as hard on a human leasing agent making the same prediction by hand?
- he treats these statistical criteria as though they settle what fairness means. Is that assumption itself contested, and does the argument survive if it isn't granted?

Then, at the end: if someone repeats the "mathematically impossible, so give up" line at the hearing, what's the accurate and short thing I can say back? I've got maybe 90 seconds before they change the subject, so it needs to be something I can actually remember while nervous.
