I am a warehouse operations manager, and last autumn my employer moved me from a site outside Leeds to a plant near Rotterdam, so my family and I have spent the better part of a year learning which of the local rules are rules and which are merely habits. That experience has reopened a question I first got tangled up in as an undergraduate, and I should like to know whether my various judgements about it add up to anything principled, or whether I am simply inconsistent and ought to abandon one of them. A colleague at the plant insists the whole business is tribal signalling dressed up as decency, which I do not accept but also cannot properly refute.

Here are the cases, in roughly the order I noticed them, with my verdict on each. Cutting into the queue at a Leeds supermarket: wrong, and wrong even when the person behind you waves you forward and even when you have only one item in your basket. Doing the same at a bank of self-scans where there is no queue at all, only a loose knot of people: not wrong, though it feels as though it ought to be. Crossing against a red signal at three in the morning on an empty dual carriageway: not wrong. Crossing against a red signal at four in the afternoon while a cluster of schoolchildren waits at the kerb: wrong, and I think wrong even if none of them copies you and none of them is exposed to any risk by your doing it. Leaving nothing on the table after a Dutch restaurant meal where the staff are on a proper hourly wage: not wrong. Leaving nothing at a place I know divides the takings among servers who are paid almost nothing: wrong. Putting nothing in the jar at a counter where I ordered on a screen, collected my own coffee and carried my own tray back: not wrong, and yet I feel a flicker of shame there that I cannot account for at all. My neighbour, who has lived here thirty years, doing ten kilometres over the limit on the ring road: not wrong, or barely. Me, a newcomer, doing the same: worse of me, which strikes me as backwards, since he is the one who knows where the cameras are. Refusing to remove my shoes in a colleague's hallway: wrong in a way that has nothing to do with shoes. Refusing a fourth helping when I have been warned that refusal offends: not wrong, I think, and yet I refuse far less often here than I would at home, and I cannot tell whether that is good manners or cowardice. Someone who cuts into queues only ever in towns they will never visit again: worse, not better, than someone who cuts into the queue in their own town.

My rough rule so far is that a convention binds you only where breaking it either harms someone you could point at or takes a benefit from a cooperative arrangement you yourself rely on, and that visitors are held to a lower standard on the first half of that and a higher one on the second, because they are guests. The rule already looks strained. It makes the empty-street crossing and the schoolchildren crossing come out the same; it cannot explain the tip jar, since I neither harm that barista nor free-ride on any scheme she maintains; and it seems to predict the opposite of what I feel about the neighbour's speeding. So: what principle, or small set of competing candidates, best captures this pattern? Please state it precisely, indicate which of the cases above motivated each clause of it, and tell me what it delivers on cases I have not listed — a cyclist who rides through a red where a separate bicycle signal exists and is showing green for traffic, a traveller who lets a friend hold their place in a queue for a popular exhibition, an expatriate who still tips generously when visiting home out of habit and resents every cent of it here, and a resident who quietly stops observing a local queueing practice because the shop that used it has been sold and the new owners do not care. I would also like a direct answer on whether the visitor asymmetry I keep reaching for has any principled basis or is a prejudice I should discard. Be honest about which of my verdicts no plausible principle can save and which I should simply give up, and if it turns out that my list has been tracking two or three genuinely distinct kinds of thing that I have been lumping together under one heading, say so and draw the divisions explicitly rather than forcing them into a single rule. Please write your answer in British English.
