I'm in the third week of a graduate reading group on the concept of liberty. We started from the familiar claim that non-domination is meant to be a genuine third option alongside negative and positive liberty — not the absence of interference, not self-mastery, but the absence of subjection to another's will — and we spent a session on the case that usually gets cited to show the third option is doing real work: the slave whose master is benevolent, never once raises a hand, and might go a lifetime without interfering, yet is unfree throughout, because at every moment what the master does or refrains from doing is up to the master and not up to the slave. That case is why I became a republican about freedom in the first place, so I want to keep it. One of the other members then built the following argument, which he says uses nothing but the republican's own premises, and whose conclusion is that the third concept is not a third concept at all but a muddled way of restating the first.

(1) On the republican account, you are unfree to the extent that another agent holds a capacity to interfere arbitrarily in your choices, where arbitrary interference means interference whose timing and content are not compelled to track your own interests and expressed opinions.
(2) What matters is the capacity, not its exercise: the slave of the benevolent master is unfree even though nothing happens to him, because the master need never answer to him for what he might do.
(3) So freedom is a matter of how much discretionary power over you others hold — power they may use or withhold at their own option.
(4) The state holds power of exactly that kind. Every constitutional limit on it is a limit the state itself laid down and can repeal, reinterpret, or suspend; a constraint that the constrained party could not undo would not be a constraint on that party's power at all.
(5) So each citizen stands to the state as the slave stands to the benevolent master: another agent can interfere in his choices and owes him no accounting for doing so.
(6) Voting and constitutional review do not change this. They are the state's good temper, not the removal of the slave's chains: the disposition to refrain is, at every moment, a disposition the state may simply cease to have, and a power that can be exercised without the say-so of those it falls on is discretionary whatever its current habits happen to be.
(7) Republicans separately hold that without the state there would be pervasive private domination, and that the state is what shields the weak from the strong.
(8) So the state is at once indispensable to the elimination of domination and constitutive of it, and the better it performs the first role, the more completely everyone is subject to a single unaccountable power.
(9) Therefore non-domination is incoherent as an ideal. Either the capacity in (2) is what matters, in which case every citizen of every state is unfree and freedom is unattainable; or it is not, in which case the slave of the benevolent master is free after all, and the republican collapses back into the thesis that law as such diminishes liberty — the thesis the whole tradition was assembled to deny.

My instinct is to break it at (4), since I don't see why the revisability of a limit should show that it isn't a limit, but I have no non-question-begging way to say so: when I offered that objection, he replied that I had simply asserted that constitutions bind, which is precisely what (4) denies. I also suspect that breaking (4) wouldn't be enough on its own, because (6) appears to make the same point again by a different route. So: which transition do you reject, and why did it look permissible when it was made — was the argument valid with a false premise somewhere, or is one of the steps simply not licensed by what precedes it? What does your chosen rejection cost the republican, in the sense of what machinery has to be added and where that machinery looks most strained? And please test your diagnosis against two cases I care about: the citizen of a stable democracy whose side has lost every election for forty years and expects to lose the next ten, and the long-term resident who is fully subject to the law but has no vote. If your account makes both of them free, tell me why that isn't a reductio of your account; if it makes one of them unfree, tell me which principle does the distinguishing. Finally, does (9) follow even if the argument succeeds — that is, is the fallback really plain non-interference, or does the escape route he blocks commit the republican to something considerably more demanding than either of Berlin's concepts? I'm not looking for references or a reading list; I'll chase down the primary texts myself.
