You are a philosopher of mind who also has a working knowledge of measurement science. Please treat this as a serious piece of conceptual work rather than a summary, and sketch your mapping in rough before writing it up cleanly.

I spent forty years in analytical chemistry, the last decade of it running a calibration laboratory: reference materials, traceability chains, uncertainty budgets, instrument qualification — the whole discipline of deciding whether a changed reading means the sample changed or the instrument drifted. I am retired, and philosophy is a late acquisition. I have been reading Dennett's essay on quining qualia, including the case of the two coffee tasters, and I find I keep translating it into my own trade as I go.

Do that translation properly for me. Restate the tasters' predicament, and the conclusion Dennett draws from it about the coherence of qualia, entirely in metrological vocabulary: measurand, standard, bias, drift, repeatability, calibration hierarchy, and the standing requirement that any check on an instrument be independent of the instrument being checked. Identify which of his premises become claims about the absence of a higher-order standard, and which of his conclusions become claims about what can and cannot be resolved from a single subject's series of reports. If you are unsure of his exact wording, paraphrase rather than quote; I have the text in front of me and I care about the structure of the argument.

Then the residue report, which is what I really want. Sort his claims into three buckets — those that survive intact, those that survive only with distortion (saying precisely how you distorted them), and those with no expression at all in my vocabulary. A table is acceptable for this. For the third bucket, decide explicitly whether the failure is a limitation of metrology, which quietly presupposes an observer-independent measurand and a well-defined accuracy relation that first-person reports may simply lack, or whether it is evidence that the posit of qualia was never as determinate as its defenders suppose. I suspect the two failure modes get conflated; tell me where.

Finally, an addition of my own. In my trade the deepest trouble arises when the certificate for a reference material was issued by the very instrument under test. Ask yourself whether the tasters' case contains an analogue of that circularity, and if it does, whether it strengthens Dennett's argument or merely restates it.
