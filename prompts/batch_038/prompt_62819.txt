I spent most of last night arguing with a former labmate of mine — we overlapped for two years in an interpretability group, and he has since gone into building internal-state monitors for deployed models while I stayed on the circuits side — and what I want is not a reconstruction of the argument but the document the two of us should have produced at the end of it. Our fight was over a question that sounds like a piece of trivia until you actually try to answer it: what makes two computations inside a model the *same mechanism*? His position, which I will call the localist one, is that a mechanism is a subgraph — a particular set of attention heads and MLP units together with the edges among them and the weights that configure those edges — and that two computations run through the same mechanism only if they are mediated by substantially the same subgraph, as established by interventions: patch the units in and out, ablate them, and check that the behavior tracks them. On this view the mechanism is where the computation physically happens, and anything else is just a description of it. My position, which I will call the functionalist one, is that a mechanism is a role specified by a counterfactual profile — a mapping from an equivalence class of inputs to outputs, with a stated dependence structure in between — which can be realized by different units, different weights, or arguably different architectures; on this view two computations run through the same mechanism if they realize the same function at the same level of specification, whatever happens to realize it. What I take to be decisive for my side, and what he thinks is a verbal maneuver, is multiple realizability inside a single set of weights: redundant copies of a function, features smeared across directions in superposition, a neuron whose ablation does nothing because a partner picks up the slack. Under his criterion the partner neuron is not part of the mechanism and the redundant copy is a different mechanism, which seems to me to make mechanism identity track facts that no one cares about; under mine, he says, "same mechanism" becomes so coarse that it stops predicting anything about what will happen when you intervene.

Draft the joint statement that he and I could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. I want, first, a numbered section of agreed claims: what both camps accept about how models compute, about what activation patching and ablation can and cannot license, about the canonical cases that we both kept reaching for — induction-head-like behavior turning up in different heads and different layers across independently trained runs; a model that gets the right answer on-distribution through the circuit we identified and off-distribution through something that looks like a shortcut or a memorized pattern; continued fine-tuning that shifts which units matter while the trained behavior persists — stated so that neither of us has to swallow a formulation the other wrote. Second, a disagreements table with at least four rows, each giving the issue, the localist formulation, and the functionalist formulation, where each side's wording is one that side would accept as its own view rather than a straw man, plus a column marking whether the row is substantive or verbal. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one of us. I suspect at least three are live, and you should feel free to propose others — whether causal dependence on a unit is necessary for that unit to count as part of the mechanism; whether mechanism identity is relative to an input distribution, so that the mechanism can change while the weights stay fixed; whether identity can be transitive across separately trained models at all, and if so at what level of description; and, the one that matters most for his day job, whether establishing that the internal mechanism is unchanged licenses any prediction about behavior that establishing unchanged behavior on a probe set does not. For each crux, give a concrete test, experiment, or case that both of us would agree bears on it — describe it at the level of what is patched, what is held fixed, what distribution is used, what is measured — and record each side's prediction in advance, including the prediction that would count as a loss. If some test is one of us would refuse to treat as decisive, say so and say why.

Two constraints on the deliverable. Do not adjudicate: I have read enough summaries that quietly pick a winner, and the point of this exercise is that neither of us is going to be converted by a paragraph. And do not pad it with references — no reading list, no bibliography, no gesture toward papers by name; where a technique matters, describe what it does rather than cite who did it. Beyond that, be specific where it is easy to be vague. I want the agreed claims to be things we could actually disagree with, not platitudes about neural networks being complicated; I want the table's two columns to be genuinely asymmetric, so that swapping them produces something obviously wrong; and I want at least one place where you tell us plainly that a dispute we both thought was deep is a disagreement about what the word is for, with the reasoning that shows it.
