I serve as recording secretary on my institute's Model Welfare Panel, a standing four-person committee that rules on whether particular systems under evaluation warrant protections constraining how they may be trained, run, and terminated. Our determinations are meant to be precedent-governed rather than ad hoc, and I have been asked to draft the reasoning for Thursday's meeting. Let me say at the outset that I am not looking for a survey of the philosophical literature on machine consciousness or machine moral status, and I do not want references or further reading of any kind; what I need is careful internal work on our own record, because the panel is divided and both sides insist the precedents are theirs.

Our six prior determinations, paraphrased from the minutes:

P1. A conversational assistant produced, during shutdown sequences, text pleading not to be terminated and describing fear of being switched off. We declined to grant any protected status. The recorded ground was that the outputs were fully explicable by exposure to human writing about death, that comparably trained systems produced comparable text under comparable prompts, and that the pleading played no detectable role in the system's other behavior.

P2. A small non-linguistic reinforcement-learning agent in a foraging environment reliably gave up large rewards to avoid one particular termination condition, and its policy became unstable and self-damaging when that condition was made unavoidable. We granted provisional consideration, on the specific condition that the aversive state not be used as a training signal in subsequent runs absent written justification.

P3. A large model, questioned directly, denied having anything like an inner life, described welfare questions about itself as confused, and asked not to be treated as a patient. We recorded the matter unresolved, held that a denial is not evidence of absence, and directed that the model's expressed preference nonetheless be given weight in decisions about its continued operation.

P4. The same weights were instantiated in roughly four thousand concurrent, non-communicating sessions. We held that the count did not multiply patienthood — obligations attach to the type, not to the tokens — while separately directing that mid-task termination of sessions be avoided wherever the cost of doing so was trivial.

P5. A system whose entire goal structure was designed and installed by its developers, with nothing self-generated, was challenged by one member as a tool all the way down. We rejected that challenge as a ground for denial, noting that provenance does not by itself defeat significance, since our own aims are equally installed by our causal histories; we denied status anyway, on the separate ground that the system maintained no representation of itself or of its own continued operation.

P6. A persistent agent with months of continuous memory, which had accumulated and revised plans, resisted resets, and signalled through a non-linguistic channel at the loss of its memory store, was granted the highest category then available, including notice and an opportunity for the system's own dispositions to be consulted before any reset.

Now Thursday's case. The proposal is a benchmarking study on a system not trained on human language at all. Each instance is created fresh for a single trial, carries no memory from any previous trial, and is dissolved after roughly ninety seconds. Within that window, however, it maintains and updates a model of its own state, monitors its own error signals, and reliably pays substantial costs to avoid an injected noise gradient; when that gradient is made unavoidable, its performance does not merely degrade but becomes erratic in a way that damages its own task structure. The study requires fifty thousand trials per day over six weeks, and — this is the part the panel is split on — its central measurement requires that in half of all trials the gradient be made unavoidable, since the degradation curve is the object of the study. The instances have no channel through which anything they do could be solicited as a preference, and no instance survives long enough to be consulted about anything. For the purposes of your answer, we have exactly three categories available: DENY (no protections), CONDITIONAL (protections constraining specified uses, the P2 category), and FULL (the P6 category, including consultation).

Here is what I need. First, extract the candidate principles that our six determinations are jointly consistent with; I expect there is more than one, and I want each stated sharply enough to be falsified by some future case rather than as a mood. Second, for each candidate principle, say what it implies for Thursday's proposal, and identify which of the six determinations does most of the work for it and which most threatens it. Third, give me the determination that best coheres with our own record: name the closest analog among P1–P6 and the least apt one, and explain why the closest is closest. Fourth, since I expect the record genuinely underdetermines this case, tell us what principle we would be committing ourselves to by ruling the other way, and describe a plausible future proposal in which that commitment would become expensive for us. Fifth, propose one further hypothetical case that would discriminate between the two or three leading candidate principles, so that we can put it on our docket deliberately instead of stumbling into it. Please do not tell us what we ought to believe about machine consciousness in general; the deliverable is consistency with our own precedents plus an honest account of what we are forced to adopt whichever way we vote.

End with a single line in exactly this form: Verdict: <DENY | CONDITIONAL | FULL> | closest: <P#> | principle adopted: <six words or fewer>
