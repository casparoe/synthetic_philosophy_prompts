I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently drafting a long feature about why the arguments for radical giving that circulated so widely over the past decade or so largely failed to persuade the audiences they were aimed at. The piece includes a sidebar in which I intend to state my own position plainly, on the principle that a columnist who asks readers to take a moral argument seriously owes them the argument as he actually holds it rather than a version sanded down for print. Two colleagues have now read a draft of that sidebar, and both came back with the same complaint from different directions: that the position is not internally coherent, though they disagreed about which parts I should cut. I cannot settle that by fiat, and I would rather find the fault myself than have a reader find it for me, so I would like a proper audit before I file. Here is the position, more or less in the terms the sidebar uses.

1. I am a consequentialist of an agent-neutral kind: what I have most reason to do is whatever would bring about the most good, impartially reckoned, with no built-in permission to weight my own projects and people more heavily simply because they are mine. I reject the hybrid move — the view that a moral theory should grant the agent a standing prerogative to devote some of her energy to her own concerns — because I do not see how a theory can simply stipulate a permission to discount the very considerations that justify it in the first place.

2. The demandingness objection, in the form "a view that would wreck my life cannot be right," gets no traction at all in a world distributed like ours. The cost it cites is measured against a baseline of comfort that a particular arrangement of resources happens to deliver, and that baseline has no claim to be respected. In a justly ordered world I would happily grant such objections weight; in this one I think they are nearly always rationalization, and the sidebar says so bluntly.

3. Supererogation is real, and I want to keep it. Someone who gives away half her income does something morally better than someone who gives a tenth, that difference matters, and the second person is not to be blamed for falling short of it. A moral vocabulary that can register only "duty done" and "duty failed" leaves out too much of what we actually care about, and I lean on the vocabulary of duty heavily elsewhere in my work.

4. There is a feasibility constraint on moral demands, but I want to state it psychologically rather than logically. Not the ability sense of ought-implies-can, but something like this: a demand that no ordinary human agent could live under for a decade without coming apart — losing the capacity to deliberate, to keep her commitments, to be someone others can rely on — is not a demand morality is capable of making.

5. The right unit of assessment for questions of this kind is a life, or a standing pattern of conduct, rather than a single act at a single moment. Whether it is wrong for me to keep a second flat in the city rather than accept a long commute is not settled by the marginal value of the difference in rent; it is settled by the pattern that the choice is an instance of.

6. I lean hardest on something I have been calling the drift test: a moral demand fails if, were people generally to try to meet it, the attempt would predictably erode the dispositions that make them capable of meeting demands at all. I present this in the sidebar as a structural point about moral theories rather than a complaint about their costliness, and I take it to be what does the real work in my argument — my objection to the more extreme versions of the giving arguments is that they are drift-prone, not that they are expensive.

I also commit myself to a verdict on a case I invented for the piece. Nadia, a structural engineer, has given a tenth of her income for eleven years; she could raise that to two fifths by moving into a job auditing projects for a lender, work she finds numbing, and by abandoning the plan she and her partner have had for years to have children. I say flatly that the second path is not required of her. And I want to say it without appealing to the thought that her life is hers to shape, which is precisely the move I have rejected in (1).

What I want from you is the audit, not a lecture: tell me which of these commitments pull against one another and how badly, ordered by severity rather than listed as equally serious; for each, say exactly what would have to give and what I would lose by giving it; identify separately any pairs that only look like they conflict, since I suspect at least one does and I would rather not be argued out of something I can keep; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I am especially interested in whether my invented case and the drift test can be delivered by the rest of the package, or whether they are doing work that nothing else in the list licenses. Two things I am not asking for: a survey of the debate over demandingness, and a list of what I ought to read next. I have read the well-known statements of both sides; what I cannot do is see my own view from the outside.
