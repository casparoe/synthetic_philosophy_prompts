Thirty-five years in comparative physiology, now retired and reading philosophy with more time than rigour. One summer at a field station I ran the old displacement trick on a digger wasp myself: she hauled her paralysed prey to the mouth of the burrow, went inside to inspect it, and when I moved the prey a few centimetres she hauled it back and inspected again — six times, seven times, and on one occasion she abandoned the routine altogether and simply walked it in. I have since learned that philosophers have a word for the rigidity, sphexishness, but it is not the word that interests me; it is the sentence people apply to her. In my reading group that sentence is always the same: "she is only following her programming." Last week it did work in two directions within ten minutes. An engineer used it to dismiss a conversational system's apparent flexibility — it only follows its programming, so nothing it produces is genuine — and another member used it about the rest of us, to the opposite effect: we too only follow our programming, so no one is answerable for anything.

What I want is a menu of the distinct precise claims that one sentence might be making, arranged from the readings no biologist would dispute to the readings that are simply false: what "programming" is standing for (genetic instructions, synaptic weights, the laws of physics, some designer's intentions), and what "only following" is standing for (inflexibility, absence of origination, absence of representation, causal determination, absence of alternative possibilities). For each reading, say whether it holds of the wasp, of the conversational system, and of me; then say which readings would actually license the engineer's dismissal, which would license the fatalist's, and whether any single reading can perform both jobs without equivocation. Finally, tell me what becomes of the readings that rest on the wasp's rigidity if that rigidity is less complete than the anecdote advertises — for a wasp that re-inspects a nest someone has been tampering with may be doing something sensible rather than something mechanical.
