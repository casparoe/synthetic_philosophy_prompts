Write a short story of 1,200 to 1,800 words about a determination of death by circulatory criteria in an operating room during a donation-after-circulatory-determination procedure. The story should be literary rather than expository, but it must be procedurally exact: a reader who works in transplant medicine should find nothing in it that could not happen.

Ground the piece in the following legal and clinical scaffolding. The model statute under which your hospital operates reads, in the section that matters: "An individual who has sustained either (1) irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or (2) irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem, is dead. A determination of death must be made in accordance with accepted medical standards." Your donor does not satisfy limb (2). He has an anoxic brain injury following a witnessed out-of-hospital arrest, and he still has brainstem reflexes; he is not dead by neurological criteria, and the story must never let a character imply that he is. Limb (1) is therefore the only available pathway, and your story should be alert to the pressure that pathway is under: the cessation of circulation can be shown to be *permanent* — it will not resume, because no one in the room will attempt to restore it — without anyone being able to show that it is *irreversible*, since the same heart could in principle be restarted. Let the story know the difference between those two words and let it worry about it, without any character delivering a lecture about it.

Now the invented element, which should structure the plot. A multi-year effort to revise the uniform statute stalled indefinitely, and rather than wait, your hospital's ethics committee has adopted a two-stage workaround that no other institution uses. At asystole, the attending signs a *provisional determination of cessation*, which is not a declaration of death but which does authorize the procurement team to remain sterile, draped, and at the table, and which starts a mandatory hands-off interval — five minutes in your hospital, though you may choose another figure and show why. At the end of the interval, a second clinician, who has no role in the recipient's care, must sign or decline to sign the *declaration of death*. Only the second signature permits the incision. The coordinator who narrates your story has authority over neither document: she times the interval, she holds the phone line to the recipient's team, she watches the warm ischemia clock, and she is the person whose job description makes her the only party with an interest in the minutes passing quickly.

The engine of the story: something happens inside or immediately after the interval that is genuinely ambiguous — a single movement on the arterial line, a respiratory effort, a waveform that could be artifact or could be the rare spontaneous return of circulation that the interval exists to catch — and the second clinician must decide whether to extend, to resuscitate, or to sign. The recipient is in the building.

Observe these constraints. Narrate in close third person or first person from the coordinator's perspective. Never enter the donor's consciousness, and do not permit the story to settle the question of whether he was dead. The word *irreversible* may appear only inside the quoted statute, inside invented institutional documents, or inside dialogue — never in the narrator's own prose. Include at least one piece of invented diegetic paperwork: a form field, a policy line, or a time-stamped entry, quoted as text. Render the first minute of the interval in real time, so that duration is felt. No character may be a bioethicist, and no character may be a villain; the conflict must be carried entirely by procedure, timing, and what people do with their hands. End on a signature or a refusal to sign, unresolved but earned.

After the story, add a note of no more than 150 words stating which of the two documents the narrator believes was justified at the moment she witnessed, and on what basis — this is the one place where argument rather than narrative is permitted.
