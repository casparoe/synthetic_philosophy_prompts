Grad seminar on certainty and scepticism, week 3, and this early cluster from Über Gewißheit is where the discussion always stalls. I'm giving you the German (Suhrkamp numbering, which is the standard one) because half of what I want to settle turns on wording I don't trust any translation with — including my own, so treat the English underneath as my rough gloss and not as a citation of anything published.

8. Der Unterschied des Begriffs »wissen« vom Begriff »sicher sein« ist gar nicht von großer Wichtigkeit, außer da, wo »Ich weiß« heißen soll: Ich kann mich nicht irren. Im Gerichtssaal z. B. könnte in jeder Zeugenaussage statt »Ich weiß« »Ich bin sicher« gesagt werden. Ja, man könnte es sich denken, daß das »Ich weiß« dort verboten wäre. [Eine Stelle im Wilhelm Meister, wo »Du weißt« oder »Du wußtest« im Sinne »Du warst sicher« gebraucht wird, da es sich anders verhielt, als er wußte.]

14. Es muß erst erwiesen werden, daß er's weiß.

15. Daß kein Irrtum möglich war, muß erwiesen werden. Die Versicherung »Ich weiß es« genügt nicht. Denn sie ist doch nur die Versicherung, daß ich mich (da) nicht irren kann, und daß ich mich darin nicht irre, muß objektiv feststellbar sein.

16. »Wenn ich etwas weiß, so weiß ich auch, daß ich's weiß, etc.«, kommt darauf hinaus, »Ich weiß das« heiße »Ich bin darin unfehlbar«. Ob ich aber das bin, muß sich objektiv feststellen lassen.

17. Angenommen nun, ich sage »Ich bin darin unfehlbar, daß das ein Buch ist« – ich zeige dabei auf einen Gegenstand. Wie sähe hier ein Irrtum aus? Und habe ich davon eine klare Vorstellung?

18. »Ich weiß es« heißt oft: Ich habe die richtigen Gründe für meine Aussage. Wenn also der Andre das Sprachspiel kennt, so würde er zugeben, daß ich das weiß. Der Andre muß sich, wenn er das Sprachspiel kennt, vorstellen können, wie man so etwas wissen kann.

Rough English, mine: (8) the difference between the concept of knowing and the concept of being certain isn't of much importance, except where "I know" is meant to say "I can't be mistaken" — in court you could say "I'm certain" in any piece of testimony instead of "I know", indeed you could imagine "I know" being banned there; bracketed note about a passage in Wilhelm Meister where "you know"/"you knew" is used in the sense of "you were sure", when things turned out otherwise. (14) It has first to be proved that he knows it. (15) That no mistake was possible has to be proved; the assurance "I know it" isn't enough, since it's only the assurance that I can't be mistaken (here), and that I'm not mistaken about that has to be objectively establishable. (16) "If I know something I also know that I know it" comes down to: "I know this" means "I'm infallible about this"; but whether I am that has to be objectively establishable. (17) Suppose I say "I'm infallible about this being a book" while pointing at an object — what would a mistake look like here, and do I have any clear idea of it? (18) "I know it" often means: I have the right grounds for my statement; so if the other person knows the language-game he'd grant that I know it, and he has to be able to imagine how one can know such a thing.

Four things I want your read on:

1. Remark 16 seems to offer an equivalence — »Ich weiß das« heiße »Ich bin darin unfehlbar« — and then two remarks later 18 offers a different gloss: »Ich weiß es« heißt oft: Ich habe die richtigen Gründe für meine Aussage. Those aren't the same claim, and they can't both be analyses of the same phrase. Is one of them merely reported (what people ordinarily mean) while the other is being argued against (what a sceptic, or a Moore, demands of the phrase)? Or is the right way to take the pair that 18 describes the working game and 16 what happens when you try to convert an ordinary claim into a metaphysical one? Which reading does the wording actually support, and what decides it?

2. In 15, what work is the parenthetical (da) doing? I've read it as localising the infallibility — not infallibility in general, but infallibility in this case — and my sense is that's what makes the demand that it be »objektiv feststellbar« bite, since you can never establish it once and for all, only case by case. Is that right, or is the parenthesis weaker than that? Relatedly: does »objektiv« here mean publicly checkable by others, or just a matter of fact rather than of attitude? 16's »muß sich objektiv feststellen lassen« sounds more like the first than the second.

3. Remark 17 has two available morals that pull in opposite directions. Either (a) no mistake is possible about this book, so the infallibility claim is true but empty; or (b) a mistake is possible but I can't form a clear picture of what it would be, so the claim is indeterminate rather than true. Which one does »Und habe ich davon eine klare Vorstellung?« select for — and does it matter that W. asks about my conception of the mistake rather than about whether a mistake exists? I keep wanting to say the second question collapses into the first, and I don't think that's innocent.

4. The hinge payoff. One of my students has a paper arguing that hinges are exactly the propositions for which question 3 has no answer, so that »unfehlbar« is vacuous for them rather than unattainable, and that this is what makes them immune to doubt without making them known. Do 8 plus 14–18 give him that? Note the wrinkle he glosses over: 8 says the knowledge/certainty distinction collapses except where »Ich weiß« means »Ich kann mich nicht irren«, but the courtroom business suggests testimony is precisely where »Ich weiß« is at home and yet means no more than »Ich bin sicher«. If thats right, doesn't the infallibility requirement come apart from knowledge altogether — and if so, what's left of the argument that hinge propositions can't be known?

I don't want a survey of positions and definately no bibliography or list of commentators; my students will have read the usual ones and I roughly know the shape of the debate. What I want is a verdict on the wording: for each of 1–4, which side it favours and which words or bits of syntax in these five remarks force that verdict. And if you think any of my framings is a false choice, say so and tell me what the third option is — that's the most useful thing you could give me here, since it's usually what goes wrong in the seminar. Sorry this ran long, but I have 75 minutes on Tuesday and I'd rather spend them on this cluster than on the Moore truisms for the fourth year running.
