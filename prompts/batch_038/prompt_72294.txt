i'm TAing an upper-level seminar on federalism and subsidiarity (crosslisted polisci/philosophy) and i'm writing the essay question for week 11, the vertical-allocation-of-power week. the last two sessions turned into a running argument I want to convert into an assessment, because honestly half the section is still just pattern-matching "subsidiarity = keep it local" and never getting past that.

here's the set-up I want the question built around — and please restate it inside the question itself, so nobody has to remember it and the whole thing can be answered closed-book. On the EU side, subsidiarity is written into the treaties as a limit on when the Union may act in a shared-competence field: it may act only where the objectives can't be sufficiently achieved by the member states and can be better achieved at Union level. But the enforcement mechanism is political and soft — national parliaments can lodge reasoned objections to a draft act, and if enough of them object the Commission has to review the draft, though it may keep it and simply explain itself, with the final say staying where it always was, with the Council and the European Parliament. On the US side there is no subsidiarity clause at all, and yet the courts enforce a hard structural limit on *means*: Congress may not conscript state legislatures or state executives into administering a federal program, although it can attach conditions to federal money, and it can regulate private actors directly and stand up its own machinery to do the work. So the inversion I want tested is this — the system that names the principle polices it politically and almost toothlessly, the system that doesn't name it polices judicially and effectively, and they aren't even limiting the same thing, since one is about the scope of competence and the other about the mode of implementation. A strong answer should explain why a scope test resists judicial enforcement (it's a comparative counterfactual about which level of government would achieve an objective better — no determinate fact to find, it requires forecasting and it collapses into second-guessing the legislature), why a means test is judicially manageable by contrast (a bright line about whether a statute directs state officials), and then should notice the escape valves that make each limit partly cosmetic: conditional grants and preemption on one side, directives that leave form and methods to the states plus funding programs on the other. I also want the question to make them commit to a view on whether either device could be transplanted into the other system without ceasing to do what it currently does. If you want a third data point you can bring in the Swiss rule that a constitutional amendment has to clear both a majority of voters nationwide and a majority of cantons, but only if it actually does work in the argument and isn't decoration.

Deliverables, then: the exam question as I'd hand it out (45-minute in-class essay, closed book, 20 marks total — write it in a voice that sounds like a person, not like a textbook), a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each has to contain and saying how the 20 marks are distributed across them. Nothing anywhere should require citations, case names, article numbers or dates — if a student wants to gesture at a case they can, but the model answer has to stand on conceptual resources alone, and please don't invent attributions to fill space. On top of that give me four or five of the mistakes that should cost the most marks, each with a line on why it's tempting, e.g. treating subsidiarity as a preference for smallness rather than as a test about which level can achieve the objective; assuming the EU's objection procedure is judicial review wearing a different hat; assuming the anti-commandeering rule actually constrains how much Congress can get done rather than how it gets it done; and conflating a competence limit with a proportionality limit.
