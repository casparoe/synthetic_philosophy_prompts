Kuhn's account of scientific development is best understood as a rejection of the picture on which science grows by the steady accumulation of confirmed results. On his view a mature field is organised by a paradigm, which supplies both the puzzles its practitioners work on and the accepted means of solving them. During periods of normal science the paradigm is not itself under test: it is the condition of there being a determinate problem to work on at all, so that an anomaly is registered as a failure of the investigator rather than of the theory and is set aside as a puzzle for later. A crisis begins only when anomalies resist and strike at the centre of the field, and even then the reigning paradigm is not abandoned until a candidate replacement is on offer. The move from Ptolemaic to Copernican astronomy is therefore not the addition of a new fact to an existing stock but the substitution of one whole organisation of the field for another.

Incommensurability is Kuhn's account of what is lost in such a substitution. The term is borrowed from geometry, where two magnitudes are incommensurable when there is no common unit of which both are integral multiples, the side and the diagonal of a square being the classical case. Applied to theories, the notion captures a failure of fit between two vocabularies. Because the meaning of a theoretical term is fixed by the theory it belongs to and by the practice surrounding it, mass as it figures in Newtonian mechanics and mass as it figures in relativistic mechanics are not one quantity wearing the same name but two different quantities that happen to be labelled alike. The two theories consequently share no statements in common, and there is no neutral language into which both can be translated. Kuhn's illustration is the duck–rabbit: the same figure can be seen as a duck or as a rabbit but not as both at once, and the change from one to the other is produced not by additional evidence but by a reorganisation of what is there to be seen. Revolutions work in the same way, which is why Kuhn describes the adoption of a new paradigm as a conversion rather than as a judgement.

The relativist reading of all this is, I think, the correct one. If there is no common measure there can be no comparison, and if there can be no comparison the choice between paradigms cannot be made on grounds at all. Kuhn occasionally writes as though the world itself changes at a revolution, and taking that seriously yields not a disagreement between two communities but the absence of any shared world for them to disagree about: what counts as a fact is settled by the paradigm that makes it a fact, and truth is internal to the framework in which a claim is advanced. The Newton–Einstein case shows what is at stake. It is often said that relativistic mechanics reduces to Newtonian mechanics in the limit of small velocities, so that Einstein preserved Newton's achievement while correcting it. But if two theories have no statements in common there is no such relation to be had: what survives in the limit is a formal resemblance between two sets of equations, not a shared content, and Einstein displaced Newton rather than extending him. Kuhn's denial that science progresses toward the truth is thus not a provocative flourish but a consequence of the semantics. Nor can an appeal to shared values rescue the situation. Accuracy, simplicity, scope, consistency and fruitfulness are common property, but what counts as accurate or as simple is itself paradigm-relative, so the values cannot be brought to bear on the question at issue: each community applies them differently, and invoking them merely restates the disagreement. What remains is preference, aesthetic or generational, and Kuhn himself endorses Planck's observation that a new view triumphs because its opponents die out.

Kuhn seems to have recognised the cost. In the postscript added to the second edition he responds to Davidson's argument that a scheme which could not be translated into ours would not be a scheme at all by retiring the loose notion of a paradigm in favour of the narrower disciplinary matrix, and in his later papers he restates incommensurability in linguistic terms, as a difficulty about translating a handful of central words. This is the perceptual point of 1962 expressed in the vocabulary of language, so the thesis is continuous across his career; and since the difficulty was never only about words, the objection that it renders science irrational stands where it always did.
