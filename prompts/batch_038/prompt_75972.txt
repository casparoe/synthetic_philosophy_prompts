I retired four years ago after thirty-eight years in condensed-matter physics, most of it spent on phase transitions and on the kind of argument that shows some proposed order parameter cannot exist. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy properly, and social choice theory has taken hold of me, largely because an impossibility theorem is the one philosophical argument a physicist arrives already trained both to distrust and to enjoy.

In the spring I am to give a paper to a philosophical society in my city: an audience of perhaps twenty, mixed, including two or three economists. Below is the core of my draft, about nine hundred words of what will eventually run to two and a half thousand. I would be grateful for your comments on it.

WHERE TO PUT A RIGHT: SEN'S PARADOX READ AS A NO-GO THEOREM

1. A theorem asserting that no procedure can satisfy three conditions at once is not a discovery about procedures. It is a statement about a formalism: it says that the formalism cannot house all three of the informal notions it was asked to house. The interesting work therefore lies in the assumption inventory, in identifying which of the three notions has been formalized in a way that overreaches what it was meant to capture. Physicists learn this early. When a no-go theorem forbids something we know happens, we do not conclude that the world is impossible; we go looking for the idealization.

2. Sen's result concerns a social welfare function, a rule assigning to every profile of individual preference orderings a complete and transitive social ordering over the alternatives. Three conditions are imposed. The domain is unrestricted: every logically possible profile is admissible. The Pareto principle holds, in the form requiring society to rank x above y whenever every individual weakly prefers x to y and at least one strictly prefers it; I take it that this is all the theorem needs, which is part of why the result is usually described as shocking. And liberalism holds in its minimal form: every individual is decisive over at least one pair of alternatives, in the sense that for that pair the social ordering follows his strict preference whichever way it goes.

3. The illustration everyone uses involves one copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover and two prospective readers, Prude and Lewd. There are three outcomes: no one reads it, Prude reads it, Lewd reads it. Lewd ranks them Prude reads, then Lewd reads, then no one reads: he would enjoy the book, but would enjoy his friend's discomfort more. Prude ranks them no one reads, then Prude reads, then Lewd reads: he thinks the book indecent and wants it unread, but holds that it is worse for it to be read with pleasure than to be read in disgust. Lewd is decisive over the pair consisting of Lewd reads and no one reads, so society ranks Lewd reads above no one reads. Prude is decisive over the pair consisting of Prude reads and no one reads, and prefers the latter, so society ranks no one reads above Prude reads. Transitivity then ranks Lewd reads above Prude reads. But both men strictly prefer Prude reads to Lewd reads, so Pareto ranks Prude reads above Lewd reads. The social ordering is cyclic, and so is not an ordering.

4. Note where each link comes from. Two are supplied by liberalism, one by Pareto, and the contradiction exists only because a single transitive ordering has been asked to carry all three. Note also that the Pareto link exists only because each man has an opinion about the other's reading: Lewd's favourite outcome is an invasion of Prude's sphere, and Prude's ranking of his own reading against Lewd's is equally a judgement about what Lewd ought to do. Restrict each man's ordering so that it depends only on his own reading, and, as I understand it, the contradiction cannot be assembled at all. That is a strong hint about where the trouble lies.

5. There are two ways to formalize the claim that whether Prude reads is up to Prude. The first is the one Sen's condition uses: a requirement on the social ordering, to the effect that a certain pair must be ranked as Prude ranks it. The second treats the right as a constraint on the situation rather than as a requirement about its evaluation. Prude has the move; the alternatives in which someone else decides his reading are not available to the collective procedure at all. On that reading there is no social ranking of the protected pair left to constrain, and hence no channel by which Lewd's preference about Prude's reading can be converted into a verdict inside Prude's sphere. I believe formulations of this second style are usually described in terms of game forms, and that Sen himself came to favour something of the kind, though I should not like my argument to lean on that history.

6. My claim is that the paradox is an artefact of putting a constraint in the wrong slot, and the distinction is familiar in physics. One may restrict a system by limiting the region of state space it is permitted to occupy, or one may leave the space alone and add a term to the Hamiltonian coupling the degrees of freedom. The two are not interchangeable. A coupling propagates: what happens at one coordinate is felt at every coordinate coupled to it, and a global conclusion can then be assembled out of local ingredients. A restriction does not propagate; it deletes alternatives. Sen's liberal condition has the grammar of a coupling, since it is a statement about how the global ordering must respond at a specified pair of coordinates, whereas the notion it is meant to express has the grammar of a restriction. Transitivity is the coupling. Once the ordering is transitive, decisiveness over one pair is no longer local: it constrains every comparison reachable from that pair along a chain of comparisons, including comparisons manufactured out of other people's preferences. On this reading what Sen has shown is that a social ordering cannot be simultaneously globally transitive and locally sovereign. That is a theorem about orderings, not about liberty.

7. There is an obvious reply and I cannot yet decide whether it defeats me. A defender of the original formulation will say that a right which merely restricts the choice set has no purchase on collective decisions, and that the point of formulating rights as conditions on social evaluation is precisely to make them bite where society actually ranks and chooses. On that view the paradox is not an artefact but a discovery: rights and unanimous social evaluation genuinely cannot both be given standing within a single ordering, and the liberal must surrender something, namely Pareto, or transitivity, or the unrestricted domain. If that reply succeeds, my paper has done no more than rename a weaker concept of a right and assert that the stronger concept was a mistake all along.

My requests are as follows.

First, accuracy. I reconstructed the theorem and the example from secondary discussion some years ago and have not checked either since. Please tell me plainly whether the three conditions are stated in the form the theorem actually requires, whether the example generates the contradiction by the four steps I list, and whether the parenthetical claim in paragraph 4 about meddlesome preferences is correct. Where a step is wrong, give me the correct version rather than a general warning.

Second, the analogy. Tell me whether paragraph 6 earns its place or is decoration. I am particularly interested in whether the contrast between a restriction on state space and a coupling term maps onto the contrast between a game-form right and a decisiveness right point by point, or whether it breaks down at transitivity, since transitivity is a property an ordering either has or lacks rather than something one elects to add.

Third, paragraph 7. Does that reply land? Is there a further objection I have missed, for instance that even a game-form right must eventually connect to social evaluation if it is to matter politically, so that the two formulations are not rivals but stages in one account?

Fourth, structure and register, briefly. Which of the seven paragraphs could be cut, and is the tone right for an audience containing people who know this theorem considerably better than I do?

I am not a philosopher and would much rather be corrected bluntly than encouraged. One practical request: please do not send me a reading list. I no longer have library access and have no reliable way of verifying citations, so I would far rather you told me that an objection is a well-worn one and explained it in your own words than named papers for me to chase. If a move in my draft is standard, saying so is the single most useful thing you can do for me.
