I teach a graduate seminar, cross-listed between philosophy and history, on causal explanation in the human sciences. This term's through-line has been what it takes for two explanations of the same outcome to be genuine rivals rather than complementary accounts pitched at different levels, and our final two weeks are on Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men: Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the Final Solution in Poland* (1992) and Daniel Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners: Ordinary Germans and the Holocaust* (1996), the latter written largely as a rebuttal of the former, with the disagreement first aired in Goldhagen's 1992 review "The Evil of Banality." I'm now writing the final and want a single essay question built around the Order Police unit both books examine. It must not be answerable by narrating the controversy: several of my students have read the review literature and the symposium volumes, and I want them to get nothing for that. Nor should it reward recall of dates, place-names, unit designations, or headcounts — a student who remembers the arguments but none of the numbers should be able to earn full marks.

The hinge I want is the datum Goldhagen treats as decisive against the situational reading: at Józefów, before the battalion's first close-range shooting action, the commander told the assembled men that anyone who did not feel up to the task could step out; only a handful of some five hundred did so; and — the point Goldhagen presses hardest — refusing to kill appears never to have been seriously punished. From the absence of a formal sanction he concludes that coercion is off the table, so what remains is disposition, which he identifies with the "eliminationist antisemitism" he takes to pervade German political culture. The question should make students see that this inference quietly substitutes the absence of a *formal* sanction for the absence of any *cost* to non-conformity, and that Browning was never committed to a punishment mechanism: the pressure in the Milgram paradigm does not come from a threat against quitting, but from the incremental structure of the task, an authority who absorbs responsibility, and — crucially here — the fact that the opt-out was offered publicly, in formation, so that declining was an act of self-marking in front of the comrades whose labour one was thereby refusing to share. But a merely defensive answer is a B. The A-level work is to say what evidence could actually discriminate a conformity-based explanation from a disposition-based one: that a disposition posited as uniform across a population is a constant and therefore cannot explain variation within it; that this cuts both ways, since a situational account that explains participation in general has yet to explain why *these* orders and *this* regime produced mass killing; that the two accounts are not strict contraries as usually stated, so the student must locate precisely where they conflict and where they merely differ in level; and that the contrasts available to a historian of a settled past are counterfactuals about other units and other victim categories rather than interventions, which raises its own question about whether the relevant variables — how antisemitic is a man, how pressured is a formation — are well-defined enough to be manipulated in the sense my students have been reading about in the interventionist literature. To keep the question from turning on contested specifics, embed a short vignette that you construct and label explicitly as stipulated rather than real: something like a reported archival finding of three comparable battalions from the same region and age cohort in which opt-out rates varied widely, tracking neither the men's pre-war political affiliation nor the antisemitic framing of the orders, but how many killings the unit had already carried out in the preceding months. Build the question so the vignette looks like it settles things in one direction while actually raising a brutalization-versus-selection ambiguity the student has to name — did repeated killing change these men, or did the unit's composition drift as the reluctant found their way into other duties?

Please deliver: (1) the exam question exactly as it would appear on the paper, self-contained, with the vignette embedded, a stated time limit of 75 minutes, and a note on whether notes and texts may be used; (2) a model answer of roughly 800 words that argues for a position rather than surveying the two books; (3) a marking scheme totalling 25 marks, organized as named moves with marks attached — identifying the formal/informal sanction conflation, stating the constant-cannot-explain-a-variable point and its converse, locating where the accounts genuinely conflict rather than declaring a difference of level, evaluating the vignette, and so forth — each described concretely enough that a second grader could apply it identically; (4) a list of characteristic errors with the marks each should cost, including at least one error that looks like insight, since I expect the most common will be announcing that the dispute is merely one of level and treating that announcement as a resolution; and (5) two or three closing sentences on what separates an A from a B, naming the single point you expect most students to miss. No bibliography, reading list, or citations anywhere in the deliverables: students will be told that citation earns nothing here, and I don't want the model answer modelling a practice the question forbids.
