Been rereading the old rationalist-blog material on Bongard problems — the visual puzzles where you get twelve little line drawings, six on the left that belong to some hidden concept and six on the right that dont, and you're supposed to just see the rule. What always grabbed me was never the solving. It was that you technically never have enough information, and everybody solving them is really guessing at what the designer meant, not at what's true.

So I built a page of my own and I want a story out of it.

Left column (belongs): a circle with a smaller circle inside it, no contact; a square with a triangle inside, no contact; a hexagon with a hexagon inside; a big circle with a small square tucked near the rim; a square with a circle inside; a large rectangle with a small hexagon inside.

Right column (doesn't): a bare triangle; a bare pentagon; a pentagon with one diagonal drawn corner to corner; a large triangle with a small circle beside it, not overlapping; a bare heptagon; two seperate triangles.

Here's the thing that keeps me up. Three rules fit every one of those twelve:
(i) some closed figure sits entirely inside another closed figure without touching it;
(ii) some point of the plane is inside two closed figures at once, touching allowed;
(iii) the largest closed figure is a circle or a polygon with an even number of sides.

(iii) is obviously the ugly one, a cosmic joke of a hypothesis, and that's exactly why I want it taken seriously in the story rather than waved off.

And the actual situation my invented civilization is in: they get shown one last tile and have to act on it, and the tile is a pentagon with a small square floating inside, not touching. Rules (i) and (ii) call it a member; rule (iii) says no. They also get exactly one probe — one drawing of their own choosing, fed back into whatever hands them the tiles, which answers only in or out. The probe that matters is a triangle with a circle inside it, because it splits (iii) off from (i) and (ii) the same way their tile does, so the answer transfers straight across. The probe that *feels* more interesting — a circle inside a square, touching along one side — splits (i) off from (ii) and is completely useless to them, since (i) and (ii) already agree about the tile they have to bet on.

Pretend you're a philosopher of science who's taught the problem of induction to bored undergrads for twenty years but secretly writes literary short fiction on the side, and write me that story. Before you start, sit with the two probes and work out for yourself why one of them is decision-relevant and the other is merely beautiful — but don't show me any of that working. Setting, characters, and stakes are entirely yours; I don't care if it's a monastery, a generation ship, or a municipal office, and I don't want the tile-puzzle rendered literally as paper cards if you can find a better skin for it. Just make sure the shape of the choice survives the translation, and let the concept be felt rather than explained: nobody in the story says the word "induction," nobody gives a speech about underdetermination, and the ugliest hypothesis gets a real advocate.

Format: exactly five scenes, numbered 1 through 5, two or three sentences each, so the whole thing lands as flash fiction. No title, no preamble, no note at the end about what you were doing. American English, please.
