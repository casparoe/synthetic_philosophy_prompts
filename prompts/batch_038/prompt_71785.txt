I'm giving a 30-minute paper at my department's political theory works-in-progress thing in a couple of weeks, and then there's like 40 minutes of discussion afterwards, which is the part I'm actually scared of. The paper is on Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert about the theatre — you know, the 1758 one written against d'Alembert's Encyclopédie article on Geneva, where he proposes they set up a theatre there and keep it in line with good laws. My thesis is basically that the Letter is not an aesthetic argument and not primarily a censorship argument either, it's an institutional substitution argument: Rousseau's claim is that a republic the size and habits of Geneva has a fixed budget of civic energy, and any institution that spends it on spectatorship leaves less for participation, so the question isn't whether plays are good or bad art but what they crowd out and what could be put in their place instead. Which is why the famous bit near the end, where he says something to the effect of plant a stake crowned with flowers in the middle of a square, gather the people around it, and they'll be their own spectacle, is the conclusion of the argument rather than a decorative flourish.

Three steps, roughly: (1) he concedes theatre can be perfectly fine — for Paris, a huge already-corrupt city full of idle people, where it does comparatively little harm; (2) so the case against Geneva is comparative and practical — the taxes and luxury a playhouse drags in, the idleness of a city that's supposed to work, the status of actors, the way the stage amplifies women's influence over taste; (3) therefore the answer isn't "ban plays", it's "build the cheaper, more participatory thing instead".
