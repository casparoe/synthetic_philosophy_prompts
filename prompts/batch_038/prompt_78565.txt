I am a junior at a fairly academically intense high school, and for the past year I have been part of an after-school philosophy reading group our history teacher supervises. We spent this term on philosophy of mind, and last month we read Norman Malcolm's Dreaming (1959) alongside Daniel Dennett's paper "Are Dreams Experiences?" (Philosophical Review, 1976). Our group split predictably: one side said Malcolm was obviously right that you cannot be having experiences while you are asleep, since nothing could count as your having had them apart from what you say on waking; the other side said Dennett had destroyed Malcolm by showing the question is empirical rather than conceptual, since the cassette hypothesis makes it at least conceivable that a dream is composed and filed in memory at the moment of waking, with no experience at all in between. I ended up thinking both camps were wrong in the same way, and I want to write the group's end-of-term essay defending that claim. I should say that I cannot search anything, so I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or quotations of either author. I want your own reconstruction of their arguments and your own argument, written so that I can check the reasoning rather than the references.

Here is the position I want you to argue for, stated as precisely as I can manage. Malcolm and Dennett disagree about a great many things, but both of them build on one shared assumption, which I will call the endorsement condition: a mental state counts as an experience only if, at the time it occurs, it carries the subject's own endorsement of it as real and as happening now, and only if that endorsement is in principle recoverable from the subject's later report or behaviour. Given that condition, the only live question between them is whether endorsement can be attached during sleep — Malcolm argues that it cannot be, on conceptual grounds, and Dennett argues that the sleep-lab evidence makes it doubtful that it is, on empirical grounds. My contention is that the endorsement condition is simply false, that a great many states we would not hesitate to call experiences lack it, and that once it is abandoned the Malcolm–Dennett dispute stops being a dispute about whether dreams are experiences and becomes a much narrower dispute about how dream content is generated. On my view, dreams are experiences in the ordinary sense — they have present-tense sensory character for the dreamer while the dreamer is asleep — and both Malcolm's conceptual argument and the motivation for Dennett's cassette hypothesis lose their force, even though the cassette hypothesis might still turn out to be true as a piece of speculative psychology. I want a real argument for this, not a summary of the debate and not a split-the-difference conclusion.

Please also deal with the empirical material, because that is where my reading group thinks I am weakest. I keep coming back to three things: the old finding that a ringing alarm or a spray of cold water can get woven into a dream in a way that makes the ending of the dream look tailored to the stimulus; the fact that people woken from non-REM sleep often report short, thought-like content rather than vivid scenes; and Stephen LaBerge's Stanford work, in which practiced lucid dreamers executed pre-arranged eye movements on cue while the sleep record showed they were in REM. The last one seems to me to be a problem for everybody: if a sleeper can know she is dreaming and still be dreaming, then endorsement and dreaming are not the incompatible things Malcolm supposed, but the dreamer is also plainly not simply confabulating on waking. Please show what these phenomena do and do not establish on my account, and do not overclaim — if some of them are neutral or even mildly awkward for me, say so and explain why they do not sink the position.

Structure the essay with exactly these sections, in this order, in continuous prose rather than bullet points:

1. The thesis (about 150 words). State the position in its sharpest form, including what the endorsement condition is and what denying it commits you to.
2. The shared premise (about 400 words). Reconstruct Malcolm's argument and Dennett's cassette proposal charitably enough that a reader who likes them would recognise them, and make the common assumption visible. Close by noting where each author would resist being described as sharing it.
3. The case against the premise (about 550 words). This is the core of the essay. Give the counterexamples to the endorsement condition — absorbed reading, daydreaming, hypnagogic imagery, being lost in music, play of any kind in which one knows the events are not real — and, more importantly, offer a positive account of what makes a state an experience if endorsement is not what makes it one.
4. The evidence, re-examined (about 350 words). Take the three phenomena listed above and explain what each shows on my view.
5. Objections and replies (about 300 words). At least three objections, and be sure to include the objection that without the endorsement condition the distinction between having a dream and merely being disposed to report one collapses, so that Malcolm's original point returns in a new form.
6. What survives (about 150 words). Say what Malcolm gets right and what Dennett gets right even if the thesis is correct.
