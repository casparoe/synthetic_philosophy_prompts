I've been reading the recent wave of popular Stoicism — the newsletters, courses, and apps built around separating what is up to us from what is not — and among the people I know who have taken it up most earnestly I keep seeing a practice for which I have no term. They downgrade how much they care about something before it is decided, on purpose, as insurance. A friend spent a year working toward a promotion and, a month before the announcement, told me cheerfully that she had stopped wanting it, since the outcome was never hers to control; she did not get it and was visibly unbothered. Another abandoned competitive running after an injury by persuading himself that finishing times had never really mattered to him. In both cases the strategy works: they are calm, and they report being happy. What unsettles me is the timing and the intent — the loss has not happened, and the devaluing is adopted precisely so that it cannot hurt — and the fact that the people around them feel they have become smaller without being able to say what exactly went wrong. It is not sour grapes as I understand it, because the grapes were never out of reach and this is not something that happens to a person but something they do deliberately and in advance.

Is there an established name for this in philosophy or in psychology? If so, how does it differ from adaptive preferences, from self-handicapping, and from the Stoics' own recommendation that one pursue things while accepting that success is not guaranteed? If there is no name, coin one, define it precisely, and mark its edges: where does pre-emptive devaluing stop and ordinary acceptance begin, and does the difference turn on motive, timing, or consequence? Please write in American English.
