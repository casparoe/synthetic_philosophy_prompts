QUESTION 2 (25 marks; suggested length 500–600 words)

In the afterword to the novel we read in week four, the author defends her unresolved ending as follows: the book is a closed system, and a closed system cannot state its own truth; the reader therefore has to stand outside it and supply what the text is structurally unable to contain. She calls this Gödelian and insists she means it literally.

Evaluate this appropriation. Your answer must (a) state the hypotheses a formal system must satisfy for the first incompleteness theorem to apply; (b) state precisely what the theorem licenses, and what it does not; and (c) identify at least two distinct points at which the novelist's analogy fails, saying in each case which feature of the theorem the analogy silently drops.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) The hypotheses. The first theorem is not a claim about systems in general but about systems of a particular shape, and three conditions matter. The system must be effectively axiomatized: there must be a mechanical procedure that recognizes proofs, so that the theorems form a recursively enumerable set. It must be consistent. And it must be arithmetically strong — able to represent the recursive functions, and hence, by way of a Gödel numbering, able to represent inside its own language the relations that hold among its own expressions. Peano arithmetic and Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory satisfy all three. Presburger arithmetic, the theory of the natural numbers with addition but without multiplication, is effectively axiomatized and consistent, yet it is complete and decidable, precisely because it cannot encode its own syntax. That last example is worth insisting on, since popular accounts almost always leave it out.

(b) What the theorem licenses. Given those hypotheses there is a sentence G in the language of the system such that, if the system is consistent, G is not provable in it; and if the system is ω-consistent, its negation is not provable either. Gödel's own argument needed the stronger ω-consistency hypothesis to get undecidability; Rosser showed shortly afterward that consistency alone suffices, by diagonalizing not on the claim that I am unprovable but on a subtler one — roughly, that if I am provable at all, then some proof of my negation comes earlier. Since G says of itself that it is unprovable, and since we are supposing the system sound enough that whatever it proves is true, G is true. So the theorem is fairly summarized as the claim that truth outruns proof: there are arithmetical truths that no proof within the system reaches. What it does not license is any conclusion about systems generally, or about the reach of human knowledge outside the formal setting.

(c) Where the analogy fails. It fails first at the level of machinery. A novel has no consequence relation. There is no set of narrative axioms and no rule by which further sentences follow from them; where there are no proofs there is no analogue of unprovability, and so nothing for a diagonal construction to bite on. When the author calls her book a closed system she is describing its finishedness, not its deductive structure, and the theorem requires the second. The failure is not merely contingent, though: one can imagine a work generated by an explicitly stated procedure — a constrained novel of the Oulipo kind — that begins to look more like a formal system; but even there what would still be missing is the arithmetic.

It fails second at the level of what is said about truth. Strictly, the result that a language cannot define its own truth predicate is Tarski's, and it is proved by a diagonal construction closely related to Gödel's. For the author's purposes, however, the distinction is inert, because both results establish the same structural point: a sufficiently strong system cannot contain an account of its own correctness. When she says the text cannot state its own truth, she is saying in literary language something that is a theorem.

It fails third, and here I think she is nearer right than her critics allow, at the level of generality. What the analogy reaches for is not a theorem but a pattern: any rigorous, self-contained framework that is expressive enough to describe its own construction will generate claims it cannot settle from within. Legal codes, moral theories, and grammars are all exposed to something of this sort, and a text that thematizes its own making is exposed to it as well. The second incompleteness theorem is the sharpest statement of the point, since it says no such system can certify itself; its warrant has to come from somewhere else. That is why arithmetic rests, at bottom, on trust — ZFC cannot establish its own consistency any more than the novel can establish its own ending — and why the reader's position outside the text is not a rhetorical flourish but a structural necessity.
