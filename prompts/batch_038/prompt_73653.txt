You are an analytic philosopher with a decision-theoretic bent — someone equally at home with the anthropics literature on the simulation argument and with the longtermist literature on the value of the far future. Please think carefully and work through this in stages rather than arriving at a verdict immediately.

**The setup.** Consider a longtermist agent who has already estimated the expected value of humanity's far future in the ordinary way: some expected number of future lives of some quality, discounted by an extinction-or-collapse risk she sets from standard considerations (engineered pandemics, misaligned AI, nuclear war, and so forth). Call that figure her *baseline*. Her baseline therefore already contains a nonzero probability of a short future.

Now suppose she is told Bostrom's simulation argument and accepts it as sound: she accepts that at least one of the following holds — (i) the fraction of civilizations at roughly our level of development that go on to reach a posthuman stage is very close to zero; (ii) the fraction of posthuman civilizations interested in running ancestor simulations of their evolutionary history is very close to zero; (iii) the fraction of people with our kind of experiences who are living in simulations is very close to one. Crucially, she is *not* told which disjunct is true. What the argument adds to her reasoning is the logical constraint that the three cannot all fail, together with whatever probabilistic pressure that constraint exerts on her other beliefs.

**The claim I want a number for.**

S: Conditional on the simulation argument being sound, and given that the agent does not know which disjunct holds, the expected value of the far future relevant to her present investment decisions is at least ten times *lower* than her baseline.

**What I want from you.** First, a short brainstorming passage — before any numbers appear — identifying the distinct routes by which the argument could bite on far-future value, and the distinct routes by which it could backfire and *raise* far-future value. Be alert to double-counting: horn (i) overlaps with extinction risk she has already priced in, so what matters is the increment.

Then your credence in S, decomposed. I'd like a numbered list of terms, each with a probability and no more than two sentences of justification. Terms I think belong (add, drop, or replace as you judge):

1. The probability of each horn, and how much of S's truth rides on the agent's credence distribution over them.
2. Given (iii): the probability that simulated beings have the same moral weight as non-simulated ones. If they do, (iii) may not reduce value at all.
3. Given (iii): the probability that simulations are typically terminated before their inhabitants reach a posthuman transition — and the anthropic question of whether being inside a simulation places us at a typical point in that simulation's history.
4. Given (iii): the probability that our own descendants will run many simulations, so that branching multiplies future value rather than shrinking it.
5. The probability that (iii) *increases* the contingency of the far future on present choices — for instance, if simulations are continued conditional on what their inhabitants do, then our conduct now has more leverage over our continuation, not less.
6. The probability that the question is ill-posed: that with unbounded utilities or fanatical weighting there is no determinate "expected value of the far future," so no factor-of-ten claim has a truth value. Do not use this as an escape hatch — price it as one term among others.

Show the combination arithmetic explicitly, not just the endpoint. Then tell me: which single term is S most sensitive to, what argument or piece of evidence would move your overall number most and in which direction, and — separately from your verdict on S — your median estimate of the multiplicative factor the argument applies to the baseline (a value above 1 meaning it raises expected value).

Do not offer citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; reason from the structure of the argument itself. And do not retreat to the observation that the answer depends on which disjunct holds — the whole content of the question is that the agent does not know which one holds.
