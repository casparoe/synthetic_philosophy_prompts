Teaching a seminar on ch. 25 of The Prince next week and I'm sick of handing out summaries — I want cases instead. Here's the snag I'm trying to press on. Machiavelli says two men can reach the same end by opposite routes, one cautious, one impetuous, and which of them wins comes down to whether their fixed temperment happens to line up with the spirit of the times. He says men basically can't change their nature, for two reasons: nature pulls them, and they've always prospered doing it one way so nobody can talk them out of it. Julius II is his exhibit — all-in boldness at Bologna, and had circumstances ever demanded caution he'd have been ruined, except he died before they did. If that's the whole story, then virtù is just temperament plus a lucky calendar, and the word stops doing any work beyond fortuna.

Except the same chapter also gives me the river: you build dikes and channels in fair weather, before any flood, so that when the water comes it's forced into canals and does less harm. That reads like actual skill, exercised at a moment when there's nothing to respond to, and it doesn't look like temperament-matching at all.

So I want three invented cases — modern, no real people, and please not a reskinned Julius in a suit — each doing a specific job.

1. The dike kind: someone does costly, unglamorous pre-provisioning during a calm stretch and it pays off in a way I'd want to call virtù.
2. The temperament-match kind, where the fit is pure luck and using the word virtù would add nothing to the description.
3. The one I care about most: a case where the dike-building is itself what causes the ruin — the barriers, or the habits and reputation built up alongside them, are what destroy the builder when the times turn.

For each, tell me what a Machiavellian observer could have seen coming in advance and what they couldn't.

Then the hard part. Does case 1 survive being redescribed in the terms of case 2? Isn't the dike-builder just a cautious man whose caution happened to suit quiet years, so his success is no less lucky than Julius's was? If you think yes, tell me what's left of virtù — and don't reach for free will or the line about fortune governing half our actions, I want something that still tells people apart. If you think no, say which feature of case 1 does the discriminating, and whether Machiavelli's second reason for why men can't change (that they've always prospered one way) quietly undermines it.
