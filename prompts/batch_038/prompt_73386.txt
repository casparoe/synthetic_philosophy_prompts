I'm a second-year graduate student in philosophy, and this term my seminar has been working through the debate between telic egalitarianism and the priority view. For our last session I was asked to reconstruct Parfit's leveling-down objection in a form that does more work than the standard intuition pump about blinding the sighted, and I ended up with an argument that seemed to me to show the objection generalizing well past egalitarianism — to the priority view itself, and then, to my horror, to the total view as well. My seminar partner thinks the argument proves too much and that I should withdraw it. I suspect she is right, but I cannot localize the bad step: every premise looks like something a welfarist of any stripe should accept, and the transitions look short. Below is the argument as I presented it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?

**Setup.** Outcomes are described as vectors of lifetime wellbeing for a fixed population of two people, P1 and P2. Three views are on the table. The *total view* ranks outcomes by the sum of the levels. The *priority view* ranks them by the sum of a strictly increasing, strictly concave transform *f* of each person's level, so that a benefit of a given size counts for more the lower the absolute level at which it falls; for concreteness let *f* be the square root. *Telic egalitarianism* ranks them by some combination of the sum and the degree of equality between the two levels. The leveling-down change (LD) is the move from (100, 200) to (100, 100): nobody gains, P2 loses a hundred, equality is achieved.

1. Any axiology that ranks A above B must be able to identify some feature of A, absent from B, in virtue of which A is better. A ranking that points to no such feature is not a claim about value at all.
2. The only features of an outcome in virtue of which it can be better are features of how individual lives go. Nothing over and above the levels of the persons in it — no arrangement, no pattern, no relation among them — can contribute value.
3. Therefore, if A is better than B, then some person's life goes better in A than in B; A must be better for somebody.
4. Now take A = (30, 190) and C = (20, 200). These have the same total, 220, so the total view is indifferent between them; but with *f* the square root the priority view ranks A above C (roughly 19.26 against 18.61). The raw person-affecting facts here are a wash: A is better for P1 by exactly ten units and worse for P2 by exactly ten units. So the feature that tips the scale for the prioritarian cannot be anybody's gain. It is the declining slope of *f* — a fact about the shape of the distribution taken as a whole.
5. By (1)–(3), a ranking whose decisive feature is the shape of a distribution rather than somebody's gain is not a genuine claim about value. So the priority view, in ranking A above C, posits value that is located nowhere.
6. That is precisely what the leveling-down objection said about the egalitarian: that in LD the extra value the egalitarian detects has no location in any life. So the objection is not a local embarrassment for telic egalitarianism. It refutes the priority view by the same route, and — this was the part I thought was elegant — it does so using the priority view's own criterion, that only absolute levels and not comparisons matter morally.
7. The reasoning is not restricted to concave weightings. Take A* = (150, 60) and C* = (100, 100). The total view ranks A* above C*, since 210 beats 200; the priority view, with *f* the square root, ranks C* above A*. Neither ranking is explained by anyone's gain: A* is better for P1 by fifty and worse for P2 by forty, and the feature that breaks the tie for the total view is the size of the sum, which belongs to the pair and not to either member of it.
8. Hence, if the argument is sound, every welfarist axiology that ranks outcomes by any function of a distribution — which is to say all of them — posits value located nowhere, and there are no comparative rankings of distributions at all.

Step (8) is the conclusion I cannot accept, and it is not only its breadth that bothers me. The principle driving steps (2) and (3) would also seem to condemn a good deal else that I want to keep: the retributivist's claim that a punished wrong is a better state of affairs than an unpunished one even when the punishment makes nobody's life go better; the claim that a rainforest nobody will ever visit is better for its biodiversity; the claim that a promise to someone now dead can still be kept or broken. And the pluralist egalitarians in our seminar have a ready answer to the leveling-down case that I could not dislodge — that being better in one respect is a real relation that need not coincide with being better for anyone, so the egalitarian can happily concede that LD is all-things-considered worse while insisting that something of value was gained.

So: which step goes, and what do I pay for dropping it? I would most like to preserve the insight behind (6), namely that the leveling-down objection ought to be statable as a general constraint on value rather than resting on an intuition about blindness, but not at the price of (8). In particular I want to know whether there is a principled line between the prioritarian's concave *f* and the egalitarian's equality term such that the first is compatible with (2) and the second is not; and if there is such a line, why the total view's summation gets through it too, given that (7) seems to treat summation and concave weighting as equally structural. If there is no such line, should I conclude that the leveling-down objection is not really an objection to egalitarianism at all, but a person-affecting restriction on value that egalitarians, prioritarians and utilitarians have precisely the same reason to reject?
