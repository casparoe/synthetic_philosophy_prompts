So I'm a novelist, not a philosopher — calibrate accordingly — and I've spent two years down a rabbit hole that keeps failing to deliver, because my book needs it.

Setup. The novel has two main characters: Ada, a proof theorist in her late fifties, and her brother Theo, who runs training infrastructure for a big lab. There's a scene that recurs three times — as kids at a kitchen table, in a hospital corridor, and in Theo's building after a demo — where they argue about whether the incompleteness theorems show anything whatsoever about minds and machines. I've drafted all three and all three are bad, and I think I've finally worked out why: I keep letting one of them win.

The material is the old Lucas line from Minds, Machines and Gödel, plus Penrose's version in The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind. Roughly: if a mathematician were some formal system, then from outside we could see the truth of that system's own Gödel sentence, and since the mathematician can't be blind to it, the mathematician isn't that system. Then the whole apparatus of replies — you'd have to know the system is consistent and know which system it is; humans might not be consistent at all, in which case the theorem doesn't apply; a machine could be consistent without being knowably so, which stalls the diagonal move; and the thing collapses into a regress where the human keeps bolting on consistency axioms and the other side keeps pointing out that the union of a computable sequence of such extensions is itself recursively axiomatizable and so has its own unprovable sentence. Plus Penrose's escape hatch that the physics in your skull may not be computable, which most people seem to treat as a separate and much shakier bet. And hanging around the edges, Hofstadter's idea that the interesting question is what jumping out of a system even amounts to, given that a system plus a statement of its own consistency is just a bigger system, still subject to the same result.

What I want isn't a verdict. I want a diagnosis of why this arguement has run in the same groove for sixty-odd years without anyone conceding anything. Everybody agrees on the theorem, everybody agrees on the standard objections, and yet the two camps don't even seem to be losing ground to each other. My hunch, offered only as a hunch: they aren't disagreeing about minds or about mathematics. They're disagreeing about what it would take for the mind-is-a-machine thesis to be the kind of claim that a Gödel sentence could refute — whether it names a particular inspectable system whose consistency its designer can certify, or something looser and open-ended that no single diagonalization could ever touch. If that's right, the debate is half verbal and half a dispute about who has to specify the target.

But maybe I've got it wrong. Maybe the real fault line is the epistemic verb doing all the work — the seeing in the claim that we can see the sentence to be true — and the sides just disagree about what that seeing is: introspection, proof, or reliable disposition. Or maybe there's a hidden empirical question in there after all, one that a good enough result about what trained systems can and can't do would settle. Or maybe one side is answering a question about what can be captured by an algorithm and the other about what can be captured by an algorithm we're allowed to look inside, and they've been sharing a headline for six decades.

So: which is it, and can you argue for it from what each side actually says rather than from a survey of the positions? Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, something empirical, a change of question — or make the case that no progress should be expected and that the deadlock is the correct permanent state.

Last thing, and it's the reason I'm asking you instead of reading more: once you've diagnosed it, point me at the one or two pressure points where each character can push and the other genuinely can't wave it away. I need them to sound like two very smart people who are stuck, not like a smart person and a fool who happens to have a job at a lab.
