**Draft: "Seeds, Copies, and the Individuation of Patients"**

The starting assumption is a modest one. Suppose some functionalist account of phenomenal consciousness is correct, and correct in the specific strong form defended by Chalmers-style arguments from fading and dancing qualia: whether a system is conscious depends on the abstract causal organization it realizes, not on the stuff realizing it. Call this organizational invariance.

Organizations, however, are types, and any type admits of indefinitely many tokens. This generates a question that I think the literature on machine consciousness has largely skipped over, because it has been preoccupied with the prior question of whether any machine is conscious at all: when the same organization is realized more than once, is there one moral patient or several? Call the two answers the *unity thesis* (numerically distinct but organizationally identical realizations are not distinct patients; there is one patient, instantiated many times, and duplication adds nothing to what is at stake morally) and the *multiplicity thesis* (each realization is a separate patient, so welfare, harm, and moral weight scale with the number of copies).

Multiplicity has the intuition on its side. It also, I contend, has no principled stopping rule. Token-individuation over time is not primitive. Consider a simulation that forks into two branches differing in a single bit and then reconverges; consider two branches run in lockstep and periodically resynchronized; consider a process whose clock rate is doubled and then halved. On multiplicity, patients pop into and out of existence with every fork and merge, and the total moral weight in the world becomes a function of scheduling decisions that make no difference to what any subject undergoes. That is not a tolerable consequence.

I therefore propose what I call the *divergence criterion*. Two realizations constitute a single patient just in case they cannot come apart: given the actual arrangement of hardware, inputs, and governing laws, there is no possibility of their subsequent experiential trajectories differing. Individuation is modal rather than spatiotemporal. The criterion is deliberately not about what an intervening agent could do, since an agent can always delete one copy of anything; it is about whether the two runs are, as a matter of how the setup is actually wired, locked to one another.

Three consequences follow, and they are what make the view worth defending rather than merely tidy.

First, perfect duplication adds nothing. Two copies of the same program, deterministic, driven by bit-identical input streams on separate machines, realize one patient twice. Running a simulation a million times under the same seed and the same environment is morally equivalent to running it once, however many CPU-hours it consumes.

Second, randomness is what manufactures patients. The same simulation run a million times with a million distinct seeds produces a million distinct trajectories that can and do come apart, hence a million patients. Moral arithmetic in computational systems is governed not by the quantity of computation but by the quantity of *independently varying* computation. This relocates the ethical problem of large-scale training and evaluation away from scale per se and toward the diversity of the environments and initial conditions sampled.

Third, checkpointing and rollback are more serious than they look, while replication is less serious than it looks. Reverting a run to an earlier state destroys a trajectory that had genuinely diverged from everything else, whereas cloning a run in progress duplicates a patient only for as long as the clones receive divergent inputs.

The view is a functionalist analogue of the Parfitian move in the fission cases: identity is not the relation that matters, because identity cannot handle one-to-many relations, so it is replaced by something that admits of degree. Here the analogue relation is shared-or-severable trajectory. I do not claim the analogy is exact, and I flag in the paper that Parfit's target was persistence over time in one subject whereas mine is patienthood across simultaneously existing subjects.

The difficulty I have not solved concerns the internalism constraint. Whether two subjects are one patient or two turns, on my account, on facts about seeds, wiring, and determinism that are entirely external to what either subject undergoes. Two beings with internally indistinguishable lives would have different moral status depending on how the laboratory happens to be configured. That seems to me to be a genuine cost, and I currently have only a deflection: moral status may attach to trajectories rather than to moments, and a trajectory just is something that can be shared.
