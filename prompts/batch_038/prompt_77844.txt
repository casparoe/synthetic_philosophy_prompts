I teach an upper-level seminar on decision theory and agency, and I'm putting together the final. I want one essay question built on the Death in Damascus case — the one where the traveler meets Death in Damascus, is told Death has an appointment with him tomorrow in Aleppo, flees there overnight, and finds Death waiting, exactly as promised. But the version I want to set is a variant of my own, because I don't want anyone able to pass by just retelling the original and reciting the evidential/causal labels. Here's the variant, and the question has to be built on these stipulations and nothing else:

Call it Death in Damascus with a mirror. Same setup, same announcement, but the traveler also knows something about his own mind. He has no evidence of any kind about Death's movements apart from the announcement. And he has noticed that his beliefs about where Death will be track nothing but his own inclinations: whenever he settles on going to a city, his credence that Death will be there snaps straight to 1, and if he abandons the plan the credence drops back toward 0. He also entertains exactly two hypotheses about how the world works. H1: the appointment was fixed before the traveler ever chose anything, and his movements are causally irrelevant to it. H2: Death simply goes wherever the traveler ends up. His split between H1 and H2 is not stated anywhere in the case.

The crux I want the question to force is this: does the mirror stipulation actually refute evidential decision theory, or does it describe an epistemic state no deliberating agent could coherently occupy? A good answer has to notice that a snap-to-1 credence about Death's location is only licensed if the traveler is already certain of H2 — and that once he's certain of H2, Death's announcement stops being news at all, which arguably dissolves the puzzle rather than deepening it. I want students to have to argue for or against that, and to say whether there's any deliberational equilibrium in the case given that every act carries zero expected utility once it's chosen. They should also handle the standing worry that a deliberating agent can't treat her own act as something she has a probability for, and the associated ratificationist idea that an act is admissible only if it maximizes expected utility conditional on being chosen.

What I need from you:

1. The question itself, printable and self-contained, around 150 words, including the variant's stipulations so a student who never heard of the original case can still do it. Build in one explicit instruction telling them to take a stand on whether the mirror stipulation is coherent rather than surveying both sides.
2. A model answer of about 700 words that actually commits to a position and argues for it. Don't let it end in a shrug. It should be reason-driven — no citations, no bibliography, no expectation that students remember who published what. Names of ideas are fine, names of papers are not.
3. A marking scheme totalling 25 marks, broken into five to seven named moves, each with its mark value and a one-line note on the specific way students typically botch it. Two of the moves should be the ones that catch the pure-recitation answer (the student who retells the story and defines evidential vs causal decision theory and stops).
4. A short note on what seperates an A from a B here — I want the single move that a B answer will reliably be missing, stated concretely enough that two different graders would apply it the same way.

It's a 75 minute in-class paper, so please don't set anything that needs a probability model on paper to get through. One or two small numerical stipulations are fine if they earn their keep, but the work should be conceptual.
