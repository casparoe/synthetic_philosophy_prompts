I am preparing to defend a reading of Aristotle on money in print, and before I commit to it I want a sober map of its exposure.

The reading has two connected parts. First, on my account what makes money money for Aristotle is the role convention assigns to it: it is a stand-in for the need or use (chreia) that does the real work of rendering unlike products — the output of the builder, the output of the shoemaker — comparable enough to be exchanged, and its standing rests on the fact that it can be altered or rendered useless by agreement rather than on any property of the material that carries it. Everything he says about the medium having to be intrinsically useful and easy to handle is, on my reading, a practical remark about a convenient vehicle, not a claim about the nature of money. Second, the condemnation of interest in the first book of the Politics inherits the same premise: money set to breeding is being turned to something other than the job that constitutes it, so the wrongness of interest is the violation of a function, not a complaint about gain drawn from a substance incapable of generating offspring.

I would like the following.

1. The defeaters, ranked from those that would sink the reading to those that are merely awkward. Identify the places where my account has to strain or explain away, and the passages whose argument seems to work better on the rival view that money's nature is fixed by its material. Describe these by content and argumentative role rather than by reference, so I can locate them myself.
2. What my reading predicts that the rival does not — for instance, whether a functionalist Aristotle should have any principled objection to token coin, credit, or money whose value is set purely by fiat — and whether his apparent silence on such cases can serve as evidence against me at all, or whether argument from silence is simply unavailable in interpretive work of this kind.
3. Whether the coherence demand behind my reading is itself the weak point. Is there a serious case that the treatment of exchange in the Ethics and the treatment of acquisition in the Politics are answering different questions for different purposes, so that consistency is the wrong standard to apply — and if that case succeeds, what is left of my thesis?
4. Whether the reading depends on a theory of value Aristotle may not possess. If chreia is read as use rather than need, or if one insists that he denies genuine commensurability between a house and so many beds, does my functionalism collapse into the deflationary view that he has no account of exchange value whatsoever — and hence nothing for a functionalist theory of money to be a theory of?
5. An honest judgment on insulation. Can my clause about mere practical remarks absorb any material-friendly text whatsoever? If yes, is that fatal in the way unfalsifiability is fatal to an empirical hypothesis, or is something structurally different going on with an interpretive thesis, such that the standard objection simply does not apply? I want a verdict, not a hedge — including the possibility that the reading is more insulated than substantive, and that it succeeds mainly because its rival is a modern gloss with weak textual support rather than because it is positively well grounded.
6. What a committed defender of my view would concede as counting against it, and what they would dismiss as mistranslation or anachronism.

For calibration: I would be most moved by a stretch of argument whose force is intelligible only on the rival reading, and second by a demonstration that my functional premise makes the conclusion about interest either trivially true or a non sequitur. Tell me plainly if either of those obtains.
