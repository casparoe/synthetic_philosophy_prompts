**Question (20 marks).** Consider the following two-player game, in which each cell lists (row player's payoff, column player's payoff).

|  | L | C | R |
|---|---|---|---|
| **U** | (8, 1) | (0, 0) | (1, 3) |
| **M** | (3, 5) | (3, 1) | (2, 2) |
| **D** | (0, 0) | (8, 5) | (4, 2) |

(a) [8 marks] Using iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, and allowing a strategy to be dominated by a *mixed* strategy, determine the set of strategy profiles that survive. At each step, state the strategy or mixture that does the dominating and verify the relevant inequalities.
(b) [4 marks] Explain why the set you obtained in (a) does not depend on the order in which the eliminations are carried out. At which step, if any, was it essential to permit domination by a mixed strategy?
(c) [4 marks] Determine the set of Nash equilibria of the game and explain the connection with your answer to (a). Are the surviving strategies rationalizable?
(d) [4 marks] Would the property invoked in (b) continue to hold if weakly dominated strategies could also be deleted? Justify your answer, using an example of your own if that helps.

**Student's answer.**

*(a)* Method note: for each of a player's strategies I check whether some mixture over that player's own strategies yields a strictly higher payoff against every strategy of the opponent, pure or mixed. Both halves of the check are required. It is not enough to verify the inequality against the opponent's pure strategies, because a strategy can yield more than another against each pure strategy and yet less against some mixture of them.

Row player, original game. U is not dominated: it yields 8 at L, more than M's 3 or D's 0, so no mixture over M and D can beat it at L. D is not dominated: it yields 8 at C, more than U's 0 or M's 3. M is strictly dominated by ½U + ½D, which yields (4, 4, 2.5) against (L, C, R), against M's (3, 3, 2). Neither U nor D dominates M on its own, since U yields 0 at C and D yields 0 at L, so this step needs a mixture. Delete M.

Column player, original game. Nothing can be deleted. L yields the column player 5 at M, more than C's 1 or R's 2; C yields 5 at D, more than L's 0 or R's 2; R yields 3 at U, more than L's 1 or C's 0. Each is therefore uniquely best against some row, and no mixture can beat a strategy at a profile at which that strategy is uniquely best.

Row player after deleting M: U = (8, 0, 1) and D = (0, 8, 4) over (L, C, R), so neither dominates the other. Column player after deleting M: L = (1, 0), C = (0, 5), R = (3, 2) over (U, D). R strictly dominates L, since 3 > 1 and 2 > 0; delete L. C and R are each still uniquely best (C at D, R at U) and survive.

Row player after deleting L: U = (0, 1) and D = (8, 4) over (C, R), so D strictly dominates U; delete U. Column player with only D remaining: C yields 5 and R yields 2, so C strictly dominates R; delete R.

Unique surviving profile: (D, C), payoffs (8, 5).

*(b)* The order of deletion does not affect the final set. Strict dominance is defined entirely in terms of the player's own payoff function and own strategy set: s_i is strictly dominated when some mixture over player i's strategies beats it against every profile of the other players. So deleting one of player i's own strategies cannot make a previously undominated strategy of player i dominated, since that only removes candidate dominators; and it cannot rescue a dominated strategy either, since we only ever delete dominated strategies and a dominated strategy is never needed in a dominating mixture. Deleting a strategy of the other player likewise cannot alter player i's dominance relations, because those relations make no reference to the other player's payoffs and are checked profile by profile. It follows that which strategies are strictly dominated is settled by the game as given, and any order of deletion ends at the same survivors. A mixture was essential only at the first step, the deletion of M: neither U nor D dominates M by itself, though ½U + ½D does.

*(c)* Every Nash equilibrium survives the procedure, because a strictly dominated strategy is never a best response to anything and so cannot carry positive probability in an equilibrium mixture: each pure strategy in the support of a player's equilibrium mixture must itself be a best response. Since exactly one profile survives, (D, C) is the unique Nash equilibrium, and it is a strict one — D beats U (8 to 0) and M (8 to 3) against C, and C beats L (5 to 0) and R (5 to 2) against D. On rationalizability: D is a best response to C and C is a best response to D, so both pass the round-by-round best-response test, and because this is a two-player game the strategies surviving iterated strict dominance and the rationalizable strategies coincide.

*(d)* No. Deleting weakly dominated strategies can change the surviving set depending on the order, and can even remove Nash equilibria. For example:

|  | L | R |
|---|---|---|
| **T** | (1, 2) | (1, 1) |
| **B** | (1, 0) | (0, 0) |

T weakly dominates B for the row player (equal at L, strictly better at R), and L weakly dominates R for the column player (strictly better at T, equal at B). Deleting B first leaves only T, against which R yields the column player 1 and L yields 2, so R goes too and the procedure ends at (T, L). Deleting R first leaves only L, against which T and B both yield the row player 1, so neither row strategy is even weakly dominated any more and the surviving set is {T, B} × {L}. Note too that (B, L) is a Nash equilibrium of the original game — the row player is indifferent at L and the column player is indifferent at B — so the first of these two orders destroys an equilibrium.
