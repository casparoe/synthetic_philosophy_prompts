A hospital committee I sit on must fix a cutoff on a continuous frailty index for allocating a scarce therapy. When a clinician objected that any threshold is arbitrary, the chair answered with an analogy: drawing the line, he said, is like drawing a boundary around a blurred region in an out-of-focus photograph — the blur is in the picture, not in the scene, so wherever you put the line you are not cutting against anything real, and one cutoff is no more arbitrary than the next. A colleague objected that this is the sorites paradox dressed in technical vocabulary.

Please audit the analogy. Give the mapping explicitly — what corresponds to the photograph, the scene, the blur, the resolution of the lens, the act of drawing a line — then say where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that threaten the chair's use of it from those that are beside the point. Two questions especially: does the analogy presuppose that a sharper picture is available in principle, and what follows if a predicate like frail admits of no such picture however finely we measure? And what does the analogy quietly assume about whether the indeterminacy lies in our instruments, in our concept, or in the patients themselves? Conclude with whether the permissiveness about the threshold survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if it does not, what better analogy would look like and what attitude toward the cutoff it licenses instead.
