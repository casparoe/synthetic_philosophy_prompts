I'm a second-year graduate student in comparative literature, and this term I've been auditing an introductory seminar on early Chinese philosophy while running a small reading group on the Inner Chapters of the Zhuangzi. To give the group some framing, I picked up a recent trade-press book on how philosophers have faced death, one chapter of which is devoted to Zhuangzi. Two friends who actually work on Chinese thought told me the chapter is basically sound, but I came away uneasy and I can't locate why. Here is the passage that did it:

"Zhuangzi's position on death is unusually bracing, and it is worth setting out carefully. When his wife died, his friend Huishi came to offer condolences and found him sitting on the ground, drumming on an upturned basin and singing. Pressed on this, Zhuangzi explained that he had indeed grieved at first, but had recognized that grief as a failure of nerve and reasoned himself out of it: before his wife was born there was neither form nor breath, and her death was one more turn of the wheel, like the succession of spring, summer, autumn and winter, so that to go on weeping would be to confess that he had not understood what had happened. His conclusion is essentially Epicurus's — death cannot harm the one who dies, because when it arrives there is no one left for it to arrive to. What makes Zhuangzi's version distinctively Daoist is the metaphysics behind it: he thinks of a person as a temporary knot in the ceaseless transformation of qi, so that strictly speaking nothing is ever destroyed, and the fear of death rests on a false picture of the self as a thing with edges. It is the same mistake the butterfly dream exposes, since the dreamer is certain he is the man and not the butterfly, and he is wrong to be certain. This equanimity is the heart of Daoism, and it explains why later Daoists developed it into a program of longevity practices and internal alchemy: they were simply following Zhuangzi's logic all the way down, trying to make the transformation of the body answer to the mind."

My suspicion, for what it's worth, is that Zhuangzi is less a consolatory metaphysician and more someone who declines to say what death is at all, and that the butterfly dream is being made to carry weight it cannot bear. But I've read only the Inner Chapters closely, twice, in a single English translation, so I may well be the one who is wrong. Could you audit the paragraph claim by claim, sorting each into what a scholar of early Chinese philosophy would accept as it stands, what is a defensible simplification for a general audience, what is genuinely contested within the scholarship, and what is a misreading? I'd like you to adjudicate the Epicurus comparison explicitly: is there a reading on which the two really converge, or does the paragraph's own qi metaphysics contradict the Epicurean premise it is offered alongside? Please also tell me what the paragraph leaves out that would change the verdict — the episodes I half-remember that seem to cut against it (the dream of the skull who declines an offer to be sent back, Meng Sun Cai at the funeral, the dying man's remarks about what he would do if the creator turned his arm into a rooster, the rebuke of the wailing at Laozi's funeral), together with the fact that some of the material the chapter leans on sits outside the Inner Chapters and is voiced by characters rather than asserted by the author. Finally, say whether the closing genealogy — from Zhuangzi's equanimity to later longevity practice and internal alchemy — survives correction, whether a specialist would resist the paragraph's tacit assumption that Zhuangzi is producing an argument with premises and a consolation rather than retraining attention by other means, and give me two or three sentences I could hand my reading group as a fairer summary.
