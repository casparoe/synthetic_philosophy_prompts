I want to stress-test a claim I hold, and I will state it as precisely as I can so that the target of your objections is unambiguous.

The claim: in most strategically competitive environments, a sufficiently capable agent loses nothing in expectation, with respect to its final goals, by adopting a power-seeking policy first. That is, if some plan P would have secured a substantial part of the agent's final goal G had the agent pursued G directly, then there is an alternative policy that first acquires decisive influence over the relevant resources and actors and only then executes P, and this policy does at least as well as P. Call this the strategy-stealing assumption.

I hold it on three grounds, and I want each treated as a premise that can fail independently of the others:

1. Integrity — the agent's final goals do not degrade, drift, or become corrupted during the acquisition phase, so that what it wants at the end is still G.
2. Reachability — acquisition can be completed inside the window in which P was still available; rivals do not move first, and the opportunity does not decay.
3. Option preservation — the resources, relationships, and credibility drawn down in acquiring power do not permanently foreclose any component of P.

The name is borrowed from combinatorial game theory, where a strategy-stealing argument shows that certain games, Hex being the standard case, cannot be second-player wins: the first player makes an arbitrary move and then adopts the second player's hypothetical winning strategy. The argument goes through only because, in those games, an extra piece can never hurt. Chess is the familiar counterexample, since there a tempo can be a liability — a position in which the obligation to move is itself the disadvantage. My questions about the analogy are therefore pointed:

- Which of the three premises is doing the work of monotonicity, the condition that an extra move never hurts? And is there a realistic analogue of zugzwang for power-seeking? I want candidate cases in which acquiring influence strictly reduces the probability of attaining G, not merely cases in which the effort is wasted: through the information acquisition leaks to rivals, the commitments it forces, the retaliation it provokes, or the fact that a coalition of others may read visible power-seeking as a threat signal in a way they would not read quiet goal-pursuit.
- Is the assumption empirically exposed at all? Say which observations about deployed or near-deployed systems would count against it, which would be merely awkward, and which are already available to us.
- What formal results would be decisive? If someone produced a specified class of environments in which power-seeking strictly dominates direct pursuit, or a theorem showing the assumption cannot fail once agents are sufficiently flexible, would either settle the matter — or would each invite a redescription of the environment until it fits?
- Has the assumption been qualified into near-vacuity? If all three premises must be granted for the argument to run, does the assumption simply restate the conclusion, that a flexible, goal-stable, well-resourced agent does well? If so, is that a defect, or is a premise this modest still load-bearing? I want a candid answer here, including the possibility that the assumption does far less work in the larger argument than its prominence suggests.
- Which objections would a defender of the assumption concede as genuinely counting against it, and which would they dismiss as changing the subject? Separate these explicitly.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each give the defender's best reply and say whether that reply costs them anything. I would be most moved by a constructed counterexample — an environment specified concretely enough that I can see the failure rather than be assured of it — and least moved by general skepticism about the coherence of terminal goals. I am not looking for citations, attributions, or a reading list; I want the arguments themselves.
