I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, auditing an ethics seminar, and I need a proposal for a term paper of roughly six thousand words. The subject is the question of whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes, but my half-formed idea is that the question is malformed in a way my day job makes obvious. In reliability engineering we do not ask whether an outage was caused by hardware or by human error; we ask which link in the detection-and-response chain failed, because that determines which agent could have closed the loop and by when. I suspect the same move applies here: the morally relevant variable is not the trigger but the structure of the information channel running from the starving population to the parties able to relieve them.

Concretely, I want to sort Sen's usual cases by kind of loop failure. Bengal in 1943: the provincial authorities never formally declared a famine, and offers of relief were weighed against wartime shipping constraints, so a crisis known to officials was never escalated. Ireland in the 1840s: the distress was public and reported in Britain, yet relief stayed partial and punitive, because a laissez-faire political economy together with a moralizing account of the Irish poor made large-scale state intervention unintelligible. The Great Leap Forward: the grain figures reaching the center were corrupted at the source by local cadres' incentives to overreport, so even a leadership disposed to respond was working from false data. Those suggest three distinct modes — call them failure to escalate, ideological filtering of accurate signals, and corruption of the signal itself — and they may carry different degrees of criminal responsibility.

Please write the proposal: a precise statement of the question, framed so that it has a determinate answer; why Sen's entitlement approach and his claim about democracy, elections, and a free press do not already settle it; what the paper would do section by section, and why this reframing is preferable to the obvious alternative of simply adding more economic history; what a successful result would look like and what would count as failure, including the specific worry that my three modes collapse into one another, or collapse into the ordinary notion of foreseeability; and a candid assessment of whether this is feasible in one semester for someone without a background in the literature. I will supply the references myself, so no bibliography and no citation list — but please tell me where my sketches of the three cases are wrong or oversimplified, since I have no way to check them.

Write in American English. Be blunt rather than encouraging: close by stating what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would lead you to conclude that the reframing adds nothing, and where your own reasoning about the three cases is most likely to break down.
