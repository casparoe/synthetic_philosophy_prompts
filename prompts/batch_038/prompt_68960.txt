so i've been mulling this for a while and what I want out of you is a satire piece, not an explainer. I came up through the rationalist-blog stack, so the Axelrod-adjacent material is deeply familiar to me — which is exactly why I want it skewered from the inside, by someone who clearly knows it cold.

The conceit: a syndicated advice column run by an evolutionary game theorist who can only answer reader letters in the vocabulary of repeated games. Give the column a name in that register and give him a name. Five letters, each with a reply, about 900 words total, deadpan start to finish.

The joke is NOT "look how funny it is to treat your roommate as a payoff matrix" — that's the cheap version and it's been written a thousand times. The joke is that he is entirely correct about the formal results and still completely useless, and that the framework quietly eats any data that contradicts it. Specifically:

1. Letter one is from someone whose roommate has started defecting on chores. The reply should be a genuinely correct diagnosis — how long is the shadow of the future here, is this an infinitely repeated game or a finite one with a known last round (the lease ends in June), what backward induction implies about the final weeks. He spots the endgame, is entirely unbothered that actual roommates keep cooperating in the last week, and files that observation under "people are irrational."

2. Letter two is from someone whose friend retaliates enormously over small misunderstandings and then never lets it go. This is where you get to be sophisticated: under noise, tit for tat is a disaster because of echo effects, you want something generous or a win-stay-lose-shift rule, and grim trigger is worse still. But he has to deliver all of that as a verdict on the friend's character — she's missing the forgiveness parameter — rather than as a fact about the limits of the model.

3. Letter three is the long one. A guy who read some pop account of the tournaments and concluded that being nice, provocable, unenvious and clear is a proven recipe for success in life, and now wants to deploy it at work. The columnist has to deflate this without turning into a cynic about cooperation, so have him make the real points: that a strategy's score depends on the field it happened to be entered against, that the second round of entrants had read the write-up of the first, that the folk theorem leaves an embarassment of equilibria and doesn't single out the nice ones — always defect is itself an equilibrium, at any discount factor. Then the punchline: he recommends tit for tat anyway, with exactly the same confidence, and nobody in the column is allowed to notice.

4. Letter four is short. Someone whose ex was pure ALLD. Two or three sentences, brutal, and correct: you cannot elicit cooperation from a strategy that doesn't care about the future, and the only moves are to change the game or leave it.

5. Letter five is from someone who has decided to cooperate with strangers on the grounds that they're probably running the same reasoning he is, so their choices are correlated with his. The reply is the standard game-theory dismissal — your opponent isn't a copy of your decision procedure, there's no mechanism linking the two, correlation isn't a strategy — delivered with total confidence by a man who has never for one second considered that the letter-writer might be onto something.

Hard constraints: no winking, no "of course real humans aren't utility-maximizers" hedges, no author's note at the end explaining the bit. "Nash" appears at most once. He can name-drop strategies freely (Generous TFT, Pavlov, Grim Trigger, ALLD, Random, Tit for Two Tats) but no paper citations, no bibliography, and no real named people as targets — the pop-account author is invented, so give his book a suitably TED-talk-y title. The columnist never states the moral. If it works, a reader who knows the literature should recognize every complaint he makes as real, and then wince at what he does with them.
