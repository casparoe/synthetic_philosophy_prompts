Background: I ran an electrophysiology lab for 35 years, mostly sensory systems, and only picked up philosophy properly after I retired. Lately I've been chewing on the consciousness stuff, and especially on this meta-problem business — the idea that the tractable question isn't how to bridge the explanatory gap but why we're so reliably disposed to insist there is one. Here's roughly where I've landed, and I want a consistency check before I inflict it on a reading group.

1. The hard problem is real, not a pseudo-problem. Something is left over once you've explained discrimination, access, attention, verbal report.
2. But the doable project is the meta-problem: a mechanistic account of the introspective and linguistic machinery that generates gap-talk. That's a lab-shaped question, the kind I used to actually run.
3. Solving (2) wouldn't solve (1). Explaining why we say there's a residue doesn't touch the residue.
4. Introspection is an instrument. Like any instrument it has finite resolution and it throws artifacts. I trust an introspective deliverance only insofar as it can be calibrated against third-person measurement — confidence ratings, thresholds, timing. Uncalibratable deliverances get no credence from me.
5. The single most vivid thing introspection tells me is that experience outruns anything I can report. That's the datum I'd fight hardest to keep.
6. I'm a physicalist; I dont want extra ingredients in the world.
7. Zombies still seem conceivable to me, and I'm willing to let that seeming carry at least a little evidential weight.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, and how hard? Rank the tensions by severity rather than handing me a list where everything is equally troubled. Flag any pairs that only look bad and dissolve once you spell out what each claim is really doing. For each real one, say what the cheapest thing to give up is and what giving it up costs. Then tell me which single revision would buy the most coherence across the whole set.
