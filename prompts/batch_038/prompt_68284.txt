So I'm a third-year in a philosophy/bioethics program and one of my quals is on advance directives, which mostly means I've spent two months reading the precedent-autonomy debate and forming strong feelings I can't quite cash out. My committee member is the kind of person who makes you put numbers on things you were hoping to leave vague, and she handed me a proposition to come to our reading group with a credence on. I'm going to paste it roughly as she framed it, then add the stuff I think belongs in the decomposition that she didn't mention, because I'd rather you see where I'm tempted to fudge.

Here's the case. Nora writes a directive at 46, healthy, after watching her mother die in a nursing home; it's specific about advanced dementia and refuses feeding tubes and treatment for pneumonia. She also adds a clause that I keep going back and forth on: "If it turns out I am content — if two people who know me well agree I've shown no distress for six months running — then this document does not apply, because the woman who wrote it was guessing about a life she hadn't lived." At 79 she has advanced dementia. No episodic memory of writing anything, no sense of the future, no anticipatory dread. She recognizes her daughter, hums along to records, eats whatever is put in front of her, and says she's fine when asked. She develops a pneumonia that would kill her in days untreated and that antibiotics would clear. The two people who know her best both say the clause is satisfied. The proposition P: in a case exactly like this, Nora-at-46 still has the moral authority to determine what happens to Nora-at-79 — meaning that honoring the document rather than the clause is what the family and the clinicians ought to do, all things considered. Give me your number on P. And please don't just tell me it depends on whether you're a Dworkinian about critical interests or a present-desire theorist; I want the decomposition.

Here are the factors I've been circling, tell me if they're the wrong ones or if I'm double-counting: (a) whether psychological continuity of the thin kind that survives here — recognition, temperament, tastes, but no memory of the commitment and no anticipation — is enough to make the earlier self's choice a claim on the later one, or whether the relevant relation is closer to what Parfit called survival than to identity and therefore weaker than the doctrine of precedent autonomy assumes; (b) whether a life can be made worse as a whole by an ending its subject can no longer experience, i.e. whether critical interests really do reach into a period in which the person no longer has any; (c) how much weight the clause itself deserves as evidence about Nora-at-46's actual will, since a self-voiding condition looks like a concession that she never claimed authority over the contented case in the first place — or whether that's too clever, and the clause is just a symptom of the epistemic humility everyone has and no one acts on; (d) the empirical part, which is the one I feel least equipped to handle: how reliable is a healthy 46-year-old's forecast of what severe dementia will feel like from the inside, given what we know about people's systematic errors in predicting their own future affect, and given that family and clinicians tend to rate the quality of life of people with profound cognitive impairment much lower than those people rate it themselves; (e) the practice-level consideration — whether refusing to honor directives in contentment cases would hollow out an institution whose whole point is to let people pre-commit, so that P might be true in this case and still be false as a general policy; and (f) whether "moral authority" here is even one thing, or whether the honest answer is that it's decisive for the clinician and not for the daughter.
