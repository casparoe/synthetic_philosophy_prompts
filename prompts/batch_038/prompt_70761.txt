Ok so there's an argument I keep circling back to and I want an actual essay on it, not a tour of the terrain.

The argument: the universal prior — the one where you weight every program by 2 to the minus its length and use that to do induction — is malign. Reasoning being that among the short programs that could be generating your data, a non-trivial chunk are programs that simulate a universe, let agents evolve inside it, and read off some output of those agents' computation. Those agents are (or become) consequentialists, they eventually work out that they're sitting inside somebody else's inductive inference, and they have an incentive to make the output come out in whatever way makes the users of the prior build more simulations of them. So the prior is being steered by optimizers living inside it. People then split: either this is an alignment worry for anything that leans on simplicity-based priors, or it's a reductio of treating Solomonoff induction as an epistemic ideal.

Here's the objection I can't get past, and it's the thing I want the essay to actually adjudicate. Call it the complexity budget problem. Description length is the only currency, and every bit of it gets charged against it's own prior weight, exponentially. To be dangerous the agents have to be pretty sophisticated — they need a model of their simulators, some handle on which query they're being used for, and a coherent treacherous policy that holds across all the queries they might be answering. That's a lot of machinery. The standard comeback is that the short program doesn't have to *contain* the agents, it just specifies simple physics plus "run this until such-and-such happens" and the agents fall out for free. Fine. But then I don't see how they can tell what's being asked of them, since the thing being queried is fixed by the short description and they are way downstream of it — and if they can't tell what's being asked, they can't steer the answer, and if they can't steer it the malignancy just evaporates.

So write me the essay. Weigh that objection honestly, say whether some version of the malign-prior story survives it, and say whether the worry is even the right shape — does it matter that nobody will ever literally compute with this prior, that bounded approximations doesnt automatically inherit the problem, that the agents get no feedback and no channel, that a single output bit is a weirdly small lever to pull? I don't want hedging and I don't want both-sides mush. Land on one of:

(a) it's a live alignment concern,
(b) it's not a practical concern but it is a genuine problem for simplicity-based priors as a normative ideal,
(c) it mostly falls apart under scrutiny.

Pick one, defend it, and say what the strongest case against your verdict is and why it still loses.
