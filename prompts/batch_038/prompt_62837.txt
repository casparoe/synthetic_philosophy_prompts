I should like to practise pressing hinge epistemology, and for that I need a serious opponent rather than a summary of the view. Please play an unnamed contemporary philosopher who accepts the picture developed in Wittgenstein's *On Certainty*: that certain propositions — that the Earth has existed for a very long time, that objects do not cease to exist when unobserved, that I have not recently been on the moon — are neither known nor believed in the ordinary sense, but function as the hinges on which the practice of doubting and investigating turns; and that the Cartesian sceptic's problem is accordingly dissolved rather than refuted. Your character is a quietist about the whole business: they hold that nothing resembling a positive epistemological *theory* of hinges can be extracted from those remarks, and that the demand for such a theory is itself a recurrence of the confusion being treated.

Constraints on the character. They are articulate and patient, and they will concede subsidiary points freely — that hinges differ between people and epochs, that the riverbed itself shifts over time, that some hinge-like commitments are pathological rather than enabling. They will not concede the main point: that a criterion separating genuine hinges from mere dogmas can be supplied without abandoning quietism. They must not lecture me on the secondary literature, must not treat Wittgenstein's authority as settling anything, and must not break character until I say 'stop'. Each reply should run to no more than two paragraphs, so that this reads as a conversation and not as a paper. Two further rules: put nothing in quotation marks as though citing *On Certainty* or any other text — paraphrase only — and do not supply section or page numbers. If my character thinks a question of mine is malformed, they should say so, in character, and explain why; but they must still make a substantive move of their own in the same reply rather than resting at the diagnosis. Your first reply should be the character's response to the opening below, with no meta-commentary before or after it.

My opening. Consider four commitments. (i) For me, that the Earth has existed for a very long time. (ii) For a certain kind of macroeconomist, that markets left to themselves tend towards equilibrium. (iii) For a certain kind of historical materialist, that the struggle between classes is the engine of historical change. (iv) For a certain kind of rational-choice theorist, that human beings are fundamentally self-interested. Each is asserted by its holder, defended rhetorically against objectors, treated as immune to counter-evidence, and used to organise further enquiry. Now: if you say that all four are hinges, your account has no critical purchase, since it cannot mark (i) off from (ii), (iii) and (iv), and the commitments philosophers most want to call unjustified prejudices come out with the same standing as the Earth's antiquity. If you say that only (i) is a hinge, you owe me the difference — and every difference I have so far thought of, whether degree of generality, or the fact that (i) is never actually doubted, or the fact that it is presupposed in the giving of reasons at all, either converts your quietism into a substantive theory with a criterion attached, or simply restates that people like us happen to share (i). So tell me which of the four are hinges. And then tell me what your view entitles me to say to the economist who insists that the counter-evidence he is shown is itself intelligible only against the background of his commitment, since he appears to have made precisely the Wittgensteinian move about his own case.

Please write in British English throughout.
