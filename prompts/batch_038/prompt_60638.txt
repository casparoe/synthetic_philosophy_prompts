Write the minutes of an ordinary monthly meeting of the council of a small town that has adopted futarchy: no measure is voted on any longer; each contested proposal is listed as a pair of conditional prediction contracts, and whichever option the market prices higher is implemented. Around 650–750 words, in the flat administrative register of a clerk who is perfectly sincere and finds nothing remarkable about any item — roll call, agenda, motions, adjournment.

The comedy should come from the mechanics being right rather than from the mechanics being absurd. Work in, as routine business: the town's chosen welfare metric, together with the chair's reminder that the metric was settled by an ordinary democratic vote years ago and is therefore a question of values rather than of beliefs, and so not tradable; an item that must be deferred because its contract has no open interest; a resident who has quietly accumulated positions across several markets until he is, in practice, the legislature; a proposed safeguard whose announcement moves the price of the safeguard and thereby changes whether the safeguard is needed; an outcome that binds the town on the strength of a difference of a few cents at settlement; and a procedural dispute about precisely which event resolves a contract. When residents raise questions of fairness or of who is bearing the risk, the clerk should record these as interruptions, and the chair should answer each one correctly, in the system's own terms, and completely unsatisfyingly.

Name no real person, book, or firm, and keep the frame closed: no editorial asides, no wink in the last line, no character who stands outside the arrangement and sees through it. Someone who works on this material should be able to tell that its instabilities have been understood rather than invented; someone who has never heard of it should still enjoy the meeting as a piece of bureaucratic comedy. The target is the practice and its internal logic, not its critics.
