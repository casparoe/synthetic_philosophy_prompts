Acting as an expert on the medieval Islamic debates about causation (al-Ghazali, occasionalism, the Ash'arite side of things) who also happens to be good at writing speeches that get read aloud by a nervous person in a loud room: think carefully, and before you draft anything, give me a short brainstorm — maybe six to eight lines — working out what an occasionalist is actually allowed to say about a person's own track record, and where the standard forms of praise break down for him. Then write the speech.

The occasion: my uncle Rashid is retiring after 38 years as the senior reliability engineer at a chemical plant, and I'm his niece and I've been handed the mic at the dinner. About 80 people. Half of them are process engineers who live inside fault trees and FMEAs and PHA revalidations and will feel quietly cheated if the speech doesn't sound like it respects what they do all day. The other half are family, plus a few people from the mosque who have never heard of al-Ghazali and would be lost within ten seconds of terminology.

Here's the thing about him. He came to occasionalism in his twenties from reading The Incoherence of the Philosophers, specifically the fire-and-cotton business — the cotton doesn't burn because the fire makes it burn, the burning gets created at the moment of contact, and the only reason any of us can plan a shift schedule is that God keeps to his habit and hasn't broken it yet. He never called himself a philosopher. He called what he did "keeping the books on the habit," meaning: the habit isn't a promise you can lean your weight on, but it is reliable enough that not leaning on it would be a kind of ingratitude.

And his record is genuinely absurd. Twenty-one years, no lost-time incident in his area, and he found things. But every retirement speech at that plant is built out of the sentence "your vigilance prevented X," and he cannot hear that sentence. It's not modesty and it's not a bit. He thinks it's simply false — there is no preventing, there's only him showing up on time for thirty-eight years and the habit continuing, and handing him credit for the second part would be handing a creature a power that isn't his to have.

So the knot you need to solve, not route around: how do you honor a record like that in front of people who only know how to honor it causally, without saying one thing he'd have to correct from his seat, and without converting the whole room to his metaphysics or turning it into a seminar? And you can't just retreat into the safe vague stuff either — no "he was always there for us," that's an evasion and he'd clock it instantly.

Requirements:
- roughly five minutes, call it 650 words
- nothing in it that is false from his point of view, including by implication and including the jokes
- the engineers' idiom gets treated seriously, not mocked and not adopted wholesale
- exactly one use of the word "cause" in the entire speech, and it has to be a use he'd sign off on
- one joke that both halves of the room land on at the same moment
- it should end as affection, not as a lesson. No citations, no footnotes, no Arabic beyond a word or two that a room like that would already know, no "in conclusion"

Also tell me, in two sentences after the speech, which single line is doing the most work in honoring the record, and why it survives his objection.
