You are a philosopher of social science with substantial experience of the methodological individualism versus holism debate, and you have served as an editor on journals and edited collections in the field. Please think this problem through carefully rather than answering from first impressions.

I work as editorial assistant to the senior editor of a forthcoming collection on social explanation. She handles every submission herself, and I have now seen four years of her decisions. Her letters are unhelpfully terse: when she rejects, she writes some version of "the social facts in this paper do work that the people in the story never do"; when she accepts, she writes some version of "here you can see exactly how it works." Both formulas seem to cover several different complaints at once, so her decisions underdetermine her criterion. I need to write a recommendation on a new submission before she returns from leave, and I would like to predict her verdict accurately and to know whether recommending consistency with her record is the right thing to do.

Her record on submissions, described by the structure of the explanation each offered:

Accepted:
1. A paper on a bank run, explaining the collapse by depositors' beliefs about other depositors' behavior, with the cascade reconstructed step by step: what each depositor expected, what she did on that expectation, what the next depositor then observed.
2. A paper on a successful protest, explained by the demonstrators' shared intention to act as a single body. The author argued at length that this intention cannot be decomposed into each person's private intention plus beliefs about everyone else's, yet the paper also gave a fine-grained account of how the shared intention produced the coordination at the barricade: who moved when, why others followed, what the police inferred.
3. A paper on a monetary policy decision, explained as the deliberately engineered product of a committee: the voting rule, each member's incentives under it, the sequence of interventions in the meeting, and the final tally. Nothing unintended about the outcome.
4. A paper on an asset bubble, explained by a convention about what the asset was worth. Participants' beliefs were explicitly beliefs about the convention rather than about other participants' private beliefs, so the author conceded the convention could not be paraphrased away into individual attitudes; but the paper showed in detail how the convention was sustained by mutual expectation, gossip, and sanctioning.

Rejected:
5. A paper explaining the outbreak of a war by the logic of the interstate system, where the author treated the system as having causal powers that are neither a matter of, nor fixed by, what the statesmen and populations in it think and do, and offered no account of anything happening through individuals.
6. A paper on a riot's escalation invoking an emergent crowd mind, described as arising from the participants but neither reducible to nor determined by their states.
7. A paper arguing that an institution persists because it meets the needs of the larger social whole, with the needs characterized entirely at the level of the whole and no feedback process through anyone's behavior.
8. A paper explaining regional economic stagnation by the spirit of the age, again with no process described and an explicit denial that this explanans is fixed by what individuals think or do.

The new submission explains why one manufacturer held a conservative investment policy for four decades across six successive management teams, most of whom privately wanted to abandon it. The explanans is a dividend covenant together with a supermajority clause in the company's articles of incorporation, backed by the fiduciary-duty regime that makes the covenant enforceable. The causal process is laid out in unusual detail: each incoming chief financial officer's reading of the covenant, the general counsel's advice, board members' expectation of a veto, shareholders' standing threat of derivative litigation, and how each team's resulting behavior reproduced the policy for the next team. But the author's stated philosophical position is that the covenant is not fixed by, and does not supervene on, what any set of individuals thinks, wants, or does — its content is set by a document and a body of case law rather than by anyone's attitudes, it binds even if every living participant wishes it gone, and it constrains individuals rather than being constituted by them. The author presents the case as proof that irreducible macro-level causation is real and charges that methodological individualists cannot accommodate it.

Please do the following:

First, before settling on anything, brainstorm at least five candidate criteria that would account for all eight of her decisions. I have in mind that some will be mechanism-centered, some will be about what the macro facts are fixed by, some about what may be predicated of collectives, some about reducibility or paraphrase, and some about whether outcomes must be unintended — but generate your own list and do not assume mine is complete. Then test each candidate against all eight verdicts and say which survive.

Second, state what each surviving criterion predicts about the new submission, and where they diverge. If some of them predict the same verdict for different reasons, say so rather than padding the list.

Third, give me your best guess at her verdict, as a definite accept or reject, with the reasons you find most probative. I want a committed prediction and a probability you would actually defend, not a survey of possibilities with the choice left to me. If you think the record genuinely cannot be read one way rather than the other, say that plainly and then still tell me which way you would bet and why.

Fourth, identify the single further case — one more submission I could describe to her — that would best separate the surviving criteria from one another, and say what each predicts she would do with it.

Fifth, turn to my own recommendation. Suppose I write the report: tell me which decision best coheres with her record, which of the eight precedents is the closest analog to the new submission and in what specific respect it is closer than the others, and what principle the collection would be committed to by ruling one way rather than the other — including any principle she would thereby be adopting that she may not have noticed she was adopting. If you think consistency with her record is not the right criterion here, say so and defend the alternative.

You do not need to cite or recommend any literature, and please do not supply references; work from the structure of the cases as I have described them. Write your answer in American English.
