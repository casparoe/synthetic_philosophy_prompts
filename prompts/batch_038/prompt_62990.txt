I run a two-day workshop for a development NGO — half the room are country-office staff who deal with permits, procurement and district officials every single day, half are compliance people from headquarters who write the policy documents those staff then have to live with. I want to hand them a sorting instrument along the lines of "where do you actually stand on this," but about *what counts as corruption* rather than about whether they'd take a bribe. Everyone there already says the right thing about bribes; the interesting disagreement is somewhere else and I want it out in the open by the coffee break.

So: eight to ten questions, each one a short concrete scenario with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions. Some constraints, because the default version of this will be useless to me:

- Not one question may use the words corrupt, bribery, unethical, illegal or wrong. They have to answer about the situation, not about the vocabulary. Half the point is that people who pick the same answer in every case turn out to use completely different words for it afterwards, and people who use the same words disagree about the cases.
- Build the space before you name anything in it. Use three axes I care about: (a) whether the giver is expecting one specific official act back, or just general goodwill and continued standing; (b) whether the relationship is supposed to outlive the transaction or terminate in it; (c) whether the person doing it would defend it out loud, in their own name, to a stranger who wasn't involved. Give me the whole grid of combinations, mark which cells are actually occupied by a position somebody holds, and for the empty ones say why it's empty — incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.
- Leaves I need present at the end: plain bribery; the facilitation payment, the thing people call speed money, where the official is doing what he's already supposed to do, just slower; a gift case where refusing would itself be the offence and the giving is expected of you whether or not you want anything; patronage as a functioning service-delivery system, where the MP who brings jobs to his own people is doing the job; hiring your cousin because you owe your family and the family got you the qualification; the hardline view that any use of public office for any private end is corruption and the rest is rationalisation; and the deflationary view that "corruption" is mostly a word outsiders use for reciprocity that works perfectly well where it happens. Add more leaves if the grid seems to demand them, and give each one its standard name if it has one — if you're inventing a label, say that you invented it.
- At least three of the scenarios should be set somewhere specific with a district engineer, a head teacher, a customs clerk, a village council chair, that kind of texture. Make up the details but don't make it a cartoon, and I want the *giver's* reasoning visible in at least one set of options, not only the recipient's.
- Be honest about the pairs of leaves your questions can't really pull apart, and say what an eleventh question would have to be to separate them.

Then one paragraph at the end predicting how a room like mine splits — who ends up in which cluster and where the argument is going to start — because I need to plan the second half of the day around it.
