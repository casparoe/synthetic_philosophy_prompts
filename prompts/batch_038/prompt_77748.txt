Take the decision to launch Challenger on 28 January 1986 and analyze it through the lens of the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. I have the engineering and organizational story already: the O-ring seals in the field joints of the solid rocket boosters, the stiffening of the rubber at low temperature, the erosion and blow-by documented on previous flights and repeatedly reclassified as within tolerance, the test data reaching back to 1977, the teleconference the night before in which Morton Thiokol's engineers recommended against launching, NASA management's forceful pushback, and the subsequent reversal of the recommendation at the management level over the objections of the engineers who held the data. I likewise have the standard diagnoses: normalization of deviance, the drift of the line between design margin and accepted anomaly, the corruption of the communication channels. What I want is the strictly epistemological reading, carried out with discipline.

Work with the two paradoxes in their usual form: the lottery paradox, in which an agent rationally accepts each of a large set of individually high-probability propositions while refusing their conjunction, on pain of accepting something she knows to be false; and the preface paradox, in which a careful author rationally believes each claim in her book while also believing, on the basis of her own fallibility, that at least one of them is false. Both are ordinarily taken to show that rational acceptance is not closed under conjunction, and both are usually posed for a single mind with stable, known probabilities.

With that in hand:

1. Fix the mapping before you argue. Whose belief set is the relevant one: the individual engineers, Thiokol's management, NASA as a corporate body, or some distribution across them? State the propositions being accepted, the evidence for each, and the acceptance threshold you are assuming. Then say whether either paradox survives the move from a single mind to an institution, or whether the institutional version is a different puzzle wearing the same name.

2. Determine which paradox the case actually instantiates, if either. The flight series looks like a lottery; the accumulated safety case looks like a book with a missing preface. Note the structural difference: lottery beliefs rest on identical statistical grounds with no individualized evidence, whereas each flight-readiness judgment here rested on flight-specific data. Ask then whether NASA's position was genuinely preface-paradoxical, or something worse: a set of individually accepted claims together with the complete absence of the aggregate belief the evidence supported. Is the failure one of inconsistency, or of a belief that was never formed?

3. Test the exculpatory reading seriously rather than dismissing it. If rational acceptance does not require closure under conjunction, then each individual launch judgment could have been rational at any plausible threshold, and no one need have believed anything false. What must be true of the underlying probability assignments for that defense to hold? Feynman's minority appendix to the commission's report argued that the official reliability figures were orders of magnitude more optimistic than the flight record could support and were, on his account, produced by reasoning backward from a desired conclusion. If the probabilities themselves were not available to the decision-makers, what becomes of a paradox that takes them as given?

4. Locate the disanalogies precisely. A lottery has independent, evidentially symmetric, fixed-odds tickets; each shuttle flight altered the evidence bearing on the next. Does that alone disqualify the lottery model, and if so, does the preface model do the work by itself, or does it also strain, since the author of a preface knows she has one?

5. Say plainly where the framework is silent. The paradoxes concern what to believe; the disaster concerns what to do, and what to do on behalf of people who were not in the room. Is applying them here a category mistake dressed as insight, or do they identify a real structural hazard in serial high-stakes decision under a fixed acceptance threshold? Compare against at least one alternative account that does better or worse, and say whether it is genuinely a different explanation or the same one redescribed.

6. Turn the analysis around: what does the episode show about the paradoxes? Does a case like this give the defenders of an outright-belief threshold something to work with, or does it belong instead to those who argue that only graded credences are doing any real explanatory work? And if the case seems to generate a norm, namely that an agency owes an explicit aggregate statement of failure probability before it acts, ask whether that norm is derivable from the paradoxes at all or falls out of ordinary decision theory, in which case what work the paradoxes are actually left to do.

Do not force the theories to speak where they have nothing to say; mark those places explicitly. I am not looking for sources, and I am not looking for the familiar moral about organizational culture except insofar as the epistemology requires it.
