I write a regular column on political philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the revival of freedom as non-domination. The problem is that every treatment of this material reaches for the same illustration — the well-meaning master whose slave happens never to be punished — and my editor has asked for something that isn't a rehash of it.

Take the role of a political philosopher who works on Isaiah Berlin's two concepts of liberty and Philip Pettit's republicanism. Think carefully before drafting, and open with a short brainstorming pass in which you sketch and then reject two or three candidate settings, saying for each why it either collapses back into the stock benevolent-master example or fails to isolate the variable I need isolated. Then give me:

1. A thought experiment of roughly 250 words, set in a present-day institution — no master and slave, no employer and employee, no prison — involving two people matched on everything negative liberty is supposed to track: the same set of actually available options, and the same amount and pattern of actual interference over the same stretch of time. They are to differ on exactly one axis: whether the power that could interfere with them is arbitrary, or is rule-bound, reviewable, and answerable to their own expressed interests. Layer in one further fact, described concretely rather than asserted: the person in the rule-bound arrangement has grown up inside it, and now wants only what it permits.

2. A demonstration, theorist by theorist. The person under the reviewable arrangement must come out freer on non-domination, less free on a positive-liberty account of self-mastery, and negative liberty must be indifferent between the two. Show the reasoning that generates each verdict from the theory rather than announcing the verdict, and flag explicitly any place where one of the three is silent rather than deciding.

3. An identification of the single feature of the case that does the work, plus a removal test: what happens to the ranking if the protected person's preferences had been formed before entering the arrangement, and what happens if the reviewable body's discretion is broadened so that appeals exist but rarely succeed.

4. The best counterattack available to each of the three theorists against the case. For the Berlinian: is purely modal exposure that nobody feels and nobody acts on a deprivation of liberty at all? For the Pettitian: have I actually satisfied non-arbitrariness, or have I smuggled domination back in through the preference-formation channel, so that the case is not a counterexample to republicanism but an embarrassment for it? For the positive-liberty theorist: are preferences shaped by one's institutions a failure of self-direction, or is that just a demand that freedom be exercised in a particular way?

5. A closing honesty note, addressed to me as the journalist: where in the case do the verdicts genuinely fall out of the theories, and where have they been stipulated by the setup? I would rather find that out now than in print.

Please don't include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I want the argument, not the references.
