The question was worth 25 points. Candidates had fifty minutes, no books or notes, and were told that references to secondary literature would neither be credited nor penalized. The question read as follows:

"Explain Williams's argument against external reasons. In your explanation, say precisely what role the subjective motivational set plays, and whether, on Williams's account, an agent's reasons are fixed by the motivations he actually has. Finally, assess this objection, raised in section: if sound deliberation may overrule any element of the motivational set, then the set is doing no real work in determining the agent's reasons; the work is done instead by whatever standard of correctness deliberation answers to, and positing such a standard is just what the external reasons theorist does. Either defend or resist the objection."

Here is the candidate's answer, unaltered except for the paragraph breaks:

"Williams's argument begins from the idea that reason claims have explanatory work to do. To say that an agent has a reason to do something is to say something that could explain his doing it, and something for which he can be faulted if he does not do it. The external reasons theorist wants to say that such a claim can be true even when the agent has no motivation to act on it and could acquire none by becoming better informed or more rational. Williams thinks this is an unstable combination: a consideration that could not enter into any explanation of the agent's action, and that he could not be moved by however thoroughly he deliberated, is not a reason for him at all. So the external reason statement is not merely unhelpful but false. In his example, the young man from a military family who feels no inclination whatever to take up a commission may be an object of his family's blame, but that does not show that he has a reason they could correctly report.

The account is made precise by the subjective motivational set, S. S is not a list of desires: it includes dispositions of evaluation, patterns of emotional reaction, personal loyalties, and the agent's projects, as well as beliefs and other things the agent counts as reasons. An internal reason statement says, roughly, that the agent would be motivated to act were he to deliberate soundly from S — allowing for the correction of false beliefs, vivid imagination of the alternatives, and the weighing of his motivations against one another. Two consequences follow that are easy to miss. First, the account is counterfactual, so Williams is not claiming that an agent's reasons are hostage to his actual desires; a man may have a reason he would acknowledge only after a deliberation he presently resists. Second, since sound deliberation permits one element of S to be criticized by appeal to others, Williams is not a Humean about reason: reasoning is not confined to finding means to ends the passions supply.

That second consequence is, I think, where the view comes apart, and the objection stated in the question is correct. If deliberation may criticize a motivation and conclude that it merits no weight at all — suppose a woman brought up in a family of soldiers deliberates and concludes that she has no reason to accept a commission, although every motivation she has, including her loyalty to her family and her own dispositions of evaluation, points the other way — then what determines the outcome cannot be S, since S pointed uniformly elsewhere. Something further must be consulted, a standard of correctness that deliberation answers to and that can overrule everything in the set. But that is precisely what the external reasons theorist means by an external reason. So the distinction between internal and external reasons loses its content once critical deliberation is admitted, and Williams's argument proves too much: it rules out the very resources his own account of deliberation requires.

There is a related moral, which is part of what makes the view attractive. If reasons depend on motivation in the way Williams says, then moral judgment depends on it too: anyone who sincerely judges that he ought to help is thereby motivated to help. Williams is usually read as an internalist in that sense as well, and this is what ties his account of reasons to the psychology of actual agents rather than to free-floating norms. It also disposes of the charge of relativism: Williams does not deny that we criticize an agent's motivations, only that such criticism ever operates from outside them."
