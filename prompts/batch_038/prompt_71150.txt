Last night my friend and I argued for the better part of three hours about Émilie du Châtelet's short, semi-autobiographical treatise on happiness, the Discours sur le bonheur, drafted in the mid-1740s and printed only after her death. I had come to it by way of her physics; she had come at it from the well-being literature, and she is finishing a clinical training rotation, so the dispute was never going to stay historical. My reading was that du Châtelet is neither a Stoic nor a quietist: her prescription is that one must shed one's prejudices, be virtuous, keep one's health, have tastes and passions, and above all remain susceptible to illusions, since most of our pleasures are owed to illusion, and rather than dispel it with the light of reason we should thicken the varnish it lays over objects. She dismisses the moralists who counsel the repression of desire, holds that happiness consists only in satisfied tastes and passions — and, failing passions, in satisfied tastes — recommends contentment with the station fortune has assigned us rather than its alteration, and prescribes the deliberate banishment of sad ideas, notably the thought of our own death and that of the people we love, in favor of agreeable ones, together with a standing rule against ever repenting. Study figures in that account as one of her own three great passions, alongside gambling and love, and as a resource that cannot be confiscated; she presses it especially on women, who are shut out of most other avenues to distinction.

My friend's objection was that nearly every clause of that program has since been tested, and much of it has failed. She cited the familiar comparison of recent lottery winners with recent accident victims who had been paralyzed, along with twin-study estimates that a substantial share of the variance in reported well-being is heritable and that people drift back toward a personal baseline; she took this to show that neither fortune nor effort moves happiness very far, so that du Châtelet's whole project of arranging one's state is aimed at a lever that barely exists. She cited the work on rumination and thought suppression: that turning attention inward tends to worsen mood, that deliberately trying not to think of something reliably makes it more intrusive, and that the defensible strategy is non-judgmental acceptance rather than the strategic replacement of dark ideas with bright ones. She also cited the line of findings suggesting that mildly low mood is sometimes accompanied by more accurate judgment about one's own prospects, which is precisely the sort of accuracy du Châtelet wants illusion to obscure. Then, somewhat triumphantly, she conceded one point: there is a modern literature holding that modest self-enhancement and unrealistic optimism go with better psychological functioning, which sounds like a vindication of her injunction to preserve one's illusions — but she insisted this concerns self-regard only, not the world at large, and so cannot rescue the stronger counsel.

What I want from you is a prediction that settles which of us was right, rather than a survey of both sides. Suppose du Châtelet is presented, in plain terms, with four things she could not have known: the adaptation and set-point evidence; the suppression-rebound and rumination evidence; the finding that positive illusion about oneself correlates with better functioning while mildly accurate pessimism sometimes correlates with worse mood; and two further developments — reliable pharmacological treatment of low mood, and the general availability to women of intellectual careers and public recognition, so that study has become a road to distinction rather than a consolation for exclusion from it. Which of her doctrines would she abandon as empirical mistakes, which would she defend with more confidence than before, and which would she keep but reinterpret? In particular, give me her answer to the sharpest question between us: does hedonic adaptation refute her program, or does she have the resources to treat it as the very fact her program was built to accommodate, given that she never promised circumstances would produce happiness but only that they can be arranged so that tastes and passions have something to work on? I want a definite verdict — what she would actually conclude, exactly where she would concede to the modern data, and whether the argument I lost last night was one I deserved to lose. Please paraphrase her reasoning rather than putting words in her mouth in quotation marks, and leave out citations and reading lists; I am after the philosophical reconstruction, not the provenance of the studies.
