I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums long enough to have watched several of their signature moves harden into reflexes, and one of those reflexes is what happens when somebody brings up Fomenko's New Chronology. The response is almost always the same two paragraphs: a recitation of the more baroque claims, and then the word "unfalsifiable," after which everyone agrees and moves on. I think that response is both correct and worthless, and I have drafted an essay arguing for a sharper diagnosis. I would like comments on the draft below before I post it anywhere.

Two requests about how to comment. First, please do not give me a reading list or citations; I am not looking for pointers and cannot chase sources this month. I want your reasoning about the argument, including and especially where you think I am wrong. Second, I would rather you find my hand-waving than have me discover it in a comment thread, so anything that looks like a gesture in the direction of statistics should be treated as an invitation to say what is missing.

**Null Models, Reported Negatives, and the New Chronology**

**1. The case as I understand it.**

Anatoly Fomenko is a real mathematician: a topologist, a full member of the Russian Academy of Sciences, and a recipient of a Russian Federation state prize for his work in geometry. Beginning around 1980, and building on the much earlier speculations of Nikolai Morozov, he and collaborators such as Gleb Nosovsky have argued that most of what passes for ancient history is a medieval production, the accepted record having been falsified by a succession of interested powers.

What distinguishes this from the usual run of pseudohistory is that it is advertised as a method rather than a set of claims. The central device is the volume function of a narrative text: for a chronicle, tabulate how much space it devotes to each year that it narrates, and plot that quantity against the year. The guiding principle is that a chronicler writes at greatest length about events near his own lifetime, so the volume function has a characteristic peak that decays with distance in both directions. Two chronicles whose volume functions peak in the same relative positions and share a general shape are then declared dependent: one is a copy, redaction, or duplicate of the other, displaced along the time axis. Aggregated across a large corpus, together with claimed numerical correspondences among sequences of reign lengths, this yields the familiar conclusions: that the Trojan War and the Crusades are a single event, that the gospel narrative belongs to the twelfth century, that the Mongols were Russians. Radiocarbon dating and dendrochronology are disputed rather than accommodated.

I want to bracket whether those conclusions are false. They are, I think, overwhelmingly so, and the physical, textual, and linguistic evidence is not close. My question is narrower: what exactly is wrong with the method? The answer matters beyond this case, because the failure mode travels.

**2. Three answers I find unsatisfying.**

*It is unfalsifiable.* True in some sense, but useless here, because the apparatus produces definite outputs: these two texts, these two curves, this displacement. It presents itself as a measurement, and the Popperian label tells you something is wrong without telling you what.

*It is ad hoc.* Also true, but ad hocness is a spectrum. Every research programme carries auxiliary hypotheses, and Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating shifts is precisely the kind of judgment call I would like to avoid resting the argument on.

*It contradicts the archaeology, the numismatics, the language history, and the absolute dating.* This is the strongest objection available and I do not want to weaken it. But it is an objection to the conclusions, not to the method, and it is unavailable to anyone who has been persuaded that the tree-ring record is a fabrication. What I want is an immanent critique: one that can be carried out by a reader who accepts Fomenko's data, his volume functions, and nothing else.

**3. The diagnosis: only one of the two relevant quantities is ever reported.**

Any test of the form *these two series agree suspiciously well* is implicitly a comparison of two likelihoods: how probable is agreement this close given that the texts are duplicates, and how probable is agreement this close given that they are independent? The first is what the exhibits show — peaks aligned, shapes similar. The second is what makes alignment evidence, and it is never estimated. There is no null model: no distribution of the statistic under independence, no survey of volume-function agreement among texts whose independence is not in dispute, and hence no route from a picture of two humped curves to a likelihood ratio.

The search space compounds this. If any chronicle may be compared with any other, candidate pairs run into the tens of thousands; each pair may be compared at many displacements along a two-thousand-year axis; and there are further free choices in smoothing window, unit of measurement, and inclusion criteria. Maximizing a statistic over on the order of a million candidate comparisons is not the same operation as evaluating it once. A rough calculation: if the chance that a randomly chosen pair at a randomly chosen displacement produces an apparently spectacular alignment were as low as one in ten thousand, a search of that size would return hundreds of spectacular alignments, all of them noise. I do not know how far the trials are from independent — overlapping smoothing windows make them strongly positively dependent, which is the sort of complication that cuts both ways — and I would like to be told whether that undermines the estimate entirely.

**4. The degrees of freedom in the statistic itself.**

Volume functions are coarse. Narrative output is tabulated in blocks of years and smoothed, so a curve spanning two millennia carries a handful of effective degrees of freedom, and it is constrained to be non-negative and few-peaked, rising to a maximum and falling away on both sides. Curves of that kind agree with one another for reasons having nothing to do with texts: normalized to the same range, two humps differ mainly in location, and location is exactly the parameter the method fits. My suspicion is that the effective sample size is in the single digits, which would leave the correlation close to uninformative before any multiplicity correction. This is an intuition, not a result, and I would rather have it corrected than applauded.

**5. Circularity in the independent variable.**

Computing a volume function requires a year-by-year alignment for the chronicle in question. For medieval texts Fomenko accepts the conventional alignment; for ancient ones he rejects it. The statistic is therefore computed inside a partly conventional frame and then turned against the convention. A subtler version of the same problem concerns identification: to say that two texts describe the same events, one must already have matched this king to that king and this siege to that siege, and those matchings are inherited from the chronology under test.

**6. The parameter budget.**

Here the vague talk of ad hocness can be made precise. Each claimed duplicate pair carries at least one free parameter, its displacement; each additional layer of duplication carries more. The parameter count therefore grows with the number of observations the theory is asked to absorb, and any recalcitrant text can be accommodated by declaring it a further duplicate. A model whose complexity tracks its data cannot be refuted by data. This is a checkable claim about the theory's structure rather than a slogan about its spirit, and I think it is the honest content behind the falsifiability complaint.

**7. A criterion, and my worry about it.**

The above suggests a demarcation test for comparative, corpus-scale methods in the humanities. Call a method evidentially live if (i) it has a specifiable outcome that it treats as counting against dependence — some pattern it would report as a failure; (ii) its false-positive rate can in principle be estimated on cases whose status is known independently of the method; and (iii) its free parameters are fixed before the disputed cases are examined rather than fitted to them. Fomenko's apparatus fails all three.

The trouble is that the criterion may be too strong. Textual criticism builds stemmas from patterns of shared scribal error, and I am not certain anyone could supply a false-positive rate for the enterprise as a whole. The two-source solution to the synoptic problem infers a lost document from agreements among the gospels; condition (ii) looks hard to satisfy there too. What does satisfy it, which is why I keep reaching for the example, is the statistical work on the disputed Federalist Papers: function-word frequencies were calibrated against essays of known authorship, the hypothesis space was small and closed, and the error rate was estimable. Comparative philology also seems to pass, because the regularity of sound correspondences is exactly a null-model discipline — it is what allows the field to say no.

Perhaps the operative requirement is weaker than (ii): not *can you compute a false-positive rate* but *does your method ever say no, and do you publish the occasions on which it did*. That is a norm about reported negatives rather than about calibration, and I am not sure it carries the weight I need it to.

**8. Why this is worth writing.**

Not because anyone is in danger of accepting the New Chronology. It is that the shape of the argument — run a corpus statistic, display a striking alignment, treat the alignment as a discovery without ever estimating how often such alignments arise by chance — is now commonplace across digital humanities, quantitative history, and parts of archaeology and linguistics. If the Fomenko case can be turned into a clean worked example of that failure, it becomes a reusable instrument rather than a curiosity.

**What I would like comments on:**

1. Is §3 the load-bearing objection, or am I attacking the statistics when the real weight of the refutation sits in §6 and §7? If you had to keep one section and cut the rest, which survives?
2. The Fermi-style multiplicity estimate in §3: is it defensible, given the dependence among overlapping comparisons? Would you redo it, and how?
3. Is the §4 intuition correct — that the correlation between two smoothed, non-negative, few-peaked curves over a long span is nearly uninformative? If you were formalizing this for a general audience, what is the cleanest way to state it?
4. Does condition (ii) genuinely exclude stemmatology and the two-source hypothesis? If it does, should I accept that and say these enterprises are weaker than their practitioners suppose, or is there a route by which they satisfy (ii) and Fomenko does not — benchmarking on simulated trees, regular versus irregular correspondences, replication on manuscript families with documented copying relations?
5. Is the Federalist Papers contrast fair, or does it collapse because the hypothesis space there was closed and small while Fomenko's is open-ended? If the latter, is openness of hypothesis space itself the real problem, and should I promote it from §6 to the thesis?
6. Does §6 amount to a restatement of Popper or Lakatos in statistical language? If so, what is its marginal content, and should it be cut?
7. Where have I strawmanned? I tried to state the volume-function argument at its strongest, but I would like your best steelman of it — in particular, whether there is a version that supplies an implicit null, for instance by claiming that peak locations are quantitatively predicted rather than merely fitted.
8. On structure and tone: should §5 precede §3? Does §8 read as a jeremiad, and is the essay overall more confident than its content warrants? I would rather be told to add hedges in specific places than to be reassured.
