Retired three years back after 32 years in a precision measurement lab — mostly calibrating thermometers and pressure transducers, which is to say spending your whole working life thinking about what an instrument can and cannot resolve. Since then I've been reading philosophy for fun, and I've somehow talked my way into a 40 minute talk to a university philosophy society this February. The room is their value-theory reading group: a dozen or so people, two of whom work on vagueness and lean supervaluationist, one formal epistemologist, and a handful of broadly Kantian ethicists who I expect will not enjoy my framing at all. Nobody there does decision theory proper.

The thesis I want to defend is that "better than" is transitive, and that the spectrum-style arguments for intransitivity are a measurement artifact rather than a discovery about value. My sketch will run like this:

1. Every comparsion procedure has a finite resolution δ. Below δ it doesn't report "worse", it reports nothing you can tell apart from "the same", and reading that output as a genuine equality is the original sin of the whole argument.
2. Chain n of those comparisons down a spectrum and the accumulated uncertainty is only n·δ if the errors are correlated. If they're independent they add in quadrature, roughly δ√n. The spectrum arguments silently assume the linear case, which is exactly the assumption no one in my old lab would have signed off on.
3. So stop comparing pairwise and compare against one fixed reference standard — the way you calibrate every instrument in a lab against a single artifact instead of against each other — and transitivity returns by construction, with the old puzzles relabelled as calibration failures.

Give me the five objections this particular room will actually raise, roughly in the order they'll come up, and phrase each one the way that person would phrase it, so the Kantian's objection sounds like a Kantian and the vagueness person's sounds like someone who has strong opinions about penumbral cases — not like a physicist doing an impression of a philosopher. I especially want to know whether the quadrature move in step 2 survives contact with people who think the analogy to instruments is a category error, since that's where I suspect I'll get hurt.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items and nothing else — no introduction, no wrap-up, no "here's your prep sheet". Each item at most three sentences: the objection in their voice, then my best one-sentence reply, then a short line on where that reply is softest. If one of the five is something I should just concede on the spot rather than fight, say that instead of giving a reply. Make number five the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
