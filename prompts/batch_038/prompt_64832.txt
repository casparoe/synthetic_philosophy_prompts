I am preparing a one-page diagnostic for a workshop whose participants are biomedical ontology engineers plus a few philosophers of science. What I want is a decision tree that sorts them by what their answers to cases imply, not by which label they would pick for themselves, so no node may mention a school of thought or a philosopher's name.

Begin from the one commitment the room already shares — that some ways of grouping things license generalization and others do not — and build the tree out of questions about cases: sepsis, treated clinically as a single thing despite having no sharp boundary; fish, a paraphyletic grouping; gene, as used under incompatible definitions by different subfields; element, before and after isotopes; a DSM category; and a category fixed entirely by a measurement procedure, such as a hypertension threshold. Candidate nodes include whether the boundary of a kind is set by the world, by our predictive interests, or jointly; whether two incompatible schemes can both be correct of the same domain; whether a kind with no shared essence can still be natural; whether projectibility explains naturalness or naturalness explains projectibility; and whether physics and clinical medicine get the same answer.

The leaves must include causal essentialism, homeostatic property clusters, promiscuous realism or pluralism, an interest-relative or deflationary account, kind eliminativism, and one leaf for respondents whose answer pattern turns out to be unstable, with a sentence naming the instability. Then report which pairs of leaves the tree cannot in fact separate, which regions of the logical space it leaves unoccupied, and for each of those whether nobody is there because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Ten to twelve nodes, tree first and answer key second, formatted to print on a single page.
