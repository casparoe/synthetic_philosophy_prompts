I've got a cross-listed philosophy-of-mathematics seminar this term — 22 people, one 75-minute session a week, and roughly a third of them are comp sci types who turned up because someone told them intuitionism was basically logic for programming languages, which is true-ish and also a disaster for how they're going to think about it. They've all had a term of classical natural deduction, so reductio is muscle memory, and three of them have already told me they figure the intuitionists are just being difficult about excluded middle.

I want to spend an entire session — say week 6, before we've touched any semantics — on a game where they crash into the failure of double negation elimination themselves and only hear the word intuitionistic afterward. So: design the session for me. Roles, rules, materials (whiteboard, index cards, no budget), the scoring scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 including debrief.

A few things I'm committed to:

- Build it as a growing-information-state thing, a tree of nodes where whatever's established at a node stays established above it. But they have to be playing *at* a node, not looking at the tree from outside — the second they can see the whole diagram it turns into a puzzle about a picture and the force is gone.
- Asserting a disjunction has to cost them something in the hand that the corresponding double negation doesn't, and I want them to feel that asymmetry physically, with slips or tokens they actually have to surrender, not as a rule I've read off the board.
- I want at least one round organised around a sequence that's being generated while they play, where whoever's generating it hasn't settled the rule yet — the creating-subject flavour. That's the round where the "we just don't know yet" reading is supposed to come apart, and it needs to come apart in the room rather than in my mouth, because that misreading is the main thing I'm fighting all term.
- Related: I've been toying with letting a player spend tokens to peek one node ahead, on the theory that excluded middle is really the claim that peeking is always free. Use it or kill it, your call, but tell me which and why.
- The comp sci contingent will instantly try to convert the whole thing into a search procedure. Point out where in the design that reflex gets recruited instead of punished — I'd rather use it.
- For the debrief, map each moment of play onto the bit of the semantics it's standing in for: forcing, monotonicity, negation as an arrow to absurdity, the disjunction property, and why having a refutation of the refutation isn't the same object as having a construction. Then be blunt about what the game cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply by talking — in particular tell me exactly what the activity does to the gap between not-yet-known and not-provable, because I suspect the game is going to lie to them about that and I'd like to know where the lie sits so I can flag it.
- Finally, what will go wrong the first time I run it. I always loose the timing, and one group always finds a loophole in the rules within five minutes; I'd much rather you find the loophole than they do.

No readings — they won't have read anything and the whole point is that they don't need to.
