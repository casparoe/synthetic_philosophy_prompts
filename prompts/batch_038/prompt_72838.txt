A friend and I argued about this for two hours last night and neither of us will give ground, so I would like a considered third opinion.

Here is the practice. In a mediated settlement — a partnership breakup, a labour dispute, a contested insurance claim — the mediator caucuses with each side and extracts two numbers: the opening aspiration, and the walk-away point, the least that side will take before it prefers litigation or a strike. He then steers the parties toward the settlement at which each has surrendered the same *fraction* of the distance between its own aspiration and its own bottom line. And yet the same practitioners, in the same week, will pronounce it fair simply to split the difference — equal absolute concessions — whenever the two ranges happen to be of comparable size. My friend insists the first rule is nothing other than the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution, and that mediators are doing applied cooperative game theory; I think he is flattering a folk custom that cannot even say which of two rules it is using.

What does the practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and of fairness, separating what it strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with. In particular: does settling in dollars already assume the two parties' utilities are comparable on a common cardinal scale, or does the per-side normalization do all the work? Why treat the walk-away point, rather than the status quo or either side's entitlements, as the baseline from which gains are measured? What does the rule assume about the sincerity of a stated aspiration, given that every handbook on negotiation recommends opening high? Is the practice coherent, or does hosting both rules commit it to two incompatible theories? And where exactly does my friend's identification with the formal solution break down — what does the model assume about the feasible set, and about what is commonly known, that a caucus room fails to supply?
