I'm giving the toast at my PhD advisor's retirement dinner — about sixty people, faculty club, a few weeks from now. She's a hardcore elminativist, full Churchland: folk psychology is a bad theory, beliefs and desires are phlogiston and caloric, the whole intentional idiom is going to get replaced by neuroscience sooner or later. For twenty-odd years she's run a rule at any dinner thrown in her honor — anyone who describes *her* with believe, want, hope, intend, mean, fear, or love has to finish their glass in front of everybody. I've never broken it and I'm not starting at her retirement, so the toast has to cover forty years of a life — her students, her stubbornness, her husband who died in 2019 — with zero propositional-attitude ascriptions to her. I can say what she wrote, said, did, argued, published, what her nervous system was up to, what happened to her and around her.

Two things to know. The rule was about her, so "we all believe..." is technically legal — but if I lean on that it's a cheap dodge she'll spot instantly, so at most once, and only if it's earning its place. And half the room are philosophers who'll be scoring me word by word, while the other half is family, including her sister, who has told everyone within earshot that this eliminativism business is a put-on and that her sibling is the most feeling person she knows.

That's the actual problem I want you to solve rather than paper over: the family half should come away feeling they've just been told something true about her, in language they can hold onto, from a toast that never once says what she thinks or feels. I also want one moment that names the obvious objection — that the view can't even be stated without the vocabulary it denies — landed as affection, not a gotcha, so the non-philosophers get it too. Watch out for "she'd have wanted it this way," which is a foul.

Hard cap of 400 words, and give me just the toast — no notes afterwards about how you kept score.
