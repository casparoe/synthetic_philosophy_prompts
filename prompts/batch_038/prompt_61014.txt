I TA a second-year historical methods seminar — 16 students, one 90-minute session, no projector (the room's screen has been broken since October) and I'd rather not hand out a printed packet because half of them won't read ahead. A friend of mine who runs records-management training for a city council keeps insisting that retention schedules are where the politics actually lives, and I want to steal that insight and make my students feel it. They all show up already saying "you have to read sources critically, look for the bias," as if that were a complete method, and I want them to leave understanding that the nastier problem isn't distorted records — it's the events and people that never generated a record at all, the kind of gap you can't detect from inside the collection, because detecting it would require evidence that the same selection process already chewed up.

Concretely I'm picturing two phases with the groups seperated. Phase one: they *are* the record-making apparatus of some small institution — parish relief office, union local, company town, municipal grain board, whatever's easiest for you to stock with roles — and something happens across a few compressed "days" of play (a strike, an epidemic, a disputed payment, a fire) that's rich enough that lots of consequential things occur. Give me role sheets that make it structurally true that some people can write and some can't, that some acts get entered in a register automatically while others only get written down if someone bothers, and that a filing-and-retention rule everyone accepts as boringly administrative quietly decides what survives. Phase two: a different set of students — ideally ones who sat out phase one — receives only the surviving box and has to produce a short factual account plus answers to specific questions, and commit to it in writing before any reveal. I want a scoring mechanism so we can literally compare their confidence against their accuracy and make the confabulation visible: they'll plug the holes with plausible stuff and not flag it as inference. Please build in, deliberately, both a lacuna they *can* spot (a torn leaf, a break in the folio numbering, an obviously missing month) and a structural absence they can't, and tell me how to make sure the second kind actually comes out of the phase-one rules rather than me sneaking cards out of the box behind their backs. Materials should be cheap — index cards, a shoebox, a notebook, whatever.

Then the debrief, which is where I most need help. Map specific moments of play onto the concepts, and give me a reveal sequence that doesn't just amount to telling the phase-two group "you got it wrong" — I'd like them to be able to interrogate the box themselves and start suspecting it before I say anything. Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (I already suspect two things: the sharp student who's done the reading and announces "so this is about archival silence" in minute three, and phase-one players being unrealistically diligent about writing everything down because they know it'll matter later), how to defuse each, and a frank paragraph on which feature of the real problem the game can't reproduce — real archives pile up over centuries and the people making them weren't in a simulation, so I know something about the phenomenology is getting lost and I'd rather be upfront with the class about what. And please make the debrief land somewhere more specific than "sources are biased, be humble," because that's the shrug version and it's useless to them. I think the book I'd point them at afterwards is Trouillot's Silencing the Past, but don't build the activity around anyone having read it and don't drop the name until the very end.
