I am the data science lead at a state agency. In six weeks our pretrial risk instrument goes before a legislative oversight board, and my director has asked me to attach to that presentation a written fairness policy: a document stating in advance, and in public, exactly what we hold the instrument to. Once published we will be held to it literally, and opposing counsel will read it far more carefully than we will. Before I circulate the draft I want a cold audit of whether its commitments are jointly tenable. I am not asking for background on the fairness literature; I am asking you to trace what commits us to what.

The instrument produces a score between zero and one, intended as the probability that a defendant is arrested for a new offense within twelve months of release. The board has asked us to report separately on two groups, defined by race. The draft commits us to eight things.

1. Within-group calibration of the published score. Among defendants scored 0.70, approximately seventy percent are re-arrested within twelve months, and this holds group by group rather than only in the pooled sample. We commit to agreement within five percentage points in every score band we actually use.

2. One threshold for everybody. A defendant at or above 0.62 is recommended for intensive supervision regardless of group. The instrument does not see group membership when it produces a recommendation.

3. Parity of classification error. We commit to reporting the false positive rate and the false negative rate of the recommendation by group, and to keeping each within five percentage points across the two groups.

4. The outcome labels stand. We have audited them, and we are not willing to write into a public document that the difference in observed re-arrest rates between the groups — 0.51 and 0.26 over the past three years — is an artifact of differential enforcement. Some of us believe it partly is. We will not stake the policy on that belief.

5. The instrument must be worth deploying. Current within-group AUC is 0.71 and 0.70. We will not field anything below 0.68 in either group: a score that weak adds nothing to the paper checklist our supervision officers already complete, and we would be subjecting people to an instrument for no measurable benefit.

6. Group membership appears nowhere in the pipeline. Counsel has instructed us that race may not be an input to the score, to the threshold, or to any post-processing step. It may be used for validation, reporting, and audit, and for nothing else.

7. A person makes the placement decision. The recommendation is advisory; a supervision officer may depart from it for written reasons, and we will publish departure rates.

8. Measurement invariance. We will publish, for each group, the distribution of scores and the observed event rate within each score band. We commit to demonstrating that the instrument behaves the same way in both groups: the score distributions will be statistically indistinguishable, and the observed event rates will match the scores in both groups.

The draft also proposes one device that I believe reconciles 1, 3, and 5, and I want it examined specifically rather than generally. We would publish two numbers. The first is the calibrated risk estimate, used in the conversation with the defendant and the officer and audited against commitment 1. The second is an allocation score: a monotone transformation of the first, chosen so that when it is cut at a single group-blind level the resulting recommendations satisfy commitment 3. Only the allocation score generates the recommendation; the calibrated score is disclosed alongside it. Nothing is discarded, the ranking is preserved exactly, and no single number is asked to do two incompatible jobs at once. My position is that the impossibility results are all statements about what one score cannot do, and that this design is not one score. I would like to know whether that position survives contact with the arithmetic.

Three things I want from you.

First, an ordered audit. Take the eight commitments and tell me which subsets are jointly unsatisfiable, which are jointly satisfiable but only at a price I have not costed, and which merely look as though they conflict. Rank the findings by severity. For each, state what would have to be struck or weakened and what striking it costs us — legally, methodologically, and in front of a hostile room. Where a conflict is strict rather than a matter of degree, give me the shortest argument that it is strict; where it is a matter of degree, tell me what would make it worse and what would make it better.

Second, adjudicate the two objections I expect from colleagues. One says that publishing calibration group by group (1) is itself a use of group membership and therefore contradicts 6. The other says that promising error-rate parity (3) while conceding that the base rates differ (4) is incoherent on its face, impossibility results or no impossibility results. Tell me which objection lands, which does not, and why.

Third, suppose we hold 1, 2, 4, and 6 fixed and try to buy 3 within five points. Describe the mechanism rather than the arithmetic: what has to happen to the threshold, what has to happen to the distribution of scores within each group, and what has to happen to 5. Then tell me whether commitment 7 is quietly doing some of this work — if the parity we report turns out to be produced by officer departures rather than by the instrument, what exactly is it that we have published?

Finally, name the single revision to the document that buys the most coherence per unit of embarrassment, and say what we lose by making it.
