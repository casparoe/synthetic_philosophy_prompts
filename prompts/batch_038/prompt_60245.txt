Been building an argument for a reading group about the cluelessness problem and I think it proves way too much, and I can't find the leak. Here it is, as clean as I can make it at 1am.

(1) An act is right only if its overall value — everything it ever causes, near and far — is at least as great as that of every alternative open to the agent.
(2) You're justified in believing an act is right only if you're justified in believing that comparison holds.
(3) The far-future portion of that value dwarfs the near-term portion: what an act does to people alive in 2400 outweighs, by orders of magnitude, what it does to anyone alive now.
(4) For any two alternatives, the far-future portions differ from each other by a lot as well — the acts lead to different people existing, and once the cast changes, everything downstream changes.
(5) But we know nothing about those far-future differences. Our evidence favors no magnitude and no sign over any other; every number you put on them is pulled out of the air.
(6) You can't be justified in believing a comparison that rests entirely on numbers pulled out of the air.
(7) So you're never justified in believing any act is right.
(8) Nothing in (3)–(6) is peculiar to consequentialism. Any view that tells you to weigh outcomes at all — threshhold deontology, a virtue ethics with a notion of flourishing, plain common sense — is exposed to the same problem.
(9) So you're never justified in believing anything at all about what to do, including that it would be wrong to kick a dog for fun and that pulling a kid out of a puddle beats walking past.
(10) And by the same route I'm not justified in believing this argument was worth writing, which is a weird place to end up.

The point was supposed to be that consequentialism is false, but I could never actually get past (7) — and then (8) through (10) happened on their own and its not obvious why the other views escape. I cannot live with (9). I'm as confident that kicking the dog is wrong as I am of anything.

A friend says the whole thing is refuted by noting that far-future effects just wash out when you compare two acts, but I think (4) already blocks that move, since (4) says the effects differ act to act. Maybe I'm wrong about that too.

So: where does it break? If it doesn't break anywhere and is simply valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to abandon and what the resulting view looks like. Up front, the one I most want to keep is (5) — total ignorance about the deep future feels like the honest starting point — so if (5) is the thing that has to go, I want to know exactly what I'm buying with its replacement.
