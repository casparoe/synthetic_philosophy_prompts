I help run a Wednesday reading group at a board game café in my town — we read some philosophy, then argue about it for an hour — and I'm also unfortunately the person who writes the café's event listings, which is why this particular argument got under my skin. Last week we were on the family resemblance stuff, and two regulars, Ted and Amara, went at it over whether the games example actually shows what everybody assumes it shows. I wrote down the shape of it afterwards because I knew I'd want to get it straight, so here's roughly how it went, in order.

(1) Ted opened by saying the whole thing isn't a claim about meaning at all, it's a claim about our articulacy: saying games have no common feature only means nobody has managed to state the definition, and unstated isn't the same as nonexistent. He added that the sample is rigged — if you list board games, card games, ball games and a nursery singing circle in that order, practically any word will look definitionless by the end of it. (2) Amara said that begs the question, because the claim isn't that no formula could be written down, it's that the similarities genuinely overlap and criss-cross with nothing running through all of them, like a thread whose strength doesn't come from any single fibre stretching the whole length; and she said a definition you have to keep padding with "or" until it covers everything hasn't found an essence, it's just a list in costume. (3) Ted then put up his own candidate: an activity is a game if and only if some goal or outcome gets recorded, the rules deliberately rule out the most efficient route to it, and the players accept those rules precisely because they make the activity possible — and someone at the table said he'd reinvented the definition from that old book about games as "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles," which Ted said was exactly the point, a philosopher did write one down. Then he invented a case on the spot and called it klabb: two players take turns naming rivers, you lose a token if you repeat one, hesitate longer than five seconds, or name a river nobody in the room can produce a map showing; no score is kept, nobody wins, and people play it for about ninety seconds at a time between other things, mostly to wind each other up. The room said instantly and without discussion that klabb is a game. Ted said that instantness is the data — a criterion got applied, and applied fast, so there's a criterion. (4) Amara said the instantness shows the opposite: nobody checked conditions, we matched klabb against paradigms (it's Taboo-ish, it's Scattergories-ish) and let that settle it. She also said his move proves too much, since by the same reasoning chair and heap and knows all have unstated criteria, so what he's refuted is a claim about vague concepts in general, not one about games. And she said his clauses leak anyway: the nursery singing circle records no outcome, my neighbour's kid and her dog have a made-up game whose rules change mid-play, and "the game" of dodging your landlord involves no voluntary acceptance of anything. (5) Ted's closer was that patching clauses is normal conceptual work and the fact that we iterate and converge is evidence there's a target to converge on, whereas the family resemblance view gives you no way to be wrong — anything can be waved through as resembling some of the others, so it's compatible with every possible classification and therefore says nothing. He also said Amara owes an account of why six people agreed about klabb in under a second without talking it over, and that agreement that fast looks more like tracking something than inventing something. Neither budged, and a third person said on the way out that they were definately arguing about two different things entirely, which may be true or may just be a cop-out.
