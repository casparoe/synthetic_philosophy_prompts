I'm the human-factors lead for a group of four community hospitals, and basically my whole job is writing medication protocols. The failure mode I keep running into is the same one over and over, and I think its the absent-minded driver problem from decision theory wearing scrubs: a nurse arrives at a step in a sequence and genuinely cannot tell whether the step has already happened. Interrupted mid-task and came back after a code, took over from the last shift, two identical-looking bags hanging, the pump alarm wiped the record of what was pushed. The driver version is the one where a guy plans a route with two exits that look the same from the road, and by the time he's at one he can't tell if it's the first or the second, so what he'd commit to beforehand and what looks right in the moment come apart — and people still argue about which of those should win. I want to know whether that argument infects anything I build.

Our protocol review committee meets twice a month and gets maybe 8 to 10 change requests each time. Some are trivial, some are about high-alert drugs where a double dose kills somebody. We get 45 minutes per request, the chair is a pharmacist, and both the nursing practice council and pharmacy have to sign off on anything that touches bedside workflow. No new IT spend this fiscal year, so "buy a smarter pump" isn't an available move — fixes have to be procedural or physical.

What I want is a decision procedure my committee can actually run on a request inside that 45 minutes.

1. The questions to ask, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each. One of the early ones should be the test for whether a step is what I've started calling history-blind: could someone dropped into that step with no memory of the last ten minutes tell whether it had already been done? Give me a way to actually administer that test in a meeting, not just a definition. And tell me how to distinguish it from the neighboring problem where the person knows exactly where they are but won't follow the plan anyway — that's a compliance issue and I handle it completely differently.

2. Once a step is flagged, the branches. As far as I can see you can make the state observable (tally mark on the bag, a physical tag, a witness), or you can take the choice away at that step (hard stop, forced function, one-person rule), or you can leave the ambiguity alone and just design the best behavior for someone standing in it (default-when-unsure is X). Lay out which branch to prefer under which conditions, and be explicit about the fact that this is where the philosophy bites: each branch quietly picks a side in the plan-versus-the-moment dispute. Say which side each one picks and what my committee is committing itself to when it picks a branch.

3. Something our legal and nursing people care about that I've never seen written down anywhere: some fixes make the *state* knowable and some remove the *choice*, and only the second kind reads as "we don't think you can be trusted at the bedside." I've watched good safety fixes die in the nursing council purely on that axis. Build me a way to score a proposed fix for how accusatory it is, so the committee sees the tradeoff explicitly instead of experiencing it as a fight.

4. Where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to a human because it's a values question rather than a facts question — how much risk is acceptable, how much bedside autonomy we're willing to spend, whether a fix that prevents one double dose a decade is worth forty extra seconds on every single shift.

5. Honesty about the seams. Which of your steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure? And is anything in the absent-minded driver debate unsettled in a way that makes part of what I'm building arbitrary — I'd rather know before it goes into a policy document.

Then run it on three requests sitting in my inbox:

(a) A proposal to let ED nurses give a repeat dose of an IV antiemetic when they aren't sure the first one went in, capped at two per patient per shift, because the current rule (never repeat unless documented) means people just stay nauseous.

(b) Our barcode scan before administration fails to read about 8% of the time, staff invented a workaround where they scan at the start of shift instead of at the bedside, and somone has proposed we delete the scan entirely. I already suspect the answer is no, but I want to know whether your procedure gets there for the right reasons or whether it's going to tell me something I don't want to hear.

(c) A mandatory two-person independent double check on a high-alert infusion that gets skipped during handoffs, because the receiving nurse can't tell whether the check happened on the other side of the shift change. The request is to add a third signature to the record.

For at least one of these, tell me straight if the procedure lands opposite to what the requester wants, and what I should do about the politics of that. And close with what the procedure can't handle — I suspect it goes quiet when the ambiguity is about *which patient* rather than which point in the sequence, and whenever the history that matters lives in someone else's head.
