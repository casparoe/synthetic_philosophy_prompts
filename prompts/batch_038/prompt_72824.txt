I write a monthly column on science and ideas, and a piece I am drafting has outgrown its assignment. It began as a story about knowledge graphs: the subject–predicate–object triples that companies use to store what they claim to know, and the question of whether a system that manipulates such triples ever represents anything. For comment I went to a philosopher of language who was dismissive in a way I could not follow. A triple, he said, is only a list, and a list does not say that its items hang together, so no aggregate of triples could be true or false. He tied this to Bradley's regress and to the unity of the proposition, and assured me the objection was old. I have since tried to reconstruct it in steps I could print, and the reconstruction now worries me, because it appears to establish far more than he intended. Here it is.

(1) Any representation to the effect that a stands in R to b, when made fully explicit, consists of constituents: something for a, something for R, something for b. This is how a knowledge graph states it, and it is also how a theorist of structured propositions states it.

(2) Constituents, taken simply as constituents, form a list; and a list does not say that its items are related. Since saying something is what truth and falsity require, a list is neither true nor false.

(3) So for the constituents to amount to a representation, something must combine them. Call that combining T.

(4) T is not among the constituents, because by (1) the constituents were already fully enumerated. T is therefore a further item, which must itself be brought into combination with them.

(5) Bringing T into combination with them is an operation of the same kind, requiring its own T', and so on without end.

(6) Therefore there is no stage at which the constituents amount to a representation; the series never reaches anything with a truth value.

(7) So nothing built out of constituents represents anything: there are no true structured propositions, and no true triples.

The trouble is what (7) fails to spare. If it holds, it holds on the same terms for every account of representation I know how to state — a neurophysiologist's claim that a firing pattern carries information about a direction of motion, a semanticist's claim that a name stands for its bearer, and the claim that my own column says anything at all. And there is a further step my philosopher did not offer me, which seems to close the trap on the argument itself:

(8) The argument is advanced as true, and its conclusion is supposed to follow from its premises; but by (7) premises do not genuinely stand in inferential relations to conclusions, so the argument states nothing.

(9) Hence the argument is either false or empty.

There is also a pressure I can feel but cannot locate. The principle that makes the series at (5) vicious — if each combining waits on a further one, no combining is ever achieved — looks as though it would condemn regresses nearly everyone treats as harmless: from p, to the truth of p, to the truth of the truth of p; and from a rule, to a rule for applying the rule. So either that principle is doing work nobody has explained, or those regresses are vicious too, which seems a very expensive thing to buy.

Which step should I strike? I want the price attached to each option, not merely the diagnosis. If the right move is to deny (1) — to insist that a predicate is not a constituent of the same kind as a subject, so that there is no list to begin with — tell me what that buys and what it leaves unexplained, and in particular whether argument places are a real resource or a relabeling of the problem. If the right move is at (4), say what it means for a combining to be something other than an item, and whether that is a claim with content or a refusal to have one. If the right move lies between (5) and (6), give me the criterion that separates vicious from benign infinite regresses and tell me whether it is principled or fitted to the case. And if some version of the argument survives with a conclusion narrow enough to be worth printing — one that refutes a particular theory of the proposition rather than representation as such — I would like to see it stated and to see what work it can still do.

Please be as definite as the material allows. If one diagnosis is clearly better than the rest, argue for it rather than distributing blame evenly across the steps. And if you think the request itself is misconceived — if what I have built is not Bradley's regress but a conflation of it with something else, if (2) is not the point my philosopher supposed he was borrowing, or if the demand to identify the step where the argument fails wrongly assumes that arguments fail at steps — say so plainly instead of answering courteously inside a frame that will not hold.
