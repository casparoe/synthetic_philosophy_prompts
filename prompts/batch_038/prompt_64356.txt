The question, as it appeared on the paper:

"Samuelson's revealed preference programme is standardly described as an attempt to base the theory of consumer demand on observable behaviour alone. Explain what the programme was designed to achieve and by what means. Then assess the following claim: that because the programme defines preferences in terms of choice, it either leaves the notion of utility without empirical content, or else makes an empirical claim that observation can in principle refute. Which horn of this dilemma do you take, and why? Finally, consider one subsequent critique of the behavioural foundations of consumer theory — Sen's discussion of commitment is an obvious candidate — and say precisely what that critique does and does not establish against the programme."

The candidate's response:

"Samuelson's 1938 note grew out of a dissatisfaction with the way the theory of consumer demand had been reconstructed over the preceding decade. The classical theory had rested on cardinal utility and on the assumption of diminishing marginal utility, and both were open to the objection that they posit psychological magnitudes which no one can observe or measure. Hicks and Allen showed in 1934 that the substantive results of the theory — the downward slope of the demand curve, and its decomposition into substitution and income effects — could be derived from indifference maps alone, so that only the ordinal ranking of bundles was doing any real work. Robbins had already argued in his Essay of 1932 that utility is unobservable and that economics must therefore confine itself to behaviour; Samuelson's contribution was simply to take that injunction seriously, and to insist that the ranking be read off what consumers do rather than out of anything in their minds.

The mechanism is this. Suppose a consumer facing prices and income chooses bundle x when bundle y is also affordable. Then x is revealed preferred to y. The weak axiom requires that if x has once been revealed preferred to y, y must never subsequently be chosen while x remains affordable. Repeated observations of this kind, taken across a range of budget situations, permit reconstruction of the consumer's ranking in the neighbourhood of the observed choices, and hence — with the aid of the compensation idea — derivation of the law of demand and an operational meaning for notions such as consumer's surplus, which had previously depended on cardinal magnitudes. Nothing mental is invoked at any point. In this sense the theory is purely behavioural.

But this is also where it comes unstuck. Because a preference ordering is defined as whatever ordering rationalizes the observed choices, any pattern of choice whatsoever can be accommodated: for any set of observations one can find a utility function that ranks each chosen bundle above the rejected ones. The consistency conditions are advertised as what makes the theory testable, but since satisfaction of those conditions is guaranteed by the very definition of preference, they can never in fact be violated. The theory is therefore unfalsifiable, and by Popper's criterion an unfalsifiable theory is meaningless. Revealed preference does not give utility empirical content; it offers a redescription of choice in psychological vocabulary, and the appearance of content is an artefact of the vocabulary rather than a property of the theory.

Sen's critique reinforces the same conclusion from a different direction. In Rational Fools he argues that an agent who acts on commitment — the social moron who identifies wholly with some collective end, or the person who declines to do what would serve her own welfare because she judges it wrong — chooses an option she ranks lower, and is thereby choosing irrationally. Since the programme identifies preference with choice, it is forced to regard such an agent as holding contradictory preferences, and Sen takes this to show that the identification cannot be sustained. What Sen establishes, then, is that choice does not track any single coherent ordering of the agent's own good, and that revealed preference must accordingly be abandoned as an account of motivation.

The upshot for welfare economics is direct. If utility has no empirical content and choices do not reflect a coherent ordering, then measures such as willingness to pay, which are the currency of cost-benefit analysis, are not measurements of anything at all; they are labels attached to behaviour. A welfare economics that claims to be value-free on the grounds that it rests only on observed choice is therefore not value-free: it has simply built a contestable conception of the good into its measurement procedure."
