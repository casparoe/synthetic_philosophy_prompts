I am reading the historiography of the great famines — Ireland in the 1840s, the relief regime of British India, Bengal in 1943 — trying to work out for myself whether such events are best described as natural catastrophes with political aggravation, or as political undertakings from the outset. In the course of that reading I keep coming across a particular configuration that I have no word for, and the labels usually offered in these debates do not seem to capture it.

The configuration is this. Once the harvest fails, the administration in charge does not treat the emergency principally as a problem of keeping people alive. It treats it as an opening. Rearrangements that had been politically blocked for a generation — consolidating fragmented smallholdings, ending a peasantry's dependence on a single subsistence crop, moving people off the land and into wage labour, subordinating food supplies and transport to some larger imperial or wartime objective — suddenly become feasible, because the emergency has suspended the ordinary politics of resistance. And then the relief itself is designed around those rearrangements rather than around the hungry. Work is offered on public projects at a rate below what the same labourer could earn in that district in an ordinary year, and the shortfall is defended on the grounds that relief must not compete with private employers or accustom the labouring population to expecting support. The daily ration is set below what a man doing the required work needs to sustain it. Admission is conditional on surrendering one's holding, or on travelling to a distant camp where the crowding and the disease finish what the hunger began. Grain leaves the affected region throughout, on the reasoning that trade must not be interfered with. Each of these decisions can be defended separately as fiscal prudence or administrative necessity; taken together they mean that the severity of the response is not an accident of underfunding but a means of getting the rearrangement done. The suffering is load-bearing.

So the thing I want identified is this: a catastrophe deployed as the instrument of a programme that already existed, with the harshness of the official response functional to that programme rather than a failure of it.

Someone suggested to me that this is what is meant by the shock doctrine, but that seems wrong on two counts: those cases are overwhelmingly about market liberalisation in the last half-century, and the account there generally requires the shock to be seized by people who did not produce it, whereas in the famines I am reading the administration's own antecedent policy often helped create the vulnerability, and the programme being advanced is not always market-shaped. Moral hazard and less eligibility name the justifications officials reached for, not the arrangement as a whole. Providentialism names a theology that made the arrangement acceptable to its authors, which is not the same as the arrangement. What I want to know is whether there is an established concept — in famine studies, in political economy, in the literature on disaster politics, or in the sociology of administration — that picks out exactly this. If the available terms are only near-misses, tell me which they are, what each of them misses, and which comes closest. If nothing fits properly, then coin something, and give me a definition sharp enough to separate this from an administration merely botching relief through incompetence or stinginess, from an administration that causes a famine outright and then declines to respond, and from ordinary political opportunism exercised in a non-emergency setting.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items and nothing else: no introduction, no summary, no closing remarks, one or two sentences per item. The first four items should each take the nearest candidate concept, whether established or freshly coined, and state what it captures, what it fails to capture, and how well it fits what I described. The fifth item should give the term you would actually use, defined so that the three separations I asked for above come out the right way.
