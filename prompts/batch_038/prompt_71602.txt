Bit of context, because it shapes what I need. I write literary fiction — three novels out, one of which was remaindered inside a year — and for the last few summers I've also taught a week-long residential course for adult writers in a converted vicarage in Norfolk. This year I have fourteen people, mixed ages, all heavy readers with strong opinions about narrators who lie to you. On the Wednesday evening I get a ninety-minute slot with the whole group in one room: a whiteboard, chairs in a loose circle, no desks to speak of, a printer I can use the afternoon before, and a box of chocolate digestives I'm happy to weaponise as stakes. I'm not a philosopher and I don't want to sound like one; I'm a novelist who has stumbled into an idea and now wants other people to feel it rather than be told about it.

What I want to build is a game or tightly structured exercise that makes the room feel two things I keep failing to explain in prose. First, higher-order evidence: evidence not about the world but about the reliability of your own take on the world — you learn something that casts doubt on whether your believing was any good, without learning anything new about the facts. Second, epistemic akrasia: you've worked out that you ought not to believe something, and you believe it anyway. My working theory, and the reason I'm coming at this sideways, is that readers of an unreliable narrator are already sitting in exactly that pickle — you get chapter after chapter of signals that the person telling you things is compromised, you conclude you shouldn't trust them, and then you go right on believing what they said on page nine because you cant un-hear it. I want them to hit that from the inside, before anyone says the words. The shape I'm half-imagining, and please push back if there's a better one: they read a passage and answer a handful of questions privately with a confidence rating; then mid-way I drop something that undermines their own process rather than the text — maybe I announce that half the room got a subtly doctored version and nobody can tell which half, or maybe half of them answered while also holding a seven-digit number in their heads and I reveal later that mental load makes people swallow more of what they read (hazy memory of that being a real finding, don't take my word for it, but it might work as a lever); then they re-answer with the biscuits riding on it. I'll write the reading passage myself out of an abandoned novel so nobody can have met it before — it needs to be the kind of paragraph where a confident answer is available and wrong unless you notice one thing that's off. Two worries I want you to solve for me: the scoring has to be honest, because if the winning move is to hedge everything into mush then the game teaches the wrong lesson, or the right one only by accident; and the drop has to land on their own thinking rather than on the text, or I've just built a quiz with a twist ending. Rules, materials, exact timings, and what I say out loud at each stage — keep the scripting tight, I ad-lib badly under presure. No bibliography, no reading list, nothing I'd have to go and look up. I want something I can actually run on a wet Wednesday.

Please write it up in these sections, in this order: **The idea in one paragraph** — about 120 words, plain English, no jargon; **Materials and room** — a short list, 60 words; **The rules and the scoring** — 300 words, including precisely what the biscuits are worth and a line on why the scheme isn't a hedging machine; **The drop** — 150 words, giving me when it lands and the exact sentence I say; **Run sheet** — 200 words, timed to the minute for ninety; **The debrief** — 400 words, mapping specific moments of the game onto the two ideas, written as the questions I ask rather than the speeches I give; **What will go wrong the first time** — 200 words, and how I rescue it mid-session without admitting defeat; **What the game can't reproduce** — 150 words on the part of the unreliable-narrator version of this that no activity can stage and that I'll have to supply myself afterwards.
