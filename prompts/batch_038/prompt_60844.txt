I TA a 75-minute discussion section for intro epistemology — 24 students, six tables of four — and this week they've read the Meno. Which is exactly my problem: they already know the punchline. I can tell them Socrates thinks knowledge beats true belief because beliefs have to be tied down by working out the reason why, and they'll nod, write it down, and feel nothing. What I want is a game where they hit the *earlier* step themselves, the one that's actually hard to swallow: that a true belief you picked up from somewhere else is genuinely just as good for getting where you're going — and in the right circumstances better, because its faster — and then watching it come loose the moment the circumstances move.

So: design the game. Rules, materials (printable, nothing that costs me money; points can be worth a token participation mark or nothing at all), a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 75 with room for the debrief, and the debrief itself.

What it has to do:

- Two routes to the same correct answers. One group gets its answers from a source that supplies no reason whatsoever; the other has to derive them from a small rule set I hand out. Nobody is told that's the variable being manipulated.
- The first scored phase has to be dead fair on the board. Every answer in it is true by construction, so both routes bank identical points, and ideally the no-reason route wins on speed. They should be able to look at the actual numbers and see that belief did the job.
- Then rounds that pull them apart: the source goes away and can't be re-consulted, the environment starts looking like it contradicts the right answer (the road appears to be heading the wrong way), and a transfer round with novel cases where the memorized answers actively mislead.
- I need a mechanic that catches people in the derive-it group who quietly just memorized the output anyway, otherwise Im measuring confidence or verbal fluency instead of the thing.
- The no-reason group shouldn't finish up looking like the class dummies. Build something in so the role isn't a status wound.

Then the debrief: map each round onto the bit of the dialogue it's staging, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do in the moment — someone will announce halfway through that this is just the Meno, tables will trade answers and the contrast will collapse, phase one will run long — and be straight with me about what the game can't reproduce. In particular: do the later rounds just rig the result in favor of knowledge, and what do I say to the student who claims the whole thing is question-begging? Also give me a stripped variant for 12 and a version for 40. Pick whatever content domain is easiest to run — a fictional town's one-way streets, a hidden card rule, a supply-route puzzle, whatever — but it has to be something none of them have prior opinions about, or I'll just be measuring background knowledge and calling it epistemology.
