I am in the middle of reworking a postgraduate seminar I give each Michaelmas on the philosophy of inductive inference, and I should welcome your assistance with a passage I intend to place near its centre.

For some years I took seriously the objection that Solomonoff's account is not a genuine theory of inductive rationality, on the grounds that it is indexed to an arbitrary stipulation: the prior weight of a hypothesis is fixed by its Kolmogorov complexity, which is itself defined only relative to some universal Turing machine, so that what counts as simple — and hence what the evidence is said to support — appears to depend on a choice we make rather than on anything in the world. I now think this objection has been overtaken by two results working in tandem. The invariance theorem establishes that complexity does not in fact vary with the choice of machine; and since we now possess serviceable computable stand-ins for it — general-purpose compressors of the LZMA family, and, more lately, large language models pressed into service as compressors — the very quantity the objection supposed to be conventional can be measured on an ordinary laptop.

I should like to put two questions to you, in the form in which I intend to put them to the class.

First: given that the priors generated by different universal machines agree asymptotically, what precisely remains of the charge that Solomonoff induction smuggles in a merely conventional standard of simplicity? I want to be able to tell my students that the residual worry is not philosophical at all but finite and pragmatic — a matter of the constant appearing in the invariance theorem, which is bounded by the length of the shortest interpreter one machine needs in order to emulate the other, and which must therefore be quite small in relation to the hypotheses working scientists actually compare. Please explain, in these terms, why the objection loses its grip.

Second, and more concretely: what checks ought a student to run in order to confirm that the compressed lengths she obtains for two rival hypotheses, using the tools available to her, track the invariant complexity of those hypotheses rather than the idiosyncrasies of her particular compressor and her particular corpus? I should like this to be an exercise she can complete in an afternoon, and I should like it to issue in something she can report as a number.

Please pitch the whole thing for final-year philosophy undergraduates who have had one course in computability theory but no measure theory, and who have encountered Hume on induction and the problem of underdetermination, but nothing whatever of the algorithmic information literature.
