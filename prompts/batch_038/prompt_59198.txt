ok, I sit on a small internal panel at my lab — three of us plus Dana, who's senior and writes the rationale for every ruling. The panel's job sounds narrow but it turns out to be load-bearing: people come to us claiming "the mechanism we characterized in model X is also present in model Y," and if we sign off, they get to reuse the monitor and the safety case that were built around it. If we say no, everything gets re-derived from scratch, which costs months. So we're deciding an identity question under time pressure and I've started to worry we don't actually have a rule, just vibes with nice-sounding write-ups.

The record so far, all of these unanimous:

1. The copying/induction circuit. Found in our 1B, then again in the 13B — different layers, completely different weights, no shared ancestry (separate runs from scratch). Same behavior on the test suite, same effect when you ablate it, and a probe trained on the small model's activations works on the big one once you fit a linear map between the two spaces. Ruling: same mechanism. Dana's line was "different bodies, same reflexes."

2. The context-instruction flag — the thing that marks text inside a retrieved document that reads like a command addressed to the assistant, and routes downstream toward caution. We had it fully characterized in model A. A collaborator found something in an unrelated family's model that behaves identically on every injection eval we have, and ablating it produces the same behavioral changes. But it's arranged differently (two attention heads feeding one MLP in A; smeared across several layers and several component types in theirs), and a probe trained on A's residual stream decodes nothing over there — no linear map recovers it either. Ruling: not the same mechanism. Dana: "same job, different machinery. We don't get to call that the same thing."

3. The same flag, in the RLHF'd descendant of one of our own checkpoints. The base-model probe fails on the finetuned model — the direction has swung maybe 40 degrees and no linear fix recovers it — and its input range widened a bit (it started responding to a class of phrasings it used to ignore). But on everything we'd originally characterized, it does exactly what it did. We walked the finetuning trajectory checkpoint by checkpoint and watched it move gradually the whole way. Ruling: same mechanism. Dana: "we never lost sight of it. There's no point where it stopped being itself." The deployed monitor had to be refit from scratch and we let them ship it anyway.

4. Within a single run: early in training the copying behavior was done by two heads jointly — kill either one alone and nothing happens, kill both and you get the full effect. Later in the same run one head does the whole job and the other has been recruited for something else entirely. Ruling: same mechanism, the fused head inherits the identity of the pair. Dana: "it consolidated, it didn't get replaced."

5. The one that went the other way. A model warm-started from an old checkpoint and then retrained hard on a different objective. The old probe direction still decodes the same feature cleanly in the new model and it's largely the same units carrying it — technically we could have refit the monitor and it would have worked fine. But ablating it now has a totally different behavioral signature and it's clearly being used for another job. Ruling: not the same mechanism. Dana: "same machinery, different job."

Now the case on the docket and I genuinely cant call it. New model, trained from scratch, different architecture revision (MoE-ish), different data mixture — no lineage to anything we've characterized and no path in weight space to walk. It has something doing the context-instruction flag job, and here's the part that makes it weird: the match isn't just behavioral. They did component-level attribution and the intervention profile matches A's mechanism one-for-one — patch it in, patch it out, and which downstream components change and how, across a couple hundred test contexts, lines up with A's after you fit a linear map between the activation spaces. That's a much stronger match than case 2 ever had. The ablation signature matches feature for feature including the odd off-distribution effects we thought were idiosyncratic to A. But: the machinery is a single MLP doing the whole job rather than attention routing into an MLP, the geometry is rotated past anything a probe from A can see, the shipped monitor is stone dead on this model, and the replacement monitor they trained has a different false-positive profile — it fires on some benign retrieved text that A's version ignored. And one loose thread: it lights up during multi-digit arithmetic, specifically around carry operations, which is in no eval suite we ran, so nobody knows whether that's a second job or an artifact.

So — (a) what does Dana write, and what does the panel rule? Best guess plus the reasoning, and tell me which of 1–5 she'll cite as the closest analog and which one the losing side will cite. (b) Lay out the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with all five verdicts. I can think of same-abstract-computation, same-causal-role-at-the-grain-we-tested, same-total-causal-role, same-geometry-up-to-a-linear-map, continuous-tracked-lineage, and monitor-transfers, but I suspect several of those are already dead and that some of the survivors are one rule at different grains — sort that out for me. (c) Which live rules actually diverge on the docket case, and what hangs on it, given that the practical question is whether a dead monitor plus a matching intervention profile is a reason to re-derive the whole safety case. (d) Cheapest further case or experiment that would separate the survivors — is the arithmetic thing a real discriminating test or just noise, and if it's real, which way does it cut? (e) And if the record genuinely underdeterminates this one, say plainly what principle we'd be adopting by ruling either way, and whether there's a reason to prefer it that isn't just assuming the answer against the other rules.
