I am drafting the conceptual-foundations section of a long report on irreversibility in small driven systems, and before engaging with any existing account of the arrow of time I want the specification: the conditions an account must satisfy to count as adequate. Since adequacy is relative to purpose, let me fix the purpose. I need an account on which it is determinate, for a given physical system and a given choice of description, whether that system has a thermodynamic arrow and which way it points — determinate enough to license the coarse-grained, irreversible modelling that is routine in stochastic thermodynamics, and robust enough to survive contact with the mesoscopic regime, where entropy-decreasing trajectories are not merely possible but measured and assigned exact statistical weights.

Please write the requirements, each accompanied by the case or consideration that motivates it. The following are candidates I have collected; the finished list should be both longer and shorter than this, since some of them will turn out to be redundant, question-begging, or mere restatements of one another.

- Compatibility with the time-reversal invariance (or, where it fails, the specific character) of the underlying dynamics, rather than denial of it.
- Recurrence: what the account says to the objection that any entropy gradient will eventually reverse, and why the relevant timescales are or are not a legitimate dismissive reply.
- Velocity inversion: a system whose microstate is reversed must evolve backwards, and the account must say why we never meet such systems.
- The asymmetry of records, memory, and evidence.
- The asymmetry of intervention and preparation — we can set up an initial condition but not a final one — together with the charge that any account leaning on this is applying a double standard, reasoning one way about initial states and another about final states.
- Radiation: diverging waves from localized sources, with no converging counterparts.
- Cosmology: the smoothness of the early universe, including the wrinkle that a smooth matter distribution is gravitationally low-entropy rather than high, so that the naive identification of uniformity with equilibrium fails once gravity is included.
- The coarse-graining relativity of entropy, and hence the question whether the arrow is a feature of the world or of a description of it.
- Mesoscopic porosity: fluctuation relations quantify second-law-violating behaviour, so the account must say whether the arrow is a matter of degree, and what remains of it where fluctuations dominate.
- The dependence of attributed entropy production on where one draws the system–bath boundary and on what one treats as the externally varied protocol.
- A requirement of my own, which I want tested rather than adopted: boundary-locality — the arrow in a laboratory should be fixed by facts about that laboratory and its surroundings, not by the state of the universe as a whole at its origin.
- Composability: how arrows nest, and what the account says about a feedback-controlled subsystem whose local entropy decreases at the expense of a controller elsewhere.
- Stability under refinement: the direction assigned to a system should not flip when the description is made more fine-grained.
- Non-circularity: the account must not posit the very asymmetry it was meant to derive, or smuggle a direction of time in through its account of measurement, preparation, or conditioning.
- Explanatory residue: if the account terminates in a special boundary condition, it must say what work that posit is doing and what remains unexplained.
- The absence of any observed domain with the opposite arrow, and an account of what such a domain would look like to observers inside or outside it.

Then the analysis I most want:

1. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable, negotiable, or camp-specific — the latter meaning a constraint that only looks like a requirement once one has already accepted a particular conception of what explaining the arrow amounts to. Name the camp in each such case.
2. Identify any subset that is jointly unsatisfiable, and say which member gives way under which view. I suspect boundary-locality is in tension with something, but I want the argument rather than my hunch.
3. Say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates, and which is most often assumed without argument.
4. Say how the list changes if the purpose changes — from the laboratory-usable standard above to a cosmological one, where the only question is why the universe exhibits any gradient at all.

Finally, briefly, and only insofar as it exposes which requirements each one fails: how a past-hypothesis-plus-statistical-mechanics account, a typicality account, an interventionist or agency-based account, and a view on which the arrow is a fundamental, non-derivative feature of the world fare against the list you have produced. I do not want a ranking. A table is acceptable for items 1 to 3 provided the reasoning stays visible.
