I sit on a nine-member Living Conditions Review Panel in a mid-sized American state. Two years ago, after our warden and our officers' union president returned from a study visit to Norwegian facilities operating under a normality principle, Halden among them, the department commissioned a redesign of Ridgeview, our medium-security institution built in the 1970s, and made every proposed change to daily life there subject to our vote. Our charter directs us to weigh the security of the facility, the safety of staff and residents, and the successful return of residents to the community, and it does not rank those considerations. The panel comprises three department appointees, two members nominated by the officers' union, two by a statewide victims' rights coalition, and two by the legislature. We record vote tallies only: no written reasons, and we have never adopted a general standard.

Our record so far, in the order decided:

1. Approved: work release at a commercial dairy thirty miles away. Residents leave unescorted at 5 a.m. and return by 7 p.m., six days a week, are paid the local minimum wage, wear GPS monitors, and are subject to immediate recall. Mandatory deductions for room and board, child support, and victim restitution leave them roughly forty dollars a week, which may not be spent in town.
2. Rejected: paying the same minimum wage, with the same deductions, for work inside the fence, in the kitchen, laundry, and maintenance shops.
3. Rejected: fitting every resident's door in the west unit with a lock operable from the inside, in a unit where the officer's station is forty feet away and every room has a working call button.
4. Approved: ending twenty-two-hour lockdown in that same west unit. Rooms are open from 6 a.m. to 10 p.m., residents move freely among dayroom, gym, library, and yard, and officers walk the tier continuously instead of observing from the booth.
5. Approved: closing the cafeteria and giving each resident a weekly food allowance set at the state's SNAP-equivalent per diem, spent at an in-unit store priced at cost, with meals cooked in a shared kitchen where knives are issued on a sign-out log and counted back each night.
6. Rejected: personal mobile phones with unrestricted calling, texting, and internet access.
7. Approved: free video calls from open booths in the dayroom, thirty minutes by appointment, recorded, restricted to a pre-approved contact list, with an officer in sight of every booth.
8. Approved: a music program. Piano and drum kit are kept in a locked, staff-controlled storeroom, practice runs from 4 to 8 p.m., and we invite the victims' coalition and the local paper to two concerts a year.
9. Rejected: converting the minimum-security wing to fenceless cottages with residents walking to jobs in the adjoining town, despite a state audit reporting zero escapes from that wing in three years and a sharp drop in assaults there since item 4 took effect.

Next month's item is a family residential unit sited inside the existing perimeter, on the footprint of the minimum-security wing: eight furnished one-bedroom apartments, each with a full kitchen including knives in a lockable drawer, a living room, and a bathroom. A resident classified minimum security, with two years served, a clean disciplinary record for eighteen months, and documented participation in the video-call and work-release programs, may apply for a stay of forty-eight to seventy-two hours with a spouse or partner and dependent children under fourteen. Groceries come through the same store and allowance as item 5. Officers are posted at the unit's single entrance and there are no cameras inside the apartments; the department's security review finds escape risk no greater than in the wing as it now stands and states that an officer can reach any apartment door within ninety seconds. Under the proposal as drafted, each resident signs an agreement providing that officers will not enter an occupied apartment except on activation of a duress alarm, at the twice-daily head counts, or in a documented emergency. The department's research office has given us two studies of extended family-contact programs, one finding a modest reduction in returns to custody and one finding no measurable effect; a private foundation covers construction and operating costs fall on the department; the victims' coalition has asked that the item be deferred, and two of its seats have said they will vote no.

What I need is an analysis of our own consistency, not a survey of the literature. Specifically:

(a) Reconstruct two or three candidate standards, each compatible with all nine votes or requiring the fewest and least damaging exceptions, and state what each predicts about the new item. Give the prediction in terms of the conditions attached, not merely yes or no, since I expect the conditions are where the standards genuinely part company.
(b) Say what weight the department's security finding and the two conflicting studies carry under each candidate standard, and in particular whether item 9 shows that outcome data has any independent force for this panel.
(c) Recommend a decision: approve as drafted, approve with specified conditions, defer, or reject, together with the conditions you would write. Identify which of the nine items is the closest analog to the new one and defend that choice against the nearest competitor, and identify which item is hardest to explain on the standard you favor and what the panel would have to say about it.
(d) State in one sentence each, in language fit for a public report, the principle the panel would be adopting by approving as drafted, by approving with your conditions, and by rejecting.
(e) Design one further item, to be calendared after this one, that would most cleanly separate the two candidate standards you rate highest, and say what each of them predicts about it.
