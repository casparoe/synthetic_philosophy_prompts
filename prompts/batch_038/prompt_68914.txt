I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm doing a piece on why perfectly reasonable people freeze up between two options that are both good. My sidebar is going to be about Ruth Chang's work on hard choices — parity, the claim that the standard three-way scheme (better / worse / equally good) is missing a fourth relation, and the small-improvement move people use to argue that the "equally good" box doesn't fit these cases.

Problem: every explainer ever written uses the same two examples, lawyer vs. artist or coffee vs. tea. I want a fresh one, and more importantly I want one that's actually *built* to do a job rather than just waved at. So here's the job.

Design me a single realistic scenario — one paragraph, under about 250 words — that hits all four of these:

1. Two options, A and B, genuinely on a par in Chang's sense: neither is better, neither is worse, and they're not "equally good" in the coin-flip-is-fine sense.
2. Show the small-improvement test rather than asserting it. Pick one concrete small improvement to A — a specific dollar figure, a specific extra hour a week, a specific named perk — and argue from the details of your own case why A+ still doesn't come out better than B. If the only way to get that verdict is "because I stipulated it," the case fails.
3. The twist, and thats the whole point: the agent has a decisive reason to pick one of them anyway, and that reason does not come from her own commitment, resolution, or act of will. It comes from outside — a fact about another person, an institutional rule, a fixed constraint she didn't choose. So reasons don't "run out" the way Chang's picture says they must in a real hard choice, even though the two options stay on a par.
4. Constraints: no trolleys, no dying promises, no organ transplants, no sci-fi, nothing life-or-death. Mundane institutional setting preferred — a city department, a school, a family business, a lab, a newspaper. And the outside reason can't be a moral trump that swamps everything; A and B have to remain on a par *as options*, with the reason settling what to do rather than making one of them better.

Then, after the scenario:

- Run the checklist. For each of 1–4, show it's satisfied and name the specific detail in your story doing the work.
- The cheating test: the reason in (3) must not already be smuggled into how you described A and B. Say explicitly whether it is. If a careful reader could object that you individuated the options too narrowly — fold that outside fact into A and A is simply better — concede it, then tell me what's left of the case after the objection is applied.
- Give Chang's best reply, and also Broome's — the line that says there is no fourth relation and the whole hard-choice phenomenon is just vagueness in the comparative. Tell me which of the two does more damage.
- Say what happens if I delete the outside reason: does the case collapse into the lawyer-vs-artist cliché, or is it still a different animal?

No bibliography, no reading list, no quotes from anybody's actual writing — I can't verify any of that and my editor definately won't either. Define any jargon in a clause as you go.

If you think the spec can't be jointly met — if (3) always collapses into (1), or always gets eaten by the option-individuation objection — say so plainly, give me the closest case you can build, and point at exactly where it breaks.

End with one line and nothing after it, in exactly this form:
Verdict: survives_chang = yes|no; load_bearing_feature = <five words or fewer>
