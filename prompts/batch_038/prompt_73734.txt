Here's the practice. In the US, farmers who buy corn seed engineered to make its own insect toxin (the Bt stuff, from the soil bacterium) aren't allowed to just plant it field to field. For most of the life of the technology they were required to plant a set chunk of their acreage in ordinary, non-Bt corn — a refuge — the size of which varied by region and by which pest the trait targeted. The stated logic has two parts. One is the 'high dose' part: the plant is supposed to express the toxin at a level high enough that an insect carrying one copy of a resistance gene dies too, which makes resistance behave as recessive. The other is the refuge part: resistant insects are assumed to start out rare, and the susceptible moths bred in the refuge are supposed to fly over, mate at random with any resistant survivors from the Bt field, and keep the resistance allele buried in heterozygotes that the high dose then kills. Compliance with hand-planted refuges turned out to be lousy, so eventually the refuge got moved into the seed bag — a small percentage of non-Bt seed blended in at the factory, so the farmer doesn't have to do anything and can't really fail to. Meanwhile, starting around 2011 there were field reports out of Iowa of western corn rootworm doing serious damage in continuous-corn fields planted to a rootworm Bt trait, and the resistance there didn't look like a tidy recessive.

A couple of things about this puzzle me, and I want them handled explicitly rather than smoothed over. First, the same regulatory world happily runs area-wide suppression and eradication programs — quarantine lines, coordinated treatment of whole districts, mass releases of sterile insects — which only work if pest populations are locally closed and don't mix much. The refuge logic needs the opposite: moths moving freely and mating at random across the landscape. Second, there seem to be two different selection stories stacked on top of each other. There's the allele-frequency story inside the insect population, and then there's the story about farmers, where skipping your refuge is individually rational and collectively self-defeating, and where the move into the seed bag looks less like a change in biology than a change in who has to be trusted.
