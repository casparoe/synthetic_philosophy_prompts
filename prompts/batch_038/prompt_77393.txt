I am in the fourth year of a PhD in the history and philosophy of science, and I am drafting the framing section of a chapter on Newton's natural theology. Nearly every survey I consult, including the one my supervisor assigns in the introductory seminar, reaches for the same image: the Newtonian universe is a clock and Newton's God is its maker, an intelligence that designed the mechanism, wound it up, and then withdrew, leaving it to run on laws of its own; the eighteenth century simply secularized the image into deism. My supervisor uses that image to license a methodological move I want to resist. If God is the clockmaker, theology sits outside the physics: it supplies a motive for expecting order but does no explanatory work inside the science, so a historian may set aside Newton's enormous output on prophecy, chronology, and the doctrine of the Trinity as private eccentricity and lose nothing in the Principia or the Opticks. I suspect the analogy is carrying a conclusion it cannot support, and I want the suspicion tested before I argue it in print.

Please audit the analogy, in the following order.

(a) The correspondence, stated explicitly. What plays the case and the wheel train, the spring, the winding, the escapement, the maker who leaves the workshop — and what in Newton's natural philosophy does each stand for? A table is welcome.

(b) Separate the disanalogies that are merely pictorial (the clock is brass, small, assembled from parts in time) from those that bear on my supervisor's inference, and press hard on the second group. I have in mind at least these: Newton's insistence that matter is essentially passive and that motion tends to be lost in collisions, so that the world requires his active principles; his view that the planetary system could not have arisen from the laws of motion and gravitation alone, and his suggestion in the later Queries that the system would accumulate irregularities and stand in need of reform; the fact that the clock enters this debate as Leibniz's accusation — that Newton's God is so unskillful a workman that he must rewind his watch — rather than as Newton's self-description, together with Clarke's rejoinder that continuous divine agency is no admission of defective workmanship; and the General Scholium's insistence that God governs all things "not as the soul of the world, but as Lord over all," on account of his dominion.

(c) What the image quietly imports that Newton's God lacks or explicitly disowns — the self-sufficiency of the mechanism, the maker's absence in space and time, the independence of law from will — and what it omits altogether, including dominion as the very ground of deity in the Pantokrator passage, the treatment of infinite space as the sensorium in which God perceives all things intimately, and a God known through providence and prophecy rather than through design alone.

(d) Two candidate replacements drawn from Newton's own writing, each subjected to the same audit. The kingdom, in which the laws of nature are a sovereign's regular manner of governing rather than an autonomous machine; and the sensorium, in which the world is not an artifact at all but the immediate content of an omnipresent mind's perception. For each, say what it buys for the physics — action at a distance across empty space without impulse, the rejection of vortices, the stability and reformability of the system — and what it costs, including whether it slides toward the pantheism or the occasionalism Newton took pains to disclaim.

(e) The verdict: does the bracketing conclusion survive, and if the clockwork image is worth keeping at all, in what reduced form and for what limited purpose?

Two caveats. I have no interest in a bibliography, and I cannot look anything up while I work, so please argue from the texts as you know them rather than gesturing at scholarship. And where my summary above overstates or misattributes something — including my assumption that the watch on the heath belongs to Paley a century later rather than to Newton himself — correct me directly instead of working around the error.
