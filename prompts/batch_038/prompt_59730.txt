Last term I sat on a three-person panel at a small regional health authority that had to decide whether a drug should go on the formulary. Our terms of reference were explicit: recommend if and only if (a) the trial hit its primary endpoint and (b) the side-effect profile is acceptable. We voted on all three questions separately. Two of us said the trial had hit its endpoint, two of us said the safety profile was acceptable, and two of us voted against recommending it — and every single one of us was internally coherent about it, because the two no-votes had each accepted exactly one premise and rejected the other, while the third of us accepted both and voted yes. So our draft finding came out saying the panel found the evidence adequate and the safety profile acceptable, one line above the box that said "not recommended," and legal sent it back to us. That sent me down the rabbit hole on the doctrinal paradox and the discursive dilemma and eventually into the impossibility result sitting behind them, and I've now written up an arguement that I'm fairly sure is bad, but I can't locate the badness, partly because the conclusion is something I really don't want to be committed to.

Here it is. (1) For a group to have beliefs at all, there has to be some rule that turns what the individual members accept into a single set of propositions the group accepts. (2) A group counts as a rational agent only if the set it accepts is consistent, and complete enough to settle the questions actually in front of it. (3) Any rule that treats members symmetrically (nobody's acceptance counts for more than anyone else's) and treats each issue separately (what the group accepts on a question depends only on members' attitudes toward that very question) is either dictatorial or can be made to produce an inconsistent set — that's just the impossibility theorem, and it's literally what blew up my panel. (4) A dictatorial rule doesn't give the group beliefs of its own, it only borrows one member's. (5) So every group is either not an agent over and above some member or not a consistent agent, and either way it has no beliefs of its own. (6) My panel is a group, so my panel has no beliefs of its own, even though the minutes are full of "the panel finds that." (7) So all group-level belief talk — a committee's view, a firm's intention, a state's acknowledgement of its own past crimes — is loose shorthand for claims about members, and everything built on it (corporate mens rea, institutional apologies, holding a whole nation responsible for something) rests on a way of speaking rather than on anything in the world.

Two things bug me and I can't tell whether either is the flaw or just noise. First, the parallel argument about individuals seems to run too: if you patiently wrote down everything I'd sincerely assent to under questioning, the list would contain contradictions, and there's no rule that makes me consistent, so if (2) plus (3) kill group beliefs they ought to kill individual ones — which is absurd, and if I have to choose I'd much rather keep (2) than lose that. Second, nothing our panel actually produced felt inconsistent to anyone in the room; we had to be told which line to strike. A friend says that means (3) is smuggling something in, but I don't see what, and I don't want to get out of this by declaring the theorem irrelevant to real cases. So: which step do you reject, why did it look innocent when I wrote it, and what does rejecting it cost me? Then say whether the repaired version still gets anywhere near (7) — because if the honest landing spot is "group beliefs are fully determined by members' attitudes but real all the same," I need to know why that isn't just (7) with nicer manners, given that my own beliefs are determined by what my neurons do and I don't think that makes them a manner of speaking. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the argument back at me.
