ok so this stopped being an abstract thing for me about six weeks ago and I can't stop turning it over.

I'm a third-year psych-epidemiology PhD student. Chapter 2 of my thesis is a pooled time series: four cohort studies of the same city, fieldwork starting in 1979, 1991, 2004 and 2013, and I'm supposed to hand back one curve of depression prevalence over 40-odd years. To get there I have to make four different measures comparable, which in practice means weeks of recode scripts and one methods section I'm not comfortable with.

The work looks like this. Each wave used a different instrument: an interviewer-run symptom checklist in '79, a short self-report screener in the 90s, a two-stage interview with clinician reappraisal in the 2000s, and the current nine-item module now. Each was built against a different edition of the diagnostic manual, and the editions don't agree with each other — how many symptoms you need, over what duration, whether there's a clinical-significance requirement at all, whether recently bereaved people count. So for every pair I have to choose a cut point that's supposed to select the same group of people, and then defend the pairing. Where I'm lucky, a subsample took two instruments on the same occasion and I can do proper item-response linking with an anchor sample, and everyone is happy. Where I'm not lucky — which is most of the series — there's no bridge sample, so I match items by wording and argue that similar phrasing implies similar content. That's the sentence I don't believe.

Here's what's actually bothering me. The field tells two stories at once. Story one, which I was taught in year one: the manual is deliberately descriptive and pragmatic; DSM-III stayed neutral about causes precisely so people who disagreed about etiology could still agree on labels; nobody in my department claims the categories cut nature at its joints. Story two, which my whole thesis rests on: the construct is stable enough across editions and instruments that a 1979 score and a 2016 score can be correlated, averaged, and drawn as a single line — and if that line goes up, it means depression went up, not that our measuring changed.

So what does this harmonization practice presuppose about conceptual change? Does pooling across editions require that the construct be the same thing, in the sense a Kuhnian would deny — that meaning is fixed by the theoretical role a term plays, so a category re-described under new criteria isn't the category you started with — or does it only need something weaker, like calibrated predictive equivalence between instruments? Please keep seperate what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably; I suspect several mutually hostile positions would license everything I do all day, and I want to know which. And where is my project underdetermined — could someone who took meaning-variance completely seriously run all my scripts unchanged and just describe the output differently?

Then there's a puzzle I can't solve on my own. My committee treats it as a hard floor that without a bridge sample you simply cannot link two instruments used in the same city in the same decade. But nobody blinks at linking across four diagnostic editions with no bridge whatsoever, plus forty years of drift in what people will admit to an interviewer. Why is the second gap treated as smaller than the first? Is that just an artifact of what's fundable, or does the practice quietly assume the editions were never rival frameworks to begin with?

And if I did take the incommensurability worry seriously, what would have to change? Stop pooling, pool but refuse to report one trend, report a family of curves indexed to each edition, or turn the chapter into a history of the instruments rather than a claim about the world? I'd like you to go through my decisions one at a time and tell me which ones are smuggling in a philosophical claim I haven't earned. I have a committee meeting in three weeks and I'd rather be ambushed by you than by them. I don't want a Kuhn summary, I've read enough of that — I want the audit of the practice.
