I'm building a lecture on the paradox of tolerance around Robert Bolt's A Man for All Seasons, and I want to read the play against Popper's claim that unlimited tolerance brings about the disappearance of tolerance, and against Rawls's reply that the intolerant should be tolerated until the tolerant have sincere and reasonable grounds to believe that their own security and that of the institutions of liberty are endangered. Bolt's More refuses Roper's proposal to arrest a man who has broken no law, holds that he would give the Devil the benefit of the law for the sake of his own safety, shields Richard Rich from the consequences of his ambition, and is destroyed by all three when Cromwell converts his silence into an overt act through purchased perjury. He looks Rawlsian in practice and a Popperian cautionary tale in outcome. Whose position does the play actually dramatize, and is More's defeat a fair test of Rawls's proviso or an artifact of corruption rather than of tolerance as such? More treats his procedural scruple as both a prudential wager and a matter of personal identity, and the play never settles which is doing the work — does that ambiguity let it have the argument both ways? Finally, the historical More enforced heresy law as Lord Chancellor, with Protestants burned during his tenure, and all of this stays offstage in Bolt: is that a distortion I should flag for students, or does the omission do real argumentative work by isolating tolerance from its sixteenth-century limits? Please stay faithful to both the play and the philosophy, and end by saying what Bolt's dramatization shows about who pays the cost of tolerance that an exchange conducted at the level of institutions cannot show.
