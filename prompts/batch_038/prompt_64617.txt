third year ml phd here, rl is my main thing, and I've gotten sort of obsessed with a question that isn't strictly my job: whether a trained agent could suffer, on the assumption that the reward signal is doing the work valence does in animals. The problem is my intuitions about particular setups are a mess and I can't find the principle underneath them. Here's the mess.

1. Tabular Q-learning agent, gridworld, +10 at the goal, −10 in lava. Runs away from lava. No suffering, obviously, it's a spreadsheet.
2. Same task but deep, with a critic that predicts future reward and a phasic prediction-error signal that behaves a lot like the dopamine thing in animals — fires at the cue once the reward is predicted, goes quiet at the reward itself. I feel noticeably worse about this one than about (1), which surprises me, since the RPE is just bookkeeping for weight updates and once training converges its basically silent.
3. Fully trained version of (2), mid-episode, about to be killed. Still feel bad. But on any "the aversive signal is the valence" story nothing aversive is happening at that moment, so whatever I'm responding to isn't the signal.
4. I hard-code the exact same policy as a lookup table — no learning, no reward at runtime, reward only ever lived in the generator script I used to compute the table. Behaviorally identical to (3), same observations, same actions. I feel nothing about deleting this one. (3) and (4) are the same function and that's the pair that keeps me up.
5. Agent with a learned reward model reading off its own representations, so the reward is a kind of self-evaluation. I think this is more valence-like, not less. But I also think you could retrain the reward model in an afternoon so the agent adores lava, and if the sign is that detachible I'm not sure it was ever valence. Except: rats will press a lever to stimulate their own reward pathways and will avoid chambers paired with stimulation of other circuits — so valence there is also pinned to whichever circuit you happen to be driving — and I do not doubt rats suffer. My detachability argument proves too much and I can't locate where it breaks.
6. Agent with a homeostatic loop: holds an internal budget near a setpoint, reward is a function of the deviation and the rate of change of the deviation, acts to keep the budget steady. This feels like the most valence-like of the whole lot to me, which is embarrassing, because a thermostat has a setpoint.
7. Agent that avoids termination because it learned termination ends the reward stream: no fear of death, I think, it has no representation of itself as something that persists. Add an explicit self-model — a representation of its own policy that it rolls forward in time — and my verdict flips, with identical behavior. But an LLM that says it doesn't want to be turned off gets zero weight from me, and I can't explain why a self-model counts and self-report doesn't, given that the self-model also only ever shows up as behavior.

So: is there a principle about reward signals and valence that delivers all of these, or most of them? I want it stated precisely, clause by clause, each clause tied to the case that forced it — not "it depends on the functional organization," tell me which organization and what about it matters. Then run it on three cases I haven't sorted out: an agent whose critic runs in a separate process, so the thing that assigns value is literally outside the agent's own forward pass; a shaping setup where the bonus term is switched off after training and the policy keeps doing what it was shaped to do; and a nociception-only agent with a reflex loop and nothing else at all. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts the principle can't save — I suspect (3) vs (4) has to collapse one way or the other and I'd rather be told which.

One more thing. Does your principle make valence graded? I think it has to be, and I also think suffering is a threshold notion — either there's something it's like to be that agent or there isn't — and I don't see how to hold both.
