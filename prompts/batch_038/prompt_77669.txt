I'm a retired field ecologist — thirty-some years doing vegitation monitoring for a state agency — and since I hung up the boots I've been auditing philosophy of science seminars and reading on causation. Next month I'm giving a talk at a university reading group on causal inference: mostly philosophers of biology who've read Pearl's popular books but not the technical work, two practicing statisticians (one Bayesian, one who's spent her career in clinical trials), and a couple of science-studies types who suspect the whole causal-inference enterprise is a story about who gets to count.

My claim is that the standard line on Simpson's paradox — draw the graph, condition on the confounder, the disaggregated table is the true one — doesn't actually settle the question that mattered to people like me in the field, because "the" effect of a treatment isn't defined until you fix a target population, and the conditioning move quietly assumes a transportability you usually have no evidence for.

The dataset I'm using is an old roadside-restoration trial of my own: two seed mixes, establishment success, split by site moisture.

- dry sites: mix X established in 250 of 500 plots; mix Y in 45 of 100
- wet sites: mix X in 45 of 50; mix Y in 160 of 200

X wins in both strata but loses overall (54% vs 68%), because X got planted mostly on the dry sites — an artifact of the order the highway dept rolled things out.

Two-minute answer for each, plus a note on where my answer is soft and what I should concede up front. Also tell me the one I'm praying nobody raises.

And for each item, be blunt about which parts are settled and which are live disputes among people who know this literature properly — I don't want to walk into that room asserting a consensus that doesn't exist.
