For a senior research paper I am working on how self-driving cars are regulated, and one feature of the practice keeps troubling me. Jurisdictions that permit autonomous-vehicle testing generally require a licensed human backup operator in the car, whose job is to monitor a system designed precisely so that it does not need monitoring; the regulators and companies involved concede that a person cannot reliably stay alert to a system that performs well for long stretches, and some firms now let a single remote operator supervise several vehicles at once. Then, when one of these test vehicles struck and killed a pedestrian, the criminal case was brought against the human backup operator, and the company that designed and deployed the system was not charged. My working label for the arrangement, which I do not believe comes from anywhere in the literature, is an accountability sink: a person installed inside the system mainly so that blame has somewhere definite to land. What theory of moral responsibility does this practice presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the arrangement strictly requires from accounts it merely happens to fit, and say which rival views of blame it rules out — for example, that fair blame tracks only what an agent could actually have controlled, or that responsibility is really about who profits from a risk and who should absorb its costs. I would also like to know whether the two halves of the practice, the monitoring requirement and the charging decision, presuppose the same theory or different ones, and what would have to change in how these vehicles are regulated if the implicit view were replaced by a strictly control-based one.
