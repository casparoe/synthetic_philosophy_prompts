In American criminal procedure an interrogator may lie to a suspect as a matter of routine. He may say that an accomplice has already confessed, that a fingerprint or DNA sample matched, that the case is closed and the only open question is the suspect's willingness to help himself. Courts have long held that deception of this sort does not by itself make a confession involuntary or inadmissible; what matters is the totality of the circumstances, and only tactics that push toward that threshold — explicit promises of leniency, threats, and in some jurisdictions the fabrication of documents rather than mere assertions — are policed. The system runs the other way for the person in the chair. Making a false statement to a federal investigator is a standalone felony requiring no oath and no proof that the lie altered anything, since a statement counts as material if it was merely capable of influencing the agency. A defendant who testifies and lies faces perjury exposure and a routine sentencing enhancement for obstruction. Prosecutors may argue to the jury that his lies are affirmative evidence of his guilt. He has a right not to speak at all, which the state may not comment on, but no right to speak falsely. And inside the courtroom the asymmetry flips again: a prosecutor has an affirmative constitutional duty to correct testimony he knows to be false, even when the falsehood came from his own witness and helps his case. A growing number of states have recently banned deceptive interrogation tactics altogether where the suspect is a minor.

What theory of the duty to tell the truth does this pattern presuppose? I keep coming back to Kant's 1797 reply to Constant, in which he refuses precisely the definition the practice seems to run on: a lie "defined merely as an intentionally untruthful declaration to another man does not require the additional condition that it must do harm to another, as jurists require in their definition." Is the interrogation room that jurists' definition made institutional — a false statement wrongs only someone entitled to the truth, and the suspect, by his situation, is not entitled? Or does the practice rest on something else: an instrumental epistemology on which truth is a resource the state extracts rather than a duty persons owe one another; a role-morality view on which veracity binds the citizen and not the office; a view about who bears the burdens of an openly adversarial encounter?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, since I suspect several incompatible accounts license it equally happily. Then take up three further questions. First, does the arrangement amount to a reversal of Kant's own example — the state occupying the position of the visitor at the door who arguably has no right to the answer he demands, and the suspect occupying the position of the householder whom Kant nevertheless forbids to lie? If so, can anyone who accepts the verdict in that case coherently endorse the practice; or does Kant's insistence that a lie violates a duty to oneself condemn both parties' lies and leave the legal asymmetry with no Kantian ground at all? Second, is the pattern internally consistent: can one system hold that silence may not be penalized, lying may be penalized, the state may lie freely in the precinct, and the state must correct lies in the courtroom? Is there a single principle that explains all four, or only a pair of principles in uneasy adjacency? Third, tell me what would actually have to change if the presupposed theory were replaced — by a Kantian one, by a straightforwardly consequentialist one, or by a view on which veracity is owed to persons but suspended where the relationship is acknowledged as adversarial — and whether the recent bans on deceiving juvenile suspects look like the application of a principle or an ad hoc concession to the risk of false confessions.
