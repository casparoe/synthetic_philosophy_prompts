I am drafting the methodological chapter of a doctoral thesis on the attribution of responsibility in the historiography of the Cold War, and my supervisor has asked me to commit my own position to paper in explicit form before we discuss it. What follows is what I have written down. These commitments have survived several drafts and I believe I hold them all simultaneously, but I have no confidence that they cohere, and I would rather find out from an audit than from an examination.

1. Blame requires counterfactual dependence: a state, or a statesman acting for it, is to blame for the Cold War only if, had they acted otherwise within the range of options realistically open to them, the Cold War would not have occurred.
2. The structural conditions of the immediate post-war years — the collapse of the European balance of power, the vacuum at the centre of the continent, the absence of any countervailing third force — overdetermined a bipolar confrontation. Any administration in Washington and any leadership in Moscow confronting those conditions would have arrived at something recognisably the same conflict.
3. Historians should nonetheless pass judgement on particular decisions by particular statesmen, and should not treat structural explanation as a licence to decline such judgements.
4. Collective agents — states, and perhaps blocs — can bear responsibility that is not merely the aggregated responsibility of their members.
5. An agent is blameworthy only where a genuine alternative was open to them. I want no part of a practice of blame that condemns people for what they could not have avoided.
6. Historical counterfactuals should be evaluated by holding fixed the more entrenched background conditions and varying only what is local and discretionary: the nearby worlds are those in which the structures stay put and the choices differ.
7. The question 'who started the Cold War' is malformed. It presumes that the conflict has a determinate beginning and two principal agents, and it thereby erases both the gradual, multi-year hardening of expectations and the agency of Britain, France, the states of Eastern Europe, and China.
8. Moral criticism of the period's statesmen must not be presentist: they are to be assessed against norms they themselves could have recognised, including the public commitments to self-determination and to the renunciation of aggression enshrined in the wartime and post-war settlements.
9. Responsibility in a conflict between two principal parties is not zero-sum. Establishing that one side bears a share does not diminish the share borne by the other, and the shares need not be equal.

What I want is an internal audit of this set, not a survey of the historiographical debate and not references, since I have those to hand. Specifically: identify which pairs or clusters genuinely pull against one another and which merely appear to, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list of worries. For each genuine tension, state the cheapest revision that would remove it and what that revision costs elsewhere in the set — in particular, whether it forces me to abandon the binary character of (1) or the discriminating power of (4). Where you think a tension dissolves once a commitment is re-scoped rather than rejected, give me the re-scoped wording, because I suspect that is where most of the work lies and I cannot at present tell whether such re-scoping is a solution or a retreat. Finally, tell me which single revision would repair the most, and which commitment is load-bearing in the sense that the rest of the set cannot be saved without it. I have no advance attachment to any item, and a blunt diagnosis is more useful to me than a diplomatic summary.
