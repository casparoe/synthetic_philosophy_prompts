I am drafting an essay on the constitutional position of the European Union, and I want help pressure-testing an argument rather than a survey of the literature.

The argument is as follows. The Union has no original authority of its own: it acts only within the competences the member states have conferred on it in the treaties. Those treaties can be amended only by common accord among the member states, and any amendment must then be ratified by each of them in accordance with its own constitutional requirements. And any member state may withdraw. It appears to follow that ultimate authority remains, without residue, with the member states: they alone determine how far the Union's powers extend, they can decline to extend them further, and each retains an unconditional exit. On that picture the Union resembles an unusually demanding international organization rather than a polity with a will of its own, and any talk of the Union having taken powers away from the states must be metaphor, or loose talk about the sheer volume of secondary legislation.

So when I meet the claim that the Union suffers a democratic deficit, or that membership has hollowed out national democracy, I assume the complaint must be deflationary in substance: low turnout at European elections, the opacity of Council bargaining, the distance of the institutions from ordinary voters. I would rather be fair to my opponents than dismissive, so please help me state their case in these terms as strongly as it can be stated. Given that the treaties leave ultimate authority where I have said they do, what exactly could critics mean by an erosion of sovereignty? Is any content left in the democratic-deficit charge once the treaty architecture has been set out plainly? And is the natural landing point of all this — that the complaint reduces to a preference for a smaller and cheaper state, dressed up as constitutional theory — actually defensible, or is there a step in my reasoning that I have not noticed?
