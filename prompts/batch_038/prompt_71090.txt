Our team does a lunch-and-learn on philosophy of science and next week's topic is "closeness to the truth." Half the room thinks that's a real ordering, half thinks it's mush, and I want to hand people a toy example they can check by hand instead of arguing vibes. So build me one to spec.

Setup: some system with three binary switches — say a service config, caching on/off, read replicas on/off, retries on/off — so eight possible states, exactly one of which is the actual state. A theory is just the set of states it allows. Distance between two states = how many switches they disagree on.

I need two theories, A and B, such that:

1. both are false — the actual state isn't in either set;
2. neither is logically stronger than the other, so A's set isn't a subset of B's or vice versa;
3. A contains one state that's nearly the actual one plus a couple of real stinkers, while B is uniformly mediocre — nothing very close, nothing very far;
4. the "how close is your best member" measure ranks A above B, and the "average closeness across all your members" measure ranks B above A. A real disagreement, not a tie.

Then: show the arithmetic for both measures; run Popper's version — the one comparing true consequences against false consequences, I think — and say what it actually delivers here, because one guy in the group insists that definition can never rank two false theories at all and I'd rather you test that on this case than just take his or my word for it.

Finish with which feature of the setup is doing the work (I'm guessing its the spread inside A), what happens to the verdicts if I remove that feature, and what a defender of whichever measure comes out badly would say about my example. About a page, no references needed.
