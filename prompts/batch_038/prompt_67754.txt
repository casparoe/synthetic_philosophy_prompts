I am a novelist, and I am about eighteen months into a book whose centre of gravity is a woman in her forties who ceased to be a Christian in her twenties, has never gone back, and nonetheless still takes her place in the pew on Sunday morning, still had her children christened, still fasts on the appointed days, still says grace at table in front of company. I built her out of a long season of reading George Eliot — the 1846 translation of Strauss that came out without her name on it, the 1854 translation of Feuerbach that did not, the novels, the correspondence — and out of two or three people I have actually known. My problem is that I cannot describe her position to anybody, including my agent, without either flattering it or flattening it, because I do not have the vocabulary for what she is doing. I am hoping that you do, and that you will supply it.

Three scenes from the manuscript, so that you have something concrete rather than a thesis.

First. Her brother-in-law says to her over the christening, so you do not believe a word of this and yet here you are, expecting either a confession of hypocrisy or a defence of the church as a social institution. She gives him neither. She says that the creed, taken as an account of how the world is, is false, that she has known it to be false for twenty years, and that this has nothing whatever to do with why she is standing there; and when he asks her what she supposes the rite to be saying instead, she tells him she cannot say, because the rite is not the sort of thing that says something, and any sentence she offered in its place — including a sentence she would gladly agree to — would be a worse rendering of it than the water is.

Second. Her daughter asks whether she ought to correct the school's assumption that they are a religious household. The mother says no, let them go on thinking as they like, and does not consider this a lie; but she would not sign a confession of faith, and she would not let anyone count her among those entitled to a vote in the congregation's affairs. Her silence runs in one direction only, and she can give no account of why that direction rather than the other is the honest one.

Third. She refuses to set up a practice of her own: no secular Sunday assembly, no invented ceremony at the table, no reading group that does the same work in different clothes. Her reason is that the whole point of the old rite is that she did not make it and it does not answer to her; anything she authored would be a prolongation of her opinions, and what she is trying to do with her body is something her opinions cannot do.

So here is what I want from you. Is there a name for this position — not for the bare fact of an unbeliever in the pews, which I know is common enough, but for the specific claim she is making about it, namely that the rite carries a content which cannot be restated as any proposition at all, true or false, so that her disbelief is beside the point and her participation is neither dishonest nor useful-but-calculated? I want to be careful about three things she is emphatically not doing, because everybody keeps collapsing her into one of them: she is not going in the hope that the practice will restore belief; she is not going for the sake of the community, the inheritance, or the children, and would go on going if she were the last person alive who did; and she is not treating the doctrines as noble stories that she consents to act out for their effects, since that would imply she can hold them at arm's length and adopt them deliberately, which is precisely what she denies.

Work as a philosopher of religion who also reads nineteenth-century literature closely, and think carefully before you commit to anything. Begin with a brainstorming pass: list every candidate concept, distinction, and tradition you can think of that touches this configuration — including ones from outside the European Christian frame, since I suspect the thing I have described may be better named somewhere else — and only then sort them. For each candidate that survives the first pass, tell me what it actually claims, and then tell me exactly where my character falls outside it. I am not after a reading list or a bibliography; I want the conceptual work done in your own words.

Then give me a verdict. Which of the candidates comes nearest, what precisely is left over that none of them covers, and is that residue substantial enough to deserve its own name? If it is, coin one: a name, a definition of no more than three sentences, and the two or three cases that sit just outside it, so that I can test whether my character belongs inside the concept or is meant to be a borderline instance of it. If it is not — if she is simply an unstable compound of things already named — say so plainly and tell me which compound she is.

Two further things I need, and I need them argued rather than asserted. First, the objection: what is the strongest case that her position is incoherent, that kneeling and eating and being carried to the font do assert something whether or not the person doing them intends it, and that she is therefore either a believer in bad faith or an evader in good faith? Is that objection fatal to her, or can she answer it, and if she can answer it, what does the answer cost her? Second, the Eliot question: my working reading is that Eliot's own unbelievers keep the moral sentiment and let the doctrine go, but that she does treat the doctrine as translatable content — the poetry in which a moral truth was historically expressed, and which can in principle be rendered back into prose — and that my character's flat refusal to translate is therefore a step further than Eliot was willing to go. Tell me whether that reading is right. If it is wrong, tell me where, and tell me whether Eliot anywhere allows for a residue in the rite that resists restatement, because if she does, my book is in more trouble than I thought.

Organise the answer in four headed sections: the shortlist and what each entry excludes; the verdict and the boundary of the concept; the objection and whether it is fatal; the Eliot question. Where you are unsure, say so and say what would settle it, rather than filling the gap with confident invention.
