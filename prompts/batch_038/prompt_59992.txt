So some background first: I have zero econ training. I do replenishment analytics for a regional grocery wholesaler, which is a fancy way of saying I spend my days inside a system that decides how many cases of paper towels go to which warehouse and what the internal "cost" of a truckload is, and it does that for something like 40,000 items with no market anywhere inside it. Nobody bids on anything. There's a demand forecast, a solver, a few hundred humans overriding both, and a spreadsheet that everyone is scared of. And it works, mostly. Which is why I got so stuck last month when a friend of mine started going on about that old argument, the one where Mises and Hayek say a planned economy can't do economic calculation at all because there are no genuine prices for the means of production, and then Oskar Lange comes back and says of course it can, you set up a Central Planning Board that announces prices, tells the managers to produce at minimum cost and to expand output until marginal cost equals the price, and then if there's a surplus the board lowers the price and if there's a shortage it raises it, over and over, like an auctioneer feeling its way around. My friend's verdict was that Lange got destroyed, because the knowledge you actually need is tacit and scattered across millions of heads and no board can ever collect it. And I keep sitting at my desk thinking: my employer collects it. Every barcode scan is logged, every truck has a little box in it, the demand for a particular SKU in Peoria on a Tuesday is not tacit in the slightest, it's in a database before lunch.

Here's what I want help with. Put Lange in a chair in a warehouse in 2025, show him my day job, and tell me what he actually says — not "he'd be delighted," I want the specific commitments. Three things I keep going around on. First: are the numbers my system spits out the prices his board was supposed to be searching for? Nobody can trade at them, half of them are internal accounting conventions or safety-stock rules dressed up as costs, and the ones that do face the outside world get set by a pricing tool aimed at a margin target rather than by any clearing process. So is my employer a vindication of his model, or is it something he'd have to admit he didn't anticipate — an allocation mechanism that doesn't need prices even in shadow form, which would mean revising his own answer rather than winning the argument? Second: his board iterates toward an equilibrium. My system never converges to anything, it re-solves overnight against a rolling thirteen-week forecast and gets thrown out and rebuilt the next night, and the not-settling seems to be part of why it survives. Would that distinction matter to him at all, or is it bookkeeping? Third, and this is the hole in my own side that I want you to press on rather than paper over: my data records what people bought, never what they wanted and couldn't get. When we stock out, the sales figure collapses toward zero, the model reads that as weak demand, and it doubles down on under-ordering — exactly backwards. We call it censored demand internally and we patch it with ugly stuff, like asking the store manager what they think they sold out of, or deliberately holding back inventory on a few stores to watch what happens. So there is a real chunk of economic knowledge that no scanner will ever see, because it's a fact about the world that didn't occur. Is that a version of the dispersed-knowledge objection that survives total digitization, and does Lange have to concede it, or is he entitled to say that's just a measurement problem for an engineer and not a refutation of anything?

And I know the comeback that's waiting for me: that's not socialism, it's a private firm, it's disciplined by competition outside its own walls and by the possibility of going under, so it proves nothing about an economy with no outside. I get the force of that. But I also watch the boundary move constantly — we used to buy trucking from third parties and then bought a fleet and now plan it internally, and the reverse happened with the in-store bakeries, we used to make the bread and now we buy it. Whether an activity sits inside the plan or in the market looks like a management decision that gets relitigated every few years on the basis of spreadsheets, not a fixed fact about human coordination. So "it only works because it's small enough" doesn't look like a limit to me, it looks like a dial. Would Lange have gone down that road, and if so how far does he think the dial turns before something actually breaks — and is what breaks computational, or is it something else? Related: the part of my job that genuinely fails is never the allocation, it's deciding what we're even optimizing for. Service level versus inventory dollars versus shrink versus not annoying the store managers — the target gets handed down to me and there is no data argument I can make against it, no mechanism that discovers a wrong objective the way a market supposedly discovers a wrong price, it just quietly costs us money for two years until someone's bonus suffers. Is that where you'd say the old objection lands after all, or is that management politics and not economics, and would Lange have drawn that line the same way? Does he get to say he was right, or is there something specific about what I've described that he'd have to update on?
