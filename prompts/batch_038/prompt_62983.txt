For three years I have been the unofficial arbiter of a cohousing community of about forty households, which means that whenever a resident insists a promise was made, an apology was given, a resignation was delivered, or a house rule was waived, the argument eventually arrives at my kitchen table and I have to rule on whether the thing they say happened actually happened. I am not a philosopher, though I have read enough Austin to know that people distinguish cases in which the intended act is simply void from cases in which it goes through even though the speaker was in no position to honor it. What I want is the principle behind my own rulings, in my own terms — not a survey of the literature and not a list of things to read. The dispute that made me start keeping a log was about a kayak, which is not important except that it produced the first entry.

My verdicts, in the order I recorded them. A resident says flatly, without looking up from his phone, that he will do the dishes tonight, and does not: broken promise, no dispute. The same resident, on another night, says in the same flat tone that he hereby promises to do the dishes, and does not: I ruled this worse, not merely equivalent, and I cannot say why the extra words add anything. A guest at a party says, plainly joking, that he promises to name his firstborn after his friend: no promise, I say; but a different man says lightly, half-distracted, that sure, he will help with the move on Saturday, and never appears: I do hold that against him, and on paper the two look alike to me. Two members negotiate a wager over the group chat, one proposes a stake, the other answers only that they are on, and the stake is never confirmed: I called it a bet. Two others shake hands over a beer and the loser later claims he was jesting: also a bet. So neither the explicit stake nor the handshake seems to be doing the work. Someone says he is sorry that the complainant feels that way: not an apology, I ruled. Someone else says he is sorry, in open contempt, naming no act: also not an apology, and this is the verdict I am least able to defend, since the words seem right and even the acknowledgment of a wrong seems present.

Here is the pair that keeps me up. A resignation emailed to a manager who never opens it, while the sender keeps working and keeps being paid: I ruled she never resigned. A marriage performed by an officiant whose credentials had lapsed, with both parties believing themselves married and living as spouses for eleven years: I ruled they were never married. Two voids, and consistent with each other. But when a visitor christened our community's new boat a minute before the member designated to do it, I ruled the boat unnamed; and when a visiting priest performed a baptism he had no authority to perform, and the family and the congregation accepted it, I ruled the baptism valid. I cannot tell you what differs between the boat and the font. Related: a manager with no authority to discipline anyone says that if you are late again he will write you up — I called that a threat, not a warning; a stranger at the pond who wants you to go through the ice says that it is thin past the point — I called that a warning, though he meant harm. And on pressure: a patient who signs a consent form unread and says whatever, I treated as consenting; a hostess who says of course, come in to a guest she dreads, I treated as consenting; but a person who says I do to a partner who controls all the money and has threatened to leave her penniless, I treated as not married; and a defendant who pleads out to avoid a longer sentence, I treated as validly consenting, though the pressure there is plainly worse. Finally, two I handled alone: I said that forgiving someone aloud in an empty room, to a person who never apologized and never will hear it, is real forgiveness in an utterance that did nothing; and I said that breaking a promise made to yourself breaks a promise, though I cannot name the party wronged.

My own attempt at a rule is that an utterance binds when the speaker would rightly be blamed for not following through. It gets the dishes and the move and the wager, but it seems to fail the contemptuous apology, where nobody owes any follow-through; the boat, where the christener is not blamed for anything; and the baptism, where the priest is not blamed either. So: state one principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precise enough that I could hand it to a stranger and get the same rulings out of them. Tie each clause to the case in my log that motivates it, tell me what it says about cases I have not listed — a toast raised to absent friends, an I take responsibility issued in a press statement, an oath sworn on a book the swearer does not believe in, a house rule waived in a group chat that nobody reads — and tell me honestly which of my recorded verdicts it cannot save. If a simpler principle would cover everything except one of them, say which verdict I should give up, and why that one is the cheapest to lose.
