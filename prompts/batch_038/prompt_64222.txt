So I've been keeping a list of my gut reactions to rescue cases and I can't get them to line up. I'd like help working out what principle, if any, they add up to. To be clear I'm not after a tour of the literature — I know there's a famous paper arguing you should flip a coin, I don't want a summary of it. I want to know what my own verdicts commit me to.

The list:

1. Six cavers, water rising, one door. One person trapped in the left chamber, five in the right. I can open one. All strangers to me. → I flip a coin. And I really don't feel like opening the right-hand door would be the better choice, which suprises me.
2. Same setup, but the one is my brother. → I save my brother, and I don't think I've done anything wrong. Nobody could reasonably hold it against me.
3. Same setup, but the five went in past a warning sign and the one didn't. → Now I lean toward the one, but only a little. A thumb on the scale, not a verdict.
4. A machine is about to give five people paper cuts. I can stop that, or stop it giving one person a paper cut, not both. → Stop the five. No coin flip, no agonizing. This one feels like it has a straight answer.
5. Five people each about to be blinded, versus one. → Coin flip again. So the switch flips somewhere between a paper cut and blindness, and I have no idea where it flips or why it would flip there.
6. Five thousand mild headaches versus one death. → Save the one who'd die. No pile of headaches gets there.
7. A 10% chance of averting a hundred deaths versus a certainty of averting nine. → I take the 10%. This is the one I keep coming back to, because in case 1 the numbers did absolutely nothing and here they're doing everything, and the only difference I can point at is that one is certain and the other isn't.
8. A policy that kills one identifiable kid, with a name and a face, next month, versus one that kills a hundred anonymous people over fifty years through slightly dirtier air. → For the hundred I just do the arithmetic and I don't feel bad about it. For one kid in a well versus five kids in another well I'd flip a coin. Structurally the same pair of choices, opposite reactions.

My own tentative explanation is that it turns on whether the people are interchangeable — with paper cuts they somehow are, with deaths they aren't, each one is a whole world on their own. But 7 and 8 both cut against that and I cant repair it.

What I want back: one principle, or a shortlist of two or three, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I've never seen without checking with you. For each clause, tell me which numbered case forced it in. Then give me its verdicts on four cases I haven't listed — include at least one where what's at stake is how long people get to live rather than whether, and one where the many are only in danger because of the choice itself. And be honest about which of my verdicts it has to overrule. If it manages to keep all eight, tell me what that's costing in complexity, because my suspicion is that it's costing a lot.
