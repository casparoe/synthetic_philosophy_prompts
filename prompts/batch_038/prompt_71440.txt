I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm on deadline for a long feature about the afterlife of the metaverse pitch — not the 2021–22 hype cycle, which I wrote about at the time and now find embarrassing to reread, but the quieter second wave, in which the interesting question is no longer whether the technology will arrive but whether anything inside it counts as happening at all. My editor wants the piece anchored in a work of fiction rather than in vendor decks and investor decks' cousins, the analyst reports, and every writer on this beat reaches for Neal Stephenson's Snow Crash, published in 1992 and cited so many times in technology journalism that it has become a kind of secular scripture for the genre. I would like to do better than the usual "Stephenson predicted it" gesture. What I actually want is a serious philosophical reading of the novel, and I'm hoping you can give me one.

The framework I keep circling has three positions on whether anything in a virtual environment is real, and I would like the novel tested against all three rather than against whichever is most convenient. The first is the virtual realism David Chalmers argues for in Reality+: virtual objects are not fake objects but real ones made of a different kind of stuff, where the stuff is computational process; on this view a virtual sword has genuine causal powers inside its world, and what befalls people there can genuinely befall them. The second is an artifactualist ontology of roughly the kind Amie Thomasson defends for fictional characters: the contents of the Metaverse are real, but real as abstract, intention-dependent artifacts brought into being and sustained by human practices — real in something like the way a character in a novel is real, which is a considerably thinner mode of being than Chalmers allows. The third is a fictionalist or pretense-based view, close to the game-studies intuition that play occurs inside a bounded sphere with its own constitutive rules: within the Metaverse nothing is strictly true, and talk about it is a series of moves in a game. I would also like the ethics brought to bear, in particular the gamer's dilemma Morgan Luck set out in 2009, which presses on whether an act performed in a virtual environment can be wrongful when no actual person is thereby harmed — and on how much of the surrounding literature quietly assumes that harm requires a flesh-and-blood victim.

So: read Snow Crash through that. What is the novel's own ontology? Think about the Metaverse's Street and the Black Sun club; Hiro's double life as a man with a house and standing in the Metaverse who is, physically, a tenant of a rented storage locker; the Librarian, which is a daemon rather than a person and is treated as neither quite a tool nor quite an interlocutor; and the fact that the avatars and environments themselves are rendered by code written by people very like Hiro. Does Stephenson write as though these are things with causal powers, as the virtual realist wants, or as images and representations, as the fictionalist does? Then the crux, which I cannot resolve on my own: the engine of the plot is a datafile that crashes both the machine and the mind of the hacker who views it, which is to say the novel makes virtual harm real only by routing it through the physical brain. Is that a concession to the anti-realist — an admission that nothing merely virtual can hurt you — or does it leave the metaphysics untouched? And is there any injury in the book that stays inside the Metaverse and is nevertheless treated as serious: loss of standing, the destruction of an avatar, theft of property, exposure of a secret? I also want to know where the reading breaks. My suspicion is that the Sumerian-as-firmware material and the Babel thread are doing something else entirely — a satire about language, information, and who gets to program a culture — and that forcing them into a debate about virtual objects would flatten the book; tell me plainly if I'm wrong about that. Finally, say what you think Snow Crash gets right about virtual worlds that the philosophy misses, point me to two or three scenes I could write around (no quotations, please, and no bibliography — I've read the scholarship and I want your reading, not a reading list), and give me a verdict on whether the ending vindicates or undercuts the virtual realist.
