Impossibility theorems in algorithmic fairness get cited in policy debate as though they closed the question — the mathematics supposedly shows you cannot have both calibration and equal error rates, so further argument is pointless. I am writing a methods section on when that move is legitimate, and I want the specification first: the conditions a no-go result of this kind must satisfy before it can bear policy weight at all.

State each requirement, the consideration behind it, and a concrete way in which a formally correct theorem could still fail it — proving incompatibility only at a shared threshold when group-specific thresholds remain legally available; adopting a criterion no regulator, plaintiff, or engineer actually endorses; ruling out randomization by stipulation rather than by argument; showing a conflict that is possible in principle but negligible in the case at hand; leaving the group partition arbitrary so that the result can be made to appear or disappear by redrawing the groups; or deriving the contradiction from labels that are themselves artifacts of the decision rule being evaluated.

Then tell me which requirements you regard as non-negotiable and which are contestable, and whether some plausible subset of them is itself jointly unsatisfiable — that is, whether a result strong enough to carry the policy conclusion may be unobtainable in principle. Close with four diagnostic questions a non-specialist decision-maker can put to any impossibility claim. Maximum 800 words; a table is acceptable for the requirements.
