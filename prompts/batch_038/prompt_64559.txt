I'm a staff software engineer, about fifteen years in, mostly backend and distributed systems, and I have no philosophy training beyond podcasts and blog posts. A few weeks ago, after our company was acquired and the payout was announced, a teammate and I got into a long argument in the parking garage. My position was that the split was outrageous. His was that outrageousness is not the kind of thing you can read off a split at all: if every step that produced it was voluntary, the result is just however lopsided it turns out, and looking at the outcome to judge it is a category error. He said this came from a philosopher named Nozick, and something about a basketball star who collected a quarter from each of a million fans and ended up rich while everyone who paid was glad to. I have that secondhand and probably slightly wrong. Rather than argue with him, I did what I usually do when I don't understand a system: I wrote down test cases from my own industry and recorded my honest gut verdict on each, and now I want to know what principle actually generates my verdicts, whether it is the one my teammate described, and if it isn't, which of my verdicts I would have to abandon to get something simple and coherent.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A former colleague wrote an MIT-licensed library on weekends. A company built a paid enterprise product on top of it; he joined briefly, left, did no work on it for eight years, and walked away with roughly $40M at the acquisition. Verdict: fine, entirely fine.
2. A man registered a generic single-word domain in 1996 for $35, built nothing on it ever, and sold it in 2015 for $2M. Verdict: not fine. Every transaction was voluntary, and I'll admit he created as little as my colleague did after year one — yet I feel completely differently about the two, and I can't say why.
3. A researcher finds a critical vulnerability in our product and reports it to us; we pay a $50k bounty. Verdict: fine, in fact admirable. The same researcher, with the same find, instead sells the exploit to a broker who resells it to a government that uses it against dissidents. No contract broken, no fraud, no force, every step signed. Verdict: not fine, and not merely imprudent. From the inside the two chains look identical, and my verdicts don't.
4. A teammate was laid off when a competitor's product displaced ours. She was excellent and did nothing wrong, and she is much worse off now. Verdict: the competitor owes her nothing, but our company owed her more than the legal minimum it gave her. I'm not sure those two halves are consistent.
5. My own salary. Almost everything I can produce depends on standards, papers, algorithms, and code written by people who were paid nothing for it and whom I could not identify if I tried. My marginal contribution in a world without that inheritance would be a small fraction of my compensation. Verdict: not fine that I keep all of it — but I cannot name a creditor, and I don't want it confiscated either. This is the case I most want a principled way to hold.
6. Our founder raised money in year one, kept 30%, contributed nearly nothing after year three, and took the largest share of the exit. Verdict: not fine. But he negotiated it in the open, everyone could read the cap table, and I signed.
7. My grandmother left her house to my cousin and not to me, for reasons I consider arbitrary. Verdict: fine, no complaint whatsoever. Large inherited wealth compounding over three generations: not fine. I cannot locate the line or justify its position.
8. Someone who bought a token on a whim in 2017 and is now wealthy. Verdict: fine for him to keep it, but I don't think he gets to complain if the rules are changed prospectively — which I suspect contradicts cases 1 and 7.
9. Taxes. I pay a lot and I don't think it's theft. But I also don't think the state is owed any particular share as such: it depends what it does with the money and how much everyone else at my income is paying. Verdict: a mess, and I know it.

My tentative principle, in the only vocabulary I really have, is that legitimacy is something you verify the way you verify a signed build artifact: you walk backwards from the current holder through the provenance chain, and each link must be either an original acquisition that left nobody worse off than they were before it, or a transfer that both parties entered knowingly and without force or fraud. If the chain verifies, you accept the artifact without inspecting what it does or how much of it there is; if a link fails, the holding fails. The obvious problem, and the reason I'm asking, is the case where the history has been squashed or rebased: you can see the current state clearly but the ancestry is unrecoverable, which seems to describe case 5 and possibly case 7. I don't know whether an unreadable chain should be treated as a failed verification, a passed one, or something else, and I don't know whether that decision is a technical detail or the whole argument. So: give me the principle or the small set of candidate principles my verdicts add up to, stated precisely enough that I could implement it as a check with defined inputs rather than as a slogan — and tell me which case motivates each clause. Then run it on four cases I haven't listed: a company that trains a model on publicly available text and code, including mine, and sells access to it; a person who bought a house whose value rose because of a transit line they did not pay for; an engineer who joined purely for the equity and resigned the week before the liquidity event; and a product that keeps users scrolling against their own repeatedly stated wishes, where every one of them accepted the terms of service. Finally, tell me honestly which of my nine verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and which it overturns — and whether the residue is evidence that my intuitions are simply inconsistent, or that the procedural view my teammate is defending leaves out something real that I am tracking badly. I would rather lose a verdict than keep a principle I cannot state.
