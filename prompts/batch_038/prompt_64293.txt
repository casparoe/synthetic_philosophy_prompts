I spent 38 years doing experimental physics — mostly counting very rare events in very large detectors — and since retiring I've been reading philosophy for pleasure, which is to say badly and slowly but with a lot of enjoyment. Right now I'm stuck on the miracles section of Hume's Enquiry, and something clicked for me in a way that makes me suspicious, because I've seen colleagues convince themselves that their own field is secretly the answer to everything.

Here's what clicked. When we saw an excess of events over the predicted background, nobody announced a discovery at three standard deviations. You looked for a mis-calibrated channel, a background you'd modelled wrong, a loose cable, a student who'd normalised to the wrong run. The excess only counted once it was strong enough that a fluke or an error would have been *more* surprising than the new physics — five sigma, roughly. And Hume's rule sounds like the same rule: take the testimony only if the testimony being false would be stranger than the event it reports.

So: is Hume's first part just our lab practice, written down nearly three centuries early by a man who never ran a control sample? Or is it something importantly different that I'm flattening out?
