My brother and I have been going back and forth on the two-envelope thing for over a year and we've got nowhere, so I want to see what a proper good-faith adversarial collaboration between us would actually produce.

He's in the camp that says it isn't a paradox at all: once you write down how the amounts were chosen, either the average amount is infinite — in which case "the other envelope is worth 25% more" is just an illegal comparison of infinities — or it's finite and the calculation comes out wrong. I'm in the camp that says that cleanup is too quick, because something real survives it.

To argue about concretely I built a spreadsheet version. The organizer flips a coin until it comes up tails and doubles the pot for every heads, then puts the pot in one envelope and double the pot in the other. With a fair coin, switching is exactly break-even at every amount you could possibly open. Bias the coin to land heads 2/3 of the time and switching is always 10% better. Either way, opening your envelope tells you nothing whatsoever about whether you're holding the small or the big one — the odds stay fixed (1/3 vs 2/3 fair, 0.4 vs 0.6 biased) no matter what number you see. I also think I proved to myself that an always-switch setup is impossible when the average amount is finite, since before anyone opens anything the two envelopes are identical by symmetry, so the average gain from switching has to be zero.
