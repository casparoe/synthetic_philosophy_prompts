I run the secretariat for a pooled fund — nine member orgs with genuinely incompatible value systems: a global health group, two animal welfare orgs that disagree with each other about wild-animal suffering, a longtermist biosecurity outfit, a Catholic charity, an indigenous land-rights group, a climate adaptation fund, a disability rights org, and a direct homelessness services provider. Quarterly we decide where the pooled money goes, and twice a year we decide whether the coalition signs onto a joint public statement. Three staff, about six hours of prep per cycle, no outside consultants, and the member board has to ratify whatever we bring them.

Everyone agrees in principle that there are gains from trade here: if I care way more than you about item X and you care way more than me about item Y, we should each get our way on our own item and both come out ahead by our own lights. In practice we've been doing it with a kludge I made up that I'll call the spread rule. Before each meeting every org rates every agenda item 0–10 for how much it matters to them, any item where top rating minus bottom rating is 6 or more gets declared tradeable, the top-rated org gets its way and logs a "credit" against whichever org rated it lowest, and credits get spent later on that orgs own priorities. It sort of works, in that nobody feels steamrolled, but it's clearly broken and I can't defend it to a new board member.

So: write me a decision procedure my team can actually run every quarter. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "no trade available, just vote on it," and where it has to hand the call to a human because the question is about values rather than facts. Ground it in whatever the theory of bargaining and moral compromise actually gives you that's usable — but it has to be runnable by non-specialists on a Tuesday afternoon, and I don't want a survey of the literature, I want steps.

It specifically needs to deal with:

- intensity. Does a 0–10 self-report mean anything at all across orgs? If not, what should we elicit instead — a budget, a sacrifice test, a walkaway price, something else — and how do we keep it from turning into a fake number anyway?
- strategic inflation. Any org that figures out the rule rates everything 10. What structural feature stops this, if any does?
- third parties who aren't at the table: the animals, people in 2200, our own donors. When does a trade that's Pareto-improving for all nine members make things worse overall, and can the procedure detect that or only flag it?
- complicity. Several members hold that abstaining on something they think is bad is fine, but actively enabling it isn't. Where exactly does the procedure draw that line, and what does it do when the line is contested?
- incommensurability. Do we need a common currency to run this at all, or can it work on purely ordinal information? I'd strongly prefer not to invent one, but tell me if that's a cop-out.

Then run the procedure on three cases from our last cycle:

1. An animal welfare org wants coalition money for a campaign against a rodenticide that's very widely used, on the grounds of secondary poisoning and suffering in wild rodent populations; the health and poverty members say the stuff prevents crop loss and rodent-borne disease in exactly the regions they fund in.
2. Our longtermist member wants the coalition to co-sign a statement urging a pause on certain gain-of-function research. Two members think the statement will cost lives by chilling ordinary virology and want no part of it, and one thinks signing is a moral trap they'd be complicit in either way.
3. The homelessness provider offers to throw its full support behind the climate adaptation item — which it's pretty lukewarm on — in exchange for the coalition dropping a shelter-eligibility condition (sobriety, I think) that it considers demeaning to its clients.

At least one of these should come out in a way I personally hate. Tell me which one, and defend why the procedure is still right to give that answer rather than being quietly adjusted until it says what I want.

Finish with a short section on what the procedure can't settle and where it will predictably misfire — including, honestly, whether we should keep any version of the credit ledger at all, or whether tracking favors is the part that's actually poisoning this.
