I work as a backend engineer; my only formal exposure to logic was a unit in a compilers course, though I have spent the last year reading about incompleteness and forcing on weekends — popular treatments plus the opening chapters of more serious texts. A recurring disagreement with two colleagues has left me unable to state what I actually believe. One of them insists the continuum hypothesis is simply true or false and that we are ignorant of which, in the same way we were once ignorant of Fermat's Last Theorem. The other insists it is not a genuine question at all — closer to asking whether the number of processes in a cluster is really even or odd once you refuse to fix a definition of process. My own reactions to particular independence results line up with neither position cleanly, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here are my verdicts. (1) The self-referential sentence of arithmetic asserting its own unprovability in Peano arithmetic: true, flatly. If it were false, PA would prove a falsehood, and I trust PA enough not to accept that. (2) Goodstein's theorem and the Paris–Harrington statement: unprovable in PA, provable in stronger systems — and I think both are true. I don't feel I am stipulating anything when I say so; I feel I am pointing at the actual natural numbers and noting how they behave. (3) The claim that ZFC is consistent: I believe it, but differently. If ZFC turned out inconsistent, I would say that claim had been false and that I had been wrong, not that the question had been empty. So there is a fact; I am just less sure I have hold of it. (4) CH: I cannot reproduce that feeling. When I try to picture the collection of all subsets of the natural numbers, I get no grip that could decide anything, and knowing that one can pass to an extension of the universe where CH fails and another where it holds makes it feel like a configuration choice rather than a discovery. My verdict: no fact of the matter. (5) Large cardinal axioms, though: when set theorists argue for an inaccessible or a measurable cardinal, it reads to me like reporting something found rather than passing a resolution — yet those axioms leave CH open, so I cannot fall back on undecidable-therefore-indeterminate. (6) V = L: I genuinely don't know. It feels less arbitrary than the truth value of CH but more like policy than (1)–(3), and I suspect that ordering is incoherent.

The pair I cannot reconcile is (2) and (4). Both are independent of the axioms on the table; in one case I happily say true, in the other I want to say there is nothing to be true. The nearest thing I have to a reason is a hunch that arithmetic is about one fixed structure which every model of my set theory must contain an isomorphic copy of, whereas the universe of sets lacks that property — but I cannot tell whether that is a principle or just my discomfort restated, and a colleague says it is too quick, both because second-order claims about the reals are supposedly just as slippery and because the notion of a model containing a faithful copy of the naturals does not behave the way I assume it does. So: (a) state a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precise enough that I could apply it mechanically rather than by mood, and say which of my verdicts each clause exists to handle; (b) tell me what the leading candidate says about cases I have not listed — whether every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, the Whitehead problem in abelian group theory, a purely finitary statement about natural numbers that has been shown independent of ZFC, and V = L; (c) say plainly which of my verdicts the principle has to overturn, since I would rather have a simple principle that costs me (4) than an elaborate one that saves everything, but I want the elaboration's price quoted; and (d) tell me whether my second colleague's position — that CH is not a real question — survives contact with the principle, or whether it was only ever an aesthetic reaction to unfamiliarity dressed up as a metaphysical claim.
