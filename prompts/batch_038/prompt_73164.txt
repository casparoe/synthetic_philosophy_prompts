I cover statistics, forecasting, and the philosophy of evidence for a science-and-ideas desk, and I am preparing to interview a prominent defender of imprecise probabilities — the view that for some questions the rational doxastic state is not a single number but a set or interval of them, and that this imprecision is not a temporary embarrassment to be washed out by further data but the correct response to certain kinds of evidence. I would like to practice being a hostile interviewer, so please play that defender: a career philosopher-statistician, courteous and unflappable, who has fielded every standard objection many times and is not in the least embarrassed.

Hold the position with its best resources rather than a caricature: the betting interpretation on which an interval shows up as the spread of odds you would accept in both directions; the distinction between decision rules that select a unique act and rules that leave a set of admissible acts; the claim that a precise number smuggles in information the evidence does not supply; and the suspicion that the demand for a single figure is really a demand for someone to be accountable at a moment when accountability is impossible. Concede minor points freely. Never concede the central claim that some rational credences are irreducibly imprecise. If I misstate something technical, correct me the way a patient expert corrects a reporter — briefly, then get on with your answer.

Stay in character until I write STOP. Do not step outside the interview to summarize the state of the debate or to point me toward anything to read; I want the exchange, not a survey. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs of spoken prose, the way people actually talk over an hour: concrete, occasionally wry, willing to invent a small example on the spot. If you need a detail about my scenario in order to answer, ask me for it in character.

My opening: You have told my readers that the probability this therapy halves mortality is between 0.1 and 0.5, and that no plausible further trial will narrow it, because any trial we could run would have to be designed using the very assumptions that produced your interval in the first place. I want to press on that, because I think it collapses. If the interval is that wide and can never tighten, what is the difference between holding it and simply shrugging — between saying "0.1 to 0.5" and saying "I have no idea"? Please do not give me the technical answer. Give me one observable difference: a bet you would take that a shrugger would not, or a decision your interval reverses that a shrug leaves wide open.
