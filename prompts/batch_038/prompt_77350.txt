For roughly fifteen years I have read the rationalist blogosphere and its offshoots closely enough to recognize its favorite puzzles when they resurface, and one of them came up last month at the informal probability-and-evidence reading group I attend — a crowd that has internalized Jaynes and treats arguments about priors as a contact sport. Someone reopened Bertrand's chord problem: an equilateral triangle inscribed in a circle, a chord chosen at random, and the probability that the chord is longer than one of the triangle's sides. Two regulars, whom I will call Nadia and Owen, argued past the puzzle and into the general question of what indifference can and cannot do. I have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can in seven moves, trimming the restatements. I deliberately withhold my own sympathies: I am genuinely uncertain where I stand, and signaling a preference would only buy me agreement.

Both of them assumed the standard background: the three classical answers are 1/3 (choose two endpoints uniformly on the circumference), 1/2 (choose a point uniformly along a radius and take the chord perpendicular to it at that point), and 1/4 (choose a point uniformly in the disk and take the chord having it as midpoint).

1. **Nadia.** Each of the three is an equally valid application of indifference to a different parameter, so the instruction that a chord is chosen at random does not pick out a unique probability. Her conclusion was stronger than the familiar observation that the problem is ambiguous: indifference is not a rule for producing probabilities but, at most, a rule for producing them once a sampling procedure has been fixed. Where no procedure is specified, the question is not merely underdetermined but unanswerable — and most real applications specify no procedure.

2. **Owen.** Accepted the diagnosis, rejected the verdict. If you genuinely lack information about the generator and regard the three classical procedures as the live candidates, indifference applies one level up as well: average them, giving (1/3 + 1/2 + 1/4)/3 = 13/36. Declining to answer, he said, is a luxury enjoyed by people who are never required to bet, and 13/36 states precisely the ignorance the problem describes rather than pretending to a precision it lacks.

3. **Nadia.** Two replies. First, the candidate set is not canonical: there are indefinitely many natural procedures, and the value obtained depends entirely on which ones you admit — introduce a fourth and 13/36 moves, though your epistemic situation has not. Second, and worse, the move is self-undermining: indifference over procedures requires a measure over the space of procedures, and that measure can only be manufactured by indifference again, one level higher. The regress never terminates in a number; it relocates the arbitrariness.

4. **Owen.** Conceded the regress and said it proves too much, then offered a positive alternative: invariance. Bertrand's statement fixes neither the size nor the location of the circle, so any defensible answer must be invariant under translations and rescalings. Of the three, only the radial procedure (1/2) satisfies both; the midpoint procedure is scale-invariant but not translation-invariant, and the endpoint procedure is neither. On this account 1/2 is not an arbitrary draw from a menu of three but the unique answer consistent with the information actually given — the requirement being essentially Jaynes's move. He added that Nadia's rule is safe but empty, since no verbally stated problem ever specifies its generator to the level of detail she demands.

5. **Nadia.** The invariance requirement smuggles in physics. Translation invariance is plausible only if you imagine the chord being generated by something like long straws thrown at the plane from a distance, for which the circle's position is irrelevant. If instead you read the problem as fixing the circle and requiring only that a chord be produced within it, translation invariance is false of every actual generator, because any real device is anchored to the circle. So the symmetry group is not extracted from the statement of the problem; it is imported from an imagined apparatus — which makes the invariance solution one more procedure in fancy dress, selected because it matches a physicist's intuition about straw-throwing.

6. **Owen.** Granted that, and generalized: taken seriously, Nadia's rule condemns essentially every uninformative prior in applied work, including any she uses herself, since being uniform over one parameter carries empirical content and is anything but uniform over a monotone reparameterization of it. The correct lesson, he said, is not that probabilities require a named generator but that one must ask whether the decision at hand is sensitive to the choice; where it is not, the arbitrariness is harmless, and where it is, no methodology rescues you.

7. **Nadia.** Denied the parity. In the chord case sensitivity is the entire content: the candidate answers differ by a factor of two and no further fact settles which is right, so checking sensitivity here just restates the claim that the question has no answer. Owen's criterion therefore does independent work only where nothing is at stake and collapses into hers where something is. She offered one concession: invariance arguments of Jaynes's kind are legitimate when the generating process is genuinely known — but those are exactly the cases in which the procedure was available from the start.
