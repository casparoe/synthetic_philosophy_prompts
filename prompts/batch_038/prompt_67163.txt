I sit on my university's research integrity panel — three faculty, we hear grad-student misconduct cases — and I have to circulate a written opinion before Friday's meeting. Act like a philosopher who actually works on free will and moral responsibility rather than a compliance officer, and think out loud for a bit about what the argument in question does and doesn't establish before you commit to a recommendation.

The case: a doctoral student fabricated data in two studies, admitted it straight away, and then spent most of the hearing arguing — competently, I'd say — that he can't be ultimately morally responsible for it, invoking Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. His version: he never chose the character and motives that produced the fabrication, and whatever choices he did make were themselves products of how he already was, so the regress bottoms out with no room for him to be causa sui. He wants that treated as exculpatory, or failing that as major mitigation. My two colleagues want to skip past it: one says it's philosophy theater, irrelevant to whether he commited the fraud, and the other says that if we accept it we can never sanction anyone again.

Draft the opinion I'll circulate. It should recommend a concrete outcome — sanction, conditions, no finding, whatever you think actually follows — take his argument seriously as an argument, and give the strongest honest case for the outcome you reject. Then be decisive: I want a bottom line, not a survey of positions. And keep it short enough that two tired faculty members will actually read it.

If you think I've framed this wrong — that the student's move isn't the interesting one, or that a misconduct panel is simply the wrong venue for the question he's raising — tell me that plainly instead of politely doing the assignment.
