You are a philosopher with working expertise in formal epistemology, decision theory, and social choice theory, and with enough time spent inside actual prediction markets to know how their rulebooks are drafted and litigated. Please think carefully. Treat this as an exercise in what a piece of institutional machinery commits its designers to, not as a survey of the literature on governance by markets.

Begin with an explicit brainstorming pass before you commit to anything: list candidate reconstructions of the design's implicit commitments, including at least two that you expect to reject, and say briefly why you expect to reject them. Then write the analysis.

**The practice.** In Robin Hanson's futarchy proposal, we would "vote on values, but bet on beliefs": elected representatives "formally define and manage an after-the-fact measurement of national welfare," speculators trade on which policies they expect to raise it, and the basic rule of government is that "When a betting market clearly estimates that a proposed policy would increase expected national welfare, that proposal becomes law." The conditional estimate is to be obtained by comparing expected welfare conditional on adopting a policy with expected welfare conditional on not adopting it — for instance by means of called-off bets.

Now suppose a small polity adopts a charter that fills in that skeleton in the following specific way.

1. Once per legislative term, the assembly votes on a single scalar welfare index: a weighted composite of published statistics (median household income, healthy life expectancy, a survey measure of life satisfaction, a measure of air quality), with the weights fixed in the text of the charter. Markets are opened on this index and on nothing else.
2. Each bill receives two conditional markets: the index five years out given enactment, and the index five years out given non-enactment. If the price of the enactment contract exceeds the price of the baseline contract by more than two cents, and continues to exceed it for thirty consecutive days, the bill is enacted automatically, with no further vote and no legislative override.
3. Contracts pay one unit per share. The platform publishes the price as a percentage and describes it in its own documentation as the market's probability. There is no instrument for expressing a range or a set of probabilities — only a price.
4. Settlement is handled by a three-member resolution committee, which must settle strictly by the letter of the written rules, using the first figure published by the designated statistical agency on the designated date. Later revisions, corrections, methodological restatements, and evidence that the agency's figure was simply erroneous are inadmissible. So are claims about what the assembly intended. The committee's determination is final and binding even if it is factually mistaken. If the agency publishes nothing by the deadline, the contract settles at the previous period's figure.
5. The same operator's sports desk, by contrast, prides itself on the opposite norm: it will void a contract and refund traders if the officially declared result is later overturned, on the ground that the contract was never really about the announcement but about who actually won.

**Two puzzles I want pressed on.** The charter's stated rationale for using markets at all is that they are unusually good at tracking facts about the future; yet clause 4 makes the settled proposition an institutional verdict rather than a fact, and clause 5 shows the same operator holding the opposite view about a structurally identical question elsewhere. Separately, the slogan that the assembly decides values while the markets decide beliefs sits awkwardly with clause 1: fixing the component weights and the five-year window does not look like a purely evaluative act, and the index is itself a lever that well-capitalized traders, and the agencies feeding the data, can act on.

**Questions — please answer all of them.**

1. *Settlement.* What theory of truth, or of what fixes the identity of the proposition being traded, does clause 4 presuppose? Is the traded object the sentence in the rulebook, the assembly's intention, or the state of the world? Reconstruct the implicit view of meaning and reference — stipulation that fixes reference, textualism about the contract, some deflationism, an authority-based substitute for truth — and identify which rival views clause 4 rules out as opposed to merely failing to use. Are clauses 4 and 5 genuinely inconsistent, or can one coherent view license both?
2. *Price as credence.* What does clause 3 presuppose about belief and about probability? Separate the commitments the practice forces (that belief comes in degrees; that those degrees are precise enough to be compared against a threshold like two cents; that averaging money-weighted, risk-averse, anonymous positions yields something entitled to be called the probability of a future contingent) from those it merely happens to sit well with. What would it take for the practice to be compatible with imprecise or set-valued credences, with a frequency or single-case chance reading of probability, or with the denial that "the index five years out given enactment" has any truth value at all?
3. *The word "clearly."* Hanson's rule turns on the market's clearly estimating an increase. What epistemology of thresholds does clause 2 presuppose when it converts "clearly" into two cents sustained for thirty days? Is that a claim about the market's reliability, about the precision of prices, about the margin of political risk a polity may acceptably run, or about something else? Compare and contrast it with standards of proof in adjudication.
4. *Value.* What theory of value do clauses 1 and 2 together presuppose? Address scalar aggregation across persons, cardinal interpersonal comparability, the assumption that a fixed five-year window exhausts a policy's morally relevant effects, and the assumption that the two conditional markets describe futures comparable enough to be subtracted. Does the practice require consequentialism, or could a non-consequentialist adopt it as an instrument without contradiction?
5. *Underdetermination.* Distinguish sharply what this design strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and mark the places where several mutually incompatible theories would license exactly the same written rules. Which single clause is doing the most philosophical work, and which clauses are philosophically inert machinery that any number of views could accommodate?
6. *Replacement.* For each of the following substitutions, say concretely what the charter would have to become and what would be lost in the exchange: settling by a truth-seeking process with appeals and retrials on new evidence rather than by the first published figure; letting traders express imprecise probabilities; replacing the scalar index with a Pareto-style multi-criterion rule; and dropping the fixed five-year horizon.

Finally, a note on confidence. Throughout, separate what is settled or near-settled among people who know these topics from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus. Where you are reconstructing an implicit theory rather than reporting a view anybody holds, say that you are doing so, and flag which of your reconstructions are most vulnerable to being simply wrong about how the machinery works.
