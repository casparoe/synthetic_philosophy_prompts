I'm about seven months into a job at a county housing authority and this argument has eaten my evenings. Context so it isn't abstract: we rank applications for a limited pot of emergency rent assistance with a logistic model. The thing we predict is "overpayment on file," which operationally means an auditor opened the file and found money missing — and auditors only ever opened the files the old paper triage sent them. Base rate in the flagged group is 21%, in the comparison group it's 9%, AUC around .71.

A lawyer who sits on our review panel — call him R — laid this out at a design meeting and I couldn't answer it then and I still can't.

1. If a score is calibrated, then within every score band the real rate of the outcome is the same for every group.
2. But if base rates differ, a calibrated score can't also have equal false positive and equal false negative rates. That's a theorem, not an opinion.
3. Under one shared threshold, the lower-base-rate group ends up with the higher false positive rate, and in our data thats the group we are least allowed to over-flag.
4. A false positive here means a family gets prioritized who shouldnt be, which means a different family loses the money, so unequal false positive rates just are unequal harms.
5. The only way to keep calibration and equalize the error rates is different thresholds per group.
6. Different thresholds per group means deciding two people with identical scores differently because of what group they're in, which is what every anti-discrimination rule and every plain-English notion of fairness forbids.
7. So the single-threshold model is unfair by (3)–(4), and the group-specific model is unfair by (6).
8. And since every real population has base rate gaps on anything worth predicting, this hits every model anyone builds.
9. Therefore "fairness" isn't a constraint on modeling, it's a mood, and the honest move is to say we're optimizing accuracy and drop the theater.

R finds (9) liberating. I think it's wrong but I can't find the crack, and in three weeks I have to stand up in front of a community advisory board of actual tenants and say something that isn't (9). I took this job because my aunt sat on a waiting list for two years while paper triage kept losing her file, so I can't deliver "just maximize accuracy" to that room with a straight face — but I also can't tell them the model is fair, because I can't show it is.

So: which step do I reject? If I have to choose I'd rather give up (4) or (5) than (6), since (6) is the one I'd have to defend out loud and I don't think I can. But tell me what each candidate rejection costs, and tell me whether the thing is valid — I suspect a good chunk of it is and that the problem is somewhere I haven't looked. I don't want paper titles or a reading list, I want the diagnosis in a form I could actually say to a room of non-specialists, including how you'd explain the trade-off to someone who's been told their whole life that the system is rigged.
