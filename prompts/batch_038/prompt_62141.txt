I'm TA-ing a small grad methods seminar on explanation in history and I've hit a wall: every time we discuss what historians are doing when they explain something, the room collapses the whole debate into "Hempel's people vs the narrativists," and then half the class turns out to be holding positions that don't fit either camp without anyone noticing. So instead of another reading response, I want to hand them a decision tree — a genuine sorting instrument — that takes a student from something they've actually written and lands them on a named position, with the branches being the questions where the positions really come apart. The trick I care about is that no node can be answerable by recognizing a label: every branch should be phrased as a question about a piece of historical writing that a student could answer on the merits, without knowing which view their answer commits them to.

Here's the raw material, which is my own and probably a bit idiosyncratic, so please push back on it. I've been assuming three axes do all the work: (i) whether the thing being explained is a single dated event or a pattern/recurrence, (ii) what kind of connective tissue holds explanans to explanandum — nomic, intentional-rational, purely chronological, or something else — and (iii) whether explanatory adequacy is a relation in the world or a relation between a contrastive question and the world. I suspect (iii) might not be an axis at all but a view about (ii), and honestly I suspect narrativity and contrastivity are the same move in disguise, a story just being a temporal ordering that fixes the contrast class — tell me if that's right, because if it is, one of my axes is fake. And here are five explanation-sketches I collected from the seminar's problem set, which you should use as the diagnostic inputs rather than abstract positions: (A) a student explains a grain riot in a provincial town in the 1840s by citing a generalization to the effect that when staple prices jump sharply in one season while wages stay flat, food rioting becomes much more likely, then notes the town's price series fits. (B) a student explains why a minister signed a bad treaty by reconstructing what he knew and wanted and arguing that signing was the sensible thing to do given that, and explicitly refuses to add a law, saying any law she could state would be so trivial it would do no work. (C) a student explains why a revolution failed by telling it — the sequence of contingent choices — and then insists that getting the sequence right *just is* the explaining, and that asking for the generalization shows you've misunderstood the activity. (D) a student explains why a fleet sailed in June rather than September by pointing out the question is incomplete until you fix what it's being contrasted with: against sailing in September, June beats the autumn storms; against not sailing at all, June was the only window the treasury could fund. (E) the one that breaks my framework — a student explains a banking collapse by tracing a chain of locally causal steps, run on deposits, credit contraction, layoffs, demand collapse, with no law stated anywhere and no story arc claimed either, and says if you asked her for a generalization she'd give you different ones at different links.

For the deliverable: a tree (or a small forest, if some cases need their own) with maybe eight to twelve branch nodes and no more than ten leaves, each leaf carrying the name of the position — or "unnamed" if you think nobody really defends it — plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment, the thing its defender would rather not be asked about. Then: mark the combinations my axes allow that no leaf occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate — I'd bet on (B) vs the situational-analysis-style view, since both reconstruct the agent's reasons, and I want to know what the real wedge between them is if there is one. Also tell me where (E) lands and whether mechanism-based explanation is a fourth value of axis (ii) or a covering-law position in denial. One last thing, and it's a constraint on style: no bibliography, no reference lists, no publication years, nothing I'd have to look up to check — name the position and describe the idea in your own words, and if you're not certain who defends something (there's a view I keep half-remembering on which the generalizations historians lean on are closer to truisms than to laws, doing no predictive work but licensing the hunt for causes), just call it something descriptive and say it might be unnamed rather than guessing an author. End with the three questions you'd put on the first slide.
