My AP Psych teacher has been going on about this idea that thinking happens in some kind of language inside your head — not English exactly, more like English is a lossy translation of it — and half my class was like yeah obviously thats how it feels, and I was like ok but how would you ever check? Then over winter break my cousin had a stroke at 19 and came out of it barely able to get a sentence out, and everyone in my family started talking about her like her mind had gone with her speech, and it definately hadn't — she beat my dad at chess twice and she could still do her own taxes on paper. That gap is basically the whole thing I want the story to be about.

So: write me a short story, maybe 1,500 to 2,500 words, about a teenager — say 16 or 17 — who after some brain injury can no longer think in any language he recognizes. His inner monologue isn't gone and it isn't silent, it's fast and full and fluent, it's just in a language nobody has ever heard, and he can't read it, can't write it down, can't get his mouth to make the sounds. A grad student at the rehab center where he does his exercises decides to treat that inner language like an undeciphered script instead of like a symptom, and works out a test she can run without knowing a single word of it: the idea that if a language really has parts and structure, then a person who can think one combination of them can think the same parts put together differently — so if she can train him to associate a few of his inner sentences with a few situations, she should be able to predict which brand-new combinations he'll get right, and his mistakes should come out in a pattern (roles swapped, one part carried over) rather than at random. I'd like the middle of the story to be that test actually being run, including it not going cleanly, and I'd like a second thread about whether he can learn a genuinely new concept in there at all — like whether every new idea he has turns out to be built out of ideas he already had, so that the inner language can grow but can never really surprise him. Emotionally I want him to start out humiliated by it and end up sort of preferring it, because it holds thoughts English crushes; maybe someone in his family keeps insisting he's still in there and he can't confirm or deny it in any way that would count. Don't have it resolve whether this is a real language of thought or something else wearing his brain — leave the reader weighing it. Please no real philosophers or papers or names dropped in the text, this is for my school's lit magazine and I can't fact-check references anyway.

Then after the story, add a short note in your own voice — not a bibliography, no reading list, I just mean a paragraph — saying how confident you are in the picture of thinking the story assumes, and singling out the one claim it leans on hardest that you're least sure is actually true. I'm specifically wondering whether that decipherment test really tests anything, or whether it would come out exactly the same if his inner speech were just damaged English, so tell me if you think I seeded a bad idea and where else you took liberties.
