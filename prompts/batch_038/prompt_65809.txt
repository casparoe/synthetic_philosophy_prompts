I am preparing a statement of my own position for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of nationalism, and I would rather have it audited than applauded. I have written down what I take myself to believe; I no longer trust my own sense of where the pressure points are, and I do not want to walk into the seminar having to defend a package that does not hold.

My commitments are these.

1. Genealogy. I accept the modernist account: the nation, as a unit of political claim-making, is recent. Standardized print languages, mass schooling, conscription, censuses, maps and museums are what produced a population that experiences itself as a people and then projects that experience backwards into a deep past. On this I follow Gellner, Anderson and Hobsbawm, and I reject the perennialist and primordialist alternatives.

2. Anti-debunking. I refuse the slide from invented to illegitimate. The recency of a tradition's manufacture tells us nothing about its capacity to bind those who live inside it, and I do not allow genealogy to do normative work anywhere in my arguments.

3. Critique regardless. Even so, I think it matters that the antiquity claims of nationalist movements are false, and that correcting them is part of what scholarship owes a public that has been told those claims. The story of immemorial continuity is an assertion about the world, it can be shown to be untrue, and I want it shown.

4. The demos. Democratic procedure cannot settle its own scope, since the question of who constitutes the electorate cannot itself be put to an electorate. The unit must be given in advance, and I think what gives it is a shared national identity: a people who already think of themselves as a we, and who stand to one another in the relations of solidarity that redistribution and democratic trust presuppose. This is liberal nationalism of the sort defended by Miller and Tamir, and I sign on to it.

5. Civic and ethnic. I hold the civic/ethnic distinction to be real and normatively decisive. A nationalism built on shared institutions and a common public language, open to anyone willing to join it, is a different kind of thing from a nationalism built on descent, and I think only the second is objectionable. I count myself a civic nationalist.

6. Cases. I think the Québécois and the Catalans have a legitimate prima facie claim to secede, and that the force of that claim rests on the existence of a societal culture together with the expressed will of its members, not on the antiquity of either. I also think claims of historical justice can be advanced in the name of a people who, on my own genealogy, became a nation only in the course of the nineteenth century, in respect of a dispossession carried out in the eighteenth.

7. Boundaries. Against the cosmopolitan line, I do not think the existing map of states is morally arbitrary. It carries normative weight; borders are not merely regrettable constraints of feasibility.

Some of these look as though they clash and do not. I cannot tell you which, and I would ask you not to assume that the ones I stated most confidently are the safest.

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion, no preamble. The first four are the tensions you judge most severe, in descending order of severity, each in one or two sentences: name the commitments involved, say whether the clash is genuine or only apparent, and where it is genuine give the cheapest revision that removes it together with what that revision costs me. The fifth item states which single change to the set would clean up the most, and what I would have to stop believing in order to make it.
