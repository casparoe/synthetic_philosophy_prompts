I'm about four chapters from finishing a novel and I have an author's note to write, plus an interview lined up where I've been warned they'll ask about the structure, so before I put my metaphysics in print I want someone to check whether it actually holds together. I'm not a philosopher. I've read around this subject for two years, mostly at night, and I know where I'm being loose — what I can't tell is which looseness is fatal. Here's the package, in no particular order:

1. The argument from the relativity of simultaneity gets me completely. Two observers moving relative to each other disagree about which distant events are happening at the same time as this one. If I say only what's happening now is real, then whether some far-off event is real turns into a matter of which observer you happen to ask, and I can't live with that. So I conclude that no slice of "now" is priveleged, and that the whole four-dimensional spacetime is just there — all of it, past and future, equally real. No moving spotlight. I take this to be settled and I take it to follow from the physics.

2. But I'm not a determinist and I don't want to become one in order to stay consistent. I think there's genuine chance in the world: the past together with the laws doesn't fix what comes next. I find the idea that everything was settled at the beginning intolerable, and not only for temperamental reasons — I think it's simply false.

3. And I think that's enough to keep the future open. My characters' futures are open in the sense that matters even though the whole block is equally real. When people tell me the block universe makes everything fated, I think they've made a mistake, and I think 2 is what exposes it.

4. Separately — and this is the one I'm most attached to — I think something real happens as time goes on. Not a spotlight sweeping across a finished block; more like the block is being made, along an edge, and that edge is objective. Not something you get by picking a reference frame. The universe is under construction and there's a fact about where the construction has got to.

5. The book itself. Three long chapters covering the same eleven minutes in three places so far apart that no signal could cross between them in that time: a research vessel in the Southern Ocean, a hospital corridor, an airport gate. I narrate them as simultaneous — literally, not "as reckoned from somewhere." My reasoning was that if no slicing of spacetime is privileged then none is ruled out either, and I get to choose one and mean it. The jacket copy reads: eleven minutes, three places, one present.

6. A verdict on a case. My airport character spends forty pages deciding whether to board a plane. I want to say she genuinely makes it the case which way things go — that she isn't discovering anything and isn't a puppet — and I also want to say the block has always contained those forty pages and there was never any question about what it contained. People keep telling me those can't both be true. I don't feel the conflict, which makes me suspect I'm missing something rather than that they're wrong.

What I want is an audit, not a seminar. Go through these pairwise where it matters and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other, which ones only look bad and are fine on inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity instead of dumping them on me as equally bad. For each real tension give me the cheapest revision and what it costs — and I want the cost stated concretely, including for 5, because I need to know whether the jacket copy survives, whether the author's note can say what I want it to say, or whether I have to go back and re-narrate a chapter. Flag anywhere I've claimed more than the physics actually delivers, even if nothing else in the list contradicts it. Don't tell me to become a presentist and don't tell me to stop caring. Then finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most damage, and say plainly what I'd be left believing once I'd made it.
