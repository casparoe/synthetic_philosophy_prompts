I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am midway through a long feature about a habit of argument in contemporary cosmology that I can describe but cannot label: theories being set aside, or propped up, without any new data entering the picture. My editor has insisted, reasonably, that I get the terminology right before I file, and this is where I have stalled. I would like you to answer as a philosopher of science who also has a genuine working knowledge of the anthropic-reasoning literature and of how cosmologists actually argue with one another; assume I am well read generally but have no graduate training in physics or in formal epistemology, and please think the problem through carefully rather than reaching for the first piece of jargon that comes to hand.

Here is the pattern, in my own words, with the three instances that made me think it was one pattern rather than three. First, the case that started it: a cosmological model agrees with everything anyone can point an instrument at — the expansion history, the primordial abundances, the structure in the microwave sky — and nobody has located a discrepancy. Yet the model implies that, given enough time, empty space will throw up fleeting, self-aware fluctuations in unimaginable numbers, each momentary and confused, and that these will vastly outnumber the long-lived, law-abiding observers who evolved on planets. So the model is declared pathological and people start hunting for a way to rule it out. The reasoning is not that it mispredicted something; it is that if it were true, beings having experiences like ours would be freakish, and since we have experiences like ours, it is probably false. Second, running in the opposite direction: a hypothesis that posits enormously more universe than observation requires — infinitely many regions, endlessly repeated histories — seems to earn support from nothing but the fact that more universe means more chances for someone like me to be around asking questions, and the standing complaint about that move is that it lets an armchair argument settle something no telescope can settle. Third, the pattern appears to travel outside physics entirely: a view about where our moral intuitions come from is not contradicted by any moral observation we could make, and yet people argue against it on the grounds that if it were true, those intuitions would be the by-product of a process that was not tracking anything, so we would have no reason to trust the very intuitions that recommend the view. When I have described this to people, the only phrase I get back is something about observation selection effects, which seems to cover everything and therefore nothing.

So: does the general move have an established name? I am aware that the physics version of my first instance has its own vivid nickname, and that is not what I am after — I want whatever concept, if any, covers the family in which a hypothesis is confirmed or disconfirmed not by its fit to observable data but by the size and shape of the population of observers it implies, together with a standing assumption that we are a fair sample drawn from that population. Concretely, I would like you to begin by brainstorming candidate terms and concepts and setting them out briefly, then evaluate each against my description before committing to an answer; for every near neighbour, say precisely how what I have described differs from it and which of them fits best; and if nothing fits exactly, coin a term, define it, and give me its edges — tell me where this stops being the phenomenon and becomes ordinary falsification, where it is the Duhem–Quine problem in costume, where it is really an argument about the reliability of my faculties rather than about observer counts, and whether my first and second instances are the same move or two different moves sharing a premise. I would also like to know what the move presupposes — a typicality assumption, a choice of reference class, a measure over observers — and whether that presupposition is defensible or covertly question-begging, since my piece has to tell readers whether cosmologists are entitled to argue this way at all. Two housekeeping requests. I am not looking for a bibliography or a reading list, only the concepts and their boundaries. And where a label is your own coinage rather than settled usage in the field, flag that explicitly and separately: I would much rather write that philosophers have no agreed word for this than invent one in print and attribute it to a literature that never used it.
