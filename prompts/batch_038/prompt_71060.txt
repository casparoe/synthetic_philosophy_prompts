I have just been seated on the panel that awards my association's long-horizon prize, and the first file in front of us is a 1970s subsistence model whose marquee prediction — a worldwide food crisis before the turn of the century — is now forty years late. Half the panel wants to call it refuted; half wants to call it right but early, on the grounds that the mechanism it posited is intact and has merely been outrun by yield gains no one could have anticipated. I have to write the report, and before I do I want to know what I actually think about this class of prediction, since my gut is not obviously consistent.

My working principle is what I have started calling the statute-of-limitations view: a long-run theory is falsified once the event it predicted has had a fair opportunity to occur and has not, where fair opportunity is fixed by the background conditions the theory itself specifies as necessary. If those conditions were never in place, the clock has not run.

Here are my verdicts, such as they are.

1. Malthus, 1798: population pressing against the means of subsistence. Two centuries on, per-capita food supply has risen almost everywhere. I do not feel the theory is refuted, but I cannot name one thing that confirms it either, and that bothers me.
2. Hubbert's 1956 projection that lower-48 oil output would peak around 1970: it did, essentially on schedule. That is what a real long-run success looks like — except that American output has since climbed back above that 1970 peak, which the curve implied would not happen. I still want to call it a success.
3. The 1980 Simon–Ehrlich wager on a basket of five metals, settled in 1990: Ehrlich lost and paid up. Yet later analyses find the outcome flips with the choice of window, and that across most of the twentieth century Ehrlich would have won. I want to say the bet settled something, and I want to say both men were largely at the mercy of dates. I cannot hold both.
4. The mass famines forecast for the 1970s that never arrived at the predicted scale because crop yields jumped: I want to call that postponed, not refuted.
5. A catastrophe averted precisely because the forecast was published and people acted on it: I think that counts as confirmation, not as a prediction that failed to come true.
6. Postulating an unseen planet to save an orbital perturbation strikes me as legitimate, because the auxiliary claim was independently testable and paid off. Lengthening a time constant to save a missed date strikes me as illegitimate. These look to me like the same maneuver, and I have no account of why I treat them oppositely.

Does the statute-of-limitations principle deliver all six, or does some rival? State the winner precisely, clause by clause, saying which case motivates each clause; tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon; give its verdict on two cases I have not listed — a prediction whose target date is pushed out each time by roughly the length of the fresh miss, and one that comes true on schedule for reasons entirely outside its stated mechanism. Then tell me plainly whether right-but-early is ever a genuine epistemic status, or only unfalsifiability wearing a more respectable name.
