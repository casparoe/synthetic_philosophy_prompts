I cover physics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and in the spring I'm delivering the opening lecture at a workshop on the foundations of quantum theory: twenty-five minutes at the podium, then fifteen minutes of questions from the floor, with no slides and no notes beyond an index card. The room will be about sixty people, and it is not my usual room. It is mostly quantum information theorists — several of whom work on device-independent cryptography and think of a Bell violation as a resource with a precise technical meaning; three or four philosophers of physics, one of whom works on Bohmian mechanics and one of whom is an articulate Everettian; a handful of experimentalists from the groups that ran the recent loophole-closing tests, including the Delft electron-spin experiment; and a scattering of graduate students who have never read Bell's 1964 paper but have read a great deal about certified randomness. I am there as the outsider who writes about their subject for people who will never read their papers, and I would rather plan for the question period than survive it. I want your help with the latter.

The talk argues that the standard press vocabulary for these results — 'spooky action at a distance,' 'particles communicating instantaneously,' 'Einstein finally proven wrong,' 'local realism is dead' — is not merely loose but structurally wrong, and that a reporter can state the finding accurately without any of it. My positive proposal is a house style for writing about Bell experiments, in four clauses. First: report the theorem as ruling out a stated condition — roughly, that a complete specification of what happens in the backward light cone of one measurement screens that measurement's outcome off from space-like separated events — rather than as refuting a vague compound nobody has defined. Second: reserve 'nonlocal' for theories in which the choice of setting over here figures as a causal input to outcomes over there, and note that ordinary quantum mechanics, on the familiar decomposition of Bell's locality condition into parameter independence and outcome independence, violates only the latter; the Bohmian theory, by contrast, is nonlocal in the strong sense, and I want to keep those two claims apart. Third: drop 'realism' from 'local realism' altogether, on the ground that the assumption doing the work is about common causes in spacetime, not about whether unmeasured quantities have values. Fourth: since retrocausal, superdeterministic, Everettian, and Bohmian accounts all reproduce the observed statistics, the news value of any further loophole-closing experiment is genuinely small and should be reported as small — which means my own beat has been overselling a settled matter for a decade. I expect the room to attack clauses two and four hardest, and I am willing to concede that clause three is partly a philosopher's quarrel over a word.

So give me the question period as it will actually go: the eight or so objections this particular audience will raise, ordered by how much damage each could do to the credibility I will have built in the first twenty-five minutes, and each phrased the way that person would phrase it — the device-independence theorist will not put it as the Bohmian does, and neither will sound like a magazine editor asking whether I've lost my readers. For each, a reply I can deliver standing up in about ninety seconds, and a candid note on where that reply is soft rather than where I'd like it to be strong. Mark separately which objections I should simply concede at the podium, which single reply has to land for the rest of the argument to hold, and the one question I am privately hoping nobody asks because I have no answer that wouldn't sound like a retreat. Where an objection turns on a technical point I am likely to garble under pressure — what precisely a single-outcome assumption presupposes and whether Bell's argument survives without it; whether the freedom-of-choice assumption is genuinely still open after settings have been selected using photons from distant stars and, more recently, quasars; what a fine-tuning argument against causal-model explanations of the correlations does and does not establish; whether the no-signalling theorem really licenses my clause two — say so, and give me the correct version in one sentence I could repeat verbatim without embarrassing myself. I do not want the objections a book-festival audience would raise, and I do not want advice about how to write the eventual article; I want to walk in knowing who is going to stand up and what they are going to say.
