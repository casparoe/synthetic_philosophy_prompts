For a seminar on Dewey's experimentalism — inquiry as the controlled transformation of an indeterminate situation, together with the claim in The Public and Its Problems (1927) that a public comes into existence only when the indirect consequences of associated activity are perceived and organized for — I am pairing the Dewey with Ibsen's An Enemy of the People. Write me a reading of the play through that framework, organized around a distinction I want to force on the students: between failing at inquiry and failing at the second-order task of constituting a public able to receive its results. Stockmann's analysis of the bath water is sound by the play's own lights; what undoes him begins when he abandons his prepared report at the town meeting in favor of a diatribe about the compact liberal majority and the stupidity of the authorities, and it is further complicated by what he discloses on the spot — that his own father-in-law's tannery is a source of the poisoning. Tell me, then, whether his parting conviction that the strongest person is the one who stands most alone is a Deweyan heresy, a forgivable loss of nerve, or the honest report of a man who has run the experiment and found no public to receive it. I want you to resist the easy way out in both directions: the play does not cast the mayor as a simple villain, since Peter's objection is cost, closure, and the town's ruin, so say what experimentalism can offer when the finding is sound and the resistance is honest self-interest, and whether its only counsel is patience. Close by identifying what Ibsen shows about the price of waiting for a public to form that a Deweyan account of inquiry cannot readily absorb.
