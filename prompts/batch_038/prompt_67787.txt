I teach philosophy of law and professional ethics, and this term I have been asked to do two things outside my specialty: contribute a chapter to an interdisciplinary handbook on responsibility (the other contributors are mostly from law, clinical medicine, and public policy), and run the one session of our graduate seminar devoted to "what we fail to do." My own work concerns the normative side — duties, permissions, excuses — and I am perfectly comfortable with the claim that a person can be blameworthy for a failure to act. What I am not competent to judge is the metaphysics underneath it: whether an omission can literally be a *cause*, or whether causal language in these cases is doing something else entirely. To get oriented I read a chapter in a well-reviewed trade book on the philosophy of everyday life, and it contains the following passage, which I have transcribed as accurately as I could:

> "For most of its history, philosophy assumed that causation is a relation between happenings — that to cause something is to bring some force, energy, or process to bear on it. On that picture, what you failed to do could never be a cause, since a non-happening is not the sort of thing that could produce anything. The consequence is absurd: the nurse who never administered the dose would not be a cause of the patient's death, even though the law, the hospital, and common sense all agree that she is. The counterfactual theorists solved the problem. Since Lewis, causation has been understood as counterfactual dependence: c causes e just in case, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred. That handles omissions for free, because had the nurse given the drug the patient would have lived; so her not giving it caused his death. The price, though, is well known. Once absences are admitted as causes, the world fills up with them: the Queen's failure to water my plants caused them to wither, and so did the failure of every stranger who ever walked past my window box. Unwilling to pay that price, a growing number of philosophers deny that there is any such thing as negative causation at all, and hold that talk of causing-by-omitting is really a matter of norms and expectations: an agent causes a harm only where the agent was under an obligation to prevent it. On this view the nurse causes the death because she had a duty; the passer-by does not, though the counterfactuals are exactly the same. Causation, in other words, turns out to be partly a moral notion."

Please act as a metaphysician who works on causation and has thought hard about the negative-causation debate, and give me an accuracy audit of that passage. Think carefully before answering, and begin with a few sentences of brainstorming in which you identify the distinct claims the passage is bundling together and the points at which you expect them to come apart — then do the audit proper. Work through it claim by claim, and for each claim tell me which of four boxes it belongs in: something a specialist would say without qualification; a simplification that costs nothing important; something subtly wrong in a way that would mislead a careful reader; or something flatly false. Two things I particularly want you to bear down on, because I suspect them but cannot articulate why. First, whether the passage is treating two different phenomena as one: an agent's refraining from something she was positioned to do, on the one hand, and the mere absence of some event anywhere in the world, on the other — and whether the profligacy complaint it mentions is aimed at both or only at one. Second, whether the last two moves are compatible with each other: the denial that there is negative causation, and the assertion that an agent causes a harm only where she had an obligation to prevent it. My own reading, which I want you to check rather than accommodate, is that someone who denies negative causation altogether should be explaining our omission-talk away as a normative gloss laid over purely positive causal facts, not building the norm into causation itself — and that these are very different theses, with very different consequences for the law. Also tell me whether the passage's appeal to legal practice is doing the work it seems to be doing: I teach that the common law's general rule is precisely that there is no liability for failing to rescue absent a special relationship, a contractual undertaking, or culpable creation of the peril, which looks to me less like an argument that causation is moral than like evidence that the law has always kept the causal question and the duty question rigorously apart. Say where you are unsure rather than smoothing it over, and do not give me references or a reading list — I want the conceptual map, not the literature.

Finally, I would like you to apply the corrected picture to a case of my own devising, because I intend to use it in the seminar and I need to know what I am getting into. A hotel employs a lifeguard, posted to watch a marked stretch of water; immediately beyond the hotel's boundary lies an unguarded public beach. Two swimmers, equally visible from the tower and equally within reach of one competent swimmer, get into identical difficulty at the same moment. The lifeguard, distracted, sees neither, and both drown. My students reliably report that her inaction caused the first death but not the second, and they reach for the duty to explain the difference. Now change one detail: suppose the swimmer in the unguarded stretch could have been saved instantly by a guest sunbathing on the public beach, who happens to be an elite open-water swimmer and who also fails to act, while the lifeguard is a marginal swimmer who could have reached the hotel's own swimmer only barely in time. When I put the case that way, several students reverse their verdict about who caused what, which suggests to me that the intuition is tracking capacity and settled expectation rather than obligation — and if that is right, the passage's concluding slogan looks not merely imprecise but wrong about the phenomenon it invokes. Please tell me what you make of that: which of the three broad positions you judge most defensible — that negative causation is real and indiscriminate, that there is none and our omission-talk is a normative overlay on positive causal facts, or that causation itself is norm-sensitive — what each would say about the modified case, and whether the flip in my students' intuitions is evidence for any of them. Close with a rewrite of the passage, about two paragraphs long, at the same level of accessibility but safe to hand to law and medical students; flag in one or two sentences what you had to sacrifice in making it accurate.
