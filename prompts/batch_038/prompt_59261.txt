I have been reconstructing the anti-luminosity argument for a reading group, and when I pushed one step further than the original I landed on a conclusion I am certain is false. Here is the reconstruction.

(1) A condition C is luminous just in case, necessarily, whenever C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that C obtains.
(2) Margin for error: if in a case α the subject knows that C obtains, then C obtains in every case that is, for the subject in α, indiscriminable from α.
(3) For any non-trivial condition, there is a series of cases α_0, …, α_n such that C obtains in α_0, fails in α_n, and each case is indiscriminable from the next for the subject.
(4) Therefore no non-trivial condition is luminous.
(5) Applying (4) to the condition knowing that p: knowledge is not luminous, so there are cases in which one knows that p without being in a position to know that one knows it, and the KK principle fails.
(6) But (4) applies equally to the condition being in a position to know that p. Take a series of cases in which the same bird sits on the same fence while the light fails by imperceptible degrees: at α_0 one is in a position to know that the bird is there, at α_n one is not, and no step along the way is detectable from the inside.
(7) So there are cases in which one is in a position to know that p without being in a position to know that one is.
(8) In such a case one cannot tell whether one's belief would amount to knowledge, and a belief one cannot tell that about is not knowledge.
(9) Since the series has no detectable cutoff, (8) applies at every point on it, including α_0.
(10) Generalizing across every such series: nothing is ever known.

I want to keep (5) and reject (10). Which step should go, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, I want to know whether whatever blocks the slide from (7) to (10) also blocks the derivation of (5): if the only principled exit is to deny that (4) applies to knowledge itself, then I have lost the very result I set out to establish.
