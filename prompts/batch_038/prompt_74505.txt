Next month I run a single 75-minute session for an interdisciplinary graduate cohort — philosophy, economics, and public policy — on the Pasadena paradox and, more generally, on decision problems whose expected value is undefined rather than merely enormous. Everyone in the room has had one term of expected-utility theory: they can compute an expectation as a probability-weighted sum, they have met the St. Petersburg game and the diminishing-marginal-utility reply to it, and they know from first-year calculus that some infinite series converge while others diverge. They have never met conditional convergence or the rearrangement theorem, and they have no measure theory. I want a plan for the session, not the session itself — please do not explain the Pasadena game to me.

The carrier will be the coin-flip gamble that pays plus or minus 2^n/n dollars, with the sign alternating, when heads first appears on the nth toss, so that its expectation series is the alternating harmonic series; alongside it goes the cousin gamble that dominates it state by state. Build the plan around the following decisions:

1. The dependency structure, with a minutes budget for each step. In particular, tell me where the mathematical detour goes: conditional convergence and the order-dependence of the sum must be installed at some point, and if I front-load it I lose the room, while if I save it for the end the gamble looks like a conjuring trick performed on them. Give me your recommendation and the reason, stated in terms of what later steps depend on it.
2. Whether the Pasadena game is itself the right carrier or whether something smaller has to carry the first twenty minutes. Say precisely what the carrier must have (unbounded payoffs of both signs, an expectation series that converges but not absolutely) and what it must lack, and why the tempting substitutes fail — a finite capped version, the plain St. Petersburg game presented as though it raised the same problem, and a case in which the expectation is missing because the outcomes are not commensurable rather than because the series misbehaves.
3. A map of the misconceptions, each with the step at which it is headed off and the cost of deferring it. I expect at least these: that this is St. Petersburg with minus signs and therefore nothing new; that the expectation is ln 2 and so nothing is wrong; that truncating the series at some large n is a legitimate repair; that the order-dependence complaint is a cheat because a gamble has no ordering of its states; that undefined expectation is an impressive name for very risky; and that a bounded utility function dissolves everything. On that last one, tell me where it belongs given that it is true — with bounded utility the probability weights alone guarantee absolute convergence — but that I want them to feel the price rather than be told it.
4. What the dominating cousin does that Pasadena alone cannot do, and at which step it should be introduced: before the first proposed repair, so the repair is born under pressure, or after, so the repair gets a fair hearing first. Take a position.
5. One closing question or short task that would show me the idea landed, together with the specific wrong answer that would show it did not — as distinct from a merely clumsy answer.
6. What I should refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud: the proof of the rearrangement theorem, expectation as a Lebesgue integral, finitely additive extensions and other technical repairs, relative expected utility, imprecise probabilities. For each, give me the honest one-sentence placeholder that does not pretend the topic is nonexistent.

Deliver the plan as a single valid JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "sequencing_thesis": two or three sentences naming the one sequencing decision the whole session turns on and why.
- "relied_on_prior_knowledge": array of strings, each stating one piece of the cohort's background that a specified step actually uses.
- "sequence": array of objects with "step" (integer), "minutes" (integer, summing to 75 across all steps), "component" (the idea being installed), "depends_on" (array of earlier step numbers), "why_not_earlier" (what breaks if this step is moved up), "board_at_end" (what is written on the board when the step ends), and "defuses" (array of misconception labels handled here, possibly empty).
- "carrying_example": object with "name", "statement" (the exact way I say it aloud), "must_have" (array), "must_lack" (array), and "rejected_alternatives" (array of objects with "alternative" and "why_it_fails").
- "misconceptions": array of objects with "label", "why_predictable", "defused_at_step" (integer), "defusing_move", and "cost_of_deferring".
- "dominating_cousin": object with "position" (step number), "work_it_does", and "reason_for_position".
- "omissions": array of objects with "topic", "why_out", and "placeholder_line".
- "comprehension_check": object with "task", "answer_that_shows_it_landed", "answer_that_shows_it_did_not", and "what_the_failure_diagnoses".

Write every value as a full phrase or sentence rather than a keyword list, and give me no bibliography: no paper titles, no author attributions, no dates I would have to look up.
