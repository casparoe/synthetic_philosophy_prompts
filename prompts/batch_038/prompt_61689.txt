I write a fortnightly column on environmental regulation and political economy for a general-interest magazine, and each autumn I set the written assessment for the twelve places on our paper's explanatory-journalism fellowship. The exercise has to do two things at once: it must show that a candidate can carry a small formal argument through to a conclusion without being told what the conclusion is, and it must show that the candidate can then render that argument in prose an intelligent lay reader will actually finish. Candidates get two hours, work closed-book, and are told on the front of the paper that citations and reading-list name-dropping earn nothing and will not be read.

This year the subject is Sen's 1970 impossibility result: the claim that a social ranking cannot in general be complete and transitive while honouring both the Pareto principle and a minimal condition of individual liberty. I do not want a question that can be answered by reciting his example about the bawdy novel, or by listing the standard escape routes with their labels attached. I have therefore built a case of my own, and I want the question to make candidates discover what each escape route costs by deriving it.

Please produce three things.

First, the question paper exactly as candidates will see it. It must open with a definitions box, since they have nothing else to work from: the set of states, individual strict preference orderings, the requirement that the social ranking be complete and transitive, unrestricted domain, the Pareto condition, and the liberal condition, each stated formally and each illustrated with one line of plain English. Then the case, which I want reproduced verbatim as I give it here.

Two neighbours, A and B, each have an ash tree on their lot infested by an invasive beetle. Let x = 1 if A has her tree removed and 0 if she leaves it standing; let y = 1 if B has his removed and 0 if he does not. A state is written (x, y). A ranks the states (0,0) above (1,0) above (1,1) above (0,1). B ranks (1,1) above (1,0) above (0,1) above (0,0). C, the chair of the municipal tree board, owns no lot in the matter and ranks (1,1) above (1,0) above (0,0) above (0,1). Under the town's rights charter A is protected over the pairs {(0,0),(1,0)} and {(0,1),(1,1)}, and B over the pairs {(0,0),(0,1)} and {(1,0),(1,1)}.

Then four parts, with the marks I have allocated. Part (a), 4 marks: the town solicitor reads the tree code as also making C protected over A's pair {(0,0),(1,0)}, on the ground that the beetle has been confirmed on A's lot; candidates must show that no complete and transitive social ranking can satisfy the liberal condition for A, B and C together, and must say which of the paper's conditions do and do not figure in that demonstration. Part (b), 8 marks: C's authority is struck down and the liberal condition binds only A and B; candidates must derive the social ranking the conditions force, show that it cannot be transitive, and state precisely where the Pareto step enters — including whether the cycle survives if that step is withdrawn. Part (c), 8 marks: candidates are to consider the rival reading on which a right is not decisiveness over a social ranking but control over one's own choice, so that A and B pick simultaneously, each taking the better of the two states available given the other's pick, and the social outcome is the resulting pair; they must determine what this yields, whether it is unique, whether the result is Pareto-efficient, what the move abandons relative to the theorem as stated, and what A's protected right is actually worth to A at that outcome. Part (d), 5 marks: a 200-word sidebar for readers of my column, explaining what the whole exercise shows about rights-talk in policy disputes, and forbidden from using any of the technical vocabulary introduced in the definitions box.

Second, a model answer of about 900 words that shows the derivations state by state rather than asserting results. I have checked the arithmetic myself: part (a) is unsatisfiable outright, part (b) generates a cycle that genuinely depends on the Pareto step, and part (c) has a unique solution. Please re-verify all three as you write, and flag it plainly if you find I have made an error.

Third, a marking scheme totalling 25 marks that names the specific moves separating a first-class answer from a competent one, part by part, and lists the errors that should cost marks: among them, reporting a cycle in part (b) that never uses Pareto, treating part (a)'s contradiction as though it needed the Pareto condition, mistaking the outcome of the choice procedure in part (c) for a social preference ranking, and pronouncing the paradox dissolved without saying which condition has been given up to dissolve it.
