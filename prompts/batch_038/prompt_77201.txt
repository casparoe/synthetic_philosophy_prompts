I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and lately that's meant a lot of religion-adjacent reporting — I'm putting together a piece on deconversion and the growth of the "nones." Last month I sat down with two people at once, which turned out to be a mistake. One was Dana, who teaches philosophy of religion at a state university and is a practicing Catholic. The other was Ray, who spent eleven years as a youth pastor in Texas and now runs a podcast for people leaving evangelicalism; he stopped believing about six years into the job. I asked them both the same soft question — what it actually felt like when the belief stopped holding — and within about four minutes they'd forgotten I was there and were arguing with each other. I basically just took notes. Here's the exchange as I have it in my notebook, cleaned up a little but not changed in substance:

(1) Ray said the thing that finished him wasn't the problem of evil, it was the silence. He had a woman in his congregation — call her Ruth — who wanted to believe, prayed daily for years, read the apologists, kept showing up, and never once recieved anything he'd call a response. He said if there's a God who wants a relationship with us, Ruth is a counterexample, full stop: you can't be in a relationship with someone you can't find, and a perfectly loving God wouldn't leave a sincere seeker unable to find him. He said this is cleaner than the argument from evil because it doesn't require anyone to weigh suffering against mystery.

(2) Dana said the argument has two problems, one factual and one conceptual. Factual: you don't know Ruth's heart. From the outside, "I sincerely can't believe" and "I'm not looking in the place the answer would be found" are indistinguishable, and there's a long tradition of saying the second is what's really going on — the resistance might be to what belief would cost her rather than to the evidence. Conceptual: the argument assumes God's overriding priority is that everyone hold the true proposition that God exists. But love doesn't require constant self-disclosure. A parent who is transparent to the child at every moment never lets the child become a separate person.

(3) Ray said the first prong makes the whole thing unfalsifiable in a suspicious direction: if every apparent sincere nonbeliever turns out to be secretly resistant, then "God wants a relationship with you" is uncheckable too, and Dana has saved theism by making it compatible with any evidence whatsoever. And he'd known a dozen Ruths, some of whom were far more honest about their own motives than Dana was being about theirs. On the second prong: the parent analogy is the disanalogy. A good parent doesn't withhold the fact that they exist. Withholding a toy, a rule, a reward — that's parenting. Withholding your own existence isn't cultivating autonomy, it's just absence.

(4) Dana conceded that hit but said Ray had narrowed too fast, and made two replies. First: theism isn't committed to God being a parent, and his argument assumes belief is the entry requirement for relationship, which plenty of theologies deny — God relates to people through conscience, through other people, through beauty and the pull of the good, without ever announcing himself propositionally. Second: you'd expect roughly this evidential situation if God wanted our response to be free. A voice from the sky would overwhelm the will; there's light enough for anyone who genuinely wants to see and dark enough for anyone who doesn't, and that balance is the point, not an accident.

(5) Ray said the freedom reply proves too much. If overwhelming evidence destroys freedom, then God has already ranked freedom above the relationship that freedom was supposed to serve — but the premise Dana is defending came from the value of relationship in the first place, so she's conceded it. He also said the empirical claim is false: he's watched people get converted by what they describe as overwhelming evidence, and they didn't come out as puppets, they said they'd gotten freer. And the first reply relocates the problem instead of solving it: Ruth was moved by beauty and had a working conscience, and what she concluded from paying close attention to those things was that there's no person behind them. If God is relating to her through those channels, it's a relationship she can't recognize as one, which is a strange shape for love to have from the inside.

(6) Dana said then they weren't arguing about hiddenness anymore, they were arguing about what God is supposed to be. Ray's argument is lethal against one specific picture — a personal agent who wants explicit, mutually acknowledged relationship with every human being — and she wasn't sure the best theologians in the tradition actually hold that picture. So hiddenness is a problem for a theology, maybe specifically for the popular evangelical one, not for theism as such. And she added, carefully, that Ray had left a pastorate, and the God his argument is built to destroy is the God he was trained to preach. She said that wasn't an ad hominem, it was a point about the argument's scope.

(7) Ray said scope-limited is still worth having, because that personal-seeking God is the one most actual believers worship and the one the tradition claims is revealed rather than inferred. And he accused Dana of motte-and-bailey: when the religion is going well, the personal God who's looking for you is the front door; when the arguments bite, everyone retreats to the God of beauty and conscience, who was never the front door to begin with. He finished by saying that if she's right that sophisticated theism isn't the personal version, then hiddenness is the wrong weapon but a different one gets sharper — the fact that the smartest theologians can't agree on whether God is even the sort of thing that could be looked for is itself evidence, and she's the one supplying it to him.

They both looked at me at the end like I was going to referee, and I didn't, obviously. But now I'm trying to write around this material and I genuinely can't tell who had the better of it, so: can you go through (1) to (7) move by move and say where each side is right, where they're partly right, and where the move just fails? I want the reasons for each rating, not just a running score. Then tell me who's more right overall, and whether that verdict survives Ray's reframe in (7) — that is, is the disagreement at the end still a substantive one, or has it quietly turned into an arguement about what the word "God" means? And what did neither of them bring up that they should have? I have a vague sense that at least two things are missing, something about whether one Ruth is even the right unit of analysis for this kind of argument, and something about how nonbelief is distributed across populations and geographies rather than just about individual seekers, but I couldn't have pressed them on either in the moment. Last thing, and it's a practical one: if I describe the hiddenness argument in the piece as the strongest argument for atheism, is that defensible given how this exchange actually went, or would I be overstating it?
