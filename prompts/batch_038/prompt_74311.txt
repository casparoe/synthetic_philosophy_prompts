I am a graduate teaching assistant on a second-year undergraduate module called Empire and its Afterlives, and next Thursday I run one of the fifty-minute seminar sections attached to it. There are eighteen students, a whiteboard, and no slides. They will have come in having read the programmatic opening of Niall Ferguson's Empire: How Britain Made the Modern World and a chapter from Shashi Tharoor's Inglorious Empire: What the British Did to India, which is to say they will have read two authors who appear to be doing the same exercise — totting up credits and debits of British rule in India and reporting a net figure — and reaching opposite totals. When the cohort last met, the hour collapsed into a referendum: one side produced infrastructure and administrative unification, the other produced famine mortality, deindustrialized handloom weaving and the drain of revenue, and nothing anyone said changed anyone's mind, because nobody could say what would change anyone's mind. The module convenor has set them a comparative essay for the end of term — I am inventing the question I plan to prep them toward, roughly: do Ferguson and Tharoor disagree about the evidence, or about the terms on which evidence is allowed to count? — and I want the seminar to make that question answerable rather than merely rhetorical. I trained in development economics before moving into history, which is probably why the frame I keep reaching for is an accounting one, and I would like you to stress-test it before I build a class around it.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not filling in the same ledger with different numbers but are using different accounting conventions, and that at least five distinct conventions are in play and get run together by students: the choice of counterfactual baseline (what the subcontinent looks like absent British rule, and whether a fragmented set of successor states, a different imperial power, or an endogenous modernization path is the relevant comparator); the treatment of goods produced under coercion and for the extractor's own purposes (whether a railway network laid out for troop movement and export freight can be booked as a credit to the governed, and whether its existence-value to later generations survives its extraction-purpose); aggregation versus distribution (whether gains to some can offset losses to others, and whether the colonized get standing as ledger subjects at all); the accounting of foreclosed alternatives as opportunity cost rather than as mere absence; and finally whether the ledger form itself is the right output — whether a single net figure is what the question demands, or whether there is a residue, involving consent and self-rule, that no arithmetic of totals can discharge. Please tell me honestly whether this framing holds up, whether any of the five collapse into one another or are smuggling in a substantive moral claim while looking procedural, and whether there is a sixth convention I am missing that in fact does most of the work.

Then plan the seminar for me rather than explaining the material to me. I need the order in which the pieces must go in and what depends on what — I suspect the counterfactual-baseline point has to be secured before anything else can be said coherently, but I cannot tell whether it should come before or after the students have been shown a case where the conventions visibly diverge. Tell me which single historical case should carry the whole hour, and specify what that case must contain and must conspicuously lack in order to do the job; I am torn between the railways and a famine, and I worry that either one, chosen badly, will let students treat the exercise as a moral verdict on a single atrocity rather than as an argument about bookkeeping. Map the misconceptions they will bring in and tell me the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off: my guesses are that Ferguson is being read as offering a moral endorsement rather than a causal claim, that Tharoor is being read as nationalist mythmaking rather than as an argument about mechanisms, that unverifiable counterfactuals make the whole debate meaningless, that better data would dissolve the disagreement, and that conceding any benefit is complicity — but tell me which of these is actually the load-bearing one and which I am overestimating. Give me one thing I can put on the board or ask at the end that will show me whether the conventions-not-evidence distinction has landed, as opposed to whether they can repeat my vocabulary. And tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level and say out loud that I am leaving out — I already suspect the cliometrics of real wages, the sovereignty and international-law arguments, and comparison with other European empires will not fit, but I would rather you made the cuts. I do not want a role-play, a mock trial, or a debate format; I have tried those with this cohort and they reward performance rather than comprehension.
