I'm a junior and I basically run my school's philosophy club — it's like eleven people in the back of the library on Thursdays, half of whom are only there because it counts as a club credit. Two weeks ago we tried to do a session on why promises actually bind you and it was a disaster, but not the fun kind. Everyone claimed a different theory. One guy said promises only matter because of the disappointment you'd cause, someone else said you create the obligation yourself by willing it, a third said a promise is just a move in a social game like castling in chess, and a girl said the whole point is that you've now handed the other person a right against you specifically. Then I ran them through five cases and they all gave the exact same five answers. So I want to build the thing properly as a sorting quiz, becuase I'm pretty sure most of them are picking whichever label sounds most impressive and then answering the cases with their gut like everyone else.

Here's what I want. Five or six branch questions, each one a concrete case with two or three options, worded so a person can answer without having any idea which position their answer leads to — nothing like "do you think obligations come from consequences," that's exactly the kind of question my club answers with their ego instead of their brain. The cases I'd want you to build it out of: a promise made to someone on their deathbed (burn these letters, give the money to this person) where they die before you could break it anyway and nobody will ever find out; a promise that got squeezed out of you by someone who had leverage over you; a promise you made to yourself; a promise about something you can't actually guarantee, like promising you'll be at the airport at six when your car might not start; and a promise to keep a secret where telling the secret would prevent something genuinely bad. Add one more if you think there's a better diagnostic hiding in the space. One of the kids said Nietzsche apparently thinks only a certain kind of person has the prerogative to promise at all, which might be a real branch or might be a red herring — you tell me.

Then the leaves. I want the named positions: the consequences/disappointment one, the self-created-obligation one, the social-practice one, the second-personal rights one, and at least one deflationary option saying a promise isn't a special kind of obligation at all, plus anything else that genuinely deserves its own leaf. But build the space first and put the names in after — show which combination of answers goes with which leaf, then say which combinations of answers no actual position occupies, and for each of those say whether nobody's there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody has bothered. Also flag honestly any two leaves your tree can't seperate; I suspect at least two of them collapse onto identical answers, and if so I want to know what extra case would pry them apart, if one exists. Every leaf should get one sentence on its most awkward commitment, the thing its own defenders don't like bringing up. And end with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves, including a tiebreak rule for people who split right down the middle. Please don't give me citations or a reading list or tell us to go look anything up, we're not doing that, we just want the instrument.
