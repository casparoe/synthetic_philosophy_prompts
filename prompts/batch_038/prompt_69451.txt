I've been keeping a running list of my own gut verdicts about claims concerning the future — sea-battle style, but with my own examples — and I can't get them to hang together. Here they are, roughly in the order I jotted them down. (1) "There will be a naval battle in the strait tomorrow": I don't think that's true, and I don't think its negation is true either; it's just open. (2) But "there will be a battle tomorrow or there won't" — that one I flat-out can't deny, it's true. (3) Same for tomorrow's coin toss: neither *it lands heads* nor *it doesn't* is true right now. (4) A total eclipse next April that the astronomers have computed down to the minute: that one feels straightforwardly true to me, not open at all — though I'll admit I have no non-question-begging reason for treating it differently from the battle, since if the physics is deterministic both are equally fixed by the present state of the world. This is the one I keep flip-flopping on. (5) Now suppose the battle does happen. Then it seems to me that yesterday's claim was already true yesterday — I want to say I'd have gotten something right, not that the world retroactively supplied a truth value once the ships showed up. (6) I'm perfectly happy to assign 0.4 credence to the battle and take a bet at 2:3 either way, but I don't think that number is just measuring my ignorance about which of two truth values obtains, since on my view there aren't two to be ignorant between. (7) The weather report saying outright "it will rain tomorrow" isn't a lie or a mistake even if it turns out dry, and yet if it stays dry I still want to say the forecast was *wrong* — wrong in some way that doesn't look like having asserted a falsehood. (8) I think an omniscient being could have known yesterday that there'd be a battle, and I also think that knowledge doesn't make the battle inevitable in the sense that would take away my ability to call it off. (9) "There was a battle yesterday" is either true or false, full stop, even if I have no idea which — so on my picture truth-value gaps are a strictly future-only disease, and I have no story about why the direction of time should get to decide whether sentences have truth values at all.

The principle I've got in mind, which I want you to test rather than assume: a claim is true now iff every way the world could still go from here makes it come out true, false iff no way does, and neither otherwise. It seems to handle (1), (2) and (3) cleanly. But I think it wrecks (5), because yesterday there were still branches with no battle, so yesterday's claim wasn't true then, and it also leaves me no idea what the being in (8) knew. So: give me a principle, or a small set of them, that actually covers this ground. I want each clause stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, each clause tagged with which of my verdicts motivates it, and the resulting account run over cases I haven't listed. The unlisted ones I care about: "there will be a battle tomorrow and it's not true that there will be a battle tomorrow"; "either it's true that it will rain tomorrow or it's not true that it will rain tomorrow"; "it might be that there's a battle tomorrow" compared with "it's settled that there'll be a battle tomorrow"; the conditional "if there's a battle tomorrow, some ships will be sunk" alongside its contrapositive — do those two really come out the same?; "an hour ago it was true that it will rain today"; and a mixed one, "Caesar crossed the Rubicon or there will be a battle tomorrow." Don't bother with names or a reading list, I want the machinery, not the arguement's genealogy.

For the answer, please give me a table first, one row per candidate principle (or per package of clauses, if they only work as a bundle), with these columns exactly: `candidate` (short label), `statement` (the principle spelled out precisely, quantifiers and all), `verdicts_saved` (which of (1)–(9) it delivers), `unlisted_cases` (what it says about each of the six cases above, briefly), `verdicts_broken` (which of (1)–(9) it forces me to give up), `cost` (one line on what the principle has to swallow to do it). Then after the table, a short bottom line: which single package you'd actually adopt, which of my verdicts you think I should drop, and whether there's a hard pair in my list that no reasonably simple principle can save — and if there is, say which pair and why its the pair rather than some other one.
