I help run a board-game club and have taught myself game theory from borrowed textbooks, but I have no formal training, so last week I could not referee an argument between two of the regulars. Please act as a professional game theorist, work carefully through the exchange before ruling on it, and test any claim that matters against a small example of your own construction.

Their argument was about pruning a game by dominance.

(1) Marcus opened by saying that if you delete strictly dominated strategies one at a time, the set left standing at the end never depends on the order in which you deleted them, so the outcome of iterated strict dominance is well defined.

(2) Elena replied that this is an artefact of only ever comparing pure strategies with pure strategies: allow a mixture to do the dominating, she said, and you can prune something in one order that survives in another, at which point the procedure stops having a determinate answer.

(3) Marcus answered that mixtures are entitled to dominate in any case, because a pure strategy can be unbeaten by every other pure strategy yet lose to a fifty-fifty mixture, and since a player is perfectly free to randomise, picking such a strategy is simply irrational — pruning it is not a technicality but the whole point of the exercise.

(4) Elena conceded that much but drew the opposite lesson: if a strategy beaten by a mixture can still figure in a mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium, then dominance pruning is deleting things a rational player may well do, so either the pruning is unsound or Nash equilibrium is; she added that the whole business presupposes common knowledge of rationality rather than mere rationality, and therefore buys less than it looks.

Rate each of these four moves on a scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, giving one numbered item per move, and where only part of a move fails, say precisely which part and why the rest holds. Spend the fifth numbered item on an assumption the two of them shared without examining. Deliver exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion, in British English.
