My phil-of-science reading group is working through Wigner's piece on how unreasonably well mathematics fits the physical world, and one guy keeps deflating the whole thing with the same picture: the space of possible mathematics is like Borges' library -- every book that could ever be written is already sitting on some shelf, so of course physics eventually pulls one down that happens to describe the world, and the "miracle" is just the Texas sharpshooter painting targets around where his bullets landed. He uses it to argue there's nothing left to explain, that Wigners puzzlement is a failure of imagination about how big the mathematical possibility space is.

Can you audit that for me? First the mapping, explicitly -- what plays the library, the shelves, the individual volume, the wandering reader, the one book that turns out to be true-ish about us, and what plays the sheer combinatorial abundance. Then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually bite against his use of it and the ones that are just decoration. I'm especially interested in whether a Borges-style library can capture the specific thing Wigner seems most struck by: a structure worked out for internal reasons -- non-Euclidean geometry, group theory, Hilbert spaces -- later turning up as the right description of a domain nobody was aiming at, and even licensing predictions. Does an abundance-of-books picture handle that, or does it only handle the weaker claim that some math fits somewhere?

Finally, take a side. Does the deflation survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole conclusion? If you think it's the wrong picture, give me a better one and say what that replacement does and doesn't license. Don't give me "both readings have their merits" -- commit to a verdict and argue for it.
