I am giving the opening remarks at the first plenary of a three-day workshop convened by a biodiversity-informatics consortium. The funded task is narrow and concrete: agree on how a species-level record should be represented in the shared taxonomic backbone that eleven databases will then be required to map onto. The room is about ninety people, split three ways. Roughly half are database engineers and ontology specialists who want a schema that validates, resolves to one stable identifier per record, and does not need a human curator to adjudicate every load; they have been told, correctly, that the philosophical dispute over species is old and unsettled, and they intend to wait it out. The rest are working taxonomists, and they are not one school: phylogenetic splitters who want every diagnosable lineage named; lumpers who think diagnosability is an artefact of how much material happens to be in a drawer; people who work on oaks and will tell you hybridization makes lineage boundaries porous in both directions; people who work on bdelloid rotifers and will tell you the entire debate presupposes sex; and two conservation lawyers, who are there because whichever concept wins the schema decides which lineages get legal protection, and who know that the count of listed units moves by a factor of several depending on that choice.

Please write the remarks: five to seven minutes spoken, so on the order of nine hundred words. The philosophy has to carry the structure of the speech rather than decorate it. I want it built on the individuality thesis pressed by Ghiselin and Hull — that a species is not a natural kind but a particular, a single spatiotemporally extended historical individual of which organisms are parts rather than members, the way cells are parts of you and not members of the club of you. The thesis should be used, not announced as a thesis. The thing the speech should make the room feel is this: our data model is already committed against it before anyone argues a word. An ontology that renders an organism's relation to its species as a membership relation such as is_a or instance_of, that treats a taxon name as carrying a definition whose satisfaction an organism either meets or fails, and that requires every record to resolve to exactly one accepted species identifier, has taken a position in the species problem — the position that species are kinds — and has taken it silently, by default, inside a schema, where nobody has to defend it. Get the engineers to see that the metaphysics is in their data types. Then turn that into the workshop's actual agenda: because species may be individuals rather than kinds, the deliverable cannot be a definition of species and should instead be a list of requirements any representation must meet. The speech should hand the room its task, not its answer.

Two further constraints. The speech must not name a winner among the species concepts, and no camp may hear its concept designated as the default; but it also must not soften into an anything-goes pluralism, which the splitters will despise and the lawyers will find useless. It should treat the ring-species complexes and the asexual lineages as cases any representation will have to fail at visibly rather than hide, on the ground that a record which lies silently is worse than one which reports its own uncertainty. Include one good joke that lands with the engineers and the taxonomists simultaneously — about identifiers, or about naming, or about the fact that half the room thinks the other half is splitting hairs while the other half thinks half the room is lumping evidence — and let it function as an invitation rather than a lecture. Nothing may be quoted or cited: no philosopher's name, no title, no paper, in the spoken text, since the metaphysics has to survive being said plainly to people who will not look anything up. Close on something the room can act on Monday morning.

After the speech, add a brief note in your own voice rather than the speaker's. State how confident you are in the load-bearing claims — above all, that the individuality thesis really has the schema consequence I have described, and that recasting the organism-to-species relation as a parthood relation rather than a membership relation is the right way to express it — and single out the one point you are least sure of: whether that is a technical objection from the ontology side I have not anticipated, a camp in the room whose complaint the speech underplays, or a place where the speech has quietly assumed a contested reading of the individuality thesis in order to make the argument go through.
