ok, context first, because it's slightly embarrassing. For about a year I've been doing unpaid grant vetting for a tiny fund some friends of mine run — maybe thirty applications a round, and we're supposed to rank them, which means comparing a small certain good against a longshot with an astronomically big payoff if it works. That drags you straight into expected-value reasoning, and last month somebody on the committee raised the Pasadena game: the coin-flip thing where the first heads comes up on toss n and you win 2^n/n dollars if n is odd and lose that much if n is even, so the expectation is the alternating harmonic series. It sums to ln 2, but only conditionally — reorder the terms and you can get any value you like — which is supposed to show the "just maximize expected value" program is on shakier ground than we act like it is. I'm also pretty sure there's a variant where the sum doesn't converge at all, so there isn't even a conditional value to fix by reordering.

Our senior guy, call him R, killed the objection in about ninety seconds with an analogy, and I've been chewing on it ever since. He said expected value just *is* a center of mass — same integral, you're finding the balance point of a rod whose density at each position is the probability of that payoff — so a game with a non-convergent expectation is a badly built seesaw. There's no balance point, the object is defective, and the right response is the physicist's: no such thing, ill-posed question, shrug, walk away; whereas any real rod of finite length balances somewhere. So the paradox is an artifact of a pathological object rather than evidence that anything is wrong with multiplying probabilities by payoffs, and then we went back to ranking applications like nothing had happened. Part of me thinks thats too easy — a physical rod's density is fixed by nature and any divergence would come from idealizing it as infinitely long, while the Pasadena game is stipulated exactly, term by term, and the order-dependence is a theorem about the series rather than a modeling fudge — but I can't get past that intuition into an argument, and R is much better at talking than I am.
