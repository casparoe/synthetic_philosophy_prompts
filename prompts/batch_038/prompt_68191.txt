I was auditing a reading group on population ethics and long-run ethics last month, and two of the regulars — Nadia and Owen — got into an argument that went three rounds past the point where anyone else could follow it. They have since asked me to find a neutral referee, and I have no idea who is right, so I am passing the exchange along as faithfully as I can. I have numbered the moves, because the two of them disagreed about which moves were even responsive to which.

Background both accepted without argument: the familiar simulation reasoning on which, if technologically mature civilizations with vast computing resources exist and choose to run detailed simulations containing observers like us, then such simulations would vastly outnumber unsimulated civilizations, so we should regard ourselves as probably simulated.

1. **Nadia:** If we are probably in a simulation, then our future is probably not ours to shape. Simulations are paused, sped up, parameterized, re-run, and switched off; a simulation of a civilization's early technological period is typically kept going only for as long as the researchers find it informative. An astronomically long, self-directed future is precisely the thing a simulation is least likely to have. So the simulation hypothesis is not a curiosity that sits outside long-run ethics — it is a reason to discount the far future heavily.

2. **Owen:** That undermines itself. The reason to think we are simulated is the premise that some civilizations survive their dangerous early centuries and go on to command enormous resources. The hypothesis Nadia is invoking is therefore itself evidence that extremely long futures are achieved somewhere, and often. Taking it seriously should raise, not lower, the credence that the far future is a real possibility rather than a fantasy.

3. **Nadia:** Be careful which future. Owen's premise says other civilizations get there. It says nothing about ours, and it makes ours worse: ancestor simulations are numerous exactly because they are cheap, and cheap runs are the disposable ones. Conceding that we are in the reference class of simulated runs means conceding that we are in the class of things that get terminated, not the class of things that decide when to terminate.

4. **Owen:** Grant all of that and the conclusion still does not move. If the unsimulated branch contains an astronomically large number of future lives and the simulated-and-truncated branch contains a merely very large number, then unless Nadia is nearly certain of very early truncation, the far future still dominates every competing consideration by orders of magnitude. Robustness to exactly this kind of uncertainty is what the long-run-priority position is sold on.

5. **Nadia:** Then Owen has proved too much. If a truncated simulated future still dominates, the simulation hypothesis is doing no work in the calculation at all — in which case it cannot be doing work in move 2 either. Either the hypothesis is decision-relevant, in which case the termination risk from move 3 bites and move 4's dominance claim is simply an assumption wearing a conclusion's clothes; or it is decision-irrelevant, in which case both of Owen's moves collapse and there was never a dispute.

6. **Owen:** The dilemma assumes the two uses are symmetric, and they are not. In move 2 I used the hypothesis as evidence about a *rate* — how often civilizations make it through the early filter at all — which is the quantity our own long-run survival turns on. In move 4 I treated it as a *discount on the size* of our future. Rate evidence and size discounts enter the calculation in different places, so a single hypothesis can be relevant through one channel and irrelevant through the other without contradiction.
