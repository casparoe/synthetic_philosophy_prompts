Thomas Jefferson's position on generational sovereignty is clear enough: no generation can bind the next, the earth is held by the living in usufruct, and on his arithmetic both a constitution and the public debt ought to lapse on their own roughly nineteen years after being made, so that each generation legislates afresh rather than being governed from the grave. Madison's reply — that continuity is what makes improvement, credit, and obligation possible — is the road actually taken. Now put Jefferson in front of three modern entrenchments: a fiscal rule written into a national constitution requiring a near-balanced structural budget, alterable only by the same supermajority that could amend the constitution itself; a legally entrenched emissions-reduction trajectory supervised by an independent expert council whose members a future parliament cannot readily remove; and the clause in Article V of the United States Constitution under which no state may be deprived of its equal suffrage in the Senate without its own consent — an entrenchment the amendment procedure cannot reach even unanimously among the living.

Give me a definite ruling on each of the three: which would Jefferson accept as legitimate self-binding, which would he reject as dead-hand rule, and would he concede the general principle that a polity may lock in a decision when the stakes are high and the future majority's judgment is predictable? Then say whether what decides the verdict for him is the mere fact of binding the future or the availability of a costly but real exit — the first two entrenchments leave one open, the third does not and cannot — and, if it is not that, say what is. Anchor each ruling in positions he actually held, mark plainly where you are extrapolating beyond the record, and give me a firm prediction that could turn out wrong rather than a balanced survey of how he might have gone either way.
