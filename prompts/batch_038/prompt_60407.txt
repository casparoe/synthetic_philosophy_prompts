I'm a junior, and this term I'm enrolled in a seminar on free will and moral responsibility. I've run into a disagreement that the vocabulary we've been given doesn't seem to fit, and I'd like to know which side is closer to the truth — or whether the seminar's framing is simply the wrong one for it.

Since last spring my roommate has run his life on a fixed template. He wakes at five-thirty, works in three-hour blocks separated by two fixed breaks, rotates through the same four meals, and is asleep by ten. He keeps a printed grid of the week taped to the inside of his closet door. When something forces a change — a fire drill, a rescheduled class, a friend who needs a ride at four — he doesn't absorb it into the day as it stands; he goes back to the closet, redraws the whole grid, and the same blocks reappear in the same order, shifted. He calls the template a scaffold that he intends to take down once he no longer needs it. He was saying that in September.

The template was a response to a bad year: an unstructured semester in which he stopped attending anything and very nearly lost his academic standing. Last month my cousin drove six hours to see me and had about two hours free on a Tuesday evening, and I asked whether he'd join us for dinner. He said no. I could tell from his face that he wasn't weighing a preference; he was following a rule. When I told him he'd turned himself into a machine, he was genuinely angry, and he made an argument I haven't been able to answer. He said that he chose the template deliberately, that he could abandon it on any morning he liked, and that his week is therefore the only thing in our apartment that belongs to the person living in it. My life, he said, is the mechanical one: I decided nothing, I do whatever I happen to feel like when the moment arrives, and my moods push me around in exactly the way his schedule doesn't push him.

Two things are tangled together here and I can't separate them. First, in fourteen months he has never once broken the template, as far as I know. Does his claim that he could stop on any morning mean anything if it has never been tested — and if I suspect that on the morning he tried, the very reasons that built the template would be waiting there to stop him? Second, I'm not sure what happened with my cousin counts as a choice at all. He acknowledged the reason to come, said he'd like to come, and then did the other thing, apparently without effort and without regret.

Our seminar has spent the term on determinism and on whether blame survives it, and none of that touches this. Nobody is disputing the causal history of the world. We're disputing whether a person who has closed most of his doors is less free than one who has left them all open, and which of us is more likely to end up being moved by something other than himself. Is his argument sound? Is anything I've said about him actually an objection, or am I just resenting a discipline I don't have? And is there anything worth saying to him about Tuesday, or would raising it only be that resentment talking?
