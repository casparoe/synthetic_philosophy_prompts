I read one of those Sunday-magazine philosophy columns, the one about whether humanity should be aiming at extinction, and it summarized Benatar's asymetry like this:

"Benatar's argument runs: pain is bad and pleasure is good, but the absence of pain is a good even when there is nobody around to enjoy it, while the absence of pleasure is bad only if there is somebody left to be deprived of it. So for anyone who doesn't yet exist, coming into being can only ever add a downside and never an upside, since the pleasures a life would contain are precisely what non-existence is already credited with not needing. That's why procreation is always wrong, and it makes no difference how wonderful the particular life would turn out to be. Note also that the whole theory is about harms and benefits to individuals: because the alternative to being born isn't being a worse-off person but not being a person at all, Benatar is stuck with Parfit's non-identity problem, and has to say the child is wronged without ever being harmed. The upshot, which he accepts, is that the decent thing is for humanity to die out painlessly. He does draw back from telling anyone to kill themselves, on the ground that once you exist you acquire an interest in going on existing — but nothing in the asymmetry explains where that interest is supposed to come from, and once you see the edifice is propped up by an unexplained partiality for people who already happen to be here, the argument falls apart."

How much of that is fair? Can you go through it claim by claim and say which parts Benatar or a careful defender would happily sign off on, which are misreadings rather than disagreements, whether the non-identity parallel actually applies to him, and whether that final charge about the interest in going on living does any work once the earlier misreadings get fixed? I actually find the columnist's conclusion appealing, but I have a feeling the summary is doing something sneaky somewhere in the middle and I can't locate it.
