A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the framework that effective altruism–adjacent groups use to prioritize causes — importance, tractability, neglectedness — and we got nowhere, largely because we kept sliding between abstract complaints. The reason I am writing to you is that a week earlier I sat in on a much more concrete version of the same fight. I was at a two-day workshop where about a dozen people were practising how to screen grant applications for a small family foundation, and two of the participants ended up in a real, substantive dispute that I think is far more adjudicable than the one I had. Neither of them was a strawman, and I honestly could not tell who was winning. Here is the exchange as near as I can reproduce it. I will call them D and M.

(1) M opened by objecting to the workshop's own template, which asked reviewers to score each factor from 1 to 5 and multiply. M said the product is used as though it were a measurement — as though it ranked expected impact — when multiplication is only meaningful if the factors are separable contributions to a single quantity, and these are not. Neglectedness, M argued, is mostly downstream of tractability: causes sit unfunded because funder after funder looked and concluded they could not be moved. So a low tractability rating and a high neglectedness score are two readings of one underlying judgment, and multiplying them penalizes a hard cause twice for a single fact.

(2) D replied that this is not double counting but the correct discount. If every informed funder who looked passed, that is evidence the cause really is intractable, and being marked down once by your own estimate and again by theirs is how correlated evidence ought to compound. D added that the genuine independence failure runs the other way: tractability is not fixed at the value you write down, because your entry changes it — attention recruits talent, generates tooling, produces data — and neglectedness is the framework's crude proxy for how much headroom your entry creates.

(3) M said that if that is what neglectedness is doing, it is mislabeled. What D was describing is not how neglected a cause is but something like traction elasticity: how much tractability rises per unit of new attention. Those come apart in both directions — a crowded field can be highly elastic, because many people working on it make the next transformative idea reachable, and an empty field can be perfectly inelastic, because nobody works on it since there is nothing to work on. M's proposal was to drop the three ratings and estimate the quantity they were circling: the derivative of impact with respect to resources, evaluated at the current funding level.

(4) D said that is the ideal and also unusable, and that the ordinal scores have two virtues a returns curve lacks. First, they force you to notice where you have no information, whereas a curve estimate will hand you a number that looks like knowledge. Second, the multiplicative form encodes a veto: a zero anywhere kills the cause, which is what a screening procedure should do. A derivative-based estimate has no veto structure and will happily rank a cause highly when its entire value rests on one shaky parameter.

(5) M answered that the veto is the bug rather than the feature, because multiplicative screening is precisely what keeps a cause in the neglected column indefinitely: anything scoring zero on tractability never gets the attempt that would have shown the score was wrong, so the framework confirms itself. And the charge of spurious precision cuts both ways, M said, since 1-to-5 scores silently assume the distance between a 2 and a 3 is the same for importance as for neglectedness and the same across causes, and then multiply those gaps together.

(6) D conceded the self-confirmation worry had some force but said it pointed to a fix, not a replacement: the three factors are scored at different levels — importance attaches to outcomes, tractability to interventions, neglectedness to the funding landscape — so multiplying them at all is a category error until you pin the level down, and once you do, the scores can be recalibrated. M's closing reply was that pinning the level down is the whole argument: pin it to the marginal dollar and all three factors collapse into one quantity, expected marginal impact per dollar, with neglectedness surviving only as a discount reflecting how far your estimate should be pulled toward other informed funders' revealed judgments.

Please work through this move by move rather than giving me a summary verdict up front. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and justify the rating on the merits — I want the reasoning, not a score sheet. Then tell me who was more right overall, and whether any part of the dispute turned out to be merely verbal, since my suspicion coming out of my own argument is that the question of whether the framework is a measurement or a heuristic was doing unearned work in both directions. Finally, name what both of them missed, if anything: premises they shared without arguing for, or an issue neither raised.

Two housekeeping requests. Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, even where a list would be tidier. And at the end, tell me how confident you are in your overall verdict, and single out the one place in your own adjudication where you are least sure you have it right, along with what would change your mind about it.
