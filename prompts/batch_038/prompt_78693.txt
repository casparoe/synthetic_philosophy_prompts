Kurt Gödel died in 1978, and so never saw how the controversy over his cosmological work developed. I should like a reasoned prediction of how he would have responded to that subsequent history.

Background, as compactly as I can manage. In 1949 Gödel published an exact solution of Einstein's field equations describing a universe filled with pressureless dust together with a negative cosmological constant tuned to the dust density, in which the whole of matter rotates and which is free of singularities. Its notorious feature is that closed timelike curves pass through every point: a suitably equipped observer could travel into his own past, there is no global time function, and hence no way of decomposing the spacetime into a succession of presents ordered as earlier and later. Gödel's philosophical use of the solution was not that we inhabit such a universe, but that the theory permits one. He took the ordinary conception of time to involve an objective lapse and a privileged present; he thought special relativity had already rendered simultaneity frame-dependent, and that the familiar rejoinder — that a frame-independent causal ordering survives, so the block universe retains an objective temporal structure — was disposed of once one possessed a solution in which no time ordering of any kind is definable. He added a distinct premise: whether time exists in that sense cannot depend on the merely contingent distribution of matter, since a feature so fundamental to our manifest picture of the world cannot be an empirical accident; the possibility of such worlds therefore tells against the reality of time, not merely against our knowledge of it. He also held it an open empirical question whether the actual universe rotates, and was inclined to think that it might.

Now suppose Gödel were shown the following five developments.

First, observational cosmology has found no trace of global rotation. The near-isotropy of the cosmic microwave background and large-scale structure surveys place tight upper bounds on any cosmic vorticity, and the concordance model is an expanding universe with a positive cosmological constant whose global structure bears no resemblance to his.

Second, in the early 1990s Hawking proposed that the laws of physics forbid the appearance of closed timelike curves. Semiclassical calculations for would-be time machines — notably a traversable wormhole one mouth of which is taken on a relativistic round trip — show vacuum fluctuations piling up on themselves and driving the renormalised stress-energy to divergence at the moment a chronology horizon would form, and later work generalised the result. The conjecture concedes that classical general relativity permits such curves, and it is vulnerable from two directions: spacetimes have been exhibited in which the divergence does not occur, and semiclassical gravity is in any case untrustworthy at the scales where it is being invoked.

Third, an asymmetry concerning exotic matter has emerged. Wormhole- and warp-drive-style time machines appear to require matter violating the null energy condition; Gödel's universe requires only ordinary dust and a negative cosmological constant, but its closed timelike curves lie so far from any observer, and demand such extreme acceleration and energy to reach, that no actual traveller could exploit them.

Fourth, shortly before his death a definability theorem was proved for Minkowski spacetime: taking as given only an inertial observer's worldline and the relation of causal connectibility, standard Einstein simultaneity turns out to be the unique non-trivial equivalence relation definable from them. This is widely taken to have settled the conventionality of simultaneity against the conventionalists, and Gödel never engaged with it.

Fifth, in quantum gravity the Wheeler–DeWitt equation contains no external time parameter, and a substantial school now holds that time is not fundamental at all but emergent — recovered from correlations between subsystems, or from a thermal state, rather than from the geometry. Nothing here owes anything to rotation.

For each of the five, say what Gödel would have made of it, and classify his response as a concession (he gives ground on the conclusion that time in the ordinary sense is unreal), a relocation (he keeps the conclusion but shifts it onto a different premise), an intensification (he reads the development as further support), or a dismissal (he treats it as beside the point). Justify each classification from his specific commitments as set out above, rather than from how a present-day philosopher of physics would react to the same evidence.

Then answer three further questions. The developments seem to pull his two available readings apart: the strong claim that time does not exist, and the weaker claim that time fails to exist in worlds of the Gödel type, with ours being an open empirical matter. To which would he retreat, if either, and what in the position described above makes you think so? Second, which single development would he regard as most threatening, and which would he most readily enlist as an ally? Third, give me a number between 0 and 1 for the probability that, having seen all five, he would still assert without qualification that time does not exist in the ordinary sense, and say briefly what moves that number.

Please write in continuous prose, with one short paragraph per development followed by a short section answering the three further questions. Reason from the content given above and from whatever you reliably know of Gödel's philosophical temper. I am not looking for references or a reading list: do not invent quotations, and where you are unsure of a text, describe the idea rather than naming it. Flag explicitly any point at which you are extrapolating well beyond what his commitments support.
