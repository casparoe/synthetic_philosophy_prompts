I am a novelist, and I am at work on a book whose conceit obliges me to render, with some fidelity, the reactions of the late Tolstoy to a body of historiography that did not exist in his lifetime. I do not want a summary of what he did think about history; I have read the long argument on freedom and necessity in the second part of the epilogue and I know its shape. What I need is your judgement of how he would have answered a question nobody ever put to him, and a map of where my own extrapolation is likely to betray him.

The question is this. It has become increasingly plausible that the destruction of the Grande Armée in 1812 was accomplished less by Kutuzov's decision to abandon Moscow, by Napoleon's five idle weeks in the burnt city, or by Russian resolve, than by louse-borne typhus, by dysentery and enteric fever, by starvation and by cold; and molecular work on the remains of soldiers buried in mass graves and hospital sites along the line of retreat, at Vilnius among them, has been read as confirming that epidemic disease did a very large share of the killing. Beyond that case lies a whole literature that now treats pathogens, climates and eruptions as movers of events: plague following the trade routes, the volcanic winter after a great eruption, the cooling of the seventeenth century and its harvest failures, Bronze Age societies unravelling under drought. This writing routinely speaks of such things as actors, and sometimes as agents.

Tolstoy would presumably welcome the demotion of the general. That is the obvious answer and I do not want it. I want to know whether he could welcome it consistently. His positive account, as I read it, does not merely deny that great men cause events; it proposes that history becomes a science only when it stops hunting for causes in individual wills and instead takes up the infinitesimally small units — the homogeneous impulses of innumerable people — and integrates them, much as the calculus handles its differentials. Underwriting that proposal is an epistemology: we know what causation is at all only because we are conscious of our own willing, and freedom and necessity are not rival descriptions of one thing but vary inversely with how much of the conditions we can see. A rickettsia has no will and no consciousness. It is not the sort of thing whose operations we grasp from within.

So: would he have taken disease history as the completion of his own argument, or as a relapse into the vice he was attacking — the substitution of a fresh external idol, the louse and the frost and the ash cloud, for the old one, the emperor — a substitution that throws away the only access to causation history ever had? Would he have judged the word agency, applied to a bacterium, a harmless shorthand or a confusion about what an account of events owes its reader?

Please set out the following in connected prose rather than in fragments, with a reason attached to each judgement you offer:

- His likely response to the empirical claim, kept cleanly separate from his response to the conceptual one. My suspicion is that he would concede the first without the least difficulty and consider it beside the point; tell me whether that is right.
- Which of his commitments actually does the work in your answer, and how much weight each bears: the epistemology of causation through the consciousness of willing; the inverse relation of freedom to knowledge of conditions; his fury at historians who convert necessity into an excuse for the conduct of powerful men; and the later religious conviction that the meaning of history is moral, and that the question of causes is subordinate to how a person ought to live.
- Whether the answer differs between the Tolstoy who composed that closing argument in the late 1860s and the Tolstoy of the 1890s onwards. If it differs, say what changes and why.
- Whether he would regard the caution that now attends this literature — the standing warning against environmental determinism, against explaining human choices away by reference to harvests and infections — as a necessary correction or as a squeamish retreat. Would he side with the peasants against the climate, so to speak, or hold that the peasants' own choosing had already been swallowed?
- The test I can apply to my own draft: what a Tolstoy written by someone who has absorbed only the caricature — fatalist, denier of heroes, mystic of the swarm — would say in this scene, and how the real thing would differ in tone, in what it declines to say, and in what it would find objectionable about my historian's vocabulary.
- Where the question is simply underdetermined by anything he wrote, such that I would be inventing rather than extrapolating; and, where that is so, which direction of invention is least dishonest to him.

Do not offer references, sources or a reading list; I am not auditing your scholarship, I am trying to hear a voice. Please write in British English.
