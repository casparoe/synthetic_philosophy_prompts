ok so I'm a junior and I've gone pretty deep down a rabbit hole on Bertrand's chord problem for my school's math club, and I want an actual argument from you, not a survey.

I get the three usual ways of picking the chord (two random endpoints on the circle, a random point along a radius, a random midpoint anywhere inside the disk) and I get why they come out to 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4. Every single writeup I read says the lesson is that the question is underspecified, that there's no right answer until you say exactly how the chord gets picked, and that the whole thing is a warning about the principle of indifference. I dont buy that lesson and I want you to make the strongest possible case for the position I've been leaning toward: that there IS a right answer to the original question and it's 1/2.

Here's what got me there, and I swear I did the experiment before I found out someone else had already made the argument. I printed a page with two circles sharing a center, the small one exactly half the radius of the big one. Then I dropped a bunch of toothpicks (each one longer than the big circle's diameter) onto the page from about eye height, a few hundred times, and counted how many landed crossing the big circle versus how many crossed the small one. The ratio sat way closer to 1/2 than to 1/4. Which makes sense to me because of the Buffon's needle perimeter thing — number of hits should track circumference, not area — and it lines up with the random-radius method. For what it's worth I never worked out what the two-endpoints method would predict for that ratio, maybe you can tell me whether my toothpicks rule it out.

The other piece is the invariance idea I read about afterwards: if your rule for choosing a chord changes when you slide the circle a few inches across the table or blow it up to twice the size, then you havent actually described a way of choosing chords, you've described a way of choosing chords relative to that one particular circle, and the word random is doing sneaky extra work. Of the three methods only the radius one survives both shrinking and sliding.

So argue that this settles it. The objection I can't get past on my own is the obvious one: there's only one circle in the problem, nobody is sliding anything anywhere, so why on earth should the answer care what would happen if you moved it?
