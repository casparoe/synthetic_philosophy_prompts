so my city council stood up a task force last month to write an official apology — and a payment program to go with it — for the highway they pushed through a mostly Black neighborhood on the east side in the 60s. Eminent domain, a blight finding, a few hundred households gone. Not one councilmember who voted for it is still in office, and a big share of the taxpayers who'd fund the payments have lived here less than ten years. I got asked to join because I'm on the neighborhood association board and used to do title work, so I'm the one who can read the old records. My actual contribution has turned into asking a question nobody wants to touch.

Here's the question. When the draft says the city is sorry for what it did, what has to be true about the city for that sentence to be anything other than a figure of speech? Reconstruct it for me. Is the practice assuming the city is a single continuing agent that survives total turnover of its members, the way a person survives turnover of cells? Or is it assuming something attaches to the institution rather than to any persons at all? Or is the we not the city but the current residents, who are somehow supposed to carry something they didn't do?

Then the inconsistency I can't get past. The same city's lawyers settle lawsuits without admitting liability, every single time, and would never let a document say the city was guilty of anything. Meanwhile the apology says we did wrong, on the steps, on the record. And the city lives inside a criminal law that is completely rigid that guilt is personal — you cannot be convicted of your grandfather's crime no matter how much you profited from it. And inheritance: descendants of the families who got the construction contracts and the cheap land keep every dollar, and nobody calls that money tainted. So the practice looks like it assumes one thing about responsibility in the morning and three different things by the afternoon.

One person at the first meeting said Jaspers — the German philosopher writing right after the war — seperated guilt into four kinds, criminal, political, moral, metaphysical, and that we were mashing all four into a single apology. That stuck with me. Is that the right diagnosis? Is the city actually making a political claim while everybody in the room hears a moral one?

What I want: tell me what theory of guilt, and of the polity, an apology-plus-payment practice like this strictly needs, and then separate that from theories that merely happen to fit it comfortably, because I suspect several different ones license the exact same ceremony. Say which rival views the practice quietly rules out. And tell me whether the other three things — no-admission settlements, personal guilt in criminal law, clean inheritance — actually contradict it or just sit next to it without touching.

Then be concrete about what would have to change if you swapped in a view where only individual persons are ever responsible for anything: the wording, who counts as an eligible claimant, whether the payment can still be called reparations or has to be renamed, whether descendants of the families who benefited should be asked for more than everyone else.

last thing, and it might be the whole ballgame: does an apology require guilt at all? or can it just be an acknowledgment of harm that the we caused, with nobody in the we blameworthy for it? because if that second thing is coherent i think half the room would happily sign it and the other half would believe they'd been sold something completely different than what was advertised, and i want to know which half is right before the vote.
