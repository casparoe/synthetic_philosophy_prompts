Argument request, and I know it's a bit heretical. Everybody who critiques reasoning-by-historical-analogy in foreign policy says basically the same thing: the trouble isn't analogy, it's lazy analogy. The fix is to do the work first — list the disanalogies, check what the base rates actually were — and then the comparison becomes a disciplined tool instead of a slogan. I want you to argue the other way: that the disanalogy check doesn't discipline anything, because deciding which of the differences actually matter is itself an analogical judgment, so the whole method just relocates the problem one step back and dresses it up as procedure.

Take it through the two cases I care about — Munich, meaning the way 1938 got turned into a standing template for never negotiating with an adversary, and the domino reasoning behind Vietnam — and show concretely how the list-the-differences step plays out in practice: who decides which differences are relevent, what they're tacitly comparing to when they decide, and why the step can be satisfied just as easily by the hawks as by the people calling for restraint. Then tell me what's left over. Is there any constraint on reasoning-by-comparison that isn't itself comparative, or should we just admit that historical analogy is rhetoric with a methodology section bolted onto it?

Give me the strongest version of the regress claim you can build, and be straight with me about where it's most exposed — I'd much rather find the weak point from you than from a reviewer. No citations, I'll handle those.
