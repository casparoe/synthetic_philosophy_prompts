Our econ-philosophy reading group ended up somewhere I don't like last week and I want to know which step to pull. Someone wrote this up on the board, nobody could say where it breaks, and the premises all looked fine to me at the time. The conclusion is something I refuse to accept.

1. Take the textbook cascade setup: agents move in a fixed order, each sees every preceeding action but none of the other agents' signals, each gets one private signal of bounded strength, and everyone wants to match the true state.
2. Each agent chooses on the basis of their posterior given everything they can actually see — the public action history plus their own signal.
3. Once two agents in a row have picked H, the public history carries at least as much evidential weight as two signals for H, and since signals are bounded there's a ceiling on what any one private signal can contribute. So the third agent's posterior favors H even when their own signal says L.
4. So they pick H, which makes the history three H's, which does the same thing to the fourth agent, and so on down the line. From the third agent onward every action is a deterministic function of what was already public.
5. A deterministic function of public information adds nothing to it. So once the cascade starts the public record stops accumulating evidence, and the crowd's belief freezes at whatever the first two coin flips happened to say.
6. Therefore the final outcome depends only on those two signals. It's wrong with positive probability, and nothing the rest of the group does — however many of them there are, however good their private evidence is — can repair it.
7. Nothing in 1 through 6 depended on the story being about urns and draws. Any setting where people observe each other's choices rather than each other's evidence, and where nobody's private information is infinitely strong, has the same shape: technology adoption, which restaurant is full, fashion, asset prices, which methods a field standardizes on, whose preprints get read.
8. So the whole wisdom-of-crowds story is an artifact of pretending people decide independently. Rational crowds cannot aggregate dispersed knowledge, and a million people doing the same thing is never evidence about the world beyond what the first two of them did.
9. But that can't be right either, because of Condorcet: if each of n people is better than a coin flip at identifying the state, then majority vote converges to certainty as n grows. That's a theorem, not a hypothesis.
10. So one of them has to go. Either cascades don't really happen, or Condorcet is wrong. They can't both stand.

Where does it go wrong? I dont want to give up 1–4 — I think that part is right, I've watched the simulations, and it matches how the model is usually presented. And I obviously don't want to give up Condorcet. If you force me to name what I'd most like to keep, it's those two, which means the damage has to land somewhere in the middle or at the end.

So: which step or steps are bad, why did each of them look fine when I wrote it down, and what does rejecting it cost? I specifically keep staring at 5, and I suspect the phrase "adds nothing to it" is doing something sneaky — information for whom, measured against what? Spell that out if it's the culprit. I'd also like to know whether 5 is conflating what the crowd *does* with what the crowd *believes*, since in the setup each person still has their own signal and presumably still has a posterior of their own even while falling in line.

And two follow-ups. Does 7 survive at all? If the structure really is that general, then all those examples are supposed to be cascades, and I'd like a way to tell a genuine cascade apart from a crowd that's just right — what pattern in the data would separate them, given that from the inside they look identical. Finally, if 10 is a false dichotomy, say what the correct conclusion is that both cascade theory and Condorcet support, and be concrete about what feature of a decision problem determines which of the two regimes you're in.
