I am a senior at a public high school that offers a year-long, faculty-supervised independent research seminar in the humanities, and before I may begin I have to submit a two-page proposal for approval. My supervisor teaches philosophy and has told me twice that she will reject anything that amounts to a survey of what other people have said; the question, she insists, has to be one that argument can settle rather than one that statistics settle. My topic is work and leisure, and more precisely an assumption that seems to sit underneath nearly every debate about overwork: that the harm of overwork is measured in hours. The two cases I keep returning to are usually presented as an extreme and its correction — karoshi in Japan, and France's statutory 35-hour week along with the right-to-disconnect rules several countries have since adopted — but pairing them that way already presumes that time at work is the relevant quantity. I suspect two distinct things are being run together. The first is the deprivation of time not devoted to work. The second, for which I have invented the label work-identity monopoly, is the condition of someone whose work has become the sole or nearly sole source of their sense of standing, competence, and reason to get up in the morning; on that description, two people who work identical hours can differ in whether they are harmed at all, and reducing one of them to thirty-five hours might leave the harm untouched or even sharpen it. Relatedly, I think leisure does double duty in these debates, meaning sometimes residual non-work time and sometimes a cultivated capacity for activity undertaken for its own sake, and only the second seems to me worth distributing.

Please turn this into the proposal. It needs a precise statement of the question, phrased so that it has a determinate answer: whether the wrongness of overwork is fully explained by the deprivation of leisure, or whether work-identity monopoly is a genuinely separate harm that does not reduce to hours, and, if the two come apart, what follows for whether hour caps and disconnection rules are aimed at the right thing. It needs an account of why the existing approaches leave that open — the Marxist critique of alienated labour, the Protestant-ethic picture of work as a calling, the treatment of leisure as one distributable good among others in egalitarian theory, and the public-health framing of long hours as a risk factor — but stated as positions and arguments rather than as a literature review. It needs the method: what I would actually do, week by week, across a twelve-week term, including how I would test whether the distinction is real rather than merely verbal; and a defense of that method against the obvious alternatives, such as designing a survey of hours and reported wellbeing or writing a history of the shorter-hours movement. It needs a description of what a successful result looks like, and, more important to me, what would count as failure: I want to be able to tell my supervisor in advance the conditions under which I would give the distinction up — for instance, if every case of work-identity monopoly I can construct turns out to be redescribable as a case of hours and fatigue, or if the distinction never changes the verdict on any concrete policy. And it needs an honest assessment of scope, saying which parts of this I should cut so that the remainder is achievable for a student with a full course load.

Two further requests. Build into the proposal the commitment to work through two test cases: the person who works eighty hours a week at something they love and would refuse any offer to stop, and the recently retired person who has all the hours in the world and is miserable. And please include no bibliography and no citations at all — I will add references myself if my supervisor requires them, and I would much rather have a proposal with none than one with invented ones. For what it is worth, my mother is an emergency physician who has said, half in jest, that she would not know what to do with a four-day weekend, and that remark is where I first noticed that the two harms come apart.
