I work for an organisation that documents protest and dissent for use in litigation and in reports to treaty bodies, and over some fifteen years of casework a pattern has begun to trouble me that I have never found a satisfactory label for. Consider the following. A person breaks a law openly, gives notice of what they are doing and why, does not run from the police, and accepts whatever punishment follows. Every outward feature of the standard case is present — publicity, non-evasion, willingness to be prosecuted. But the communicative structure that makes such an act intelligible is absent, and it is absent for a reason having nothing to do with the person's conduct. They cannot vote in the jurisdiction whose law they are breaking. Or they are legally present but permanently ineligible for citizenship, so no representative has any reason to count them. Or they are governed from a capital in which they hold no seat, and have never held one. In each instance the act is addressed to a public whose settled convictions simply do not extend to the person addressing it.

What strikes me is how different this is from the better-known situation in which a community drifts from principles it still professes, and the lawbreaker's point is that the community is being inconsistent with itself. In that case the appeal runs to something already shared; the protester says, in effect, that you are failing by your own lights. In the cases I keep meeting, no such appeal is available, because the excluded person was never inside the circle of concern that the appeal would invoke. Their disobedience looks the same from outside and works completely differently from inside. I can see three questions I cannot answer on my own. First, has this configuration been given a name of its own — something more specific than treating it as an awkward limit case, or as an instance of the general puzzle about who gets to decide the boundaries of the demos? Second, how exactly does it differ from what the best-known theorist of civil disobedience already concedes, namely that the practice presupposes a roughly just society and a shared membership in it? Is the situation I am describing simply the negation of his second condition, or does it amount to a distinct problem that survives once his first condition is satisfied? Third, and most important for my purposes: what is the right classification of such acts? Are they civil disobedience properly so called, justified on some different ground than an appeal to shared principles; or does the concept simply fail to apply, so that we need another category altogether? And if we say the latter, how do we prevent that verdict from becoming a way of describing every unlawful act by an excluded person as mere law-breaking, thereby making the label available only to those already inside the polity?

I am not after a single word. I would like the phenomenon carved carefully: what it is, what it is not, which established concepts sit nearest to it and how it departs from each, and where its edges lie — including the harder cases, such as the long-term resident who cannot vote in national elections but can in municipal ones, or the adolescent below the franchise age. Please write your answer in British English.
