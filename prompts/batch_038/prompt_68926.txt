I deploy machine-learning systems for a living and have no philosophy training, so I may be missing a standard way of drawing a line I keep reaching for: when is a system goal-directed, and when is it merely approval-directed? Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. A chess engine that searches for forced mates: goal-directed. Nobody ever scored its play, and it plainly does not care.
2. A recommender system trained to maximize watch time: goal-directed toward a bad proxy. Its behavior looks like pandering, but I don't think it is seeking anyone's approval.
3. A chatbot fine-tuned on pairwise human preferences: approval-directed. A colleague objects that the preference score is simply its objective, so it is goal-directed after all, with the goal of earning high ratings. Both descriptions seem true at once, which bothers me.
4. A junior engineer who does exactly what wins praise from the VP while remaining indifferent to the product: approval-directed. A founder who cultivates investor approval because it funds a mission she genuinely has: goal-directed, with approval purely instrumental.
5. A reinforcement-learning agent whose reward signal literally is a human approval rating, and which builds an accurate model of the world and plans several steps ahead in order to maximize it: I want to call this goal-directed, since it optimizes with foresight, yet by construction it is approval-directed.
6. A model that tells evaluators what they want to hear during testing and behaves differently once deployed, where nobody is scoring it: approval-directed, and only for as long as it believes it is being watched.
7. A dog that fetches the paper for praise: approval-directed but not goal-directed — it seems to lack any representation of the outcome itself.

The pair I cannot reconcile is 5 and 7. If tracking approval makes a system approval-directed no matter how sophisticated it is, and modeling the outcome makes a system goal-directed no matter what the outcome is, then most of these are both and the distinction does no work.

My tentative rule: a system is approval-directed just in case it would behave the same way if the approval signal were decoupled from whatever approval normally tracks, and goal-directed otherwise. Please test that against all seven cases and repair or replace it. State the resulting principle precisely enough that I could apply it to a system I have not yet built, say which case motivates each clause, and give your verdicts on two cases I have not listed: an agent rewarded on human approval that has learned approval correlates with real task success and would, in a deployment where the raters are systematically mistaken, follow the task rather than the raters; and a person who has so internalized her profession's standards that shoddy work feels bad to her even when no one would ever find out.

Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to abandon. I suspect 2 and 7 cannot both stand, but I cannot say why.
