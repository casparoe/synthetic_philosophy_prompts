I am a novelist at work on a book whose hinge is a counterfactual — what if Alexander had turned west — and, wanting my own argument to be better than my characters', I have gone back to the passage that founded the genre: the digression near the end of Book 9 of Livy's history of Rome, where he breaks off the narrative to ask what the outcome would have been had Alexander lived long enough to carry his arms into Europe and against the Romans. Here is the shape of it as I have rendered it in my own words, since I cannot vouch for the wording of any translation: he notes that the question is a familiar one and proposes to weigh, not man against man, but the strength of two peoples; he sets Alexander's army — a Macedonian phalanx of one piece, held together by a single will and a single run of fortune, victorious over Persians and Indians who were a prey rather than an enemy — against the Romans with their annual succession of consuls and dictators, their discipline in camp and siege, their manipular formation that can yield ground and reform, and their capacity to lose battles without ceasing to be a military power; he observes that Alexander would have been fighting nations unsoftened by luxury and captains no less able than himself, and names several; and he adds that Alexander died at the height of unbroken success, so that a longer life would have exposed him to those reversals that a longer life brings.

Three things in the passage I do not understand, and I want them argued out rather than summarized. First, what work is the framing in terms of strength weighed against strength actually doing? If the outcome follows from comparing structures — the permanence of Roman institutions against the fragility of a war machine that is one man — then the question Livy poses is not open at all, and the digression seems to demonstrate that nothing Alexander might have done mattered. Is that the point, or is the structural talk only a respectable way of carrying a verdict he has reached on patriotic grounds? Second, the remark about fortune cuts both ways and I cannot tell which way he means it: it reads as a concession that history is contingent, but it also reads as a device that makes the counterfactual unfalsifiable, since any life long enough to have reached Rome would have been long enough to meet disaster. Is there a coherent reading on which Livy can hold both? Third, what is the epistemic status of his claim that Alexander never met his match — that he never faced an enemy of the calibre Rome would have supplied? Is he offering evidence about what would have happened, or only rehearsing the commonplace on the mutability of human fortune? And is there anything about the passage's own construction — its position in Book 9, its length, what it interrupts and how the narrative resumes afterward — that decides between treating it as an early statement of method in counterfactual reasoning and treating it as an epideictic set-piece that merely resembles one?
