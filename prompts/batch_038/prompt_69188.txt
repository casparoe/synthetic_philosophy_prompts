I fell down a rabbit hole this weekend watching Olympic diving and figure skating, and then reading about how the scoring actually works, and now something about it doesnt sit right with me at all.

Here's the machinery as I understand it. Diving: a panel of seven judges scores each dive, the two highest and two lowest marks get thrown out, the remaining three are added up and multiplied by the dive's degree of difficulty, which is a fixed number sitting in a rulebook — a forward three-and-a-half is worth more than a forward two-and-a-half, as a matter of published fact. Gymnastics did something similar when it ditched the perfect 10: a routine now scores as a difficulty value plus an execution score that starts at ten and gets deducted from, with the top and bottom execution marks again discarded. Figure skating: every element has a base value, judges then assign a grade of execution on top of it, plus separate marks for "components" like skating skills and interpretation, and those marks get averaged after dropping outliers, with each judge's numbers published only as J1, J2, J3 so you can't tell who said what.

Two puzzles I want taken apart:

1. The dropping. It only makes sense if you think there's one real value out there — how good that dive was — and each judge is a slightly broken thermometer giving you a noisy reading of it, so the weird marks are just error and averaging the rest gets you closer to the truth. But is that the right model for how good a performance was? If the most experienced judges are the ones who've seen a thousand of these and have the sharpest eye, wouldn't their confident outlier verdicts be exactly the marks you want to keep and weight *more*, instead of binning? Trimming seems to assume all seven eyes are equally good and equally bad. What view of aesthetic value does the trimmed mean actually require, and which rival views does it rule out without ever saying so?

2. The difficulty table. Some committee decides, and can revise by vote, that this skill is worth 3.8 and that one is worth 3.2, and then everybody treats that number as measuring something rather than making something up — athletes build whole careers around it, coaches complain when it changes. So the same sport behaves like quality is objective enough to be tabulated to one decimal place, and like it's whatever the governing body currently says it is. Are those compatible? Is there a coherent reading where the numbers track something real even though a committee sets them, the way a thermometer scale is arbitrary but temperature isn't, or is the practice papering over a genuine incoherence?

Related to both: the anonymity thing and the whole apparatus for policing national bias assume the ideal judge is a disinterested one, unmoved by whose kid it is or which flag they're carrying. Does that square with the idea that good taste comes from being steeped in a tradition, having loyalties, having favorites you're comparing everything against?

So what I want: reconstruct the theory of value this whole apparatus is committed to; separate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely happens to fit comfortably; point out where it's philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where several very different theories would license the exact same rulebook; and tell me what would have to change in the scoring itself, not just in the philosophy, if you swapped in a view where the joint verdict of the few genuinely qualified judges just is the standard, and the crowd's opinion has no authority at all.
