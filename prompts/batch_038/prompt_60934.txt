I cover neuroscience and philosophy of mind for a magazine, and I've got a freelancers draft back with a paragraph on pain asymbolia that I'm not comfortable signing off on. My editor thinks it's fine. I'm not a philosopher or a neurologist — I just have a nagging feeling that two or three of the claims are doing more work then the evidence can carry, and I can't say which ones.

Here's the paragraph as written:

"Pain asymbolia is a rare condition, usually the result of a stroke on the left side of the brain. The nerves and the pain system itself are untouched: pinch or burn someone who has it and they'll tell you it hurts, point to the spot, and describe the quality of the pain. What they won't do is flinch, pull away, guard the limb, or mind. Some of them smile. The damage isn't in the pain machinery at all but in the relay that hooks it up to the emotional centres — which is why the condition is so awkward for philosophers of pain. Pain turns out to come in two separable pieces, a sensation and a badness, and you can lose one while keeping the other. Unpleasantness isn't built into pain; it's a tag the brain adds on afterwards, and the tag can be unhooked. That's also why this matters clinically: mindfulness-based pain programs and pain retraining seem to work by inducing a temporary, harmless version of the same split, teaching people to register the sensation without the suffering."

Can you go through it and sort it into three piles for me — what someone who actually works on this would accept as written, what's a defensible popularization that just needs hedging, and what's wrong or unsupported? Specifically I want to know: does the lesion really sit outside the pain system, or is that where the draft is fudging? Is "they lose the badness and keep the pain" the settled reading of these patients, or is there a rival reading on which they aren't in pain in any full-blooded sense at all — and if so, what turns on the difference? Does the mindfulness sentence follow from anything true in the rest of the paragraph, or is it borrowing credibility from the lesion cases? And am I right that the draft quietly leaves out the thing that makes asymbolia obviously not a gift?

Also, if parts of this are describing something that isn't quite asymbolia, tell me what the real phenomenon it's half-remembering might be, so I can send the draft back with a fix instead of just a list of complaints. Don't bother with references or a reading list — I can chase those down myself once I know what I'm looking for. Just tell me what to cut, what to soften, and what survives.
