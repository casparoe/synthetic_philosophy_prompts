Next month I'm giving a talk at a U.S. law school's law-and-religion workshop. My thesis is that the German church tax is a live normative option for a religiously plural state rather than a European curiosity: an establishment whose harm is measured by the cost and indignity of exit, rather than by the bare fact of state recognition, can treat minorities better than strict separation does. My argument has four steps. First, what actually injures a non-adherent is being unable to leave a scheme cheaply and without stigma, not seeing the state acknowledge a church. Second, the American no-aid principle carries establishment effects of its own, because it favors communities that can finance themselves privately and whose practices are portable, while penalizing those that require buildings, schools, and salaried clergy. Third, an exit right that is genuinely cheap converts establishment from a status into something closer to a subscription. Fourth, the real design question is therefore whether exit can be made cheap enough, and I argue that a civil declaration of exit carrying no ecclesial penalty — and no requirement that the defector announce a loss of faith — is the constraint from which everything else in the model should follow.

The room will be mostly American First Amendment scholars: several liberal separationists who hear coercion in any taxpayer funding of religion and in any official endorsement; two or three originalists who think the Establishment Clause reaches only coercion or the creation of a national church; one comparative constitutionalist who has actually worked on German public law and will notice if I misstate how the machinery functions; and a scattering of students. No one there has worked on France or England, so American intuitions will do all the load-bearing.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they would come, phrased the way they would phrase them and with the doctrinal hooks they would reach for: compelled support, denominational preference, the state's role in determining who counts as a member, monitoring and entanglement, the position of Muslim communities that lack public-law-corporation status, what a wave of mass exits does to a system that depends on a stable contributory base, and the deflationary point that the levy is really a payment for social services the churches already deliver. For each, a reply I can give in about two minutes, plus an honest note on where that reply is soft. Then tell me what to concede in the opening three minutes so the fight happens on ground I have chosen, which single reply has to land for the paper to survive, and what the objection is that I am hoping nobody asks. Flag separately anything the comparativist will put differently from the Americans.
