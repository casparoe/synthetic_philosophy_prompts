So — bit of an odd one, bear with me. I write novels, third one's currently defeating me, and in the gaps I've been reading Kierkegaard's long review of that Gyllembourg novel — Two Ages, the 1846 one — mainly because I wanted to see how a working reader of his period described the machinery of literary opinion. And I've come away converted, which worries me slightly, because converted people are dull company.

The position I now hold, roughly: what the daily press created wasn't a better-informed readership but a phantom. The public isn't a collection of actual people with actual opinions — it's an abstraction the press manufactures and then reports on, and no one is ever in it or responsible for anything said in its name. And the process it drives isn't democratisation, it's levelling, which I take to mean something more specific than flattening of incomes or of taste: the collapse of qualitative distinctions plus the disappearance of personal liability for speech, because once anyone can say anything anonymously through a medium, saying and being silent stop being different acts, and no one ever has to answer for a word. I think this is basically right of review aggregators and of algorithmic recommendation too, and I've started using it as a diagnosis of why conversations about books feel both endless and weightless.

Tell me what would count against it. I don't want the standard "Kierkegaard was a reactionary snob" brush-off, and I don't want a reading list — no bibliography, no citations, I can't look things up and I don't want to be handed homework. I want the defeaters, ranked: which ones would actually kill the view, which are merely awkward. Specifically I'd like you to dig out the empirical claims buried inside what presents itself as a conceptual analysis — e.g. does anonymous aggregation really flatten rather than fragment and inflame; was earlier literary culture actually more authoritative, or just slower and equally vicious; is it even possible for responsibility to attach to speech at that scale. Tell me which of these a committed Kierkegaardian would concede as damaging and which they'd wave away as a category error.

Then the uncomfortable question: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? Because I can see that any evidence against it gets absorbed — a passionate movement counts as further reflection, a pluralistic culture counts as a new mode of levelling, and silence counts as chatter too. If it's insulated like that, is that fatal, or is it acceptable for a diagnosis that isn't trying to predict anything? And what does it predict that rival accounts dont — say, the view that this is just ordinary status gossip with better bandwidth, or that it's purely an attention-market effect?

Finally: the Corsair business. He'd just been run out of town by a satirical paper, mocked in the street, before writing this. Should that count against the theory, and how much? Give me a straight answer, in British English, and don't spare the position just because I've fallen for it.
