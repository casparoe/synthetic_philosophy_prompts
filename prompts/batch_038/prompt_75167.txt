The question we were set:

Question. Mahāyāna literature on skillful means includes a story in which the Buddha, recounting a former life as a ship's captain, kills a passenger who intends to murder the other five hundred aboard, and is praised for the killing rather than blamed. Does this story show that Buddhist ethics is a form of consequentialism? State what would have to be true of an ethical view for it to count as consequentialist, then assess the story as evidence, addressing in particular whether the doctrine of karma and the emphasis on intention support or undermine a consequentialist reading.

The response:

The captain case looks consequentialist on its surface, and I think the surface is partly genuine but partly misleading.

The captain kills one man who intends to murder five hundred, and he does so out of compassion both for the intended victims and for the would-be killer, whose own karma would have been catastrophic; he also resolves to take the consequences of the killing on himself rather than let any of the merchants become murderers. The story's verdict is that the act was skillful, that it generated merit rather than demerit, and that the man killed was spared a far worse fate than death. This is plainly a comparison of outcomes: the captain weighs one future against another and takes the better one. So at least within Mahāyāna the precepts function as rules that may be overridden whenever overriding them produces more good, which is what consequentialism says.

Three considerations nonetheless persuade me that Buddhist ethics as a whole is not consequentialist. First, karma is fixed by volition rather than by result: the texts hold that an act is wholesome or unwholesome in virtue of the intention behind it, so an agent who means well and causes disaster has not acted wrongly, while one who causes benefits out of greed has. A theory that locates rightness in the agent's state of mind rather than in the state of the world is not a consequentialist theory. Second, the doctrine of not-self defeats the consequentialist reading at its root, since if there is no enduring person there is nothing for outcomes to be good or bad for, and no legitimate basis for adding welfare across the boundaries of persons; the reduction of persons to a single impersonal sum is exactly what the separateness-of-persons objection objects to, and Buddhism is committed to that reduction. Third, karma operates as a system of moral accounting that distributes happiness in proportion to virtue, so the best overall balance is already guaranteed by the structure of the world rather than by anyone's choices; a principle that adjusts happiness to desert is retributive rather than maximizing, and it leaves agents no calculation to perform.

The standard scholarly reading therefore treats the captain story as showing that Buddhist ethics weighs consequences, while its critics reply that the precepts are what really matter and that a single narrative exception proves the rule. And because the monastic code and the skillful-means literature express one continuous tradition that agrees with itself in substance, the fair conclusion is that Buddhist ethics is pluralistic: consequentialist when the stakes are high, and governed by intention when they are not.
