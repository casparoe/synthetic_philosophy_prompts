Three months ago I joined a political party for the first time in my life, and last week the local branch made me its secretary, mostly becuase I'm the only person under 40 who turns up. So now I'm inside the machinery, and it is stranger than I expected. Concretely, here's what my month looks like:

- I've been given a target of twelve new members to sign up before the end of the quarter. Nobody asks what those people believe when they sign, nobody asks them again afterwards, and the number is what gets reported upward. That's the whole metric.
- Before the "listening sessions" on housing policy, the regional office sends a briefing that already contains the conclusions plus a list of approved phrasings. The sessions happen, people talk for two hours, we write everything down carefully, and the conclusions don't move. The notes go in a folder.
- Our councillor voted against the line once, on a bus route she knows better than anyone, and was told gently afterwards that supporting her at the next selection would be difficult.
- When someone at a meeting says they agree with us on almost everything but think our position on immigration is wrong, the room treats them worse than an opponent from the other party. An opponent is expected to be wrong. This person is a traitor to something.
- The newsletter, which I now write, is about two thousand words on why the other lot can't be trusted and about two hundred words on what we actually propose.

I've been reading Simone Weil's short wartime essay about doing away with political parties altogether, and I half-remember her arguing that a party's real first aim is its own growth without limit, that parties are machines for manufacturing collective passion rather than thought, and that membership trains you to ask whether something helps the party instead of whether it's true. I think she wanted candidates to stand without any label at all so voters judge the person's declared positions, and representatives to vote on each question as their own judgment went.

My question isn't whether she's right about abolishing parties. It's what the stuff above presupposes. What theory of belief sits underneath the membership target? It seems to treat people's convictions as interchangeable and irrelevant, which is a bizarre thing for an organisation whose stated purpose is getting its ideas into law — so what is it actually optimising for, if not agreement? And what theory of group reasoning sits underneath a listening session with pre-written conclusions? Is that practice committed to the view that deliberation can't change anyone's mind, or only to something weaker, like that it can but is too slow and too risky to be allowed near the timetable? Please separate, for each of these items, what the practice strictly needs from what it merely happens to fit comfortably with, because I suspect most of it is underdetermined: you could probably justify the whip and the newsletter out of three completely different pictures of what politics is, and they'd look identical from the inside. Tell me which parts are like that.

Then: are the parts consistent with each other? The membership drive wants as many bodies inside the tent as possible, while the way we treat a partial sympathiser wants the tent small and pure. Do those presuppose the same thing about what a party is, or does one assume a coalition of interests and the other a community of conviction? And lastly, on Weil's version — if you actually replaced the label on the ballot with the candidate's own declared positions and let representatives vote however they judged, what would have to change in the practices I listed? Which ones would just die, which would survive completely untouched, and which would mutate into something that looks the same on paper but does the same job anyway?
