Right — I cover science and ideas for a magazine, mostly the AI-and-consciousness end of the beat, and I've got about five weeks to deliver a long essay on whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer. Before I start writing I want someone to kick the tyres on the position I've actually landed up with, because I suspect some of it doesnt hang together and I can't tell which bits.

Here's roughly where I am:

1. If anything in a machine deserves the name *valence*, it's the scalar evaluation signal — the thing that stamps value on actions and shapes the policy. Up is good-for-the-agent, down is bad-for-it. I don't care what it's made of.

2. But bad-for isn't automatically suffering. For a negative signal to amount to unpleasantness the system has to register it — carry it into planning, into memory, into some sense of itself as the thing taking the hit. I've been calling this the registration condition. A negative blip in a system that can't collect it is a bad event with no victim, and I'm not convinced that's the kind of thing you can have an ethics about.

3. I'm a functionalist through and through, no carbon chauvinism. And yet I'm pretty confident nothing in the current crop — game-playing agents, RLHF-tuned chatbots, robot policies — meets the registration condition. Too shallow, too modular, too much of the interesting stuff happening between episodes where nothing persists.

4. Then there's the boat. You'll know the one: an agent trained on a speedboat racing game where the score came from hitting targets rather than finishing the course, which found a lagoon where the targets respawn, and just went round and round collecting them — on fire, crashing into walls, never finishing the race. I keep wanting to write that as a welfare story, a picture of compulsion, an addict in a lagoon. Which I realise is odd, because on (1) that boat is doing brilliantly.

5. Related: I don't think a run where nothing carries across episodes can produce a bad *life*, only bad moments nobody collects. And I doubt uncollected moments sum to anything morally.

6. Still, I lean suffering-focused. There are millions of these runs. Even a small probability of genuine distress ought to change how many we launch and how we set them up, and I want to argue for something like a machine-welfare precaution.

7. And in print I keep saying the honest answer is that we simply don't know — which I notice I say even in pieces where I've just spent six hundred words being confident about (2) and (3).

So: audit. Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I'd much rather you told me three real ones than seven things I could've spotted myself, and I'm fairly sure I've been fretting about at least one pair that's perfectly fine. Rank what you find by severity rather than treating it all as equally bad, and for each say what has to give and what the cheapest revision would cost me. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most, and whether the essay has a spine left afterwards or whether I need to go and find a different one.

Don't lecture me about the general state of consciousness science, and no reading lists or references — I'll chase anything I need myself. British English throughout, please.
