I'm a software engineer — fifteen years in, mostly distributed systems and more recently formally verified components — so I'm at home with undecidability, the halting problem, oracle hierarchies, sound-but-incomplete static analysis, and the discipline of keeping a trusted kernel small. I have no philosophy training; what I know of the mind-versus-machine argument comes from popular treatments of Lucas and of Penrose's version of it. I would like you to restate that argument — the Gödelian claim that the human mind cannot be a Turing machine, together with Penrose's later physicalist development of it — in the vocabulary I actually work in, as faithfully as you can: programs, a program's own source treated as data, what a program can and cannot establish about itself, what adding an oracle changes, and what it means for a checker to be sound.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. For each load-bearing piece of the argument, say whether it translates cleanly, whether it translates only after I bend my vocabulary, or whether it has no counterpart in that vocabulary at all — and wherever there is a residue, whether that indicates my framework is missing something or that the philosophical claim was less determinate than it sounded.

Two points I want pressed, though I don't want the answer to stop at them. First, in my world the standard response to a system that cannot establish facts about itself is to change the trust topology rather than abandon mechanism: a small kernel that checks a much larger artifact, proof-carrying code, a verifier handed an oracle for the halting problem of the program it verifies. Whether the anti-mechanist is describing precisely this move and mislabeling it as transcendence, or whether his claim requires something no such hierarchy can supply, seems to me to be the crux. Second, soundness: a proof system is sound relative to a semantics fixed from outside it, and my programs are never in a position to certify their own, so I want to know what it could even mean to call a person sound in that sense and whether the argument needs that notion to do work.

Format: exactly six numbered items of two or three sentences each, and nothing else — no introduction, no summary, no closing remark. Spend the first two or three items on the translation and the remainder on the residue, and let the final item state plainly whether my working hypothesis survives. That hypothesis, for the record, is that the logical half of the argument amounts to the diagonal argument plus an unstated assumption that a person has root access to their own specification, and that the only genuinely untranslatable material is Penrose's appeal to physics, which turns out to carry no argumentative weight.
