I'm the senior interpretive writer at a small university natural history museum. Between the four of us we produce roughly forty exhibit labels a year, and since a donor-driven rebrand in the late nineties our house style requires that any label on an anatomical structure, a behaviour, or a whole organism answer one question in a single sentence of no more than twenty-five words: what is it for? Half my team has a general science background and none of them has ever studied the biology of function; the finished labels go to the collection's curator for sign-off and to a funder whose guidelines prohibit what it calls teleological language. Over the weekend I argued for three hours with a friend who teaches philosophy of biology, about a label for the human appendix that our digestive-system case is scheduled to reopen with in the spring. I said the appendix has a function if the current evidence that it acts as a reservoir for beneficial gut bacteria holds up; he said I was confusing what a trait does with what it is *for*, that on his account the second question can only be answered by a history of selection, and that a label written my way would be false even if the reservoir hypothesis turned out to be correct. I came away unable to say which of us was talking sense, but certain that neither position can simply be handed to a writer with a deadline and a word count.

What I need from you is not the argument resolved but a decision procedure my team can run on a proposed "what is it for" sentence in ten minutes or less: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each one, the point at which the procedure terminates in *this trait has no function we can state* rather than in a shrug, and the points where the decision must go up to the curator because it is a judgment call about how much we are willing to claim. It should be explicit about where the two senses my friend and I were fighting over come apart and which one a label should follow in each kind of divergence, and it should tell me what to do with the byproduct structures in our collection, where nothing was ever selected for the feature we are describing. Write it so that someone who has never encountered the distinction can follow it, and so that it flags the two failure modes I actually fear: a label that implies a trait was shaped for a role it merely happens to perform, and a label so cautious and so historical that the visitor learns nothing whatever about the animal in front of them. No survey of the literature and no citations — I want the checklist, not the case law.

Then run the procedure on three of our own cases: the appendix, where the present-use evidence is decent and the history is speculative; the calcified aortic valve in our pathology display, which can no longer do the job a valve does; and a hafted stone adze in the ethnographic wing directly across the corridor from the anatomical cases, where writers keep reaching for words like "evolved." At least one of the three should come out against my own stated preference, and you should say plainly where the procedure will misfire — the kinds of label it cannot settle at all. Hard limit: nine hundred words for everything, procedure and worked cases together, with the steps numbered.
