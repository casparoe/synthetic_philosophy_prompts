My sister has been on the editorial board of a small human rights org for six years and she's basically the one who decides whether their annual report gets to use the word genocide. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it:

- Rwanda in the 90s: yes, no argument.
- Srebrenica: yes — even though it was one town and the victims were mostly men and boys who were separated out and shot, while the rest of the same war never got that label.
- Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge: no, for the roughly quarter of the country that died, on the grounds that the victims were mostly Cambodians and the motive was political. But yes, separately, for the Cham and the Vietnamese killed in those same years.
- A decades-long program of taking indigenous children and boarding them out with majority-culture families: yes, even though almost nobody was killed and the government swore it was all for the kids' benefit.
- A place where the minority language is banned in schools and the old shrines are getting bulldozed: no. She said the people are still there in their own villages, marrying each other and farming their land, so its cultural destruction, not genocide.
- A city starved out under siege: yes, because the starvation was aimed at the population and not at the fighters.

Now a new file landed and the board has been deadlocked 3-3 for weeks. A country is building a dam and a big mining operation on the ancestral land of a herding people of maybe 40,000. Nobody is being killed or rounded up. But the wells and the winter pasture go under water, the fish they depend on are gone, and the government won't let them resettle together — the compensation is individual plots scattered across three provinces, and the schools out there teach only in the national language. Demographers the board hired say that within two generations there won't be a distinct community left to speak of. Every state document is about development, jobs, and relocation packages; nobody in the government has ever said a word about the group as a group.

Which way should they vote if they want to be consistent with their own past calls, and which of the old cases is the closest analog? I'd also like you to lay out the candidate rules her precedents are compatible with — strict treaty definition, or tracking whether a group can go on existing as a group, or something else — and say what each rule predicts about the dam case, since I suspect they don't all point the same direction. And if the precedents genuinely don't settle it, tell me what principle the board would be committing to by voting each way, plus what one extra detail about any of the old cases would have told us which rule she's really running.
