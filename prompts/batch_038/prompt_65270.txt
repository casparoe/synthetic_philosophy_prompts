I teach philosophy of science and serve on the five-member jury of a foundation prize for long-range empirical forecasting, awarded every five years to a prediction made at least twenty-five years earlier whose outcome has since been settled. At our November meeting we must dispose of a single contested nomination, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion in advance. Please draft that opinion in my voice — 1,000 to 1,300 words — with an explicit recommendation at the top that the jury could vote on.

The nomination concerns a 1974 forecast by a late development economist regarding a densely settled river basin in a low-income agrarian region. He predicted that, because population growth would outrun the extension of cultivable land, grain output per head in the basin would fall below a stated subsistence threshold by 1995, and that the ensuing famine would kill on the order of a million people. The documentary record before us establishes three things. First, a famine did occur in 1993–94, and the death toll fell within the predicted range. Second, the specified mechanism did not operate: yields per hectare in the basin rose by more than half over the two decades, owing to new seed varieties and expanded irrigation, and grain availability per head in 1994 was higher than in 1974. The famine was produced by a civil war that destroyed the distribution network and by a state procurement policy that removed grain from the region. The demographic pressure the forecaster identified was real, but it is not what starved anyone. Third, the nominee's former students, who filed the nomination, argue two further points: that the forecast was right but early, since the pressure it identified has reappeared as groundwater depletion and out-migration, and since, had the war not intervened, the outcome would have obtained on schedule; and that the same theorist's companion forecast for a second region, which failed outright, should be discounted because an auxiliary assumption about the pace of irrigation adoption proved mistaken there.

Our charter defines a qualifying prediction as one whose outcome obtained substantially as described, and the jury is divided over how to read that phrase. Two colleagues would award the prize: the date and the death toll were correct, they say, and one of them points to the 1980 wager between Paul Ehrlich and Julian Simon over the ten-year movement in the prices of five industrial metals — a bet Simon won when all five fell — as precedent for scoring forecasts purely by outcome. One colleague would decline it, on the ground that the outcome was produced by causes the theory did not contain, so the success is no evidence for the theory. I think both positions are partly right, and I want the opinion to say precisely where.

Please recommend award, refusal, or award subject to conditions. Because the foundation has asked the jury to leave behind a standard rather than merely a verdict, also formulate the rule you would apply to timing-displaced predictions generally, in terms the other jurors could apply to a different case next cycle. The opinion must address: whether a prediction can be confirmed by an outcome that arrived by a causal route other than the one specified; whether the counterfactual appeal to the war is a legitimate auxiliary hypothesis or an ad hoc rescue, and what would distinguish the two on these facts; whether right but early can be anything other than unfalsifiable, and if it can be, what a forecast would have to look like for that defense to be available to it; whether the companion failure in the second region should count against the nomination or is properly discounted; and whether outcome-only scoring of the Simon–Ehrlich kind is even available as a model here, given what that wager did and did not require of its participants. Take the affirmative case at its strength — in particular the worry that demanding a demonstrated causal mechanism imports a standard no long-run social forecast could ever satisfy, so that the prize would end up going only to safe, short-horizon, near-tautological calls. Do not pad the document with a bibliography or a list of authorities: every fact the jury needs is stated above, and what it needs from me is argument it can act on.
