I'm a staff engineer on a platform team, and I've spent the last few weeks down a rabbit hole I don't have the vocabulary for, so I'd like to borrow yours. I have no formal philosophy training — one semester of logic a long time ago — so please reconstruct positions rather than assume I know them. The trigger was an argument with a colleague about our CI dashboards, which turned out to be an argument about the word "if." Here is the practice, described as concretely as I can.

We maintain three artifacts that all traffic in conditionals and none of which agree about what one is. First, our API specification, written in the IETF style with capitalized MUST and SHOULD keywords, is full of sentences of the form: if a client sends a malformed payload, the server MUST respond with a 400 and MUST NOT mutate state. Our conformance suite checks these by replaying recorded traffic and asserting that no run violated a clause. The resulting "spec conformance" metric has read 100% green for two years, and it is green partly because whole families of clauses are never triggered by the recorded traffic at all: an untriggered clause simply generates no violations. My colleague's defense is that this is exactly right, that a clause whose antecedent never obtains places no obligation and therefore has nothing to fail, and that the number is meaningful precisely because it is not measuring anything about those clauses. I find this unsatisfying but I can't say why beyond "then what is the dashboard for." Second, we run mutation testing: we inject small defects into the source and ask, of each one, whether some test would have failed. Nobody disputes that the injected program never ran in production, that the question is about a program state that does not exist, and that the answer is worth roughly forty minutes of extra CI time per commit. Third, our incident-review template has a section asking what would have been different had some earlier decision gone the other way. The team treats "if the deploy had been gated behind a canary, the outage would not have reached all users" as a genuine finding worth an action item, and treats "if the deploy had been gated behind a canary, the outage would have reached all users anyway" as exculpatory — yet both are claims about a history that did not occur, and we are clearly not indifferent between them.

What I want is an account of what each of these three practices presupposes about conditionals, and whether the three presuppositions can live together. Specifically: (1) Reconstruct the semantic or pragmatic commitments of each practice in your own terms — what has to be true of "if ... then ..." for the conformance dashboard to be a legitimate metric, for a mutation score to be a meaningful number, and for the postmortem section to distinguish a finding from an exculpation. If these commitments have standard names, give the names so I can look them up later, but please don't hand me a reading list or citations; I can't check them and I care about the content. (2) Note that my colleague's defense of the dashboard and my defense of mutation testing seem to use "if" in incompatible ways, and tell me which of us is committed to more. In particular, mutation score is usually explained to newcomers as the probability that a randomly injected fault would be caught, which sounds like a conditional probability, and I have read enough to suspect there is a live philosophical question about whether a rational agent's confidence in an indicative "if" claim just is their conditional confidence in the consequent given the antecedent, with no separate truth conditions at all. Does our mutation-testing practice depend on resolving that question, or does it float free of it? (3) Consider a minimal pair of my own construction, about the same incident. Indicative: if the deploy didn't take down the database, something else did — this seems perfectly reasonable to everyone on the team, since the database did go down. Counterfactual: if the deploy hadn't taken down the database, something else would have — this strikes almost everyone as a different and much more contentious claim, one that asserts a latent fragility rather than a bookkeeping fact. Explain what makes the pair differ. Is the difference a difference in truth conditions, in what we are entitled to assert given what we know, or in something else? And does the answer tell us whether the postmortem section is asking about counterfactuals at all, or about something more like a normalcy or disposition claim that our template mislabels? (4) Tell me what would have to change in the tooling if we stopped treating spec clauses as vacuously satisfied — if we required, say, that a clause only counts as checked when its antecedent has actually been triggered at least once, and reported a discharge rate conditional on triggering plus a separate trigger count. Which philosophical position licenses that two-number scheme, which positions forbid it, and is there a semantics on which the original 100% figure was measuring something real after all? (5) Finally, and most importantly for my purposes: separate what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with. I expect at least one of the three is philosophically underdetermined — several rival accounts of conditionals would license it equally — and I'd rather know which one that is than be told that our whole toolchain rests on a single contested theory.
