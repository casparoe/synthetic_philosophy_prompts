**Draft: "Fittingness First, Vehicle Second"**

The question *what is an emotion?* is standardly posed as a choice among three families of answer. Emotions are evaluative judgments or construals: to fear is to take something to be threatening. Emotions are perceptions of value: to fear is to have one's object quasi-perceptually presented as fearsome, with something of the immediacy and the informational encapsulation of sensory experience. Emotions are feelings, in the lineage that runs from James onward: to fear is to feel the bodily upheaval that the situation has caused.

Each family has a well-known bill. Judgment theories owe us an account of fear in creatures with few or no evaluative concepts, and of recalcitrant emotion — the fear that keeps its grip on someone who judges the snake behind the glass to be harmless. Perceptual theories owe us an account of what perceiving a value could be, and must absorb the cognitive-base problem: some prior state has to fix which particular is presented as fearsome, and if that base is doing the representational work, the perceptual emotion itself threatens to become explanatory surplus. Feeling theories owe us an account of how the registration of a pounding heart could be *about* anything, and of the emotions whose feel is thin, intermittent, or absent — the years-long resentment, the dread with no phenomenological centre.

My claim is that the question is malformed, though not for the reasons usually given. It is not that "emotion" fails to pick out a natural kind and should be abandoned in favour of whatever constructions a given affective science finds useful. It is that the three families are not competing answers to one question; they are answers to two different questions that the debate has kept run together.

The first question is normative: what makes an emotion correct or incorrect, fitting or unfitting? Here I take the answer to be uncontroversial in shape. Fear is fitting iff its object is fearsome; indignation is fitting iff the situation is one of offensive unfairness; grief is fitting iff there has been a loss of the sort that warrants it. Fittingness conditions are object-directed and self-involving: they concern how something stands with respect to what the agent cares about. Crucially, they do not tell us what sort of state the emotion *is*. Nothing in the specification of the standard settles the vehicle that realizes it.

The second question is local and empirical: in this creature, and for this emotion type, what sort of state is it that is governed by that standard? And here I want to insist that there is no reason to expect a uniform answer. Some emotion types will be realized by states that are inferentially integrated and revisable in the light of new belief, and those states will deserve to be called judgments. Others will be realized by states that are fast, cue-driven, and resistant to the agent's own considered view — and those will deserve to be called perceptions, whether or not the perceptual analogy can be cashed out globally. Others again may be realized primarily by states that monitor bodily condition, and will be feelings in the Jamesian sense. The debate over the nature of emotion does not thereby get dissolved; it gets *disaggregated* into a family of local questions, each answerable by different evidence and none of them answerable by the conceptual analysis that has dominated the literature.

Two consequences follow, and they are what I most want to defend.

First, the cognitive-base objection to perceptual theories is transformed rather than met. The demand that fear rest on a belief that its object is dangerous is really a demand that the agent possess some state licensing the application of that fittingness standard to that particular. But on my account there is no requirement that the licensing state and the emotion be of the same type, or even that they have the same content. A belief that the corridor is dark may license a fittingness standard that is then applied, by a wholly different kind of state, to a shape in the corridor. That the base and the emotion overlap in content is something my view *predicts* in the cases where they do, and it explains that overlap by the shared standard rather than by redundancy. I grant this needs work; the account of the licensing relation is the thinnest part of the paper.

Second, feelings are demoted but not eliminated. The felt component of an emotion, I suggest, is best understood as a second-order monitor of the emotion's own intensity and of the resources it is claiming — a mechanism that makes the state self-regulable and reportable, rather than a constituent of what the emotion is about. This explains a datum the other accounts struggle with: phenomenological intensity varies enormously across episodes of the same emotion type, and across the same episode over time, while the fittingness conditions do not vary at all. On my account that is exactly what we should expect, because intensity-monitoring and correctness-tracking are different jobs. I flag this as the most speculative claim in the paper.

What the view costs me is a unified theory. If it is right, "emotion" is not a theoretical term of affective science at all, but a normative term we apply to whatever states are governed by standards of a certain shape. The obvious worry is that such standards are too cheap: nearly every pro-attitude has one, and then fittingness-first fails to single out the emotions. My reply is that emotional standards are distinctive in being both object-directed and self-involving, unlike the standards governing desire. I am not confident that this reply does the work.
