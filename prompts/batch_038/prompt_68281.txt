Please adopt the role of a formal political epistemologist: someone equally at home in the probability theory that underlies aggregation results and in the normative debates about democratic authority into which those results get recruited. Think carefully before you write, and where the mathematics is what decides the question, work it through rather than describing it at arm's length.

Some background, because it explains why I am asking. I spent thirty-eight years as an analytical chemist, and for the last fifteen of those I served on, and occasionally chaired, federal advisory panels and consensus studies. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy, and I have become preoccupied with the jury theorem: the result that a majority vote among individually modest but better-than-chance judges converges on the correct answer as the body grows. I spent a career inside institutions that took votes and produced reports, and I now find that I cannot tell whether the practice I took for granted was justified. So I have written my commitments down as plainly as I can and I want them audited.

1. On questions of public fact that reach a legislature — whether a substance is toxic at a given exposure, whether a fishery is collapsing, whether a fiscal projection is sound — the electorate at large is a better tracker of the truth than any expert panel I could have convened, and the reason is the jury theorem's asymptotics: many individually modest but better-than-random judges, aggregated by majority rule, converge on the correct answer with probability approaching one. I take additional comfort from a claim I have read defended, that a large and cognitively diverse collection of mediocre problem-solvers can outperform a smaller homogeneous collection of excellent ones.

2. I do not trust a vote taken before the voters have argued with one another. The best judgments I ever saw a panel produce came on the second day, after members had revised their estimates in the face of one another's evidence; the private first-round ballots were worse than the settled report. Where a body can deliberate before it aggregates, it should, and I would like deliberative requirements written into procedure rather than left to custom.

3. The competence assumption is an empirical hypothesis, and it must be checked domain by domain before the theorem may be invoked in that domain. On the technical questions closest to my own former specialty, I believe the typical voter is worse than a coin flip, that the better-informed fraction is not large enough to compensate, and that public information campaigns would not change this materially.

4. No competence test, no literacy requirement, no weighted or plural vote is legitimate. Every historical use of such devices that I know of has been an instrument of exclusion, and I would not reintroduce one even if it could be shown to make collective decisions more accurate. Universal suffrage is for me a requirement of standing as an equal, not a device for getting right answers.

5. Complex questions should be decomposed. Rather than voting directly on a conclusion — whether to license a technology, say — a body should vote separately on each of the factual and normative premises the conclusion depends on, and then derive the outcome logically. I take this to be strictly more reliable than a single vote on the conclusion, because each premise-vote enjoys the theorem's guarantee, whereas the conclusion-vote merely aggregates a confused mixture of reasons.

6. Voters ought to report their judgment rather than their expectations about other voters' reports. Nevertheless, I have voted against the candidate I judged best in order to help defeat one I judged worse; I thought that rational at the time, I advised colleagues to do the same, and I would do it again.

7. A majority can get it wrong in any particular case, which is why I support supermajority requirements for certain decisions, judicial review, and the entrenchment of some rights against ordinary majorities. I hold this at the same time as the claim in (1) that the probability of error tends to zero as the body grows.

The task has two stages. First, as a mapping exercise and before you evaluate anything, go through the seven items one at a time and say for each which assumption of the theorem it depends on, which it strains, and which it leaves untouched. Then audit the set:

- Identify the genuine tensions and rank them by severity, most serious first. For each, explain the mechanism of the conflict rather than merely announcing that there is one, and say what would have to be surrendered or weakened to remove it, and what is lost by that surrender.
- Identify which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and say precisely why they dissolve. Not everything here conflicts with everything else, and an audit that flags all of it equally is of no use to me.
- If any commitment is not so much in tension as simply mistaken, say so plainly and show the error, including where my formulation states the wrong quantity or the wrong comparison.
- Finish by naming the single revision that would repair the most, and state what my position would then no longer be able to claim.

Two requests about form. I have deliberately not told you which of these I would be most reluctant to abandon; I want the ranking to follow from the structure of the commitments rather than from my attachment to them. And please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I want your reasoning, and I have no way of checking references. Assume I already know the theorem's statement; I do not need it recited to me.
