I am the deputy director of the Cedar Bend Flood Control District and for eleven years I worked under its director, Vera Lindqvist, who retires at the end of the month. I am giving the speech at her farewell dinner and I want you to write it for me.

Some facts you will need. In 1993 Vera's first boss, an Army Corps engineer, handed her a paperback of Machiavelli's *The Prince*, told her to read the chapter on fortune and skip the rest, and she has been reading that chapter ever since. Thirty years of it have come out of her as a single compressed line she uses at council meetings, in grant applications, and once, unforgivably, on a district safety poster: "You can't stop the river. You build the dykes before the rain." That is her paraphrase, not Machiavelli's wording, and there is a plaque of it in our lobby. It is also, in a way I have only recently understood, half of what the chapter says. What it drops is that the dykes do not prevent the flood — they only make it less ruinous when the water comes, because fortune is overwhelming precisely where nothing has been ordered to resist her. And it drops the harsher half: that a person's way of proceeding either suits the times or it does not, that the cautious man is undone when the moment calls for impetuosity and the impetuous man is undone when it no longer does, and that no one can change his own nature, so the temperament that saved you in one decade is the same temperament that ruins you in the next, and you could not have been otherwise. That is the argument the speech has to make, to her face, in front of her staff.

The room will be about a hundred and twenty people: her engineers and field crews, three county commissioners, a state senator, her husband, her grown daughter, her nine-year-old grandson, one member of our advisory board who teaches political theory at the community college and will notice immediately if I mangle the book, and roughly fifteen people from the east bench and from Ellsworth whose houses flooded in August 2017. In 2017 Vera ordered the Sattler bypass opened from the cab of a truck in about ninety seconds, which took farmland and spared most of Ellsworth; the pumps at the Ellsworth station failed anyway, and nine blocks stood in water for four days. Some of those families think the bypass was the right call and some think she traded their streets for a levee that was never going to fail. Both groups will be at the dinner. The speech has to touch 2017 in a sentence or two without reopening it, without claiming it went well, and without offering the people in that room the consolation that it was nobody's fault.

Requirements:

1. Nine hundred to a thousand words, six or seven minutes spoken. Write it to be read aloud by a non-writer: no clauses that need a second pass, nothing that requires a footnote tone.

2. It must be fully intelligible to everyone who has never heard of Machiavelli. They should understand that a book and an argument are at stake without needing to know the author's name, his century, or his reputation; name him at most once, late, and only if it earns its place.

3. No quotation marks around anything attributed to Machiavelli. I cannot check a translation and neither can you, so paraphrase throughout and do not put a single line in his mouth. For the same reason: do not let anyone in the speech say or imply that he wrote that the ends justify the means, do not use "Machiavellian" as a synonym for cunning or treachery, and do not call him the founder of political science.

4. The correction of Vera's slogan is the structural center of the speech, and it has to arrive as a gift rather than as a correction — she is retiring, she is beloved, and the people whose houses flooded are listening to how I characterize the judgment she made. Work out how a sentence can hand her the half she has been leaving out while making her thirty years of saying the other half look like the right thing to have said. This is the hardest thing in the assignment and the rest of the speech exists to support it.

5. It must not promise her successors control. The point of the doctrine for a room of engineers is not that preparation wins; it is that preparation changes what a disaster costs, and that the next one will come in a form the preparation was not designed for.

6. One joke. Aim it at me, or at the book's reputation, or at the poster. Not at Vera, not at 2017, not at the commissioners.

7. End in a way that can be said aloud to a room containing both gratitude and a grudge that has not gone away.

8. After the speech, add a note to me of a hundred and fifty to two hundred and fifty words, not for the room, identifying the two or three places where my version of the doctrine compresses or departs from what the chapter actually argues, and defending the compression as a speechmaking choice. In that note, also tell me what the speech loses by leaving out the chapter's notorious claim that fortune is a woman who yields to the impetuous and the young rather than to the cautious, and lets herself be beaten — I have decided not to use it, but I want to know what I am giving up, because that is where Machiavelli's own preference for boldness over caution lives and dropping it softens him. Argue the note; do not cite anything, do not recommend reading, and do not invent scholarship.
