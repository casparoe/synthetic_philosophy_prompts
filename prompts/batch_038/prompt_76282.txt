Our neighborhood tool library is shutting down and I've somehow been volunteered to say a few words at the end of it — Saturday, in the actual shed, folding chairs, bad coffee, about forty people. It ran for eleven years: a sign-out book on a nail by the door, no locks, no deposit, no fee, roughly 200 households sharing ladders, a pressure washer, a carpet cleaner that everyone hated, that kind of thing.

Here's what happened. We announced in March that we'd be winding up at the end of August — lease, insurance, the usual boring reasons — and it stayed completely fine for months. Then in July somebody started tearing pages out of the sign-out book, and by August tools were just going. Not fenced for cash, mostly just kept. The point is that once everyone knew the exact date it was over, the thing came apart in about six weeks, and everybody in that shed could feel it happening and nobody could stop it, including the people who kept signing the book honestly right up to the last day.

Two things make this hard to write. First: the person who tore out the pages will be there Saturday, everyone knows who it is, and nobody has said anything about it. She apologized to me privately in the car park. Not to the room. Second: there are two economists in the group who'll know exactly what I'm gesturing at if I gesture at it, and they've been the most cheerful people about the whole collapse, which I find unnerving.

So — write the speech. Six minutes or so. It has to be a farewell and a thank-you first; these are my neighbors, not a seminar. It has to be fully followable by the nineteen-year-old who joined in January and by the guy who only ever came for the carpet cleaner. Don't say tit for tat, Nash, folk theorem, or shadow of the future out loud. Make the ideas do the work without naming them, because what I actually want is a speech that takes seriously the possibility that the last six weeks weren't a moral failure at all but something structural about knowing when a game ends — and says it in a way that doesn't let anybody off the hook either, and doesn't curdle into a shrug.

I read some Axelrod years ago and the part that stuck with me was the nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear bit — mostly clear. The sign-out book *was* the mechanism, wasn't it. Legibility. And the pages are what got torn out first, before anything was stolen. If that's actually load-bearing for the story, use it; if I've got it wrong, tell me.

It also has to decide what to do about her. Say something to her in the room, or don't — but pick one and justify it to me, because I've been going back and fourth for days. And I'd like it to hold the question I can't answer myself: whether eleven years of people being reliably decent about ladders counts as cooperation, or was only ever mutual advantage with a friendly sign on it, and whether it makes any difference to what we should feel on Saturday. Don't resolve that into a bumper sticker. The economists keep saying we were lucky nobody ever kept score, and I still don't know if that's meant as a compliment.

End it somewhere that isn't false. We are not relaunching and I don't want borrowed hope in there.

After the speech, give me a short note: how confident are you in what it claims about why this held together for eleven years and why it came apart in six, and which single point are you least sure of? Also flag anywhere you've taken liberties with the theory or leaned on a contested reading of it to get an emotional effect.
