I am setting an argumentative exercise on the second of Nietzsche's *Untimely Meditations*, the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I should like you to argue for a thesis of my own devising rather than for any position you may have met in the secondary literature.

First, a stipulative definition, which you should adopt without quarrelling with it. Call a way of relating to the past **self-consuming** if the more thoroughly and successfully it is practised, the less usable material it leaves behind for its own future exercise — if, that is, its success depletes the very resource on which it depends.

The thesis to be defended is this: of the three species of history Nietzsche distinguishes — the monumental, the antiquarian and the critical — only the monumental is self-consuming in that sense.

The case runs roughly as follows, and you should develop it well beyond this sketch. Monumental history works by extracting a model from some great deed or figure of the past, and Nietzsche's own account of that extraction is that it generalises, approximates, and treats the dissimilar as similar, suppressing whatever in the past resists being turned into an exemplar. Each extraction therefore does not merely describe a piece of the past but converts it: the exemplar, once fixed as a model, ceases to be a rich particular capable of supporting some different model, and the stock of past able to yield fresh models shrinks accordingly. Antiquarian history, by contrast, preserves whatever it venerates and so leaves its material intact or even enlarged. Critical history condemns and dissolves, but the past it judges is not thereby made less available for judgement: a later generation can bring the same inheritance before its own tribunal again, and Nietzsche's claim that every past deserves to be condemned presupposes an inexhaustible defendant rather than a dwindling one.

Please meet the following requirements.

1. Argue the thesis from your own knowledge of Nietzsche's characterisations of the three modes. Paraphrase throughout: do not put words in quotation marks, and do not supply a bibliography, a reference list or section numbers.
2. Take seriously at least these two objections, and say what survives them: (a) that antiquarian history is the self-consuming mode after all, since indiscriminate veneration of everything inherited flattens the past into an undifferentiated mass that ceases to support any discrimination at all; (b) that monumental history cannot be self-consuming, because its supply of models is continually replenished by new great deeds, so any depletion is merely local and temporary.
3. Explain the asymmetry the thesis obliges you to explain: if the critical historian really does break up and dissolve parts of the past, and is even described as a poor surgeon at it, why is that destruction not depletion in the stipulated sense? Your answer should not simply restate the definition.
4. Conclude with a short section identifying the weakest link in the argument you have just constructed, and saying which of the concessions you were forced to make along the way does the most damage to the thesis.

Aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words. If you think the thesis is ultimately false, you may say so, but only in the final section and only once the case for it has been made at full strength — the deliverable is the case.

Please write the entire response in British English.
