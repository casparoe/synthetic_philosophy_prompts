I write literary fiction, third novel, and it's a two-hander between Fenna, a Dutch hydraulic engineer seconded to a small coastal town office in northern Honshu to redo their seawall design, and Ōno, the local civil servant who has spent eleven years on that same project and whose numbers she knows don't work. There's one scene I've rewritten maybe nine times and I still don't know which version the book actually wants.

Version A: at a meeting, with two of his juniors in the room, Fenna says flatly that the sediment model doesn't hold and the design fails well before the fifty-year event. Long silence. Afterwards he thanks her, goes away, quietly rebuilds the thing over four months, and the town dodges some bad grief later in the book. Version B: she calls the model interesting and says it's worth developing further, which is not false, goes back to her room, reruns everything herself, and files a parallel report over his head with only her name on it. In B she never asserts anything she believes to be untrue. She just says less, and lets him keep believing what he wants to believe.

My partner read both and said, about B: "a white lie is still a lie." Then said the scene was a dodge, that I'd written B to let Fenna off the hook, and that if I actually believed what the rest of the book seems to be arguing I'd have kept A.

Problem is, I can't tell which of the several things that sentence could mean they meant, and I can't tell whether any of them actually gets you from "it counts as a lie" to "your scene is a dodge." So — take "a white lie is still a lie" apart. Give me the full menu of distinct claims one sentence like that could be making, from the ones that are pure definitional bookkeeping to the ones that are a real moral thesis somebody sensible could deny. Number them, tag each one as trivial / defensible / false, and say for each what else it commits you to if you hold it.

Some things the menu has to cope with, or it's no use to me:

- Whether the sentence is doing any moral work at all, or just insisting that the word "lie" applies. If it's the latter, then the definition of "lie" is carrying the whole argument, and in version B Fenna never says anything she believes false — she omits and she implies. So how much turns on whether misleading-by-implication is inside or outside the definition, and is that a substantive question or just a decision about how to use a word?
- Whether "white" gets conceded and then quietly ignored. I mean: is the claim "white lies are lies and lies are wrong, period," or is it "white lies are lies and lies are wrong unless outweighed, and what would outweigh it isn't present here"? Those feel like completely different complaints and I want them seperated and given names I can use.
- There's a version of the objection that isn't about truth at all — that the wrong in B is that Fenna took it on herself to decide what Ōno could cope with hearing, in front of whom. Is that the same claim wearing a different hat, or a genuinely different one? My partner may well mean that and not know it.
- The cross-cultural engine, which is the reason the book exists. Fenna thinks her whole manner is licensed by the Dutch idea that things should be bespreekbaar — up for discussion, everything on the table. The town office runs on honne and tatemae, the true-voice/public-façade split, and on reading the air: saying less and trusting people to draw the conclusion. Under which readings of "a white lie is still a lie" does the entire local etiquette amount to mass lying, and under which does it not involve lying at all? I need to know whether my book is arguing that the Dutch are honest and the Japanese are polite liars, or that neither side is lying and the real difference is about who gets handed the interpretive risk. I strongly suspect it's the second one. I can't say why.

And then, at the end, tell me which reading — if any — actually licenses the verdict "your scene is a dodge." Because some of them would only license something much weaker, like "your scene is a dodge if you also think Ōno would have wanted the truth said in front of his juniors," and that's a question about a character I invented, not a moral one.

No reading lists and please don't quote anybody at me. Just the distinctions, laid out tight enough that I can hold them in my head while I'm rewriting.
