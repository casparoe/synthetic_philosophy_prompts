**Question 2** (~700 words, closed book, one index card of notes allowed): Two years ago the Republic of Kavrel emerged from a thirty-year dictatorship. In 1994 the old regime enacted the Public Confidence Decree, which made it a crime to publish "statements calculated to undermine public confidence in the national economy." The Decree was adopted by the procedure the old constitution required, printed in the official gazette that same week, applied only prospectively, was never amended, and stayed on the books until last year. It was enforced by the ordinary courts — but those courts read "calculated to undermine" in divergent ways. One line of decisions required proof that the speaker intended to cause alarm; another held that the likely effect of the words was enough; and there was no appellate body to settle the disagreement, so which test you faced depended on which courthouse you were charged in. A secondary-school teacher, Marek Dura, was convicted in 2001 under the second line of decisions after telling a local newspaper that the state's grain figures had been invented, and served four years. Dura died in 2019. His daughter now asks a Kavrelian court to declare the 2001 conviction void from the outset, so that his record can be cleared and his pension entitlements restored. Two routes are open:

(i) the court holds that the Decree was never law, so the conviction was a nullity;
(ii) the court holds that the Decree was valid law when applied to Dura, and leaves the matter to the new legislature, which would have to pass a retroactive statute vacating convictions under it.

Which route would Hart urge, which would Fuller, and what is each of their reasons? What does the disagreement between them actually turn on? Would either of them expect (i) and (ii) to come out differently in practice for someone like Dura's daughter? Argue from the facts about the Decree, not just from the general shape of the two theories.

---

And this is what the student handed in:

"Hart is a legal positivist, so for him the question of what the law is is seperate from the question of what the law ought to be. Validity comes from a rule's source rather than its content — in Hart's later work, from a rule of recognition accepted by officials. The Decree was passed the way Kavrel's constitution required and published properly, so it was valid law in 2001 however awful it was. Hart would therefore say the court can't just declare it never existed, because that's pretending. But he wouldn't send Dura's daughter away empty-handed either. Hart's preferred answer in the informer cases is a retroactive statute: he admits retroactivity is a bad thing but treats it as the lesser of two evils, since at least the legislature then does openly what it's doing instead of dressing up a moral judgment as a finding about validity.

Fuller rejects the separation thesis. He says law has an inner morality made up of eight principles — rules must be general, published, prospective, clear, non-contradictory, possible to comply with, stable, and administered the way they were announced — and that a system which fails these isn't a bad legal system, it isn't a legal system at all. A decree that is fundamentally unjust, like this one, which put a man in prison for four years for saying the grain statistics were invented, is not law. So Fuller backs route (i), which gets the morally better result, and Hart is left with something true but useless.

Worth noticing though that Hart isn't a pure positivist. In The Concept of Law he devotes a chapter to the minimum content of natural law, arguing that any viable society needs rules restricting violence and protecting property because of human vulnerability, approximate equality and limited altruism. That concession means Hart already accepts that a thoroughly wicked system can't count as a legal system, so the two are much closer than the labels suggest. In the end the disagreement is mostly terminological: both think the Decree was monstrous, both want Dura's daughter to get her declaration, and they only disagree about whether the monstrosity is described inside or outside the concept of law. Kavrel should take route (i) because it's quicker for the family, and both Hart and Fuller would say the outcome is what really matters."
