I teach an upper-level political philosophy seminar whose second unit runs from Rousseau's Discourse on Inequality to his Letter to d'Alembert, and this term I have added a screening of Peter Weir's The Truman Show on the Friday before our discussion of the Letter. The pairing was originally a scheduling convenience, but the longer I sit with it the less incidental it looks. Rousseau's objection to the theater is not chiefly that plays are frivolous or that actors are disreputable; it is that a spectacle is a machine for producing a certain kind of person — one whose sense of his own worth is mediated by the gaze of others, who undergoes strong feeling vicariously and is thereby discharged of acting on it, and who mistakes the pleasure of being moved in a darkened room for the exercise of virtue. Seahaven appears to be that machine scaled up to an entire life. Truman's neighbors are extras, his closest friend is a performer taking his lines through an earpiece, his wife slips product placements into the middle of marital arguments, and his terror of open water was deliberately implanted in childhood so that he would never want to leave. Even the weather is scheduled.

What I want is a serious reading of the film through the Letter — not a thematic gesture in Rousseau's direction, but a mechanism-by-mechanism comparison. Take his principal claims in turn: the account of pity and the way a performance spends the moral sentiment it arouses; the diagnosis of amour-propre and the dependence on others' opinion that public display cultivates; the treatment of Molière's Misanthrope, where the worry is that the play makes plain honesty ridiculous and worldly sociability admirable, and so trains the audience's sympathies in the wrong direction; the insistence that theater flatters rather than reforms the mores of its public, so that a corrupt audience has its corruption confirmed and a virtuous one finds virtue tedious; and above all the positive alternative, the open-air festival in which the people are their own spectacle, see themselves and one another, and are bound together by that mutual visibility. For each, tell me which elements of the film bear it out, which are simply indifferent to it, and which actively strain it. I would also like the film's other spectators taken into account: Rousseau's theatergoer is at least bodily present in the assembly, whereas the audiences the movie cuts away to — the bartenders, the man in the bathtub, the two security guards — watch Truman from an armchair, and then change the channel the moment he walks out the door. Does that difference rescue Rousseau's account, or is it the purest confirmation of it?

The part I most want you to engage, because I cannot make it work on my own, is that the film seems to invert Rousseau's prescription rather than illustrate his diagnosis. Seahaven is in a real sense the festival he asks for: a small, walkable, mutually transparent community in which everyone knows everyone, nobody is a stranger, and the boundary between performer and spectator has collapsed. What the film suggests is that the collapse runs in both directions — the citizen who is also the spectacle is not thereby liberated but is the most thoroughly administered person alive. So I want you to say whether the Letter has resources for this; whether Rousseau's confidence in transparency and mutual visibility is a genuine blind spot that the movie exposes; or whether Seahaven is missing something constitutive of the festival — that it is a crowd of one, with no participants, and that his point was precisely that the effect depends on a plurality of people performing for one another. Be as scrupulous about the film as about the text: if Christof's paternalism, which is the strongest thing in the movie's case against Truman's departure, tracks Rousseau's own willingness to let the legislator speak through a borrowed divine authority for the people's own good, say so, and then say where the parallel breaks down. If some of what I have sketched here is me overreaching, tell me which pieces. Assume I know the Letter well but have not seen the film in a decade; do not recapitulate the plot beyond what the argument requires, do not attempt verbatim quotation from either source, and close by telling me what, if anything, the film settles about the anti-theatrical case that argument alone could not.
