I should explain the situation before the argument, because I suspect the two are not easy to separate. Since 2021 my wife and I have been converting a former Methodist chapel on the edge of Barnsley into a bouldering centre, with a small café where the vestry was. I am a joiner by trade and did much of the work myself, but the building has been unkind to us: the survey did not reveal the extent of the rot in the roof timbers, and a much larger climbing centre opened in Sheffield twenty minutes away about a year after we started. We have now spent four years and £40,000 in cash, on top of my own labour. To finish the thing properly would cost another £90,000, and the finished building would be worth perhaps £50,000; unfinished, the shell would fetch about £15,000. My wife, who keeps the books for a haulage firm and is not a sentimental woman, has been telling me for eight months that we should stop. I have not been able to agree with her, and because I could not agree with her and stay quiet about it, I wrote the following out on the back of a delivery note. I have read around this subject for a year or two on my own — enough to know the phrase escalation of commitment, and enough to have met the usual rejoinder that the money is gone either way and therefore cannot be a reason for anything — but I do not think that rejoinder touches what I have written, since nowhere in it do I suppose that the money might come back.

Here is the argument as it stands. (1) The only things that can serve as reasons to continue are facts about what will happen if I continue; the past is spent and cannot be altered by anything I do now. (2) Among the facts about what will happen if I continue, however, are facts about what other people will afterwards expect of me, and about what sort of decision-maker I shall have become. (3) If I walk away from this chapel, everyone who knows of it will revise their view of me: they will expect me to walk away from the next thing as well, and will be correspondingly less willing to lend me money, go into partnership with me, or take my word on a price or a date; and I shall myself have spent four years practising the habit of quitting. (4) So the four years and the £40,000 are not inert after all — through (2) and (3) they enter into what will happen if I continue, and by (1) they are therefore reasons to continue. (5) Separately, my having committed four years and £40,000 is itself evidence about the project: the man who committed that sum over that length of time, standing on the site with the quotes in his hand and the roof open above him, knew considerably more about the prospects than I can now reconstruct, and I have no better means of discovering what he judged than by looking at what he did. (6) The larger the commitment, the stronger that evidence is, since a bigger stake is harder to explain away as carelessness or enthusiasm. (7) It follows that the more I have sunk, the more reason I have to go on. (8) Nothing in (1) to (7) distinguishes a project that is merely unpromising from one that is hopeless, nor a modest sunk cost from an enormous one; whatever force (3) and (6) possess, they possess in every case, and with greater force in the worse cases, since the worse the case the more there is to abandon and the more plainly one has staked one's judgement on it. (9) Therefore the size of a sunk cost is always a reason to continue, and there is no such thing as a sunk cost fallacy: those who speak of one have simply failed to notice (3) and (5). (10) Yet it seems to me plainly irrational for a man to spend £90,000 completing something worth £50,000 when he could sell the shell for £15,000, and the more he has already spent on it, the plainer that irrationality becomes rather than the less.

I cannot live with (10), and I cannot see how to keep it without pulling something out of the chain above it. So: tell me which step or steps you would reject, and why each of them looked entirely respectable to me when I wrote it down. Tell me also what the rejection costs, because I should much rather keep (3) and (5) than anything else — those two feel like discoveries, and I suspect that whatever is wrong with my reasoning is wrong with them last of all — and I am not willing to give up (1) either, since if the past as such can generate reasons I do not know what a reason is. If some properly restricted version of the thought in (3) and (5) survives, I should like to see it set out, together with whatever conditions it needs in order not to run away with itself in the way (8) seems to demand. And I should like a straight answer to a question I cannot settle alone: is this a sound argument that I am simply too close to judge, or is it a rationalisation with the arithmetic tidied?
