Our department's student-run station produces a monthly series in which a graduate student impersonates a dead author and fields questions strictly in character, drawing only on what that author actually published and on the circumstances of the moment we have set the interview in. A faculty adviser enforces the rules and cuts any question that invites an anachronism or a piece of generic introspection. Last semester's Hume episode collapsed because the interviewer opened by asking about Hume's childhood and the impersonator then spent twenty minutes reciting famous lines the whole audience already knew. Our next episode places Camus in Paris in the late autumn of 1957 — the year his essay against the guillotine appeared in a joint volume alongside Arthur Koestler's essay against hanging, and the year he was awarded the Nobel Prize — and I have the twenty-eight-minute slot. I have read the essay twice and I can already hear it being quoted back at me; what I need is a set of questions that makes the position work for a living.

Draft nine questions. For each, give the question itself, then a bracketed line noting the answer he is most likely to give on the strength of his published positions, then a follow-up that actually closes off that answer instead of rephrasing the original. I want the set to cover the following: the empirical shape of his deterrence argument, and in particular what his position would rest on if the comparative statistics came out the other way, since two regular listeners are law students who think that literature has moved considerably since 1957; his comparison of the state's killing to the criminal's on grounds of premeditation and months of calculated waiting, pressed against a retributivist who never claimed deterrence in the first place and therefore does not seem to be touched by the comparison at all; the opening account of his father's nausea after witnessing an execution and the essay's broader claim about euphemism, secrecy, and publicity, asked in a way that forces him to say whether that material is evidence or rhetoric; his proposal to substitute lifelong labor for execution, including whether he has simply recommended a penalty harsher than death on the sole strength of its being reversible, and what he says to the man who serves twenty of those years before a court discovers the mistake; the half-measure he floats of letting the condemned choose a lethal injection for himself; and the argument that the extreme penalty made a certain kind of sense in a civilization that believed in atonement and divine judgment, together with the obvious implication for whether his abolitionism depends on living in a world that has stopped believing.

Two further questions should come from his record rather than from the essay. One on the 1945 clemency petition for Robert Brasillach, which Camus signed though he loathed what Brasillach wrote, while Sartre and Beauvoir refused to sign: ask him what principle is doing the work when the condemned man's cause is one he despises, and whether signing was a matter of consistency or merely of paying a price he thought worth paying. One on Algeria: the essay appeared while the war there was intensifying, with killings on both sides, and his position seems to forbid the state to execute a convicted man while leaving unsettled what the state may do in a conflict like that one — ask him for the principle that separates the two cases, and if you fold in the Stockholm exchange of that same year, as it is usually paraphrased, in which he was pressed to choose between justice and his mother, frame the question so that he cannot simply answer with the paraphrase. Close with the question that ought to come last: what would count, for him, as evidence against his own position, or what concession from a defender of capital punishment could he accept without abandoning it. Three constraints: nothing in the set may ask him to summarize his view, to say what the essay means to him, or to talk about his life; every question must be answerable by someone playing Camus in the autumn of 1957 who has read only Camus; and each must be short enough to be spoken aloud without sounding like a seminar paper.
