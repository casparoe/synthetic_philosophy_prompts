Ok so I'm in the middle of a philosophy of science course and we just did the Duhem/Lakatos stuff about ad hoc rescue and degenerating problem shifts, and my essay ended up being about Malthus. I've landed on a position I want to stress-test hard before I commit to it in the essay, because I can tell my tutor is going to go straight for it.

The position: Malthus's principle of population was right but early. Ie the mechanism he described — population tending to outrun the means of subsistence, so that any improvement in living standards gets eaten by more mouths until the mortality check bites again — is basically correct, it just hasn't come due yet, because for the last ~200 years we kept stumbling into escape hatches (agricultural intensification, the demographic transition, global trade). On this reading the theory wasn't refuted by the last two centuries, it was *postponed* by them.

What would count against that? I want a proper map of its exposure, not a summary of the debate. Specifically:

- Sort the defeaters from genuinely fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which bucket most of them fall in. I suspect the fatal ones are not the ones people usually reach for.
- The two-checks problem. If both the preventive and the positive check are available to the theory, is there any conceivable demographic history it actually rules out? Population explodes and people get poorer — confirmed. Population stabilises and people get richer — also confirmed (preventive check). Tell me what it would take for the theory to be risky at all, and whether the right-but-early version is any riskier than the plain version.
- The postdiction issue. People say Malthus got vindicated by the finding that real wages were roughly flat for most of human history until around 1800. Does fitting the past help a claim about the future here, or is that just retrodiction doing no work for the prediction that hasn't come off? I can't tell if this is a real objection or a cheap shot.
- The auxiliary hypothesis question. Saying the Green Revolution and falling fertility "delayed" the mechanism — is that an auxiliary with independent support, or a patch manufactured after the fact to cover the anomaly? What's the actual test for telling those apart, and does the Malthusian pass it?
- The mirror-image rival: Boserup's idea that population pressure itself drives agricultural innovation. If a Malthusian and a Boserupian can both read every single data point as confirming their own story, is there an observation that could break the tie, or is this dispute not empirical any more? And if it's not empirical, does that damage my "right but early" claim in particular, or only the rival?
- I keep wanting to describe the Malthusian law as a *tendency* that keeps getting interfered with. Is that structurally the same as ceteris paribus laws elsewhere, and does that make the move legitimate or just dress up an excuse?
- The Simon–Ehrlich wager: inflation-adjusted prices of the five metals Ehrlich picked all fell over the 1980s and Simon won. Malthusians say prices are the wrong measure of scarcity and a decade is nothing. Is "the window was too short" a principled reply or goalpost-moving — and is there a version of that reply that would be principled? Say what it would have to look like.
- What does the right-but-early position predict that its cornucopian rivals don't? I want at least one thing that's checkable in principle even if it's checkable only over a century.
