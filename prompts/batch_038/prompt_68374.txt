So, no academic background here — I work in freight logistics — but I read Anarchy, State, and Utopia a couple years ago off a library copy and I've been stuck on the part about animals ever since. I've finally written up my thoughts as a short essay to post on a philosophy forum I lurk on, and I want honest comments on the draft before I do. Two ground rules: please don't rewrite it for me, and don't send me a reading list, because I can't get hold of journal articles anyway and I'm not looking for homework. What I want is (1) tell me flatly where I've misdescribed Nozick, if I have; (2) which of my four moves actually does work and which is padding; (3) an honest answer on whether move 2 is just the first thing everyone says, because I suspect it is; and (4) a ruling on the shadow-pricing bit in move 4, where I argue against my own idea and then can't tell which side wins.

DRAFT (rough, ~800 words, holes I know about):

Nozick's view is usually summarized as: people get rights that work as side constraints, animals get a kind of utilitarianism, meaning we should maximize the total happiness of animals rather than treat each animal as having a claim that can't be traded away. This gets described as a half measure, a grudging concession that leaves animals below people. I want to argue the description is backwards. Animals get the one thing Nozick spends the whole book attacking, and it doesn't look like that because the label sounds like a gift.

Move 1: two currencies, no exchange rate. His theory runs two moral currencies that never touch. Where only people are involved you can't sum across them; where only animals are involved you can. But every case anyone actually cares about has both in it — the chicken and the cheap meal, the lab mouse and the patient, the wetland and the housing development. For those cases the framework doesn't say the answer is one thing rather than another. It says there's no comparison to be made at all. That isn't a conservative verdict, it's no verdict, and I think that silence, not the ranking of animals below people, is the real cost of the split.

Move 2: the concession runs the wrong direction. The argument against summing for people is that there is no single subject who experiences the total — only seperate individuals who each carry their own losses. If that's the reason to refuse summation, then it's a claim about the shape of the moral situation, not about intelligence or planning or self-consciousness. Animals are separate individuals too, each carrying their own. So handing animals summation isn't handing them a smaller portion of the same thing; it's handing them the thing he calls a moral mistake and then calling it an entitlement.

I know he says the relevant capacities come in degrees and that there's no sharp line, and I'm not going to pretend that's comfortable for him. But notice degrees don't touch moves 1 and 3, which are about what happens on either side of the line, not about where it sits.

Move 3: total, not average. He says total happiness. If it's genuinely total, then adding animals whose lives are worth living raises the total, so the animals' entitlement pushes toward more animals rather than happier ones. A system that breeds an enormous number of chickens into lives that are barely worth living could then be doing better by the animals' own entitlement than a small flock in good conditions — which lands close to a defense of exactly the practice the clause was meant to condemn. Switching to average fixes that, but then the entitlement is maximized by there being fewer animals, and eventually by there being none at all once lives get bad enough, so the clause that was supposed to protect animals licenses deleting them. Both readings give a result that looks wrong, and I don't see anything in his account that gives grounds for picking between them.

Move 4, where I'm stuck: shadow pricing. Keep the side constraints, but say a harm that a constraint blocks doesn't just vanish — it casts a shadow, and the shadow counts as a reason to make the harm up elsewhere, provided you never pay for the making-up by violating a constraint. That's supposed to get you something exchange-rate-like without ever summing people. The problem shows up two sentences later: if a shadow has a size, and sizes can be compared, then you've built a weighting scheme, and a weighting scheme is summation with better manners — the thing the constraints existed to block. So either shadows have no size, in which case they do no work and move 1 stands untouched, or they have a size, in which case I've reinvented the view I started by rejecting. I genuinely don't know which horn to grab. Related: I think he floats the idea of a smarter species that treats us the way we treat animals, and I can't remember whether he means it to shake the line between us and them or only to make the reader squirm, and it matters for move 2, because if it's the first then he's already half-conceding my point.

Conclusion I'm not sure I believe: the human/animal split isn't a placeholder he intended to refine. It's a diagnosis. Any theory that wants constraints for one kind of creature and aggregation for another owes an account of how much a blocked harm is worth, and there's no way to give that account without either collapsing into weighting or leaving the mixed cases — all the interesting ones — with no answer at all.

End of draft.
