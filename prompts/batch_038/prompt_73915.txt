I've been circling the Ellsberg material for years now, mostly through the rationalist blog lineage and the decision-theory posts it points at, and our local meetup finally gave it a proper session last Thursday: one urn with a known fifty-fifty split of red and black balls, one whose composition nobody reveals, bets offered on drawing red, and then the single-urn version with ninety balls and red, black, and yellow. Nearly everyone in the room bet on the well-described urns, and when a member pointed out that this pattern of preferences is precisely what Savage's sure-thing principle rules out, the organizer waved it away with the old proverb — better the devil you know than the devil you don't — delivered as though it settled the matter. Half the room heard a substantive defense of ambiguity aversion; the other half heard someone naming a bias and mistaking the naming for a justification. I could not decide which, and I would like to arrive at the next session with something better than a shrug of my own.

So I would like the proverb treated as a candidate principle of decision theory and pulled apart into the distinct precise claims it might be making, ranged from the platitudinous to the genuinely radical. Several intermediate readings are visible to me but I cannot state them cleanly: a claim that the unknown composition was fixed by someone who may have selected it adversarially with my betting behavior in mind, so that my first-order probabilities over the draws are not what a naive symmetry argument suggests; a claim that ambiguity amounts to extra dispersion in my own estimate rather than extra expected loss, which bites only for an agent whose utility is bounded or who is loss-averse about downside rather than caring solely about the mean; a claim about hedgeability and about the value of further information, i.e. that the known devil permits insurance and revision in ways the unknown one does not; a claim that the two urns differ in how far the description I have been given can be trusted, which is a claim about testimony and not about probability at all; and a reading on which the proverb reports a psychological regularity and carries no normative content whatsoever. For each reading, please tell me what apparatus it requires, whether an ordinary subjective Bayesian with a prior over urn compositions can hold it without embarrassment, whether it can be captured by letting an agent carry a set of priors and choose so as to do acceptably across all of them, and whether it actually earns the preference for the known devil in each of the setups — the two-urn case, the ninety-ball case, a variant where the unknown composition was determined by a coin flip performed in front of me, and a variant where it was chosen by an opponent who saw my bet before committing. I am also curious which readings leave the agent vulnerable to being pumped across a sequence of choices and which do not, since that seemed to be the objection the organizer was really dodging.

Finally, say which of these readings a proverb-quoting layperson most likely intends, and give me your verdict on the organizer: was there a reading on which his remark genuinely answered the objection, or was the room's skeptical half correct that he had only relabeled the phenomenon? A list I can print and hand around would be ideal, with each candidate reading stated in a single sentence before you assess it, and with the trivial, the defensible, and the false clearly marked rather than blended together.
