Anglo-American insurance law has long required that anyone buying a policy hold an insurable interest in its subject matter, but the requirement is applied so unevenly that I cannot make it cohere. In life insurance the interest need exist only at inception: a policy taken out by, say, a spouse may later be sold to an investor with no connection to the insured who stands to gain from the death, and there is a large secondary market in precisely such policies. Property insurance works the other way — the interest must persist up to the loss, so a seller who has closed on the house generally cannot collect for a fire that occurs afterward. Most states also prohibit arrangements in which a stranger induces someone to take out a policy for the purpose of immediate resale, which suggests the law attaches weight to the moment of origination specifically. Meanwhile, the same legal system cheerfully enforces wagers in which neither party has any exposure at all: licensed bets on athletic contests, and single-name credit default swaps bought by institutions that hold none of the reference bonds and would lose nothing on the default except the premium.

What account of markets as generators of knowledge does this pattern presuppose, if any? My tentative reconstruction is that the doctrine encodes a principle to the effect that a party's belief about a risk is admitted into the price only when that party has something at stake in how the risk resolves — that exposure is what makes a bid informative rather than a guess. But that principle looks flatly contradicted by the naked credit default swap, and it is not obviously what the life-versus-property asymmetry tracks either. Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as the material allows. Consider whether something non-epistemic does the real explanatory work — moral hazard and the incentives the insured faces, the historical unenforceability of gambling debts, the direction in which the transfer runs, or simple path dependence across two different bodies of law. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely sits comfortably with it, and tell me which single doctrinal feature most sharply discriminates among the candidate theories. Finally: suppose a regulator adopted as its working premise that rational parties sharing a common prior should never want to trade with one another, so that any purely speculative transaction is itself evidence that one side is ignorant or not reasoning properly. What would have to change in insurable-interest doctrine, in position limits on futures and swaps, and in the secondary market for life policies — and would that regime be any more coherent than the one we have?
