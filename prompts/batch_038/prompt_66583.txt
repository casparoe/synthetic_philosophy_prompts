I serve as a handling editor at a small journal in the philosophy and foundations of physics. Roughly thirty submissions per round bear on the reality of the quantum state, and they are triaged by three editors, only one of whom is a specialist. What I need is a triage sheet that can be run in about twenty-five minutes from the abstract and introduction alone, that sorts each submission with respect to the psi-ontic / psi-epistemic distinction, and that tells us when to stop and hand the paper to the specialist rather than decide anything ourselves. I would like you to write that sheet.

The distinction as I understand it, and as I want the sheet to use it: an ontological model posits a space Lambda of ontic states, associates each preparation procedure with a probability distribution over Lambda, and associates each measurement procedure with response functions giving the probability of each outcome conditional on lambda. A model is psi-ontic if distinct pure quantum states are represented by distributions with disjoint support, so that lambda is compatible with at most one pure state; it is psi-epistemic if some pair of distinct pure states is represented by overlapping distributions, so that lambda leaves open which state was prepared; and it is maximally psi-epistemic if, for every pair, the overlap of the two distributions equals the quantum overlap of the states.

The sheet my co-editors currently use reads as follows. I know it produces wrong results, but I cannot say precisely where it goes wrong, and I would rather you diagnose it than take my word for any part of it.

1. Does the author describe the quantum state in terms of knowledge, information, or belief? If so, the paper belongs to the psi-epistemic camp; if the author says the state is real or physical, it belongs to the psi-ontic camp.
2. Any psi-epistemic proposal is refuted by the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem unless the authors explicitly reject preparation independence. Silence on that assumption is therefore grounds for returning the paper as internally inconsistent.
3. Is the model measurement-noncontextual? If it is psi-epistemic and noncontextual, the Kochen–Specker theorem rules it out; if it is psi-epistemic and contextual, it survives.
4. A psi-ontic classification commits the author to a realist interpretation with definite outcomes; a psi-epistemic classification commits the author to an instrumentalist or anti-realist one.
5. Any submission that denies there is an ontic state space at all is psi-epistemic by default, since it treats the quantum state as a bookkeeping device.

The submissions I want you to run the corrected sheet on are these four.

A. A hidden-variable model for a single qubit. Lambda is the Bloch sphere; each pure state is represented by a distribution whose support is strictly larger than the corresponding point, with the overlap for any pair equal to the quantum overlap; all single-qubit statistics are reproduced. The authors state that their response functions depend on which measurement is actually performed, not merely on the eigenvalues of the measured observable.

B. A defence of Bohmian mechanics against the charge of being psi-ontic. The authors accept the classification for what it is worth but argue that in their theory the wavefunction plays the role of a law governing the particles rather than that of a physical state of the system, so that the criterion sorts their view into the wrong box. They add that their model has definite particle positions and deterministic dynamics, and that Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph does not worry them since they are psi-ontic anyway.

C. A programme paper claiming that Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph does not threaten a knowledge-based view of the state, because preparation independence fails: the ontic state of a system is held to depend on both its past preparation and its future measurement settings. The authors sketch how a toy two-state model might be built on this basis. There is no explicit Lambda, no distributions, and no response functions.

D. A paper arguing that the ontological-models framework is itself misconceived: there is no lambda to distribute over, and the quantum state is an agent's personal commitment, revisable by experience, with no claim to represent anything observer-independent. The author objects to being assigned to either side of the distinction.

Please deliver the following.

- The corrected sheet: numbered questions in the order they are to be asked, each with what counts as a sufficient answer at desk level, what answer stops the sheet and routes the paper to the specialist, and the limited set of conclusions an editor may draw from it. Its outputs should be routing decisions, not verdicts on the physics.
- A short diagnosis of each of the five steps above, tied to the step that replaces it, saying what it gets wrong rather than merely that it is unreliable.
- Which of your own steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and what a non-specialist should do at each of those points.
- A list of what the sheet cannot establish from an abstract and introduction alone, in particular what distinguishes a model that is psi-epistemic for a restricted fragment of the theory from one that is psi-epistemic for the whole of it, and why that difference decides whether a no-go theorem applies.
- The sheet run on A, B, C and D, giving for each the output and the reasons. Submission C is one I favour and have already told my co-editors I want sent out for review; if the sheet says otherwise, say so plainly and do not soften it to spare me.

Two constraints. The sheet must be usable by an editor with one undergraduate course in quantum mechanics and no measure theory, and it must fit on a single printed page, with the diagnoses and worked cases appended separately. And please do not append a reference list or suggest further reading: I need the instrument, not the literature behind it.
