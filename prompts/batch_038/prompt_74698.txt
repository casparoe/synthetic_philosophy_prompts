I have just been made the decision analyst for a regional emergency management agency. Roughly ten times a year I sit down with county officials to choose among preparedness measures, and the same pattern appears almost every time: they will take a measure that guarantees a modest, visible benefit over one with a substantially higher expected benefit but some chance of accomplishing nothing. An outside consultant we hired told us this is textbook irrationality — the Allais paradox, a violation of the independence axiom, the certainty effect — and that our job is to correct it. A colleague of mine says the opposite: that Allais's own point was that the axiom rules out a preference for guarantees that is perfectly defensible, and that the officials are entitled to it. The county emergency manager signs off on whatever I bring her, usually at the end of a single two-hour session.

Please write me a decision procedure I can run in that room, in under an hour, in front of people with high school algebra and no economics: a sequence of questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each. It should say explicitly where the procedure terminates in accept the officials' certainty preference, where it terminates in flag the preference and present the expected-value ranking instead, and where it stops without an answer and hands the question to elected leadership because it turns on values rather than facts. I would rather lose the formalism than lose the distinctions that matter — in particular, the difference between a certainty that is real and one that exists only inside the model — and I want you to mark which of your steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume.

Two further requirements. First, the procedure must include a diagnostic for whether the officials' pattern is the common-consequence kind of violation or the common-ratio kind, and must say what follows differently from each diagnosis; I have been told these are not the same complaint, and I have no idea how to tell them apart without administering some further choice to the room. Second, our consultant's memo used a phrase I would like to be able to test for. I will call it certainty laundering: a measure presented as a guarantee whose guarantee is manufactured by an assumption in the analysis — a funding line that must be renewed every budget cycle, a partner agency's pledge, a historical base rate silently treated as a floor — rather than by anything in the world. Build a test for that into the procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases, showing every step:

1. County A must choose between a buyout of 20 homes in a mapped flood plain, which certainly removes those 20 homes from harm, and a levee upgrade with a 90 percent chance of protecting 200 homes and a 10 percent chance of protecting none of them. It must then choose between an 11 percent chance of protecting those same 20 homes, with an 89 percent chance of protecting none, and a 10 percent chance of protecting the 200 homes, with a 90 percent chance of protecting none. The officials take the buyout in the first pair and the 10 percent levee option in the second.

2. County B prefers a heat-emergency plan whose protection depends on a cooling-center commitment the city council reauthorizes every year, over a grid-hardening measure with a higher expected number of lives saved.

3. A case of your own construction from this domain in which the procedure returns a verdict I will not like — most likely one in which my own agency's recommended package is the thing that has to be flagged.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which of these decisions it will get wrong, and where I would be better off saying the model is silent than manufacturing an answer out of it.

Please write the entire response in American English.
