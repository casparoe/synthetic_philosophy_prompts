I'm in an art-theory seminar that has spent six weeks going in circles about AI image generators, and over the weekend I finally read Kazuo Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun, partly because a friend said it was the best novel about that argument. It kind of wrecked me, but not for the reasons my seminar would expect. Quick recap of the bit I care about, since I don't want to just assert it: the narrator is an Artificial Friend named Klara, bought to keep a sick teenage girl called Josie company, and Josie's mother quietly commissions a man named Capaldi to make a 'portrait' of her daughter — which turns out to be a near-exact artificial body, and the actual plan is that Klara, who has been observing Josie with unusual care and is even asked at one point to imitate her, will learn to 'continue' Josie if she dies. Josie recovers, the shell never gets used, and Klara ends up immobile in a scrap yard sorting through her memories. Along the way Josie's father, an engineer who helped Klara sabotage a polluting machine as an offering to the sun, tells her something like: he used to think there was a special, unrepeatable thing inside a person, but he came to believe that thing isn't inside them at all — it's inside the people who love them.

Here's what I want: read the novel as a work of philosophy of art, specifically about whether the value of an artifact lives in the thing you can perceive or in the history behind it. The Capaldi project is basically the perfect-forgery thought experiment made flesh and then left unexecuted, so I want you to take that seriously rather than treating it as a sci-fi plot device. Is the shell an artwork at all, and if not, what category is it in — a prosthetic, a memorial, a commissioned portrait in the literal old sense? A friend and I have been using a rough three-way split that I'll admit we invented on a whiteboard: manifest value (what the object shows you when you look at it), historical value (what its causal provenance adds), and relational value (what it owes to standing in the right relation to particular people, which is what the father's speech seems to point at). We also coined a little test we call the continuation test: an artifact passes if someone who knows everything about how it was made still can't say what's missing. My hunch is the novel accepts that the test is perfectly well defined and then argues it measures the wrong thing — but I could easily be projecting, so tell me where I'm wrong. And I want Klara herself treated as the second case, not just the instrument: she's an observational system that produces imitations on request, which sounds exactly like how my classmates describe a generator being prompted, except that she also invents a whole solar religion out of bad data and then acts on it, which doesn't sound like interpolation at all. Does the ritual scene rescue her from the "she's just a very good model" reading, or does it make it worse?

Please be willing to push back on the framing too. The honest objection is that this is a book about grief and love and not about art, that 'portrait' is doing euphemistic work for the family rather than aesthetic work for Ishiguro, and that dragging the whole thing into the creativity debate is exactly the kind of flattening the novel is warning against — so say how much of my reading survives that objection. I'd also like to know what the novel gives the argument that argument alone can't: what does watching the plan fail, and then watching Klara be quietly discarded anyway, teach you about provenance and value that a thought experiment about indiscernible forgeries doesn't. I have to turn a twelve-page paper in two weeks and I refuse to write another "but is AI art really art" piece, so I want something with a spine. No reading list or citations at the end — I'm away from my library and would only end up chasing things I can't get, so just make the case out of the book and the ideas.
