So here's a thing that's been eating at me at work and I can't tell whether I'm being principled or just squeamish.

I manage a team of about eight, and one guy — call him Dan — has had a rough six months. Missed deadlines, snapped at a client on a call, weird gaps in his day where he's just offline and unresponsive. Last week he came to me and said he'd been to his GP and got a diagnosis, and he wants me to route him through occupational health and treat the whole business as a health issue rather than a performance issue. He was really insistent on that framing. His reasoning: if it's a health thing he gets time off and accomodations, and nobody has to have the conversation about whether his behavior was acceptable.

HR loves this. They immediately started describing it as a medical matter, and one of them said something to the effect of we don't pass judgment on medical conditions. And everyone's visibly relieved, because now nobody has to say the uncomfortable thing.

Here's my problem, and I can't articulate it, which is why I'm asking: I don't think anyone in that room actually stopped believing Dan's behavior was bad. The client is still annoyed. The team still had to cover for him. The disagreement about whether what he did was okay hasn't been resolved, it's been relabeled — we swapped "was that okay?" for "is he sick?" and then acted like the second question was the one we'd been arguing about all along. And now we all get to feel generous instead of evasive.

At the same time I know exactly how the counterargument goes. Plenty of conditions really are medical, the line between bad behavior and symptom is a real line that matters, and if I go around saying the health framing is always a dodge I end up sounding like someone who thinks depressed people should just pull themselves together. That's not where I'm at.

Can you help me get clear on what's actually going on here? Specifically: is there a principled line between cases where the health framing is honest — where it genuinely dissolves the moral question — and cases where it's a polite wrapper we put on a conflict about conduct and values so nobody has to adjudicate it? And is the fact that Dan asked for the framing himself supposed to change the analysis, or is that precisely what makes the wrapper work so well?

I've read enough old-school rationalist blogging to get suspicious whenever a definition stretches to fit whatever's convenient, and this feels like a case where one word is doing a huge amount of load-bearing work for people who'd rather not say out loud what they're deciding. But I could totally be the one motivated-reasoning, so tell me if I am.
