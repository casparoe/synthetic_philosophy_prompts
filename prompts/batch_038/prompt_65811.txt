I run a reading group on probability, about ten people — half stats/ML, half philosophy grad students — and every meeting it collapses into the same argument. Somebody says chances are out there in the world, somebody else says probabilities are just in your head, and then it turns out they'd both take exactly the same bet on the coin and nothing gets resolved. So I want to build a sorting instrument that separates people by *which deference principle they actually accept* rather than by what they say about the metaphysics of chance.

Write it as a decision tree that can also be run as a quiz: seven or eight branch nodes, each a concrete case with real numbers in it (a coin, a die, an oracle, an announced chance, a bet with a dollar figure), each with two or three options, phrased so a participant can pick without knowing which position any option leads to. Then a scoring key that takes an answer string — 1a, 2c, 3b, ... — and maps it onto a named leaf.

Starting commitment is just "there are such things as chances, and learning one should normally move your credence." Leaves I want to end up with at minimum: the Principal Principle treated as analytic/definitional (part of what makes something a chance at all) versus treated as a substantive norm that needs an independent argument, accuracy-based or betting-based; the camp that says Lewis's original principle misfires when your evidence is inadmissible and should be replaced by the conditionalized version, versus the camp that wants to keep both; the view that there's no principled line between admissible and inadmissible evidence so the whole thing is indeterminant; the deflationist who denies the principle makes any claim about the world; the radical subjectivist for whom chance just is somebody's credence and there's no deference norm left to state; someone who thinks a deterministic world has no chances so the principle is vacuous; and the Everettian who lets branch weights play the chance role.

Two cases I definitely want in there: the oracle one, where a perfectly reliable crystal ball tells you the coin came up heads, so the principle naively applied tells you to hold at 1/2 while your evidence tells you 1; and the averaging one, where you're 60/40 between the chance being 1/2 and it being 1/4, so the weighted credence is .4, and it's a real question whether that falls out of the principle or needs an extra premise about total evidence. Also work in one node where the announced chance is about something that already happened, so we can see who thinks deference is only forward-looking.

At least three nodes should be ones where the metaphysics and the deference behaviour genuinely come apart, and you should say which those are — that's the whole point of the exercise, showing my stat people that they have a metaphysics and my philosophy people that they have betting dispositions. Mark any combination of answers the tree permits but that nobody seems to occupy, and diagnose the empty cell: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves the tree can't actually separate instead of pretending it can — I suspect the analytic-definitionalist and the deflationist will look identical on every case I can think of.

No bibliography, no citations, no reading list. Names of positions and of people are fine, I just can't check references anyway. Keep each node short enough to read aloud at the table.
