I sit on the review panel of a public body's central risk unit, and before we rule on a proposal I need a clear explanation of a conceptual issue in the philosophy of probability. Our analysts presently record two things for each forward-looking judgement: a point probability, and an interval around it. The proposal would add a third field, explicitly billed as an implementation of Keynes's weight of argument, defined as the number of causally independent streams of evidence bearing on the judgement, scored from zero to five, with the rule that a score of three or more licenses a narrow interval and that a lower score requires the interval to be widened by a fixed margin. The covering note invokes the Keynes–Ramsey dispute, claiming that Ramsey's insistence on numerical degrees of belief left no place for the quantity of evidence and that the new field restores it.

Please explain the following in prose, treating the proposal as a serious attempt rather than as a straw man, and saying plainly where it does connect with Keynes and where it does not.

First, what weight of argument is on Keynes's own account: how it relates to the probability relation itself, why he thinks it need not admit of numerical measure, and what his conditions for the numerical measurement of probability involve, including the part played by the principle of indifference and by the requirement that the alternatives be indivisible and of a uniform form. Make clear what it means for him that some probabilities are not merely unknown but incomparable.

Second, whether the proposal survives contact with that account. Take a concrete case: two urns, each containing a thousand balls; of the first we know that black and white are present in equal numbers, while of the second we know only that every ball is either black or white, with the proportions unknown. Say how Keynes's framework treats the probability of drawing a black ball from each urn, and how it treats the weight of the two arguments, and then draw out what that case shows about identifying weight with the width of a reported interval. Explain as well whether weight is a second-order probability, a degree of confidence in one's first-order probability, a count of independent evidence streams, or none of these, and what Keynes's criterion of relevance commits him to about which pieces of new evidence increase weight at all.

Third, and most important: Keynes holds that where two arguments yield the same probability, the one resting on the greater weight is to be preferred. Explain what work that preference is doing. If probability alone measures the rational degree of belief, where does the additional reason come from, and is Keynes entitled to it? I want the strongest case on each side and your own verdict on it, not a survey of positions.

Fourth, explain why translating Keynes's uncertainty into contemporary idioms — ambiguity aversion of the Ellsberg kind, non-additive probabilities, or a credal set of probability functions as in imprecise probability theory — is at least partly a misreading, and what in his account resists each translation. Say whether the state of confidence he later appeals to in discussing long-term expectation in The General Theory is best read as weight under another name or as something different again.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a formal letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. Something on the order of fifteen hundred words.
