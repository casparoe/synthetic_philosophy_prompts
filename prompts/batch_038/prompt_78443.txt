Background first. I'm 71, retired four years ago from a national lab where I spent most of my career breaking things on purpose — fracture and fatigue testing, mostly ceramics and some metals. Since I stopped going in I've been reading philosophy at night, badly but happily, and lately a lot of the AI-safety writing. In June I'm giving the convocation address to the engineering graduates at my old university. About 200 of them, plus parents, plus faculty. Fifteen minutes, so call it 1,500 to 1,700 words, spoken out loud by an old man with no podium skills.

The room: almost nobody has taken a philosophy course. Two of the graduates are starting jobs in AI safety research and will notice if I get the concepts wrong. My daughter is in the second row — she builds large ML systems at one of the big labs, loves her work, and will physically flinch at a strawman, so nothing I say should be something she has to defend against on Monday. The dean is there too; he just put a required ethics-of-AI module into the curriculum, which I think is well meant and shallow, and I'd rather not insult him from the stage.

Here's what I want to talk about, and I want it to be the spine rather than the garnish: the difference between a guarentee that holds on average and one that holds in the worst case, and what each of them costs. I lived this argument. In my field you can design so that the structure is very unlikely to fail inside its service life, carry a fat scatter factor, and test your way to confidence in the mean. Or you can assume the crack is already in there, design so no single crack can take the aircraft apart, and then go look for it on a schedule. Those are different philosophies with different price tags, and the second is not automatically the virtuous one. It buys you something else.

The concrete image I want is the Weibull plot we kept on the wall of the lab. You bend nominally identical specimens and they scatter; the mean strength is a perfectly respectable number that tells you nothing whatsoever about the one specimen that's going to kill somebody, because in a brittle material the whole thing is governed by the largest flaw present and you never get to meet the average. Use something like that — plain, physical, no equations.

Then the AI half. There's a live argument about whether you should be satisfied with a system that behaves acceptably across the distribution of situations it will plausibly meet, or whether you should insist there is no reachable situation in which it does something irreversible. I want the address to hold three things at once without collapsing into any of them:

- worst-case thinking is not the same as pessimism, and average-case thinking is not the same as carelessness. Most good engineering is deliberately average-case, because worst-case design is so expensive the thing never gets built, or gets built too heavy to fly. Say that plainly; it's the part the safety writers usually skip.
- the cost structure is genuinely different here, because some failures aren't recoverable and there's no fleet to average over — one unit, one chance.
- and the disanalogy I can't paper over: in engineering somebody hands you the load envelope, and failure has a definition, the wing is on the ground or it isn't. Neither of those is given in this case. Nobody can say what the envelope is, the system may be able to move itself outside whatever envelope you drew, and whether something counts as a failure is a value judgement that reasonable people sitting in that auditorium disagree about. Then say what survives the disanalogy anyway — I think something does, and I think its the fail-safe half rather than the guarantee half. You stop promising no failure and start constraining what failure is permitted to look like.

My old lab chief used to say that a design which fails late is not the same as a design which fails well, and that you buy the first one with money and the second one only with thought. If you can get that idea in, credited to a man I worked for and not as a quotation from anywhere, I'd like it.

Now the part I care about most. I want the speech built to the standard it's describing, not just about it.

It should be fail-safe rather than safe-life. If the engineering-to-AI analogy doesn't land for a given listener — the parent who's never seen a scatter plot, say — the talk should still have given them something true and worth having. No single load-bearing claim where, if it snaps, the whole thing comes down. Then tell me afterwards, in five lines at most, where the one place is that the analogy really is carrying weight, and what the speech degrades to if an audience rejects it there.

And don't make it a sermon or a warning. My worst case on the day is that I sound like an old man telling 22-year-olds the future is dangerous and his discipline knew better. Build it so that even if that's how it lands for somebody, it was an interesting fifteen minutes and not a scold.

Constraints: no named companies, products, models, papers, researchers, and no dates for anything AI-related. No predictions about when anything happens. No future-is-in-your-hands material — if a sentence would work just as well at a business school graduation, cut it. It has to be speakable: short sentences, and mark a couple of places where I can stop and let something sit.

On facts. I'd like one real disaster used concretely, and I'd like you to use these and not add others, because I can't check what you invent and neither can the audience. The de Havilland Comet was the first commercial jet airliner, into service in 1952. Within about a year three were lost in mid-air; two were traced to structural failure from metal fatigue, which was not well understood at the time. The trouble concentrated at the corners of square cut-outs in the fuselage — the windows and an antenna opening. The type was withdrawn from service and test airframes were pressure-cycled until they failed. Afterwards the industry moved toward designs that tolerate damage and get inspected, rather than designs certified never to crack. If any of that smells wrong to you, drop the disaster and use my lab instead — don't repair it.
