I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and I am finishing a column on the recurring impulse to abolish spectators — from Rousseau's 1758 letter to d'Alembert urging Geneva to refuse a playhouse, through the participatory theater experiments of the last century, to today's community-arts funders who measure success by how few people sat down. As a sidebar I want to run a straight-faced parody: a real-looking Request for Proposals from a mid-sized American city's Office of Public Amusements, which has adopted Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert as its strategic plan and is now procuring a festival season in which nobody watches anybody.

Please write about 800 words in American English, in the flat procurement register of a municipal funding document — no winking, no stage directions, no asides to the reader. The humor must come from getting Rousseau's reasoning right rather than from generic bureaucracy gags: the theater reforms no morals and only flatters the ones we already have; pity lavished on an imaginary sufferer is pity withheld from a real one; the actor's trade is to sell his own person publicly for applause, so paid performers cannot hold civic honor; comedy renders the upright man ridiculous while the well-bred rake carries the room; a small working city cannot afford the idleness and expense of a standing playhouse; and the substitute is open-air celebration in which each citizen becomes a spectacle to the others, seeing himself in everyone around him. Let the document's own fine print expose the pressure points without ever stating them — somebody must design and lead the dances, which quietly reinstalls an author and an audience; the eligibility rules breed a black market in unpaid amateurs; and the FAQ must field the awkward question of why the office's founding consultant wrote a little opera that was performed before a king.

Use exactly these section headings, in this order:

1. Purpose of this funding round (about 100 words)
2. Scope of work: the crowd as its own spectacle (about 150 words)
3. Eligibility: who may not apply (about 150 words)
4. Deliverables and milestones (about 100 words)
5. Assessment criteria and weightings, with percentages (about 100 words)
6. Frequently asked questions (about 150 words)
7. Why each clause is Rousseau (about 150 words) — an unfunny coda for readers who have never opened the letter, naming the argument each section exaggerates.

Do not put any words in Rousseau's mouth in quotation marks; paraphrase him throughout. Do not make a living person the target of the satire, and do not append a bibliography or a list of sources.
