I'm in the last stretch of a masters in environmental ethics and I've been trying to get a handle on how pest control actually shakes out for the bugs themselves. My flatmate works in municipal vector control so we argue about this over dinner like twice a week and neither of us is budging. I've built a little scoring framework and I want you to actually use it rather than defaulting to your own intuitions about what's "green" or "harsh."

The framework says any pest-control intervention moves invertebrate welfare through exactly three channels. First, the acute channel: the harm and death it inflicts directly on targets and non-targets in and around the time of application, including drift and residue. Second, the abundance channel: the change it makes over the following seasons in how many insects get hatched into existence at all, where each of those lives has some expected lifetime welfare that might be positive or might be negative. Third, the trophic channel: the knock-on effects up and down the web, on the parasitoids and predators and pollinators and the plants that the herbivores were eating. Everything else — human disease burden, yield, cost, resistance evolution, public aesthetics, legal constraints — you should assume is being handled somewhere else by someone else and just ignore it.

Here's the list I want run through it:

- prophylactic neonicotinoid seed treatment across a whole corn/soy rotation, applied before any pest has actually shown up
- a targeted Bt spray against a caterpillar outbreak in suburban woodland
- truck-mounted permethrin/naled adulticide fogging for mosquito control across an entire city every summer
- a self-limiting gene drive that crashes an invasive Aedes population on a small island
- second-generation anticoagulant rodenticide used to remove invasive rats from that same island
- classical biological control: releasing a host-specific parasitoid wasp against the target pest
- pheromone mating disruption in an orchard, where basically nothing gets killed outright

For each one give me a single number from -10 (catastrophic for the invertebrates involved) to +10 (about the best they could hope for), a sentence or two of justification, and then a rough percentage split across the three channels saying where most of that number is coming from, adding up to 100. Then sort all seven into tiers. Then the part I actually care about: everything above assumes the average wild insect life is net positive, so more bugs hatching is a good thing. Tell me which of your scores would flip sign or change order if I assumed the opposite, and name the single entry where you think that assumption is doing the most damage. Last thing — flag any case where you think my three-channel decomposition is just plain misleading because the trophic channel swamps the other two and shouldnt be scored separately.
