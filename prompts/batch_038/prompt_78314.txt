I am a third-year student serving on the three-person editorial committee of a student-run philosophy journal, and the course I am taking this term on collective decision-making has made me aware that our weekly practice is philosophically fragile. For each submission we score it against three written criteria — the argument is sound, the contribution is original, and it falls within the journal's remit — and acceptance requires that all three be met. We currently take one vote per criterion and derive the decision from the pattern of those votes, but our faculty advisor has pointed out that we would sometimes reach the opposite result by voting once on accept or reject, and that we have no principled way of choosing between the two methods or of noticing when the choice is being made covertly: our chair sets the order in which criteria are put to a vote, and because we run the sequence aloud, editors occasionally revise an earlier criterion judgment once a later one has been discussed, so the same paper can come out differently depending on where it started. One of us has also begun explicitly lobbying for the criterion-by-criterion route whenever he expects to lose the direct vote.

Please write a decision procedure our committee can run in under fifteen minutes per paper, by members with no training in social choice theory. It should give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a default aggregation rule together with the stated conditions under which it is overridden, the rule governing the order of criteria and the permissibility of revising an earlier criterion judgment, and clear markers wherever a step is a matter of judgment rather than procedure. It should also specify what we must record during the vote so that our published rejection notice can name the criterion on which the paper failed without misrepresenting any individual editor's position, including in the case where every one of us thinks the paper is acceptable on each criterion separately.

Then run the procedure on three submissions: one on which two of us find the argument sound but only one finds it original; one where the outcome turns on whether originality is assessed before or after scope, and where the author has already been told which order we use; and one where the procedure yields an accept that the committee, having seen the reasoning laid out, believes is a bad decision. For that last case, state plainly that this is what the rule delivers and say exactly what it does and does not license us to do about it. Finish with the kinds of disputes the procedure cannot settle at all.
