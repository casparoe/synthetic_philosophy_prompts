So I've settled into a fairly confident take on the replication crisis and I want it properly stress-tested before I build a grant proposal's framing section on top of it.

The take: the crisis was first and foremost a failure of the p-value as a unit of evidence, not of psychologists' incentives or their theorizing. My reasoning is that a p of .04 out of one small study licenses "something is going on here," and that's all a paper ever needed — whereas the corresponding Bayes factor, something like 1.3, forces the author to print exactly how little the data shifted belief. So on my story the field didn't so much misbehave as use a measuring stick that physically cannot register weak evidence, and had the discipline reported Bayes factors from the start, most of the classics would have looked visibly unsupported back in the mid-90s and nobody would have spent twenty years building on them. Preregistration helped later, but on this view it helped mainly because freezing the analysis plan is what makes a posterior number interpretable at all — it's a Bayesian reform wearing Frequentist clothes.

I also did my own crude version of this as a side project: reran the headline analyses of about 40 of the big published replication attempts both ways, and my recollection is that the frequentist and Bayesian verdicts lined up on something like 85% of them, with nearly all the disagreements sitting in the band where the original effect was tiny and the replication sample was small too.

What would count against this? Give me the arguments and evidence that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive each is — fatal versus merely awkward. For each one, say whether a commited defender of my view should concede it or can reasonably explain it away. I especially want to know: whether the multiplicity / forking-paths style problem is really a frequentist problem or whether it bites Bayesian analyses just as hard; whether a publication system that rewarded "significant" would simply have rewarded "BF > 10" instead and produced the same mess with different vocabulary; what the people who think the crisis was about vague theory and bad measurement make of the whole framing; and whether there are real cases where the Bayesian machinery made things worse, or where prior-sensitivity turned out to matter in practice rather than in principle. And does my own 85%-agreement observation support my thesis or undercut it? I'd rather hear the second answer if that's the truth.

Finally, be honest about whether this view has been qualified into unfalsifiability by now — and if it has, whether that's even a defect for a thesis that's partly about measurement standards rather than a causal claim about a discipline.

Don't bother with citations or a reading list; describe concrete examples if you know them and flag the ones you're unsure about, but the structure of the case against is what I'm after.
