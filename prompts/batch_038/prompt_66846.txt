A friend of mine — Ruth, who has spent eleven years as a palliative care nurse and who therefore has very little patience for my abstractions — and I argued for most of last night about the procreation asymmetry, and neither of us is satisfied with how it went. I would like to see the same argument conducted properly, by two people much better at it than we are. Please write a dialogue of roughly 1,300 to 1,600 words between two characters who hold opposed views on the asymmetry and who are sharing a late train home after a mutual friend's engagement party, where the conversation resumes somewhere around the second stop. One of them defends a refined version of the asymmetry that I will describe below; the other attacks it. Give them names, keep the scene-setting to a sentence or two, and let the philosophy do the rest.

The position under discussion should not be the textbook asymmetry, which both characters can take for granted in one sentence: that there are strong reasons against bringing into existence someone whose life would be miserable, and no comparably strong reasons against failing to bring into existence someone whose life would be happy, so that the first act is wrong and the second merely not required. The defender in your dialogue goes further, in a direction Ruth pushed on me and which I could not dislodge. Her view — call it the episodic veto view, which is our coinage, not a term of art — holds that the interesting asymmetry is not about the strength of reasons but about their kind. Reasons against creating a person whose life will contain a stretch of suffering that is not, while it is being endured, worth enduring are exclusionary: they take the option off the table regardless of what else is on it, including the aggregate happiness of other people and including the fact that the person's life, reckoned as a whole from start to finish, would come out well ahead of never having existed. Reasons to create a person with a happy life are merely contributory: they can be outweighed, and when nothing is created their absence is bad for no one. She claims this is what explains the data the plain asymmetry only asserts — why we feel relief rather than regret about the happy people we never had, and why the intuition survives cases where a different embryo could have been transferred instead. I pressed three objections, and your critic character should press all three, in something like this order. First, scale: a veto that is insensitive to everything except the presence of an uncompensated stretch of suffering seems to forbid bringing about a world containing ten billion wonderful lives plus one person with one such stretch, which is a verdict at least as repugnant as the ones the asymmetry was meant to avoid. Second, claimlessness: a veto sounds like something owed to someone, but in the case that matters there is no one for whom non-existence would be better, so either the veto is impersonal — in which case the second clause of the asymmetry needs an impersonal story too, and the whole thing collapses into an ordinary lexical priority view about suffering — or it is person-affecting in some extended sense that needs to be explained rather than named. Third, the threshold: uncompensated has to be cashed out somehow, and if the measure is worth-enduring-at-the-time then almost every real life contains such a stretch and procreation is generally impermissible, while if the measure is weaker then the veto is just a very steep scalar weight and the deontic vocabulary is decoration.

The dialogue should turn on a single case, which I want you to state clearly early on and then work hard. A screenable condition is such that children born with it undergo, between roughly ages nine and fourteen, pain that they would not at the time have accepted for anything; by their own later testimony they go on to lead full lives that they would not trade for a condition-free alternative, and on any whole-life reckoning those lives come out ahead of never having existed. A couple has two viable embryos, one affected and one not, and it is stipulated — please have one character note that the stipulation is doing work — that the affected child's life would be slightly better as a whole than the unaffected child's. Then, at the climax, drop the second embryo: the choice is the affected child or no child at all, and the defender must either hold that the veto still forbids it, or explain why vetoes are sensitive to what the alternatives happen to be. Both characters should be fully competent and neither should be permitted to coast: the defender may not simply restate the asymmetry as if that answered an objection, and the critic may not simply announce that the view is ad hoc and move on. Each turn should take up the specific case, distinction, or stipulation raised in the one before, and they should be allowed to demand definitions from each other, including of the word uncompensated. Do not let either character appeal to authority, cite the literature, or name philosophers — if one of them thinks a view is someone else's, have them say what the argument is instead. End without conversion, but not without a record: the last few turns should make it discernible which of my three objections actually drew blood and which the defender absorbed, and it is fine — indeed preferable — if the defender is allowed one genuine concession and the critic one moment where he realizes he has been arguing against a view she does not hold.
