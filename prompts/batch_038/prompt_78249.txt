You are, for this exercise, a moral philosopher with a second career as a regulatory draftsman: someone who has published both on the metaphysics of responsibility and on the prose conventions of administrative instruments. I am a fourth-year doctoral student working on the control principle — roughly, the idea that an agent may be morally assessed only insofar as what she is assessed for depends on factors within her control — and on the consequences of applying it consistently. My chapter argues that each further application strips away another slice of the assessable agent until the residue is too thin to bear any weight at all, and I would like a piece of writing that lets people feel that conclusion rather than be told it. It is for a department tradition: the last work-in-progress session of the term opens with something short and funny that is nonetheless doing real philosophy, and the room is full of people who work on moral luck, so they will notice immediately if the philosophy is sloppy. (The idea came to me only because my partner is a paralegal who leaves sentencing manuals on the kitchen table, and I have developed an unhealthy affection for their register.) Please think carefully before drafting rather than reaching for the obvious jokes; the value of the whole exercise lies in the accuracy of the details.

Write an excerpt, roughly 900 to 1,100 words, from the manual of a fictional body — something on the order of a Commission on Culpability Adjustment — which has been given statutory authority to legislate the control principle into criminal sentencing and has carried out that mandate with complete good faith and administrative competence. Adopt the genre conventions of a sentencing guidelines manual: numbered sections and subsections, a definitions part, cross-references, a culpability table, worked examples contrasting Defendant A with Defendant B, application notes and commentary, and at least one dissenting statement from a commissioner of your own invention. The target of the satire is the project of filtering luck out of moral assessment, together with the managerial style that assumes any normative distinction can be rendered as a worksheet — not any real agency, and no living or dead person by name. The comedy should come from the commission getting things right, never from its being stupid; ideally a reader who knows the literature should be unsure, for a paragraph or two, whether a given provision is a reductio or simply correct. Please have the manual do the following, in whatever order you find most natural:

- Establish a base culpability score and then a schedule of downward adjustments for the standard varieties of luck: the agent's constitutive make-up (temperament, capacities, and inclinations she never chose), her circumstances (including the situations she happened to be tested by), and the way her actions turned out. Do not neglect causal luck; the commission's visible inability to draft a workable subsection on it, with successive abandoned versions preserved in the commentary, is worth more than a punchline.
- Include a provision requiring findings about what the defendant would have done had circumstances been otherwise — the counterpart who never got the chance to collaborate, to refuse, to fire the shot — with commentary that acknowledges the evidentiary difficulty of such findings and declines to let it block the adjustment.
- Handle the two drivers who took identical care, one of whom kills a child who darts into the road, and the successful and unsuccessful poisoner, and make the manual insist on the distinction between being culpable to a degree and being culpable for something: equally bad in degree, unequal in scope. Give it an application note reproving judges who keep converting scope back into degree at the sentencing hearing.
- Let the arithmetic work. When the adjustments are totaled correctly they should exhaust the base score, and the commission's remedy — a floor on residual culpability, a minimum non-zero finding — should be defended in the commentary by nothing stronger than a refusal to accept what the schedule produces. The self-undermining structure should be plain to a careful reader without ever being announced.
- Write the dissenting commissioner's statement as a serious position rather than a foil: roughly, that luck is not a contaminant to be distilled out of moral life but partly constitutive of what there is to assess; that the faultless driver's anguish is not an error awaiting correction; that a life project can be retroactively vindicated or ruined by how it turns out; and that the manual's agent, once every lucky contribution is netted off, is an extensionless point with nothing left to answer for.

Before the excerpt, give me a brief planning note of five to eight bullets identifying which actual move in the moral luck literature each section encodes and which feature of it you are exaggerating, so that I can check the satire is fair. Then the piece itself, with the table rendered in plain text or markdown. Two constraints. First, do not put any words in quotation marks attributed to a real philosopher — every quoted voice, case name, form number, and commissioner should be invented; assume your reader may be checking whether you are inventing or remembering. Second, no citations, bibliography, or suggestions for further reading; the manual's own definitions part should carry whatever philosophy a reader needs, since the audience includes people who have never read Williams or Nagel and should still be able to follow every provision. Keep the register deadpan throughout, with no winking at the reader and no footnote explaining the joke; if any section drifts toward the merely silly, revise it back toward the correct, so that the document reads as though it could have been issued in earnest.
