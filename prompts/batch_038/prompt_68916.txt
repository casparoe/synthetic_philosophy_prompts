I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a national magazine, and this season my editor is pairing each long feature with a live public event. My piece is about how moral norms actually die — specifically, the disagreement between the view that practices such as dueling, footbinding, and the Atlantic slave trade ended because people were eventually persuaded by moral argument, and the view (Appiah's honor thesis, and behind it Williams' insistence that ethical change is conversion rather than argument, worked through thick concepts rather than thin ones) that what did the real work was a change in what people found shameful: the practice stopped being something a person of standing could do long before most people stopped believing it was wrong. I want the event to let an audience of strangers feel that disagreement rather than hear me describe it.

The session: ninety minutes, roughly thirty adults who have never met, mixed education levels, a hired room with movable chairs, a microphone, a projector, and effectively no budget — paper, pens, printed cards, and a table of refreshments that I control. Nobody will have read the column. I want no thesis, name, or -ism spoken until the final five minutes.

Design the activity. Specifically:

1. Invent a fictitious status practice for the room, with whatever name you like, that meets these constraints: participants can be brought to endorse it within ten minutes using only social rewards; it has a genuine, articulable harm, so the argument against it is not a strawman; it carries its own thick evaluative vocabulary — a locution on the order of "someone of standing would not…" rather than "it is wrong to…" — and you should supply that term and the sentences in which it does its work; and it has no recognizable analogue to anything currently contested in politics, because I want people to notice the mechanism rather than their priors.

2. Write the argument against the practice: two hundred words at most, genuinely good, and framed entirely in the thin vocabulary of harm and fairness, so that its weakness against the thick concept is a structural fact and not a failure of drafting.

3. Give me the honor manipulation: the status currency, how it is administered publicly, who controls it, and the exact script I read aloud. It must not announce itself as a manipulation while it is happening.

4. Build in the two-stage structure I think the real cases have: the argument converts a small minority of early movers, and that minority is what makes the practice dishonorable for everyone else. I do not want an activity that makes the argument look impotent — only slower and differently shaped than the honor shift.

5. Instrument it. Before any reveal, capture both what participants actually do at a decision point where their choice is public and at a second point where it is secret, and a one-line written answer to the question of what changed their mind. I want the group's own self-reports to contradict its own behavior on the record, because that mismatch is the payload: participants in real moral revolutions describe themselves as having been persuaded by argument, which is one reason the historical evidence is so hard to read. Tell me how to collect and display this without humiliating anyone.

6. A minute-by-minute timeline, a materials list, and the debrief, mapping each moment of the activity onto the thick/thin distinction, conversion versus argument, and the honor mechanism.

7. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the participant who spots the manipulation early and announces it, the person who refuses to play, the status signal that backfires into resentment — and how to handle each in the room. Be equally frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself: the generational timescale, the fact that real honor communities carry material stakes a hired room cannot provide, and the honest objection that a facilitator with a hidden agenda and control of the refreshments has proved nothing at all about history. Give me the answer I should have ready for that last one.

No bibliography — I will source the column myself. What I need is something specific enough that I could hand it to a co-host and run it next month.
