**Question 3 (20 points).** (a) State what dialetheism claims, and explain how that claim differs from the claim that some logic is paraconsistent. (b) Set out the liar paradox and the dialetheic treatment of it, making explicit what semantic machinery the treatment requires. (c) Assess the cost of the treatment: what, if anything, does admitting true contradictions force you to give up?

Rubric: 6 points for (a), 8 for (b), 6 for (c).

**Student's answer.**

(a) Dialetheism is the thesis that some contradictions are true: for at least some sentences A, A is true and the negation of A is true as well. Priest describes such sentences as having the value "both." Paraconsistency is a claim of a different kind, not about truth but about consequence: a logic is paraconsistent when it fails to validate *ex contradictione quodlibet*, the inference from A and not-A to an arbitrary B. Because the two claims belong to different categories, they come apart, and they do in fact come apart. Relevance logicians reject explosion for reasons having to do with whether premises bear on conclusions at all, not because they suppose any contradiction to be true; likewise, someone who queries an inconsistent database by paraconsistent means is not committed to believing the conflicting records jointly. Paraconsistency is therefore best seen as the enabling condition of dialetheism rather than as a rival to it.

(b) Let L be the sentence "L is not true." By the T-schema, L is true if and only if L is not true. If L is true, then by the biconditional L is not true; if L is not true, then by the biconditional L is true. Classical logic leaves two exits, neither attractive: deny that the T-schema applies to L, which makes truth no longer transparent, or accept the derivation and, with explosion available, conclude that everything is true. The dialetheist accepts the conclusion but refuses the collapse: L is true and L is not true. The machinery required is a paraconsistent semantics, standardly Priest's LP, which assigns each sentence one of three values — true, false, both — designates true and both as the values preserved by valid inference, and gives the liar sentence the value "both." Triviality is blocked because premises of the form A, not-A no longer license arbitrary conclusions. The same latitude applies to Kripke's fixed-point semantics, which is best read as a more cautious dialetheic proposal: it too abandons the assumption that every sentence is either true or false, and it leaves the liar sentence with a nonclassical semantic status rather than forcing a verdict.

(c) The cost is routinely overstated. LP retains everything classical logic retains at the level of formulas: every classical tautology is still a tautology in LP, and no theorem is lost. Adopting LP is thus a conservative extension of classical logic in the literal sense, and the only casualty is the law of non-contradiction, read as the claim that no contradiction is true. Rules such as modus ponens and disjunctive syllogism survive, since both preserve truth for the material conditional and for disjunction. Nor does the dialetheist owe an argument that contradictions are widespread: the view is compatible with holding that gluts arise only in special semantic cases, so ordinary and scientific reasoning proceed unchanged. Trivialism, the thesis that every sentence is true, is a distinct claim, and nothing in dialetheism entails it.
