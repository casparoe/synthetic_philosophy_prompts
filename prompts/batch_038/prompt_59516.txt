You are an expert in axiomatic bargaining theory who also has substantial experience designing classroom simulations that generate usable data rather than noise. Please think carefully and work through the design constraints before producing any handouts: I want to see your reasoning about how the payoff numbers have to be chosen for the activity to work, not just the finished materials.

**The setting.** I teach an upper-division course on game theory and its philosophical foundations: 24 students, 12 pairs, one 75-minute session. They have met Nash equilibrium and have heard the phrase "Nash bargaining solution," but they have not seen the axioms, and they have certainly never heard of Kalai and Smorodinsky's rival. The session's job is to make them feel the dispute between independence of irrelevant alternatives and individual monotonicity as a live question about fairness — before either axiom, or either solution concept, is named. Nothing participant-facing may contain the words "Nash bargaining solution," "Kalai–Smorodinsky," "irrelevant alternative," or "monotonicity."

**The core mechanic I have in mind.** Each pair negotiates the same joint project across three rounds, with roles fixed as Player A and Player B for the whole session. Each round presents a menu of joint options with point payoffs to A and B; pairs have a fixed few minutes to agree on one option (or on a fractional mix of options, if you decide to allow that — see below); if they fail, both receive the round's disagreement payoff. Points accumulate on a public leaderboard, with a small identical participation credit for everyone, so no real money is involved but the stakes are felt.

What matters is how the three rounds differ:

- **Round 1** establishes a baseline feasible set in which at least four candidate answers are all genuinely distinct and all computable: the point maximizing the product of gains over the disagreement point; the point on the frontier where each player's gain is the same fraction of that player's maximum achievable gain; the equal-gain point; and the point maximizing the sum. Where pairs actually land relative to these four is the data of the session.
- **Round 2** leaves the Pareto frontier exactly where it was but adds one new option that raises A's maximum achievable payoff substantially while being unattractive to both — an option no pair would ever jointly select. It must be built into the fiction so that it looks like real news rather than like a puzzle piece ("A has acquired something" rather than "a new coordinate has been appended"). This is the round where A's intuition and B's intuition come apart, and I want the handouts to give each side a sentence of cover-story support for their reaction.
- **Round 3** re-expresses one player's payoffs in different units — the same underlying options, but A's numbers multiplied and shifted so that nothing that should matter has changed. Pairs who have been anchoring on a fifty-fifty split of the total will now split it very differently, and I want them to notice that they have just let a change of currency move their idea of a fair deal.

Additionally, I want a **within-class control**: six pairs receive Round 2 with the extra option printed on their handout and six receive the identical game without it. Build this split into the materials and into the protocol, and make sure the two versions are indistinguishable to the students at the time.
