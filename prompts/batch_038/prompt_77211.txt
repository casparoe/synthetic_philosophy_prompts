I chair a standing internal review panel at a company that trains and deploys reinforcement-learning agents at scale: non-player characters in a live-service game that persist across sessions and accumulate interaction histories, warehouse controllers trained in simulation and shipped to fleets, and a research pipeline that runs several million short training episodes a week for benchmark sweeps. The panel exists because our board asked for one after a director read about digital sentience, and it is currently split between members who think the whole exercise is cargo cult and members who want the benchmark sweep suspended pending review. We get about five submissions a month. Four people sit on it: two ML engineers, a product manager, and a contracted ethicist who reads philosophy of mind but is not a specialist. We have forty-five minutes per submission, we are allowed one round of written clarifying questions to the submitting team, we cannot commission new experiments, and the output has to be a one-page determination that the head of engineering signs and that would survive a journalist asking why we treat one system differently from another. What I need from you is the decision procedure itself: a reusable instrument we could hand to a panelist who has never encountered the literature on valence and run on a submission in forty-five minutes, working only from the fields on our form — architecture summary, reward specification, whether a learned reward model is involved, episode length and number of parallel environments, whether the policy persists between sessions and carries memory, whether the agent has any self-model or self-monitoring, what happens at episode termination, and whether the agent can produce language about its own states.

The procedure should not be a list of considerations; it should be an ordered set of tests with explicit criteria. For each test, tell me what feature of a submitted architecture would satisfy it, what would fail it, what a sufficient written answer from the submitting team looks like as opposed to a hand-wave, and roughly how likely the test is to produce a false positive (flagging something inert) versus a false negative (clearing something that matters). It should terminate in one of four outcomes — no welfare relevance, monitor and re-review at the next architecture change, mitigate specific features before further deployment, or escalate to a named human because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact — and it should say which questions are escalations by design rather than by our ignorance. I want it to take seriously the central substantive issue, which is whether a reward signal in a trained policy can be a bearer of valence at all, or whether it is a bare optimization target with the hedonic role stripped out: reward as a learning signal, reward as something the system represents as good-for-it, reward prediction error as distinct from reward itself, dense shaping versus sparse terminal signals, aversive termination penalties, and whether the presence of a persistent, integrated state that outlives the triggering event (something mood-like rather than a transient scalar) changes the analysis. It should also tell me how to handle the features that make our cases differ from each other: persistence and memory, the number of parallel instances, whether harm occurs only during training or continues in deployment, and whether a language-capable agent's own reports about its states should be given any evidential weight. Please tell me which tests are load-bearing and which are decorative, where reasonable people who know this material would order the tests differently and what would change if they did, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Do not give me a reading list or citations — nobody on this panel is going to do a literature review — but do name the positions you are relying on where naming them changes what the test asks for.

Then run the procedure on four submissions and show the completed determination for each: the persistent game NPCs with a learned reward model and cross-session memory; a warehouse controller with dense shaping trained for two hundred thousand episodes in simulation and then frozen; the weekly benchmark sweep of several million short episodes, each ending in a large negative terminal signal for collisions and each instance discarded at episode end; and a language-capable assistant fine-tuned partly with reinforcement learning from human feedback, which our product team is nervous about and which I suspect the procedure should treat quite differently from the others. At least one of these four should come out in a way I will not like — I expect the sweep, which everyone here assumes is trivially irrelevant, is the one that generates the awkward result — and I would rather see the procedure produce it cleanly than see it quietly tuned to avoid it. Finally, and this matters more than the format: please separate, explicitly and throughout, what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested commitment as though it were the consensus view. Where a test only works if you accept a particular position — functionalism about phenomenal states, the claim that affect is essentially the tracking of bodily or systemic condition, the relevance of personal identity and persistence to moral status, or the assumption that scale multiplies rather than merely replicates what is happening in one instance — say so at the point where the test appears, and say what the procedure should recommend under the rival assumption. If any part of my framing above is simply mistaken, correct it rather than building on it; I am particularly unsure whether reward prediction error is the right place to look, and I would rather be told that the distinction I am asking the panel to track is not doing the work I think it is.
