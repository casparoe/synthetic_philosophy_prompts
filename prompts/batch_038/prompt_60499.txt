Our philosophy of mind reading group has been marooned on the dreams material for about three weeks now and I've finally found a way to make the argument concrete, but I want a second opinion before tomorrow. We read Malcolm's Dreaming and Skepticism and Dennett's Are Dreams Experiences?, and the person who's informally running the group — I'll call her Wren — has these extremely settled verdicts that don't quite line up with either paper, or maybe they do and I just can't see how. Her track record, pulled from every case we've thrown at her so far:

1. You wake up and describe a vivid, twenty-minute, full-colour dream: that was not an experience. Nobody has experiences in their sleep, full stop, and she doesn't soften it for nightmares or for the ones where you taste the food.
2. A night with zero recall at all: she objects hard to calling it eight hours of unconsciousness. You weren't unconscious, you were asleep, and she thinks that's a difference that actually does work.
3. You wake with a free-floating sense of dread and no content: she says there's no remainder. There's no fact about what was chasing you, nothing filed away that you merely failed to retrieve.
4. Her flatmate goes "wasn't there a dog in it? there's always a dog," and Wren immediately finds the dog obvious and adds a whole scene involving the dog — she counts that as part of the dream. Dreams don't come sealed and cut to length for her.
5. She thinks "what was it like to dream that?" is only ever answerable as "what did it seem like to you when you woke up," and that's not a lamentable limitation, it's the entire content of the question.
6. Woken at 3am, reports a dream in detail, by morning sincerely recalls nothing: she says there was a dream at 3am.
7. Her justification when pressed is roughly that an experience leaving no trace anywhere, not even in principle, just isn't an experience — there has to be something to show for it, sometime.

Last night another member brought in the case we're supposed to discuss tomorrow, and Wren hasn't seen it yet. It's a sleep-lab volunteer who talks in their sleep. On one recording, during REM and with no arousals, the subject narrates a whole episode out loud and in order — names, a plot, a line of dialogue — unmistakably asleep throughout. In the morning they sincerely report no dream at all, and when the researcher plays the tape back they don't recognise it, say it doesn't feel like theirs, and can't add a single detail or correct anything. So the content was produced aloud, in real time, during sleep, and there's no waking impression of it whatsoever — not even a manufactured one triggered by the playback.

So: what's Wren going to say — was there a dream, and was there an experience? I'd like you to lay out at least three different rules that are each consistent with all seven of her earlier verdicts, show where they diverge on the tape-narrator case, and then actually commit to a prediction rather than hedging (she's the type who gives you one clean answer and defends it). Say which rule you'd bet on and what in the list tips you toward it. Then tell me the single extra case we should put to her next week that would separate the surviving rules, and what in her answer would count as a tell. Finally — are any of the seven verdicts already in tension with each other, and if so, which one is she most likely to be pushed into giving up?
