I direct the integrity function at a humanitarian organization with field offices in eleven countries, and I am rewriting our staff standard on gifts, favors, and payments to public officials. The current text is a bright-line rule — nothing above a nominal value, no payment to any official for any reason, declare every kinship connection — and it is failing: staff comply on paper, work around it informally, and I cannot say which of the individual rulings I have signed off on over four years I would defend if they were published side by side. Before I redraft anything, I want to know what my verdicts actually commit me to. Here they are, with the operative detail in each.

**Gifts and hospitality**

1. A box of pastries left for an underpaid public-hospital nurse by a grateful family after her shift, with no request attached and no expectation of another visit. Not corrupt; refusing it would have been a small cruelty.
2. An envelope of cash handed to a surgeon by the same family before the operation, to move the patient up the elective list. Corrupt, and I would treat the staff member who paid it as complicit.
3. A week of hunting hospitality extended by a timber company to a provincial forestry official, entered in the official's public gift register, after which the official votes against the company's permit application. Not corrupt — though I notice that the disclosure seems to be doing all of the work here, and I cannot articulate what work it is doing.
4. A protocol officer accepts a ceremonial dagger from a foreign ministry on behalf of a visiting minister and deposits it in the state collection. Not corrupt. The minister pockets the identical dagger from the identical giver. A serious breach. Nothing about the object, the giver, or the recipient's state of mind has changed between the two.

**Kin and patronage**

5. A village headman awards a borehole contract to his brother-in-law's firm at the prevailing rate; the well functions and cost the village nothing more than the alternatives. Not corrupt, though I would require the relationship to be declared.
6. The same headman awards the contract to an unqualified cousin at twice the prevailing rate. Corrupt.
7. A newly elected mayor fills a deputy-mayor post with the campaign manager who ran the winning operation. Not corrupt; that is what elections are for.
8. The same mayor creates a sanitation clerk post and fills it, bypassing the qualifying examination list, with that campaign manager's nephew. Corrupt.

**Payments under pressure**

9. Forty dollars at a checkpoint to release a truck of insulin that will otherwise spoil in the heat. I would instruct staff to pay it and log it, and I do not think it is corrupt.
10. Forty dollars at the same checkpoint, to the same guard, to release a truck of cigarettes. Corrupt, and the driver should refuse.

**Cases with no office and no money**

11. A journal peer reviewer who quietly accelerates and softens her report on a former classmate's manuscript. I want to call this corrupt, though there is no public money, no public role, and no rule of law involved.
12. An established novelist enters her own manuscript in a prize restricted to unpublished debut authors, under a pseudonym, and wins — arguably deservedly. I want to call this corrupt too, even granting that hers was the best entry.
13. A voter who sells her ballot for fifty dollars. Corrupt, I think. But a kidney donor who sells her kidney for five thousand dollars is not corrupt — that transaction is exploitative, which I take to be a different complaint about a different thing.
14. A state that sells residency to foreign investors at a published price, with no discretion exercised by any official at all. Not corrupt, though I find it harder to say why this differs from case 13 than I expected to.

What I want from you:

**(a)** Is there a single principle, or a small ordered set of them, that delivers these verdicts? State each candidate precisely enough that a field officer could apply it, and identify which clause of it is motivated by which case — I suspect several of my verdicts are each doing independent work, and I want to know how much machinery the set requires.

**(b)** Tell me which pairs cannot both survive. My own suspicion falls on 5 against 8, on 9 against 10, and on 13 against 14, but I may be wrong about where the pressure is. For each genuinely irreconcilable pair, say which verdict the better principle forces me to abandon and what the abandoned verdict was tracking that the principle loses.

**(c)** Where a simpler principle would overturn one of my verdicts and a more complex one would save it, tell me the price of the complexity in operational terms. A standard that requires the person applying it to weigh four considerations under time pressure, in a second language, with an armed official waiting, is not a standard we can issue; I need to know whether the account that fits my intuitions is one a human being can actually carry.

**(d)** Apply your principle to cases I have not listed: a regulator who is scrupulous in office, never meets the firms she oversees, honors a two-year cooling-off period in form, and takes a senior job at one of them under arrangements made informally before she left; a minister whose adult son is hired at market salary by a firm with business before the ministry, without the minister's knowledge or involvement; an aid agency paying a local intermediary a commission per permit obtained, where the intermediary's own costs are opaque to us; and two agency heads who each approve the other's expense claims and exchange a bottle of whiskey every Christmas.

**(e)** Give me the diagnostic boundary between corruption and the wrongs it is constantly confused with in our case files — conflict of interest, undue influence, regulatory capture, cronyism, exploitation, and simple breach of trust. I want a question to ask in each instance that discriminates, not a list of synonyms.

**(f)** Assess the parochialism of the result. My verdicts appear to presuppose that an officeholder can be separated from his person, his kin, and his obligations of hospitality, and several of the settings we work in treat that separation as unintelligible, where refusing a gift severs the only relationship through which any public business gets done at all. Say whether your principle yields different verdicts in those settings without becoming relativist — that is, whether there is an invariant underneath, or whether what I have is one historically specific settlement wearing the clothes of a definition.

**(g)** Finally, distill whatever survives into the shortest test I could print on a card. Then tell me where that test will generate false positives, condemning locally obligatory conduct as corrupt, and where it will generate false negatives, permitting genuine predation because it does not match the shape of the card. I would rather know both failure modes now than discover them in an audit.
