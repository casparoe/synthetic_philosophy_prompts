Second-year here, about to write a seminar paper on Aristotle's account of money (Ethics V.5, Politics I), and I want to check whether his story is actually the one I believe before I spend six weeks defending it. My gut verdicts on what counts as money:

- cigarettes in a prison yard: money, even though everyone would rather smoke them.
- a $25 card for one specific shop: not money. The same card once half a small town starts settling debts in it: money.
- bitcoin: money-ish. I dont want to say flatly no, but it fails whatever test the gift card passes.
- company scrip good only at the mine's own store, where the mine is the only employer: money — and unjust money, where the injustice feels internal to what the thing is rather than tacked on.
- a time-bank hour: money. Except I also think an hour of a surgeon isn't worth an hour of a cleaner, and I think Aristotle's right that exchange has to be proportionate to the worth of the producers or the community comes apart. Those two can't both survive, can they?
- wergild, the fixed payment that closes out a feud: money, though it never circulates and never buys anything at all.
- interest: with him about payday loans, against him about a thirty-year mortgage, and I can't say what the difference is.

Is there one account — his, or his with repairs — that delivers all of these? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, tell me what it makes of two I haven't considered (a central-bank digital currency that expires if unspent, and a pure gift economy), and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I suspect the time bank and the wergild, but I'd rather be told.
