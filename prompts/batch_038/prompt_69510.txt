I am writing a novel whose plot turns on a counterfactual: my Hitler dies in the Munich putsch of November 1923, and by the mid-1930s a regime recognisably like the Third Reich has nevertheless arrived, assembled by other hands out of the same materials. In committing to that premise I have committed to a position — that the catastrophe was determined far more by structure than by a man: the peace settlement, hyperinflation and then the Depression, the sympathies of the army and the judiciary, a fragmented party system that made parliamentary majorities impossible, and a racial antisemitism already generations old and available to anyone who wished to pick it up. Tell me what would count against that position.

I want the considerations ordered by how decisive they would be rather than listed: which facts about the period cut hardest against it, and which arguments about how counterfactuals of this kind are to be judged do the same work at a remove. Say where the position's fate is hostage to a general claim — that individuals rarely alter large historical outcomes — as opposed to the German case alone, since I can imagine losing the first without losing the second. Distinguish what a convinced structuralist would concede as genuinely damaging from what they would dismiss as begging the question against them.

Above all, I want an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified into something that cannot fail. If almost any plausible successor can be described as delivering a functional equivalent, is my premise asserting anything? Say whether that is fatal to it, or the appropriate way to hold a claim of that sort — and say what the structural account predicts that a Carlylean one does not. For my part, the thing I expect to be hardest to explain away is the specificity of the sequence: that so much of what followed appears to have happened in the order one man wanted it to.
