Next month I am presenting a working paper at a departmental workshop in experimental and applied microeconomics. The room will be roughly twenty people: mostly experimental and behavioral economists who run incentivized choice tasks for a living, three of whom have themselves run studies eliciting attitudes toward ambiguous prospects; two axiomatic decision theorists who think about these matters in terms of the sure-thing principle, comonotonic independence, and the family of relaxations of expected utility; and one asset-pricing researcher who estimates ambiguity premia in market data and is openly skeptical of anything inferred from undergraduates in a lab. Nobody in the room works on philosophy of probability.

My thesis is that the classic single-urn Ellsberg task does not measure a stable attitude toward ambiguity at all. It measures a second-order belief about who or what generated the ambiguity. Subjects in that task are not merely facing an urn with an unknown composition; they are facing an urn that somebody filled, and a great deal of the standard pattern of betting against the unknown composition is, I claim, a suspicion that the composition was selected by an agent with discretion, possibly an adversarial one. My positive proposal is a design that holds the composition unknown in every treatment and varies only its source: a mechanical randomizer whose draw the subject can audit; a privately chosen composition set by an anonymous stranger with no stake in the subject's payoff; and a privately chosen composition set by an anonymous partner who earns more when the subject loses. I predict the familiar pattern is weak in the first, moderate in the second, and strong in the third, and that the parameter a maximin-style model would estimate should covary with treatment rather than being a property of the subject. I also argue, as a second move, that the standard defense of the received interpretation — that beliefs were elicited separately and subjects were still ambiguity averse — is not a clean test, because inserting a scored probability report changes what the urn means to the subject and thereby contaminates the choice data it was meant to explain.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. I want the eight or so objections this audience will actually press, ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper rather than by the order in which they will arrive, and identified by which part of the room raises it. Phrase each one the way that person would phrase it: the axiomatic theorists will not talk about what subjects suspect, they will talk about state spaces, act-wise comparisons, and whether any of my treatments actually deliver ambiguity in the technical sense; the experimentalists will talk about contamination, demand characteristics, incentive compatibility, between-subjects confounds, and power; the asset-pricing researcher will ask what my mechanism predicts outside the lab.

For each objection give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, a note on where that reply is softest, and a mark of whether it is an objection I should concede partly and up front, in my opening, before it costs me credibility. Then tell me separately: which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the hour, what the weakest point in my own argument is that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook, and the question I am praying nobody asks.

Two constraints on the sheet. Do not name studies, authors, datasets, or journals; where an objection depends on an empirical result, describe the design and the finding in general terms instead, since I would rather have a clean account of the argument than a reference I cannot check. And do not soften the objections into ones I can easily answer — the value of the sheet is entirely in how well it predicts what these particular people will say.
