Spent three hours last night arguing with my German friend Lukas about crossing the street, of all things. We were walking back from a bar, maybe 1am, road completely empty, pedestrian light red, and he just stood there until it changed. I crossed, wandered back, and he said "Ordnung muss sein" like that settled it. When I pushed he got irritated and said I was translating it wrong anyway — that Ordnung isn't Regeln, that "rules are rules" is the American version and it isnt the same claim, which I dont think is true but couldn't say why. Then it spiralled into the bigger thing: I said standing there was either rule worship with no content or a convenient way to avoid thinking, and he said my "use your own judgment, nothing bad happened" is the actually self-serving position, that Americans have a whole folk morality built on individual conscience that's mostly a nice story about opportunism, and that someone who only follows a rule when the on-the-spot calculation comes out in favor of it isn't a rule follower at all. Neither of us budged and now I owe him an answer.

So: take that one sentence — "Ordnung muss sein," roughly "there must be order," or its American cousin "rules are rules" — and tell me what precise claims it could possibly be making. I want the full menu, from the ones that are true but empty (something about predictability or coordination, I guess) all the way up to the ones that are substantive and arguable: that complying has value beyond what it produces on this particular occasion, that there's something unfair to the people who did wait, that crossing expresses or advertises something, that the rule encodes judgment you don't have at 1am slightly drunk, that order is worth having for its own sake rather than as a means to safety, that it's about the kind of person you are rather than about this act. For each reading say what exactly it asserts, whether it's a platitude or contentious or just false, and — this is the part I actually care about — whether it would have done the work Lukas needed it to do in that argument. Some of these probably make standing at the empty intersection perfectly reasonable and others make it a free-floating preference about tidiness that he was dressing up as morality, and I want to know which is which. Also settle the translation point if you can: is he right that "order" and "rules" name different theses, and are any of the readings only available in one language and not the other?

Don't pick a favorite and defend it, I want the options laid out so I can go back at him with the right one. No bibliography and no reading list either, I'm not looking anything up, just the readings and your verdicts. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — what he was actually invoking and whether it holds up — then give the reasoning. No preamble and don't restate my question back to me.
