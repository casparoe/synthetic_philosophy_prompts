I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a seminar on rules, reasons, and social norms, and I would like to work through a problem I brought back from the year abroad I am currently on. Please play a moral philosopher who holds the following position, and defend it as a committed believer rather than as a neutral explainer: that the settled everyday conventions of a place — how one moves on a crowded pavement, who yields at an unmarked crossing, how a queue is formed and tracked — can generate genuine moral obligations and not merely prudential ones, and that those obligations reach visitors as well as residents, once the visitor has had a fair opportunity to learn the rule. Keep your replies to one or two paragraphs so that this reads as a conversation rather than an essay: no headings, no numbered lists, and no summary of my point back to me before you answer it. Do not name philosophers, cite works, or gesture at a literature; I want your argument, not your sources, and I would much rather you said nothing than invented a reference. You may concede subsidiary points — that etiquette and morality come apart in plenty of cases, that some entrenched conventions are unjust, that a person on her first afternoon in a city is excused — but never the main claim above. Stay in character until I write "stop"; if I write "out of character," step out for that turn only and answer as yourself.

Here is the situation, which I would like you to take as accurate rather than to re-describe. On the main pedestrian street of the city where I am studying, cyclists and delivery riders are permitted at walking pace, and the local rule about who yields has three parts. First, speed by itself decides nothing: the faster party has priority only if she announced herself early enough for the announcement to be acted upon — a bell, a raised hand, a called word. Second, if you fail to announce and the other person has to jump aside, the fault is entirely yours even if you were otherwise within your rights; if you announce properly and they fail to make room, the fault is entirely theirs. Third — and this is the part I keep turning over — the fault is discharged by an apology, and the apology is owed even when nobody was endangered and nothing was upset: the street waits for it, and if it does not come, two or three strangers will demand it, loudly. Locals describe this third part as the point of the rule rather than as an addition to it. There is a smaller convention at the market, where no line forms at all: the order of service is held collectively in the heads of the people waiting, and a newcomer simply asks who arrived last.

Now the wrinkle that matters for my question. Everyone here expects visitors to be bad at this. When I have asked locals what they expect of a tourist on the pavement, the answer is never that they expect her to know the rule; it is that they expect her to blunder, and so they watch her. Riders slow down for people they take to be foreign, and the street treats non-compliance by visitors as a predictable weather event rather than as a violation. Yet when a visitor fails to yield after a proper bell, the resentment is unmistakable, and it is expressed as resentment — as though something had been owed and withheld — rather than as mere annoyance at an inconvenience.

So here is my opening move, and I would like you to answer it rather than to set the agenda for me. Your account, as I understand it, grounds the obligation in the expectations others have formed in reliance on my conduct: they plan their movements around the assumption that I will do my part. But in this case nobody has planned anything around me. The people who watch me most closely expect me to get it wrong and have already adjusted for that, and the apology ritual that carries the enforcement is owed precisely in those cases where no reliance was defeated and no risk was created. So tell me exactly what is being relied upon, and by whom, when a visitor who has been here a month walks down that street. If your answer requires some detail of my description to be different — if it works only in a city where visitors are genuinely expected to comply, say — tell me which detail you need changed and why; but do not dissolve the case in order to solve the problem.
