I'm taking a tiny elective this term called something like "Mortality and Meaning" — six of us around a table, and the prof keeps insisting we're not doing grief, we're doing metaphysics. Last week was Epicurus on death, plus Nagel's paper pushing back. I think I got the shape of it: Epicurus's idea is that the bad has to land somewhere, and death never lands, since when you're alive it hasn't arrived and when it has arrived there's nobody left for it to arrive at. Nagel says the harm is a loss, so you compare the life you actually had with the longer one you'd have had, and the comparison doesn't need a moment to occur at — same trick that lets a betrayal you never find out about still be a harm to you. Fine, I follow both moves in isolation.

The problem is my friend Priya, who after class built what she insists is a valid argument running *from* the Epicurean premise all the way to a conclusion neither of us can swallow, and she's using it to say the deprivation view is a dodge. Here's her chain:

(1) Something is bad for you only if there is some time at which, in virtue of it, you are worse off than you otherwise would have been — harms have to have an adress.
(2) At every time you exist and are alive, you are not dead, and your condition up to that time is exactly what it would be in a life that kept going; so none of those times is a time at which you're worse off in virtue of dying.
(3) At every time after your death, there is no you, so there's no subject to be worse off then either.
(4) So by (1), death is never bad for the person who dies.
(5) And (2)–(3) don't care how long the life was: dying at 30 leaves no time at which you're worse off any more than dying at 80 does, because the fifty missing years are years with nobody in them. So no death is worse for its subject than any other, and living longer is never better for you than living shorter.
(6) Now run the same test backwards, on coming into existence instead of leaving it. If someone's life is mostly misery, then there are times — the miserable ones — at which they exist and are badly off, and they are badly off at those times in virtue of having been born, since had they never been born no suffering would have been happening anywhere. So (1) is satisfied here: being born can be bad for you, at a time, with an adress.
(7) Therefore, from (4)–(6): parents who conceive a child doomed to a wretched life harm that child, and the person who later kills them at 30 — or the driver who runs them over — harms them not at all. Conception can be a harm; murder never is.

Priya says (7) is just the price of (1) and I should feel the burn of it, and that the deprivation view is caught in a fork: either it keeps (1) and eats (7), or it drops (1), in which case it owes us a story about when and where *any* harm lands — and she reckons dropping (1) also commits you to your reputation being ruined at your funeral harming you, and to never having been born being a misfortune for the countless people who never existed, which she says she'll happily rub in my face. I sort of want to keep (1), because without it "harm" starts floating free of lives entirely, but I definately can't accept (7). So: which step do you reject, why did that step look obviously true when I first read it, and what's the bill for each of the exits — including the one where you just bite the bullet and accept (7)? If (6) is the weak link, I want to know exactly how it differs from (2), because they look to me like the same move pointed at opposite ends of a life.
