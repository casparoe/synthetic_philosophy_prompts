I write novels, and my oldest friend Dana is a critic — she's the one reader whose verdict on whether a sad book actually earns its sadness I care about, she's brutal and she's never been wrong about my work, which is exactly the problem, because my third one goes to her next week and I'd rather predict the hit then stand in front of it.

Her record, as best I can reconstruct it from twenty-odd years of arguments:

- Never Let Me Go — thinks it's the best novel published in her lifetime, and her reason is specifically that nothing in it gets redeemed. She also says she understands the whole cloning-and-organs argument better for having read it, and that's her standing answer to people who ask why she pays for pain: certain things can't be learned any other way.
- The field where Tommy screams, in that same book — she calls it the most earned scene in contemporary fiction, because the prose refuses to tell you how to feel about it.
- Atonement — loves it, and what she loves is that the happy ending turns out to be something the narrator made up to comfort herself. A book that hands you consolation and then shows you the invoice is, she says, doing something no other form can do.
- The Shawshank Redemption — sobbed, had a fantastic time, calls it cheap and won't defend it. Her line is that it made her pay and then paid her back. She says its well made, that's the problem.
- The Road — quit twice, finished it, says the ending with the boy being taken in by that family is a bribe and that it retroactively spoiled the whole book for her.
- Manchester by the Sea — perfect, because Lee is allowed to not be okay. No lesson, no growth, no moral. It's the one she'd show an alien.
- Requiem for a Dream — only got through it because it's 102 minutes and she knew when it would stop; says if it had been a series she'd have bailed partway through. Misery porn.
- Titanic — says she had more fun at that than at anything else in the cinema that year and doesn't feel remotely bad about it.
- The hospice documentary I made her watch — calls it the most powerful thing she's seen in a decade, watched it once, will never watch it again, says rewatching real people die is tourism.
- She wouldn't touch anything sad for a year after her father died, then reread Never Let Me Go that winter and says it was the only thing that helped.
- She maintains she never actually feels sad about Anna Karenina because she doesn't believe Anna exists. I've pointed out that this doesn't survive contact with the hospice documentary, and she says fine, both things are true.

Now my book. It's a fictionalised account of a real accident from about twelve years ago — names changed, some events invented, one of the survivors read it and blessed it, so it isn't theft in the simple sense, but she'll know exactly what it's from, and two of the people it's built on are still alive in that town. It's 190 pages, so she'll know when it ends. It's narrated by a neighbour who isn't grieving and doesn't understand what she's watching. It teaches nothing: no argument, no new angle on loss, no social point, nobody in it grows. It refuses consolation — no meaning, no redemption, no wisdom, it just stops. The prose is, everyone keeps telling me, gorgeous; two early readers cried and then immediately said how beautiful it was. And the last page is a long description of light on a kitchen wall that those same readers called strangely comforting, which I honestly can't tell is a refusal or a payoff.

What's she going to say? I want the rules that actually fit every verdict above — not the one she'd state out loud, which would be the Never Let Me Go one — and what each of those rules predicts about my book. Then your single best guess at what she'll write, including whether "she'll love it" and "she'll recommend it" come apart here the way they do with Shawshank. Then one further case, another book or film sketched in a sentence or two, that would break the tie between whichever two rules are still level after mine — and tell me which of her existing verdicts she'd have to give up if she turns out to be running the rule you think she's running.

Five numbered points, a sentence or two apiece, no intro and no wrap-up. Be blunt, I'd rather know now.
