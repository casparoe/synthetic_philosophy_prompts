For six years I have run a Saturday-morning discussion group in the meeting room of our county library: between twelve and twenty adults, nearly all of them retired, none of them with any university training, and me least of all. Next month's session is the one I have been putting off. The subject is how to tell a claim about the past that deserves to be taken seriously from one that does not, and I intend to put three cases in front of the room — the drowned island described in one of Plato's dialogues, the argument familiar from a hundred paperbacks that the great stone monuments of several continents were raised with help from visitors from the sky, and Fomenko's re-dating of world history, which holds that the accepted chronology of antiquity is far too long because what we call the classical world is a duplicated and displaced version of the Middle Ages. One hundred minutes, a whiteboard, no handouts, and roughly how the hundred minutes should divide.

I want the plan for the session rather than the session itself. Which idea has to be in place before any of the examples can do work at all — my own guess is that they must first come to see that a claim about the past commits you to something about what you would expect to find and not to find, but I do not know whether that can be stated before an example or only drawn out of one, and I suspect that getting this backwards is what ruined the last time I attempted it.

Then which single case should carry the weight, and what that case must have and must lack. I have a fourth candidate that the other three do not resemble: a self-published book circulating in this valley arguing that the stone chamber and the low walls along the ridge above town were built by an ocean-crossing people long before Columbus, and that the historical society buried the survey that showed it. I can bring photographs, and the author may well attend. Should that be the carrier, because it is local and concrete and everyone in the room has walked past it, or should it be held back to the end as the test, because raising it early will turn the morning into a quarrel with a neighbour?

Then the misconceptions, in the order they will arrive, and the point in the sequence at which each must be met: that all history is interpretation anyway; that the established view has been wrong before and will be wrong again; that nobody can prove a negative; that the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence; that history is not a science, so none of these tests apply to it; and that the amateurs are at least asking the questions the professionals will not. Tell me which of these will destroy the session if it is left standing, which are harmless at this level and can be waved through, and — this is the part I most want — which one of them is a sound principle being misapplied, so that it ought to be conceded and sharpened rather than knocked down.

Then a closing check that shows whether the idea landed rather than whether the room has come round to my opinion: a case they have not seen, and one where the honest answer is not simply that the experts win. And finally, what I should refuse to attempt in a hundred minutes with this audience, stated to them out loud at the start.

One thing the plan must not depend on: contempt for the people who believe these things. Two of my regulars do, and they are not stupid. I was taken in by the sky-visitor books myself at nineteen and stayed taken in for the better part of a year, so I know precisely what the pull consists in, and any sequence that requires the room to laugh will fail with the two people I most want it to reach. Nor do I want a reading list; nothing I have ever handed out in that room has been read.
