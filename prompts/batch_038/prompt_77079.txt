Quick help prepping for a talk. Joint STS / philosophy-of-technology seminar, about fifteen people: mostly historians of technology who think any talk about what a designer "intended" is naive, one analytic social ontologist who is going to want to know what kind of property *political* is supposed to be, a critical-infrastructure geographer who works on municipal water and lead service lines, one die-hard Winner person who assigns the 1980 Daedalus article every single year, and a couple of city engineers who audit public works part time.

My thesis is deliberately annoying: artifacts don't have politics, maintenance regimes do. Sketch of the argument:

1. Define a regime as the standing arrangement of who has to show up, what breaks if they don't, who pays for it, and who gets blamed. Call it political when it allocates ongoing costs and ongoing veto power to specific people.
2. Design-time facts underdetermine that allocation. The same bridge, the same culvert, the same database schema can sit under two completely different regimes across a decade.
3. So politics can't be a property of the artifact. It's a property of the arrangement that holds the artifact in the state it's in.
4. The script/de-scription move (Akrich and company) is a special case that only works while the thing is new and still being negotiated in use. It can't handle the politics of decay, which is most of the politics of infrastructure. Deferred maintenance is where the exclusion actually lives: nobody designed anyone out, the exclusion is in who never gets the repair budget.

Three cases I'll walk through: Winner's low-clearance Moses bridges (I plan to lean hard on the fact that the historical story there has been picked apart, since it helps me), a mid-sized city's lead service line replacement program, and a widely-used open source package maintained by one unpaid person in Ohio.

I want a prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the seven or eight objections this particular room will actually raise — not the generic determinism-vs-constructivism ones I'd get from an intro class — in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each of those people would phrase them. For each: a ninety-second reply, a flag for where my reply is thinnest, and a note on which ones I should just concede up front to buy credibility. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And be blunt about any objection where the honest answer is that I've merely re-described Winner and swapped the label, because I suspect that's true of at least one of my three cases — tell me which one and how bad the damage is if the geographer pushes on it before I've gotten to the lead pipes.

One more thing: I keep saying regime's politics rather than the artifact's, and I know that sounds like I'm smuggling the artifact out of the question entirely. If the social ontologist opens with that, what do I say in ninety seconds that doesn't collapse into "institutions have politics, duh"?
