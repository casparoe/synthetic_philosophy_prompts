Before I read any more proposals about when a human being has died, I want the specification. Assume the account has to do double duty: it must be applicable by a clinician at the bedside, and it must license the removal of organs from the patient it declares dead. Write the desiderata any such account has to meet — what it must get right in practice, which distinctions it cannot collapse, which facts about the biology of dying it must not contradict — and for each, give the case or consideration behind it together with a mark for whether it is non-negotiable or only the preference of one camp.

Two points I most want pressed. First: can a single account both underwrite the dead-donor rule and accommodate donation after circulatory death, where circulation is stopped for a few minutes and could in principle be restored? If the requirements come apart, say precisely where. Second: how should the list be revised if we stipulate a patient on extracorporeal support whose cerebrum and cerebellum have undergone complete necrosis, but who has an implanted device that reliably drives brainstem reflexes, including spontaneous respiratory effort — which desiderata does that case engage, and does it decide anything?

Close by naming the single desideratum that eliminates the most existing accounts, and then by saying what would change your mind: which consideration, weighted differently, would reorder your list, and where your reasoning is most likely to break.
