You are a political philosopher specializing in theories of property and the justification of state coercion. Think carefully before answering, and open with a brief brainstorm of the distinct jobs the analogy below is being made to do.

A friend and I argued for three hours last night. He holds that taxation is theft, and the image he leans on throughout is the protection racket: the state demands money, on threat of force, for a service you never agreed to buy, cannot decline, cannot buy from anyone else, at a price it sets unilaterally. If the same demand were made on your doorstep by a man in a leather jacket, we would not hesitate to call it extortion. I think the comparison is a cheap trick, but I could not say exactly where it fails.

Please audit the analogy rather than adjudicate the slogan. I want (1) a table of correspondences — state/racketeer, tax/protection payment, citizen/shopkeeper, monopoly on force/monopoly on the block, plus exit and consent — with each row marked as holding, holding only loosely, or failing; (2) the disanalogies, divided into those that bear on his use of the analogy and those that are genuine but beside the point (I suspect "the state provides roads" belongs in the second group, since racketeers too sometimes keep the peace in their territory); (3) what the analogy presupposes and therefore cannot prove — above all, whether it already assumes that citizens' holdings are antecedently just and secured independently of the state, which is the very thing in dispute; and (4) whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and in what weakened form if it does.

Close by proposing a better analogy from each side — one a sympathetic non-libertarian could accept, one a libertarian who distrusts the mafia image could accept — and say what each of those can and cannot carry.
