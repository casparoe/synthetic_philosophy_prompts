Two members of my philosophy-of-language group have argued past eachother for a year: a strong two-dimensionalist (primary intensions are part of what's said, scenarios are epistemic possibilities, zombie-conceivability does real work) and a Kripkean one-dimensionist of the Soames stripe (contents are propositions, some of them necessary a posteriori, and a primary intension at best records how a speaker would apply a word, not what the word means). Draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration should end with.

Give me: a short shared glossary up front, and where a term like "scenario" or "intension" can't be shared, both translations side by side; numbered claims both can sign, including about Hesperus, about water, and about what the zombie argument is and isn't supposed to show; a disagreements table with each side's own acceptable wording of its position plus a verdict on whether the row is substantive or just two vocabularies; then the cruxes — I suspect one is whether a primary intension belongs to content or only to a speaker's dispositions to apply words, and another is whether two-dimensionalism explains away the necessary a posteriori or merely redescribes it. For each crux, a test both accept and each side's prediction, written down beforehand.

Use this case for the first crux; it's mine, neither of them has seen it. In 1904 a physicist coins "zalt" for the quantity conserved in collisions of his corpuscles, a theory that turns out to be false; the conserved quantity we now call momentum. What did "zalt" refer to back then, what does it refer to now, and in a scenario where the corpuscle theory is true but the conserved quantity is something different, does "zalt" pick out that other thing? End with one concession each side thinks the other has genuinely earned.
