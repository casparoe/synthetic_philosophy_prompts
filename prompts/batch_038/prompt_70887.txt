I have been reading the sequences and their comment threads for something like fifteen years, and the habits are hard to break: I would much rather discover where my own argument is soft than have a room discover it for me. I came to aesthetics only recently, by way of the question of whether a trusted friend's assurance that a film is beautiful can give me anything worth calling knowledge, and a local seminar series has now invited me to give a thirty-minute talk followed by forty minutes of discussion. What I need from you is a question-period prep sheet — not a defense of my view, and not a single recommended reading, since I have no time to acquire one and would rather not be handed a bibliography. What I need is the shape of the room's resistance.

The room is about fourteen people who meet on Thursday evenings. Five or six are analytic epistemologists who work on testimony and disagreement; they will hear everything I say as a claim about justification, reliability, or transmission, and they will want to know which. Two are aestheticians, one sympathetic to a strong first-hand acquaintance requirement and one who thinks aesthetic testimony is unremarkable and the whole problem manufactured. There is a literary theorist who is suspicious of my framing before I have finished my second slide, a couple of philosophy-of-mind graduate students, and two or three autodidact regulars from the meetup crowd who are the most likely to run hard at the line that what I am describing is not knowledge-talk at all. My thesis, briefly: I distinguish feature testimony — a critic's description of how a slow movement turns a repeated figure into a summary of everything that preceded it — from verdict testimony, the bare claim that the work is beautiful. I hold that the first is unproblematic and does all the useful work, and that the second, even when true and even when produced by an excellent track record, yields a belief that is true but inert: it can be revised in the light of further testimony but not in the light of the work itself. That, on my account, is why such a belief is not an aesthetic judgment, and why consulting critics is best understood as acquiring descriptions of features one must then go and see for oneself. The defect I am locating is not in the justification of the belief; it is in the belief's failing to be the kind of state that an aesthetic judgment is.

Give me the objections this audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they will arrive, each phrased the way the relevant subgroup would phrase it — the testimony epistemologists in transmission vocabulary, the hostile aesthetician in acquaintance vocabulary, the literary theorist at the level of the framing rather than the argument — with a two-minute reply to each and an explicit note on where that reply is soft. Rank them by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, and identify which one is load-bearing in the strict sense that losing it takes the thesis down with it. I already suspect several: that my feature/verdict split collapses, since a good review's verdict just is compressed feature description; that true-but-inert marks a difference in evidence base rather than in kind, because a belief formed on testimony is perfectly responsive to its own evidence; that the same charge of inertness would condemn deference to instruments, clinicians, and specialists everywhere, so I have described a general phenomenon and dressed it in aesthetic clothes; that verdict testimony is checkable, since I can simply watch the film next week, which makes the belief no more inert than any other; and that the whole position is non-cognitivism wearing an epistemologist's coat. Tell me whether those are the right five, which of them is the sharpest, and what I am still missing. Then tell me what I should concede in the opening five minutes so that I am not spending the question period defending it, what I must hold absolutely, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks — along with your judgment on whether I should pre-empt that question inside the talk or simply take the hit in the room.
