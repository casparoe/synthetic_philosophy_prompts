**Question:** "Aquinas is the medieval author who first supplied a principled justification for tyrannicide." Assess this claim, drawing on *De Regno* and on the *Summa*'s treatments of sedition and of killing.

**Student answer:**

Aquinas is usually credited with turning tyrannicide from a rhetorical topos into an actual argument, and the credit is deserved. He starts from the definition of the office: a king rules for the common good, a tyrant for his own private advantage, so tyranny is not merely bad government but a perversion of kingship itself. That is why he can write, in the question on sedition, that "a tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler" (II-II q.42 a.2 ad 3). Once the regime has ceased to be just, the ordinary prohibition on disturbing the peace no longer binds, and Aquinas attaches only a prudential caution about doing it at the wrong moment. The same reasoning runs through *De Regno*, which he wrote for the king of Cyprus: where no human remedy is left, one must have recourse to God, but where the excesses of the tyrant are altogether intolerable, the community itself must act.

To his credit Aquinas is not calling for lynchings. He warns that striking down a tyrant frequently produces something worse, since the successor may be harsher than the one removed and the act of removal can itself throw the city into civil strife. But that warning is about timing and cost, not about principle: the deed remains permitted, only inexpedient.

The killing itself is covered by the same doctrine that covers self-defence in II-II q.64 a.3, where Aquinas argues that one act may have two effects, the one intended and the other beside the intention. The man who strikes down the tyrant intends the deliverance of the city; the tyrant's death is beside the intention, and is permitted in proportion to the good sought. On this footing Aquinas can say, as he does in Book II of *De Regno*, that a people who conferred royal power on a ruler may take it back when he abuses it, since he holds it only conditionally. It helps that Aquinas repeatedly describes the tyrant in bestial terms, and one does not commit homicide against a beast. In all this he is developing what John of Salisbury had already said in the *Policraticus*, except that Salisbury's case is mostly exhortation and biblical example, whereas Aquinas supplies the underlying theory of office, common good, and authority. That is the sense in which he is the first to justify tyrannicide properly: not that he licenses it lightly, but that he gives the reasons that make it an argument rather than a slogan.
