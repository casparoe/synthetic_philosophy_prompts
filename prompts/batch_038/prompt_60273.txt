Since last spring I have been one of three partners running a twelve-acre vineyard in a river valley, and this September a decision that used to be routine has turned into an argument I cannot settle on my own. We pick by hand, and each year we book the same contract crew, a mobile crushing unit, and a refrigerated truck; all three require a firm date fourteen to sixteen days out, all three take non-refundable deposits, and all three are in demand from neighboring growers, so waiting until the outlook is clearer simply means losing them. Picking into heavy rain is close to the worst thing that can happen to the fruit, so the date matters enormously. I have therefore been reading the extended forecasts obsessively, and I have ended up with two advisers who tell me opposite things about what those forecasts actually mean.

The first is a friend who spent his career building simulations for an engineering firm. His position is that the atmosphere is only air and water obeying fixed rules, so the weather two weeks from now is already as settled as a fact can be, and that when a forecast assigns a thirty percent chance of rain on the day I must choose, that number describes the forecaster's ignorance rather than anything in the sky. He infers that the sensible use of money is to buy down the ignorance: a denser network of instruments, a finer computational grid, a subscription to the premium tier of a forecasting service that advertises twice the resolution of the standard model, since every increment of precision narrows the range of open possibilities, and in the limit the percentages would collapse into a yes or a no. The second adviser is a neighbor who flew cargo aircraft for twenty years and reads the technical discussions about forecasting for pleasure. He says my friend is missing something structural, and he tells the story of the meteorologist in the early sixties who, to save time, restarted a simulation partway through using numbers typed from a printout carrying three decimal places rather than the six the machine held internally, and obtained a completely different weather history. On his account the atmosphere magnifies differences far too small for any instrument to resolve, so a forecast only slightly wrong today is wildly wrong eleven days out, and beyond some horizon no computer and no sensor network can help; the thirty percent is then not merely a confession of ignorance but as close to a real limit as nature offers, and my money would do better buying flexibility — a crew willing to shift by a few days, an on-site backup for the crushing — than precision that cannot possibly pay off. I have tried checking the arithmetic myself and am not sure I have it right: if a small initial error multiplies by some fixed factor every few days, then gaining a few more days of useful horizon seems to require shrinking the starting error by an enormous multiple, so that tenfold improvements in measurement buy only a couple of extra days. If that is the correct way to think about it, it changes what I should spend money on; if it is a confusion on my part, I would rather be told so plainly. I would also like to know whether these two men are genuinely contradicting each other or talking past one another about different things that can both be true, because it strikes me that my friend is making a claim about how the world works while my neighbor is making a claim about what anyone situated inside it can come to know, and I cannot see how to hold both without giving something up.

Whatever the resolution, I still have to commit to a crew date, so please carry the analysis through to that choice: if the thirty percent is not a fact about the weather but only about the forecasters, am I being irrational to weigh it as though it were, and is there a more defensible way to decide under a number whose status I do not understand? Finally, please be candid about the state of the underlying questions rather than smoothing them over. I would like you to distinguish what is settled among people who work on this — settled enough that a specialist would not hesitate — from what remains genuinely disputed, including whether the horizon my neighbor describes is a hard limit or merely a very steep practical one, and whether it is legitimate to describe uncertainty in a long-range forecast as anything other than ignorance about a world that is itself fully settled. If a position you advance is a contested one, say so explicitly, and do not dress it up as the consensus view.
