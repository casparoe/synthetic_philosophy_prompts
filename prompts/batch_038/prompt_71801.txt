I sit on the science advisory panel for a state wildlife commission — six of us, mostly academics plus two agency biologists, and we meet by video once a month, usually to rubber-stamp stuff. This month is different and I've been volunteered to write the opinion that goes into the packet. Here's the case. A conservation group has petitioned to list the Ashfall springfish, a small fish that lives in exactly three spring-fed creeks on the east side of our basin, as endangered under our state statute. The statute protects "species and subspecies, and, for vertebrates, distinct populations," which sounds fine until you actually have to apply it. The petitioners' whole case rests on a mitochondrial DNA study showing the springfish lineage is deeply divergent from the widespread creek minnow downstream, plus two fixed differences in scale counts and a breeding run that starts about six weeks earlier. Agency staff recommend rejection: they point out that where the spring creeks meet the main river the fish hybridize freely and produce fertile offspring, that the genetic work is one marker at one locus, and that a newer survey turned up springfish-looking fish in two creeks that aren't part of the petition. Meanwhile the tribe that co-manages the upper basin has filed a letter saying the fish has had it's own name and its own run for as long as anyone can remember and that they are not interested in a taxonomy seminar.

The panel is split and the split is philosophical, though nobody has said it out loud. Two members think a species is whatever lineage is diagnosable, so the petitioners win. Two think interfertility and ongoing gene flow settle it, so staff wins. One thinks the only thing that matters is whether it plays a distinct ecological role, and one just wants to know how much the listing will cost the highway department. My own worry, which I want the memo to face rather than dodge, is precedent: there's a second petition already queued for a population of the same fish on the other side of the basin, and if we list every population that can be told apart with one mitochondrial marker, our list fills up, our budget doesn't, and the program loses what little credibility it has with the legislature. There's also the awkward detail that a grad student who staffs our panel thinks the divergence signal could be mitochondrial capture — an old hybridization event in which a downstream ancestor donated its mitochondria — which if true would mean the marker is recording a history of contact rather than of seperation. I'm obviously not asking you to solve the species problem, but I do want the memo to notice that the two camps aren't actually answering the same question, and to say what a panel is supposed to do when its enabling statute leans on a term that biology hasn't settled.

So please draft the opinion I'll circulate before the meeting on the 14th. Take a side — list the fish, reject the petition, or defer with specific conditions attached — give a concrete recommendation with a time frame, and state the rule the panel should apply to the next petition too, so the decision isn't just about this one fish. Then give the strongest honest case against your own recommendation, especially on precedent and cost, and explain why it doesn't carry the day. And please don't hand me headings or bullet points or bold anything, no tables either: just continuous prose the way I'd write an email, with your recommendation stated in the very first sentence, kept to something I can read aloud in the meeting, maybe 700 words.
