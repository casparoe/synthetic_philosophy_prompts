You are a philosopher who works on practical rationality, self-control, and personal identity over time — someone equally at home with the literature on hyperbolic discounting and with the question of what makes the person now the same agent as the person who made a promise. Please think carefully rather than quickly, and treat the brainstorming stage as genuinely open: do not settle on a view before you have surveyed the alternatives.

Some background, because it explains why I am asking rather than reading about this. Nine months ago, six friends and I formed a pledge circle. Each month a member announces a goal, specifies a behavior, and puts money into an escrow the rest of us administer; if the pledge is broken, the money goes to a charity the signer positively dislikes. (We thought that was clever at the time.) The circle votes on whether to enforce when a member says they want out. Until last month the votes were trivial. Then I cast the deciding vote on a case I have not stopped thinking about since, and it made me realize I have been voting by feel — and that my feels may not add up to anything.

Here are my verdicts, drawn from actual votes and from cases we argued hypothetically.

1. A member pledged in March to write five hundred words a day for three months, with a forfeiture per missed day. In month two his mother had a stroke and he moved in with her for six weeks. Verdict: released, money returned. The pledge never contemplated this.
2. Same shape, except that the interruption is a better-paying job that consumed his evenings. Verdict: not released; he pays. I do not think "I got busy" differs in kind from "I did not feel like it."
3. A member pledged in January to buy no video games for a year. In August he wants to buy one, insists his future self will be fine with it, and argues that a single purchase derails nothing. Verdict: bound. This is the case pledges exist for.
4. A member pledged in January to drink no alcohol for a year — a health pledge, not a statement of values. In August, at his sister's wedding, he wants one glass of champagne, with no health consequence worth mentioning, and tells us sincerely that the January version of himself would want him to have it. Verdict: released.
5. A member, furious the day after his business partner cheated him, pledged to forfeit his escrow if he ever contacted the partner again. Six weeks later, entirely calm, he wants to reconcile, and says the pledge was made by someone who was not in his right mind. Verdict: released — a pledge signed hot cannot be wielded against the cool self. But I also think the angry version of him knew something the calm version no longer does: he knew from the inside how persuasive the urge to call would be. So this verdict strikes me as unstable even as I hold it.
6. A member who signed a two-year savings pledge at twenty-four has, at twenty-seven, undergone what I would call a genuine conversion: she left finance, was ordained, and now describes the goal the pledge served as repugnant rather than merely unappealing. Verdict: released. She is not the person who signed.
7. Another member signed the same kind of two-year pledge at twenty-four and now, at twenty-seven, says he has changed too — three days after a friend offered him a stake in a business that would require the savings. Verdict: bound. Pressed on the difference between his change and hers, he answers honestly that he no longer cares about the goal either, that his indifference is exactly as total as hers, and that the only distinguishing feature is that he can name the day it began.
8. A member pledged to his wife, who has since died, that he would finish restoring the garden they had planned. He would now rather not, and there is no one left to whom the promise was made. Verdict: bound — though I cannot say for whose sake.
9. Escrow sizes. I think a fifty-dollar forfeiture should be enforced mechanically and that a five-thousand-dollar forfeiture for a trivial slip should not be enforced at all, because at that magnitude it ceases to be a commitment device and becomes a threat. But I also think the reason people set large forfeitures is precisely that small ones do not move them — so my verdict advises the desperate member against buying the only instrument that would have worked for him.

My tentative principle is this: a pledge binds you just in case the person you would be if the temptation were not sitting in front of you would endorse being held to it. Case 4 is why I like it. Case 5 is why I distrust it, since the person with no temptation in front of him also seems to want to make the call, and the pledge was meant to speak for the one who knew better. Case 7 is why I suspect the principle may be empty.

What I would like from you:

(a) Begin with a diagnostic survey, before committing to anything: lay out the candidate principles genuinely available here — lifetime welfare across the signer's whole life, endorsement under cool conditions, the etiology of a preference change rather than its content, psychological or narrative continuity between signer and breaker, the pledge as a promise owed to others, and anything else you think belongs in the space. For each, note which of the nine verdicts it delivers and which it misses.

(b) Then commit to one principle, or at most two, stated precisely enough that a stranger could apply it to a case they had never seen and arrive at my answer. Every clause should be traceable to the case that forced it in; if a clause exists only to purchase a verdict, say so, and say what it costs.

(c) Tell me which of my nine verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and for each whether the honest move is to overturn the verdict or to add a clause. If you recommend overturning one, tell me which I should least resent losing.

(d) Apply the principle to three cases I have not raised: a ten-year pledge made at nineteen, enforced against a thirty-year-old who speaks of his younger self with contempt but has otherwise changed gradually and continuously; a pledge whose trigger condition can be verified by no one but the signer; and a member who now pledges each month to make next month's pledge, so the chain renews itself without ever being chosen again.

(e) Finally, address the vote I actually cast. I enforced case 7. Tell me whether that vote can be defended, or whether I should say at next month's meeting that it cannot.

You are welcome to conclude that my verdicts do not cohere and that no principle worth having saves all nine; that is an acceptable answer provided you show me exactly where the incoherence bites. I would rather have one clause fewer and a rule I can apply mid-vote than an elaborate system I cannot.

Please close with a single sentence stating the principle you recommend, and then, as the very last line and in exactly this form so that I can log it: \answer{<name of principle>; overturned: <case number(s), or "none">}
