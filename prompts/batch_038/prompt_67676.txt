I am trying to work out what I am committed to when I say that a symbol is grounded, and the only method that seems to move me is to record verdicts on particular cases and then hunt for the principle they presuppose. What follows is my list, including two pairs I cannot reconcile. I am not after a survey of the literature on this subject; I want a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I can see what each clause is buying and which of my verdicts it obliges me to give up.

The cases. I use 'system' loosely: some are machines, some are people.

1. A laboratory robot arm whose grip-force variable is wired to a strain gauge and modulated moment by moment so that it does not crush what it holds. It has no language whatsoever and cannot answer a single question about grip force. Grounded — paradigmatically so.

2. A text-only model with no images, no sensors and no body, which is told in words that a bicycle wheel is spun about its axle and hung from a rope attached to one end of that axle, and which correctly predicts that the wheel will precess rather than fall, and correctly predicts how the rate of precession changes when the spin rate is increased. Grounded. I am quite confident here.

3. The same model, pressed on apples — where they grow, how they bruise, why a cooked apple softens, what a bruise looks like from the inside — answers everything, offers apt analogies I did not supply, and reveals no detectable limit. Not grounded. It has only re-described apples.

Cases 2 and 3 look to me like the same achievement, performed by the same system, with the same information. That is the first thing I want explained.

4. A person blind from birth who uses 'crimson', 'chartreuse' and 'iridescent' fluently and creatively, and who volunteers, unprompted, that iridescence is a property of surface structure rather than of pigment and would survive being printed in greyscale. Grounded — at least as much as case 2. My verdict would not change if I learnt that every scrap of their knowledge came from other people's descriptions.

5. A vision-and-language model in which 'crimson' is associated with a region of pixel space and with captions written by sighted speakers, but never with wavelengths, paints, or anything it can act upon. Not grounded. This sits badly against my verdict on case 4 and I cannot say why.

6. A robot whose symbol for water is coupled to a sensor that fires for anything transparent and pourable, so that it reliably calls gin 'water'. It acts on the sensor, and only on the sensor. Grounded, though mistaken; grounding, I think, does not require getting the extension right.

7. A translation module with no contact but strings, converting Japanese text into English text which is then read by a grounded module that acts on the world. Not grounded in itself, yet the composite plainly is. Grounding looks here like a property of wholes that parts may or may not inherit, and my principle ought to say which.

8. An agent trained entirely inside a physics simulator, acting on simulated blocks and fluids, with no channel to anything outside it. Grounded — but relative to the simulator's contents. Unsettling, since it shows that 'the world' had been doing silent work in all my earlier verdicts.

9. A nineteenth-century astronomer who identified a new element from a bright yellow line in the solar spectrum and reasoned correctly about it for more than two decades before anyone on Earth held a sample. Grounded. He had a line on a plate and a correspondence.

Two tentative principles of my own, neither of which survives contact with the list.

Principle A, the intervention clause: a symbol is grounded in a system X if and only if X can use it to answer correctly a range of counterfactual questions about the referent's behaviour under interventions that X itself specifies, where the answers are not recoverable from the statistical regularities in X's inputs. Case 1 fails it outright, which is already a problem; case 3 seems to pass it, which is a worse one.

Principle B, the chain clause: a symbol is grounded to the degree that it is derived, through a finite chain of reports and inferences, from tokens in some system that were causally coupled to the referent. This rescues cases 4 and 9, but it appears to rescue 3 and 5 as well, since every sentence in the corpus was written by someone whose own use was coupled to something. If B is right, grounding is a matter of degree, everything linguistic is grounded to some degree, and the concept stops marking anything. I suspect that is what is really wrong with B; I cannot tell whether that suspicion is an argument or a prejudice.

What I should like back: which of my verdicts must be surrendered, and which may be kept at the cost of an extra clause; a principle stated clause by clause with the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on cases I have not listed, in particular a model that has read every published account of a surgical procedure and can perform it competently when connected to a robot, and an adult who has carried the word 'quark' since childhood, uses it correctly in one sentence, and can say nothing further that anyone would recognise as an explanation; whether the gap between cases 2 and 3 is a difference in the systems or only in my willingness to test them; and finally, your own application of the resulting principle to the system answering this request, with the verdict it yields reported whether or not it is flattering.
