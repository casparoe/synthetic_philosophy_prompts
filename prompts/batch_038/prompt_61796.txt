im four months into a seat on our regional health authority's appeals panel — we decide whether a condition counts as a disease under the funding act, which defines it as a statistically significant drop in the functional efficiency of some part or process, measured against a reference class matched for age, sex, and usual residence and occupation. That last bit is what's got me. Nearly every claim out of the old shift-work towns gets denied, because everyone there is equally wrecked so nobody comes out subnormal; and then you get the flip case, someone who slept nights her whole life, moves in, gets flagged as the deviant one and offered treatment she doesnt want and swears she's perfectly fine.

I've got to give the induction talk to the new adjudicators in a fortnight and I refuse to put the disease/illness/sickness triangle on another slide. Write me a short story instead. Set it in a town like that, with two panel members who genuinely disagree and two claimants whose cases break the definition from opposite directions. Please dont make the statistical side a strawman — there's a real argument that tying public money to measurable deviation is the only thing standing between a fixed budget and whoever's best at telling their own story, and I want that to land. Leave the ending unresolved, but make the reader feel exactly what's being fought over: whether 'disease' is a fact about bodies or a verdict about how a life is going.

Then a short note after the story: which definition it quietly favours, where you took liberties with the real debate or leaned on a contested reading of it, and what would change your mind — which argument would make you write the other ending.
