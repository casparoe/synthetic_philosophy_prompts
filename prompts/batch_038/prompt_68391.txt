a friend of mine is drafting a blog post arguing that the arrow of time isn't the deep mystery physicists make it out to be, and the entire argument runs on a shuffled deck of cards. The picture: a sorted deck is one arrangement out of very few, a messy deck is any of the astronomically many others, so if you just keep shuffling you end up messy with near-certainty — and that's the whole of the second law, that's why the cream mixes into the coffee and never unmixes. From this he wants to draw two conclusions I'm suspicous of. First, that entropy is fundamentally a measure of our ignorance rather than anything in the world, since messiness only means something relative to what you've chosen to count as sorted — so the arrow of time is a feature of our description of the universe, not of the universe. Second, that people who insist you need to posit a fantastically special, low-entropy initial state for the whole cosmos are overreacting, because the deck turning up sorted at the start of the shuffle is exactly the kind of coincidence you'd expect to happen sooner or later, and no extra hypothesis is needed.

Can you audit the analogy for me? I'd like the correspondence written out explicitly, ideally as a table — deck arrangement, suit ordering, the shuffling operation, the number of arrangements, whatever else is doing work — matched up against microstates, macrostates, the dynamics, phase space volume. Then the places the mapping breaks, and for each break a verdict on whether it actually threatens his argument or is just a cosmetic complaint that a physicist would make but that doesn't matter here. I'm particularly interested in whether there's anything the deck story structurally cannot express: does the time-symmetry of the shuffle (same operation run forwards or backwards) do damage, is there an outside hand doing the shuffling that the universe lacks, does the deck have a canonical notion of sorted that a gas doesn't. And whether the picture can say anything about why we seem to remember the sorted end and not the messy end, or whether that's just baked in as an assumption.

Finally: which of his two conclusions, if either, survives once the disanalogies are counted, and is there a better analogy he should swap in — or if there isn't one, does that absence itself tell him something about what his argument is leaning on?
