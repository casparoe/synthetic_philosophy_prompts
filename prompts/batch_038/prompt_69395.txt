I write a weekly column on science and ideas and I've got a piece coming up on the way everyone in AI has started talking about models as children — "raising" them, "parenting" the RLHF stage, whole labs describing a training run like a school year. I want to open it with a fake artifact rather than another thinkpiece lede.

So: write me an end-of-term school report, the British kind a head of year produces for a thirteen-year-old — the subject-by-subject grid, the effort and attainment grades, the short pastoral comment at the bottom — except the pupil is a literal child machine in Turing's 1950 sense. Not a chatbot. The thing he proposed you'd build as a small, fairly empty "child programme" and then educate, rather than trying to simulate a finished adult mind directly, complete with the fresh-notebook image (lots of blank sheets, very little mechanism), the reward-and-punishment discipline, the teaching process as distinct from mere conditioning, and a deliberate random element. Riff on all of that, but paraphrase — don't quote him anywhere, because I'll be fact-checking every line of this piece myself and I don't want wording you're only 90 percent sure of.

The comedy has to come from the report form being applied scrupulously and correctly to something it doesnt fit, not from winking at the reader. Concretely I want:

- the tutor can't inspect the pupil's internal state, so every character remark is an inference from behaviour under questioning, and the prose keeps sliding into "appears to", "when asked", "on the evidence of one session"
- an attainment grade that's obviously the same figure twice for two quite different subjects, with a note that the pupil's performance isn't reproducible from run to run because of the random element, and the tutor's strained attempt to phrase that in report-card language
- rewards and punishments logged as behaviour points, plus one line conceding that the teaching process involves more than conditioning, written in the flattened voice of a school behaviour policy
- an exams section: the pupil was entered for a viva voce rather than a written paper, the examiner came away unconvinced, and the tutor quietly admits the exam may have been testing imitation rather then learning
- a house-parent's note about informal behaviour — the pupil being occasionally illogical or unhelpful on purpose — logged as a conduct issue rather than as evidence of anything
- and the pastoral comment at the end, where the tutor realises the whole document says more about the school's categories than about the pupil

Target: not Turing. He should come out of it looking prescient and oddly humane. What's being skewered is the current habit of dressing a training run in childhood-and-classroom language and then treating the metaphors as findings. Keep it deadpan throughout, no asides, no explaining any of the references inside the text, no joke about being an AI. Around 650 words, formatted like the actual document. And no sources, reading list or footnotes at the end — I know where it comes from.
