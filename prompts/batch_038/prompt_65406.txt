I serve on the interpretive standards panel of a national history museum, and next month we vote on the framing text for a re-hung gallery on the Bengal famine of 1943. I would like a draft decision memo, written to be circulated to the panel in advance of the meeting.

The background the panel treats as settled is this. The province lost somewhere between roughly one million and nearly four million people, depending on the estimate. The fall of Burma in 1942 cut off a large share of its rice imports. British authorities removed boats and rice stocks from the coastal districts under denial policies aimed at a possible Japanese invasion. Wartime financing produced severe inflation. A preferential supply scheme protected war-industry workers, civil servants, and the armed forces in the cities. Requests that shipping be diverted to carry grain were largely refused, with wartime shipping scarcity given as the reason. And the provincial government never formally declared a famine at any point. Set alongside these, the gallery's two commissioned reports also record a cyclone and tidal flooding in southwestern Bengal in late 1942 and a rice crop disease, and both note that the relative weight of these factors remains disputed. One report stresses that aggregate food availability was not dramatically below normal and that those who starved were overwhelmingly people whose purchasing power collapsed while prices rose; the other argues that there was a genuine shortfall as well, and that treating the famine purely as a failure of distribution understates the harvest evidence.

Three framings are on the table for the gallery's central panel. The first describes the event as a disaster, with the wartime disruption and the weather presented as its causes. The second avoids verdict language altogether, calling it a catastrophe made vastly worse by decisions about who was to be supplied and who was not. The third describes it as a crime, on the ground that a government with the means to prevent mass starvation chose other priorities. A delegation of descendants' organizations has asked us to go further still and use the word genocide, and the trust that funded the original gallery has written to say it will not support a re-hang whose central label applies criminal language to a wartime ally.

Within the panel, one member argues that the word famine is itself already evaluative, so neutrality is not on offer and we should choose openly. Another proposes a double-entry label that states the natural and the political causes in parallel columns, deliberately unweighted, as the only honest treatment of an unsettled question; I suspect this launders a verdict as balance, but I cannot articulate why, and the memo should engage the proposal squarely rather than dismiss it. Our charter requires that framing text assert no evaluative conclusion unsupported by the preponderance of scholarship, which does not help much here, because the difficulty is telling causal contribution apart from moral imputation.

The memo should therefore address, at minimum: whether describing these deaths as a crime requires that anyone intended them, or whether culpability can attach to prioritization, refusal, and delay — and what separates saying that policy caused the deaths from saying that the deaths were a crime. If the starvation ran through markets, prices, wages, and the collapse of particular groups' ability to command food rather than through an order to starve anybody, who or what is the responsible agent, and can a wall text assign responsibility to an office, a cabinet, or an administrative regime that never made such an order? Whether the genocide request should be granted, refused, or answered in some other register, and what the museum owes a community whose grievance may concern recognition rather than vocabulary. How the text should carry a live scholarly dispute about causal weight without either suppressing the disagreement or turning a public gallery into a seminar. And whether the trust's objection is a reason about the label or only a reason about our budget — and how to say so plainly without appearing indifferent to the museum's dependence on that money.

Please end with an unambiguous recommendation: one of the three framings, or a fourth that you construct, together with the sentence or two that would actually go on the wall, criteria specific enough that the panel can apply them to objections raised in the room, and a fair statement of the strongest consideration against your own recommendation. Something in the range of 1,000 to 1,400 words. I do not need citations, footnotes, or a bibliography — I need reasoning we can act on.
