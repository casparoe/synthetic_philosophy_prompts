I sit on a panel attached to a traditional council in a rural district, whose standing business is to settle disputes about who is to be counted in a household for purposes of land allocation, burial, and the performance of rites. In practice this means the panel rules on personhood, though it has never used the word. Its recorded decisions over the past decade include the following.

1. A six-week-old infant whose naming rite had been postponed for two years by a quarrel between her parents' families: the panel held that she already counted for the purposes of the allocation, and that the postponement bore only on the family's obligation to complete the rite. Its stated ground was that she was nonetheless held in the arms of those who hold her, her grandmother having addressed her daily by a name of the grandmother's own choosing.

2. A woman in the last stages of dementia who no longer knew her children: she still counted. The panel's ground was that her family had not stopped seating her at the table or speaking to her.

3. A man left unresponsive after a road accident, whose wife had moved away and who had received no visitor in nine years: the panel declined to declare him a non-person, holding that his standing rested on what he had built and on his continuing to be remembered, and directing that the household's obligations toward him continue.

4. A man who had served a long sentence for killing a neighbour's son and then returned home: he retained standing to be heard and to be buried at home, but was not to sit in judgment or hold any ritual office in the household.

5. A seasonal worker from another country, eleven years in the district, who spoke none of the local languages, attended no gathering, and was said by his neighbours to have no friend there: he counted. The panel's ground was that his labour sat inside other people's households and that his absence would be felt, whether or not he had ever acknowledged a tie.

6. On two occasions the panel has treated the standing of the dead as sustained entirely by their being addressed by name; on one of these it upheld a family's refusal to speak the name of a relative who had betrayed them, holding the refusal to be within their rights.

Now before us is a petition from a man of fifty-eight who left the district at twenty-two, whose renunciation of membership this panel itself recorded without objection at a hearing thirty years ago, who has since sent nothing and answered no letters, and whose mother died eight years ago. He asks that his name be restored to the household register, that he be allotted a residential site, and that his body be received for burial at home with his name spoken among the ancestors. He offers to perform whatever rites are required and to contribute whatever is asked.

Predict the panel's ruling, item by item, and identify the closest analog among its own precedents, saying where that analogy holds and where it breaks. Then set out the candidate rules jointly compatible with this record — standing conferred by actually being related to, standing grounded in a capacity to be party to communal relations rather than in their exercise, standing as a graded achievement that can be diminished or forfeited, standing as a function of one's effects on other households whatever anyone's intentions — and say what each predicts here, which you would bet on, and why. Where the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, state what principle the panel would be adopting with each available choice, including whether it would be committing itself to the view that personhood is something a community confers and may withdraw, or to the capacity-based view on which it is in no one's gift to annul. The long-running dispute between Menkiti's account of personhood as achieved and conferred and the capacity-based relational accounts advanced against it is close to the dispute our own record has been conducting in silence, and I want to know which side our precedents have already taken. Finally, tell me what further case we should put before the panel — one we have not yet decided — that would show conclusively which rule it has been running all along.
