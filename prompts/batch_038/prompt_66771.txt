I'm meant to give the rationale for our department's new essay policy on AI assistants at a faculty meeting on Thursday, and I have a problem: the argument I wrote to justify it proves far too much, and a colleague has allready told me so in an email I don't know how to answer. Here it is.

(1) S understands why p only if S is able, unaided, to work out the answers to the relevant why- and what-if questions about p.
(2) An ability that is never exercised is lost.
(3) If a tool is permanently to hand that supplies an answer faster than S could work it out, S will not exercise the ability on that range of questions.
(4) So where such a tool is permanently to hand, S loses the ability to work out those answers unaided.
(5) By (1), S then fails to understand why p.
(6) Textbooks, lecture notes, libraries, teachers, calculators and search engines are all such tools.
(7) So no student who has ever been taught, used a library, or done arithmetic on a calculator understands why anything.
(8) Some of my students plainly do understand some things. I've examined them; the best essay I read last term was by someone who revises almost entirely from videos she didn't make.
(9) Therefore (1) is false.

The trouble is I want (1), or something close to it. Its the premise that makes understanding worth more than a pile of true beliefs, and the only premise that gives me any reason to restrict anything at all. And I obviously want (8). But (7) is where the argument eats my entire profession, and I genuinely can't tell whether this is a valid argument with a premise I should have seen was too strong, an invalid one, or somehow both at once.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look so innocent when I wrote it down? Then price the alternatives — tell me what I'd be giving up by dropping each of (1), (2), (3) and (6) instead, and which of those costs is the cheapest to swallow. And if there's a repair, some way to keep an ability requirement without landing on (7), say what it would have to look like, and whether the line it draws is a principled one or just a description of the tools I happen to have grown up with and now find comfortable. Don't bother with references or a reading list, I know the territory — I need the diagnosis, not the bibliography. British English please, since whatever I do with this ends up in a handout for the faculty.
