I work on the computer vision side of an ag-tech startup — cameras on a sprayer boom, we detect weed and pest hotspots in row crops and pulse micro-doses of chemistry only where it's needed instead of blanket-spraying the whole field. In our trials that cut volume by something like 85%. A few months back I wrote an internal memo for the annual impact report where I tried to translate that into "insect deaths avoided," with a monte carlo over population densities and drift, and it detonated a slack channel that has now been arguing about it for four months. Nobody has moved an inch, and they keep re-litigating the same two moves at each other.

Here's the two sides as best I can restate them. Priya (PM, does a lot of effective-altruism reading on her own time) says: if insects can suffer at all — and she points at that UK thing a few years ago where they decided crabs and octopuses count as sentient, which she takes as evidence that arthropod sentience isn't fringe — then blanket insecticide application kills an astronomically large number of them, so spot-spraying might be the single biggest animal-welfare win in agriculture, bigger than anything else on the report by orders of magnitude. She's explicit that she's doing expected value under uncertainty: even a few percent credence that a bee or a caterpillar has any experience at all, times trillions of individuals, dominates. Marco (agronomy, used to do field ecology) says the arithmetic is garbage in a specific way: we can't count the insects that were never born, and the ones spared from the sprayer go on to be eaten alive by parasitoid wasps, or starve, or get cannibalised in a denser population, so "more insects alive" might mean more total suffering rather than less. He also thinks welfare is the wrong frame for an ecosystem entirely and that the only defensible metrics are non-target effects, resistance evolution, and biodiversity. When I asked what he'd accept as evidence that an insect suffers, he said something like: an animal that avoids a location where it was harmed even when there's food there and no current threat, and he doesn't think we have that for the species in our fields. Priya's response is that the bar is set arbitrarily high so it can never be cleared, and that if we applied it to mammals we'd conclude they don't suffer either.

My guess — and it's just a guess, I have no philosophy background — is that the facts aren't actually where the fight is, since I think both of them would sign off on the same field data if you showed it to them. Something underneath is doing the work: maybe it's about what you're supposed to do with a low-probability, huge-number claim (her expected-value move vs. whatever his refusal really is), or maybe it's about whether "suffering" is even the right word for a nervous system with a few hundred thousand neurons, in which case they might be using the same vocabulary for two different things and just talking past each other. Or I could be wrong and there's a real empirical question buried in there that a decent experiment or a population model could actually settle. So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Verbal, a clash over what counts as evidence, a hidden empirical or formal question, bedrock intuitions about who counts morally that neither of them can argue for, or two people answering different questions under one heading? Diagnose it using what they actually say rather than giving me a survey of positions, and then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a measurement, a formal result, a change of question — or make the case that no progress should be expected and why. I don't need you to pick a winner. I mostly want to know what I'm actually listening to, and secondarily what the hell to put in the report.
