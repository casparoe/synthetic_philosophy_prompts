I spent most of last night arguing with a friend of mine — a clinical psychologist who reads a fair amount of evolutionary theory — about whether Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper undermines the adaptationist explanations he was defending. Toward the end he typed out his position in a message, and I would like to know how much of it I should concede. Here it is:

'Gould and Lewontin showed that natural selection cannot explain most traits, and that genuine adaptation is the exception rather than the rule. Their example is the spandrels of St Mark's in Venice: the tapering triangular spaces between the arches that carry the dome. Those spaces are there only because you cannot set a dome on rounded arches without them — pure byproduct — and then the mosaicists filled them in, so the decoration ends up looking like the whole point of the structure. A spandrel in biology is the same kind of thing: any trait that is not an adaptation, whether it is a vestige, a holdover from an ancestor, or a fluke of genetic drift. What they proposed was a change in the burden of proof — non-adaptation becomes the null hypothesis, and the adaptationist has to demonstrate that selection actually built the trait. The Pangloss comparison makes the same point: adaptationists assume every trait is optimally designed for whatever it does now. They called these spandrels exaptations, and Gould went on to argue that the human brain and language are exaptations rather than adaptations. The upshot is that the debate is basically over: biology now treats byproducts as the default, which is exactly why the adaptive stories in evolutionary psychology are just-so stories and need not be taken seriously.'

Several things strike me as off, but I could not pin them down at one in the morning, and he is the one with the science degree. Please audit the passage sentence by sentence: which claims a philosopher of biology or a working evolutionist would accept as a fair reading of the 1979 paper, which are defensible simplifications, and which are misrepresentations. In particular, I would like to know whether the paper really makes a claim about how many traits are adaptations; whether the term spandrel really covers vestiges and drift-produced features, or something narrower; whether exaptation is the paper's own term and whether it means the same thing as spandrel; and whether the null-hypothesis framing is Gould and Lewontin's proposal or a reconstruction supplied by their critics and by later methodology. Finally, tell me whether his conclusion survives the corrections — does anything that is true in the summary support dismissing adaptationist accounts of human psychology wholesale?

Lead with a single sentence giving your overall verdict on the summary, close with the one correction you think matters most if I want to reopen the argument, and keep the whole thing under 500 words, in American English.
