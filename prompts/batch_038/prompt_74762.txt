I chair the Rules Committee of a university games society that has run an unbroken game of Nomic — the game in which proposing changes to the rules is itself one of the moves — for about nineteen years. Our rulebook is home-grown apart from the opening provisions. Four of them matter here:

- Rule 101: Any member may submit a proposed amendment in writing at least one week before a general meeting; an amendment is adopted if two-thirds of the members present and voting support it.
- Rule 104: These rules may be amended in no other way.
- Rule 106: Where these rules are silent, ambiguous, or in apparent conflict, the Rules Committee shall determine their meaning; its determination binds the society until the rules are amended.
- The membership form, which every person signs on joining, says that a member agrees to be bound by the rules "as amended from time to time."

Of our twenty-seven current members, nine joined within the last two years; nobody alive in the society today signed anything at its founding.

At last month's meeting, Amendment 214 passed 24–3. It did three things. It added Rule 111: no member may be removed from the game, or deprived of a vote, except at their own written request. It added Rule 118: "Rule 111 may not be amended, repealed, suspended, or circumvented, now or at any time, by any procedure whatsoever, including by any amendment of Rule 118." And it added Rule 119: any future amendment purporting to breach Rule 118 is void and of no effect whatever the vote, plus a severability clause stating that if any part of Amendment 214 is void the remainder takes effect.

The three members who voted against it have petitioned the committee to declare the amendment void and strike it from the register. The petition's author is a close friend of mine, a jurisprudence graduate student, and the two of us spent most of last night at his kitchen table arguing about it. His case, as fairly as I can state it: Rule 101 confers a power, and a body exercising a conferred power cannot use that power to withdraw it from those who hold it later, so the twenty-four voted away rights belonging to members who were not present; Rule 118 is self-referential, and a rule cannot validly authorize the destruction of the very rule that authorizes it; the maxim that no legislature can bind its successors reflects a choice between a continuing conception of sovereignty, on which the amending power always survives intact and entrenchment is impossible, and a self-embracing one, on which a body may use its power once to constrain how it is exercised later, and a private game with no sovereign must sit on the continuing side; and pragmatically, if entrenchment works once it works forever, since next month someone could entrench a rule ending the game, or a rule making the chair's word law, with no route back.

My own view, for what it is worth: Rule 101 excludes nothing by silence; Rule 104 makes Rule 101 the exclusive route and 214 took that route; a rulebook that cannot entrench anything cannot protect a member from expulsion by a bare two-thirds, which is precisely what 214 was proposed to do; and a doctrine about sovereign legislatures does not travel cleanly to a private association whose members signed up for rules "as amended from time to time."

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should:

1. Recommend one specific disposition — certify Amendment 214 as adopted in full, certify it in severed part, or refuse to certify it — and state concretely what the secretary should enter in the register and what the chair should announce at next week's meeting.
2. Address directly whether Rules 118 and 119 lie within the power Rule 101 confers, taking each of my friend's four arguments in turn and saying which you accept and which you reject.
3. Grapple with the self-reference rather than gesture at it: whether 118 can protect itself, and what follows from the fact that 119, which voids future amendments, is itself a rule that a future amendment could repeal.
4. Say whether the membership form's "as amended from time to time" clause carries the weight I want to put on it, given that nine members signed it without knowing what 214 would contain.
5. Set out a general standard the committee can apply to future self-referential proposals, and apply it to Amendment 215, already on the agenda, which would repeal the entire rulebook and vest all rulemaking authority in a single arbiter. Whatever principle you adopt will decide 215, so say plainly whether it permits or forbids it.

Argue from the text of our own rules and from principles you can state in plain sentences. Do not cite cases, treatises, or philosophers, and do not include a bibliography; I want your reasoning, not your references. Keep it to something the committee can read in ten minutes.
