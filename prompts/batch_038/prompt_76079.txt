I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist at a national laboratory, mostly designing and calibrating instruments other people then used to make discoveries. I retired four years ago, and since retiring I have read a good deal more philosophy than physics, which my former colleagues find amusing and my wife finds expensive. One habit I have kept from the laboratory is that I want to be told what a method assumes before I am told what it proves.

That habit is why I am writing to you. I sit on the council of a learned society in the physical sciences, some four thousand members, which awards one medal a year. The recipient is chosen by a twenty-four-member medal committee voting by ranked ballot with elimination rounds, under a statute that says only that the medal shall go to the candidate preferred by the committee. Nobody has ever said what preferred means, and this year that has turned out to matter.

The facts of this year's ballot, all of which are on the record. Five nominations were received. First preferences ran: a plasma theorist, 8; a spectroscopist, 7; a crystallographer, 5; a low-temperature experimentalist, 3; a historian of physics, 1. The historian was eliminated first, the experimentalist second, the crystallographer third, and the theorist then took the medal on transfers, thirteen votes to eleven. The ballot is secret but the returned papers are kept, and at my request the secretary has now computed the complete table of head-to-head contests. It shows that the experimentalist, eliminated in the second round, would have beaten every one of the other four in a straight pair. Three members have since told me, without embarrassment, that they did not vote sincerely: they placed a nominee they considered hopeless at the top of the ballot in order to change the order of elimination. Whether that changed the winner can be tested by recomputation; what those three would have written had they not been playing for position cannot be recovered at all, and I want the difference between those two questions kept clearly in view.

A colleague on council, a statistician by training, has circulated a two-page note. Its argument, in substance, is this: Arrow's impossibility theorem establishes that with three or more candidates no ranked method can be fair; our rule is therefore not badly chosen but arbitrary in principle; there is consequently no such thing as the will of the committee, only the will of whoever fixed the agenda and the order of voting; the honest remedy is to abolish the ballot and let a five-member selection panel decide by discussion and consensus, as comparable bodies do; and once fairness has been proved impossible the only question left is whom we trust, and he trusts people rather than arithmetic. Several members have found this persuasive, and I expect it to be put as a motion at our meeting on the fourteenth.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the medal committee and to council beforehand: eight hundred to twelve hundred words, recommendation first in two or three sentences, then the reasoning, and something we can actually act on in one sitting. I have read Arrow's result only in summaries, so I am not asking you to take my side; if the note is substantially right, say so. It should do the following.

1. Diagnose this year's result plainly: what exactly went wrong, whether the defect belongs to elimination rounds in particular or to ranked voting as such, and what recomputations on the retained ballots would settle the manipulation question and what they cannot settle.
2. Audit my colleague's appeal. State the conditions the theorem actually imposes, in language a numerate physicist will follow, and say which of them our present rule meets and which it breaks. Say whether breaking the independence condition is a real cost or a bargain. Be explicit about whether a five-member panel deciding by consensus counts as a dictator in the technical sense, and about whether such a panel escapes the theorem or merely fails to produce a ranking at all, which strikes me as a different and possibly worse failure.
3. Say whether manipulation is an Arrow problem at all. I have come across a later result of Gibbard and Satterthwaite and suspect it is the one that bites on the confession at dinner. Give its content, its limits, and whether it exposes every rule equally in practice or only in principle; if some families of rules sit outside it, say what they cost us.
4. Examine the assumption that the theorem applies to us at all. Our committee is not a random sample: its preferences may be ordered along one axis, whether seniority, field, or attitude toward instrument-building versus theory. Say what would have to be true for that structure to restore a fair rule, and what I should look for in this year's ballots to test it.
5. Recommend one course. Keeping elimination rounds, a pairwise-consistent rule, approval or score ballots, a panel shortlist with the full committee choosing between two or three names, and deferral are all live options. Give the exact wording of the motion I could table, the words I would propose for the statute in place of preferred by the committee, and a schedule that has it working before nominations open again in September. Also tell me what to do about this year's medal. I would rather not re-run the vote, and I want to be told whether upholding the result is defensible or merely convenient.
6. Give my colleague's case at its strength, not as a caricature. Deliberation turns up facts about a body of work that no ballot records; a small panel can weigh one field against another without pretending the two are commensurable; and its members can be held to account by name, which a rule cannot. If that case changes your recommendation, or argues for a hybrid, say so.

No footnotes, no reading list, no appeal to authority: half of council will not look anything up and the other half distrust what they cannot follow. Define each technical term at first use, and assume a reader who is intelligent, numerate, and impatient.
