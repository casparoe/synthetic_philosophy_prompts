Sortof a cosmology question, but really an epistemology one. I want you to argue for a position, not map the debate — pick a side and defend it.

The position: the Boltzmann brain objection, as cosmologists actually deploy it, is self-undermining, and so BB-domination isn't a reason to reject a cosmological theory.

Setup so you know what i'm aiming at. Take some theory T — an eternally inflating picture with a de Sitter-ish future and some well-behaved measure, whatever — on which freak observers that fluctuate into existence for a moment, complete with vivid false memories of a long coherent past, hugely outnumber ordinary observers like us. The standard argument goes: if T were true I'd almost certainly be one of those freaks; I'm plainly not, because my memories hang together, my evidence is mutually consistent, my experiments replicate; so T is probably false, and we should prefer the measures that don't generate freaks.

What I want argued is that this is illegitimate, in two steps. First, the "I'd almost certainly be a freak" premise only goes through if you treat yourself as a random sample from T's observers, and no physical theory hands you that prior — typicality is a self-locating assumption smuggled in as though it were a theorem about the measure. Second, and worse, the argument is unstable in the way debunking arguments get unstable: taking the first premise seriously enough to conclude you might be a freak undercuts the reliability of the reasoning that carries you to the conclusion, since a freak's beliefs about measures, probabilities, and evidence are all noise. You can't disconfirm T with an argument whose premises you're only entitled to trust if T is false.

So: argue that the correct response to BB-domination is to give up typicality as an inference rule in cosmology, rather than to go shopping for a measure that makes us normal. And don't dodge the hard parts — say what happens to ordinary anthropic and inductive reasoning once typicality goes, whether some version of the objection survives because it doesn't route through a random-sample premise at all, and whether your view amounts to immunizing cosmology from a genuine reductio, i.e. whether every crazy measure now passes.

Be committed about it. Take a definite line, give your reasons, say what you'd give up to keep it and where you think the view is weakest. No neutral survey, no "on the other hand some philosophers hold".
