The book summarizes the debate as follows:

'In 1920 Mises observed that where the state owns the means of production there are no market prices for capital goods, and without prices there is no common unit in which to compare the costs of alternative production processes — so a socialist economy could not calculate rationally, though Mises was careful not to claim it would grind to a halt at once, and allowed that a socialist country trading with a capitalist world market could borrow those prices as a crutch. In the 1930s Oskar Lange answered him. Socialism does not need real capital markets, Lange argued, because a central planning board can simulate them: the board announces a set of prices, instructs every state enterprise to produce the output at which marginal cost equals the announced price and to produce that output at minimum cost, and then raises the price of whatever proves to be in shortage and lowers the price of whatever proves to be in surplus, iterating until the books balance. It is the procedure competitive markets perform by buying and selling, carried out instead by arithmetic, and Lange thought the board could do it better than a market, since it could choose any distribution of income it wished and could take account of the external effects that competitive firms ignore. Hayek conceded the logic and changed the subject: even if a board could solve the equations in principle, it could never assemble the data, because the knowledge that matters economically — which machine stands idle, whose particular skills suit which task, what a local shortage means this afternoon — is dispersed among millions of people, much of it tacit, and changing faster than any questionnaire can track. The practical verdict arrived in 1989–91, when the planned economies of Eastern Europe collapsed; the debate closed with the Austrians vindicated on the theory as well as the practice.'
