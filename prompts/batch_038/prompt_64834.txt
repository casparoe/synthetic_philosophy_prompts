I'm writing a chapter for a methods collection and I keep getting stuck on a fight I want you to settle in essay form. Here's the setup, which is loosely based on a real kind of situation but you should treat it as my invented case:

A regional archive ran a big oral history project in the mid-90s with domestic workers — about 200 hours of tape. Only 40 of the interviews ever got transcribed, because the transcription budget ran out. Then in 2015 the whole thing was digitised and the finding aid went online. But the online catalog only lists the 40 transcribed interviews as item-level content; the other 160 hours appear at collection level only, as one line reading roughly "audio recrods, untranscribed, various speakers." So the tapes exist, they're digitized, they could in principle be streamed tomorrow — and almost nobody will ever find or use them, because a search for a name, a street, or a neighborhood returns nothing.

Now the controversy I actually want the essay on. Two camps:

Camp A says the silence here is a property of the descriptive layer. The material isn't missing, it's unaddressable; the silence was manufactured by the catalog, so the fix is more description — transcribe, index, name every speaker, attach subject headings. Silence on this view is a technical, repairable failure.

Camp B says that's naive. The operative silence was made upstream, before anyone touched a keyboard: by who got asked to speak (the project interviewed the workers and never their employers), by the fact that these women's words were only ever going to be preserved as testimony about labour and not as anything else, and by the terms on which they agreed to be recorded at all. On this view adding 160 hours of beautifully indexed description doesn't undo a silence — it just produces a larger, better-searchable version of the same skewed record, and dresses it up as complete.

Write me the essay. Take a side: does the operative silence in this case live in the description, where better metadata can actually repair it, or does it live upstream in the conditions of record creation, such that description at best relocates it and at worst launders it? Weigh the strongest version of each camp — steelman B in particular, since A sounds obviously right to most people — and don't hide behind "well, it's really both, it's complicated." A conditional verdict is fine, e.g. description repairs silence of type X but can't touch type Y, but then you have to say precisely what X and Y are and why the line falls there.

1200–1500 words. No bibliography and no reading list — I don't want to be told what to go read, I want the argument. Use the case as the running test throughout rather than as a decorative opening, and make sure you engage head-on with at least one concrete sub-question: does making those 160 hours findable change anything at all for a historian who, realistically, will never listen to them?
