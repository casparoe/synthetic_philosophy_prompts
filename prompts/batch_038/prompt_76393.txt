I have reconstructed, in explicit form, a piece of reasoning that our reading group on the problem of easy knowledge found each step of irresistible and the whole of intolerable. I want a diagnosis, not a survey of the debate.

**The case.** You acquire a used bench instrument, call it G, which classifies each resistor placed in its cradle as IN or OUT — within tolerance or outside it — and shows the verdict on a small screen. The seller tells you nothing about its accuracy. You have no independent way of testing individual resistors: your multimeter is dead and no reference samples are available. What you do have is a single reel of resistors and a lot of time. So you run this procedure. You place r₁ in the cradle; G displays IN; you take the display at face value and come to believe — and, as the argument assumes, to know — that r₁ is within tolerance. You also know, by looking at the screen, that G displayed IN for r₁. Putting the two together, you know that G displayed IN and that r₁ is within tolerance: you know that G was right about r₁. You repeat for r₂ through r₄₀, all drawn from the same reel, and you end up knowing forty propositions of the form "G was right about rᵢ."

**The argument.**

1. If a source produces in you a belief that p, and that belief amounts to knowledge, then it can amount to knowledge even though you have no independent reason to think the source reliable.
2. If you know that p and you know that q, and you competently put them together, you can come to know that p and q.
3. By (1), for each resistor you test, you can come to know its tolerance status from G's display alone.
4. By (2), for each i, you can come to know that G displayed a verdict for rᵢ and that the verdict was correct.
5. Forty known instances of an unfamiliar instrument's readings being correct is at least as strong an inductive base for a general claim about that instrument as any base you would accept elsewhere: in ordinary cases you would readily come to trust an unfamiliar thermometer going forward on the strength of forty verified correct readings.
6. So after the fortieth test you know that G is reliable — that it gets tolerance status right, and that its future verdicts may be trusted, including on components and conditions unlike any you have tested.
7. Steps (1)–(6) drew on no information about G except G's own deliverances. They can therefore be run again for any source that yields knowledge of the kind (1) describes, including your vision and your memory: you can come to know that your vision is reliable, and that your memory is reliable, without any independent check on either.
8. If you can know that your vision and memory are reliable without independent checks, you can know that you are not systematically deceived, since systematic deception is unreliability, and you hold thousands of known instances of your faculties getting things right.
9. So skepticism about the external world is refuted by a procedure anyone can carry out in an afternoon with forty resistors.
10. (9) is false.
11. Therefore at least one of (1)–(8) is false.

**My own inclinations, offered only to locate me, not to steer you.** I would rather keep (2), because giving it up seems to make a mess of ordinary conjunctive reasoning, and I would much rather keep (10). I am uneasy about (5), but I cannot tell whether that unease tracks a real defect or is just discomfort with the conclusion.
