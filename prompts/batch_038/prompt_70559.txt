I am preparing one session of my graduate seminar on explanation in the historical sciences, and the text I want to work through with the students is a short but surprisingly slippery pair of paragraphs from Engels's letter of 25 January 1894 to W. Borgius, in Donna Torr's translation. It is the passage materialists almost always reach for when they want to deny that particular great men matter, and I find that it is usually quoted rather than read. I want to read it properly, and I would value your help in doing so. The relevant text is this:

"We regard economic conditions as the factor which ultimately determines historical development. … (a) Political, juridical, philosophical, religious, literary, artistic, etc., development is based on economic development. But all these react upon one another and also upon the economic base. It is not that the economic position is the cause and alone active, while everything else only has a passive effect. There is, rather, interaction on the basis of the economic necessity, which ultimately always asserts itself. … Men make their history themselves, only in given surroundings which condition it and on the basis of actual relations already existing, among which the economic relations, however much they may be influenced by the other political and ideological ones, are still ultimately the decisive ones, forming the red thread which runs through them and alone leads to understanding.

(b) Men make their history themselves, but not as yet with a collective will or according to a collective plan or even in a definitely defined, given society. Their efforts clash, and for that very reason all such societies are governed by necessity, which is supplemented by and appears under the forms of accident. The necessity which here asserts itself amidst all accident is again ultimately economic necessity. This is where the so-called great men come in for treatment. That such and such a man and precisely that man arises at that particular time in that given country is of course pure accident. But cut him out and there will be a demand for a substitute, and this substitute will be found, good or bad, but in the long run he will be found. That Napoleon, just that particular Corsican, should have been the military dictator whom the French Republic, exhausted by its own war, had rendered necessary, was an accident; but that, if a Napoleon had been lacking, another would have filled the place, is proved by the fact that the man has always been found as soon as he became necessary: Caesar, Augustus, Cromwell, etc. While Marx discovered the materialist conception of history, Thierry, Mignet, Guizot, and all the English historians up to 1850 are the proof that it was being striven for, and the discovery of the same conception by Morgan proves that the time was ripe for it and that indeed it had to be discovered.

So with all the other accidents, and apparent accidents, of history. The further the particular sphere which we are investigating is removed from the economic sphere and approaches that of pure abstract ideology, the more shall we find it exhibiting accidents in its development, the more will its curve run in a zig-zag. So also you will find that the axis of this curve will approach more and more nearly parallel to the axis of the curve of economic development the longer the period considered and the wider the field dealt with."

Please help me understand the following, in order.

1. In the sentence "all such societies are governed by necessity, which is supplemented by and appears under the forms of accident," I think Engels is running two incompatible formulations past the reader in a single clause. "Supplemented by" suggests that accident is a second factor added to necessity; "appears under the forms of" suggests that accident is only the phenomenal guise that necessity wears. Which reading does the rest of paragraph (b) actually support, and what turns on the difference — in particular, whether necessity is supposed to be visible in particular events or only recoverable across long stretches of them?

2. The Napoleon sentence is syntactically a mess and I want a clean paraphrase of it for my students. Who or what is the agent of "rendered necessary," and what exactly is being called an accident: the man's existence, his identity, or his occupancy of the role? What work is done by the phrase "just that particular Corsican," and does the sentence commit Engels to a distinction between a functional role and its occupant that he never states explicitly?

3. Classify the substitution thesis — "cut him out and there will be a demand for a substitute … good or bad, but in the long run he will be found." Is it a counterfactual claim about how things would have gone had the man not existed, an inductive generalization from the list of cases offered, or a partly constitutive claim about what counts as filling a role at all? Engels says the generalization is "proved by the fact that the man has always been found as soon as he became necessary." Assess that inference honestly: does the enumeration prove anything, or does it depend on a criterion of "having become necessary" that can only be applied retrospectively, once the man has in fact appeared? Is the thesis falsifiable as stated? If it is not, tell me how it could be restricted so as to become falsifiable, and what would be lost by the restriction.

4. Do the qualifiers "good or bad" and "in the long run" give away the game? They seem to concede that the substitute may differ substantially from the man removed and may arrive late. If so, spell out precisely what Engels leaves to the individual and what he takes away — timing versus content, token versus type, the fact that a dictatorship of some sort occurs versus what it does once it occurs. I suspect the concession is larger than the passage's rhetoric admits, and I want that suspicion either vindicated or corrected.

5. The final sentence extends the same reasoning from soldiers and dictators to intellectual discovery, citing the French and English historians who were "striving" toward the materialist conception before Marx, and Morgan's independent arrival at it. Is the argument stronger here or weaker? Multiple independent discovery is respectable evidence of something, but does it support the same sense of "had to be" that the Napoleon case requires, or is Engels sliding between "someone would have produced something of the general sort" and "this content would have been produced"?

6. Explicate the zig-zag paragraph, which my students find opaque and which I confess I cannot fully unpack myself. What are the two curves, what does it mean for an axis to approach parallelism with another, and why should distance from the economic sphere widen the zig-zag while length of period and breadth of field straighten it out? Is this in effect a claim about deviations cancelling over long runs, and is it consistent with paragraph (b)? There seems to be a problem: if the curve zig-zags most in precisely the domains where politics, religion and philosophy live, then accident dominates exactly where the great men are, which looks like the opposite of the dispensability point. Resolve the tension if you can, or sharpen it if you cannot.

7. Finally, the framing question for the session is a modern counterfactual — roughly, whether the German catastrophe of the 1930s required one particular man, or only a demand for some occupant of the role he filled. Working strictly from the distinctions Engels has actually drawn in these paragraphs, and not from any historical knowledge of your own, what is the most he is entitled to claim about a case of that shape, and what is he clearly not entitled to claim? A partisan of the older heroic view — the sort of thing Carlyle pressed — would object that Engels has described the constraints on historical change without accounting for its sources of novelty; is there anything in this text that answers that objection?

Two constraints on your reply. Please reconstruct the argument of paragraph (b) as a numbered sequence of claims with conclusions attached, and identify which single claim is load-bearing, so that I can show my students where the whole thing fails if anywhere. And please do not send me to the secondary literature, to editions, or to a reading list; I have all of that. If some interpretive turn seems to depend on a translation choice you cannot check from the English alone, say so and mark it as uncertain rather than guessing at the German.
