I'm building a stress test for the Jain saptabhaṅgī — the sevenfold qualified predication — and I want you to design a case to my spec rather than just explain the doctrine back to me.

The spec: give me one ordinary, realistic scenario (a lab, an office, a family argument — nothing metaphysically exotic, no omniscient kevalin, no time travel) plus a single claim about it, such that

1. the claim genuinely earns an "in some ways it is" verdict, an "in some ways it is not" verdict, and a real indescribability (avaktavya) verdict — not just as a formal filling-out of the list;
2. all four of the respects a Jain standpoint is supposed to be relativized to — substance, place, time, and mode or nature — are held fixed. Same thing, same location, same moment, same intrinsic character. So the easy move, where asti and nāsti just turn out to be about different dravya/kṣetra/kāla/bhāva and there was never any tension, is blocked by construction;
3. the tension is generated by the scenario, not asserted in it. Writing "and so it both is and is not" is cheating — the case has to make that come out.

Then show your work. Say which of the seven modes apply and why, given the pinned parameters. Tell me honestly which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if you release one of the four parameters — does it collapse into plain equivocation? And give the counterattack from someone who thinks the whole scheme is just a disguised way of handling indexicality: presumably they'll say you snuck in a fifth respect (a relation, a description, a question under discussion) that isn't among the official four. How good is that objection against your particular case?

If you think the spec can't actually be satisfied, build the closest case you can and point to exactly where it fails — don't quietly relax a constraint and pretend you didn't.

One more thing: flag where the scholarship is settled and where it isn't. I don't want a contested reading of what avaktavya is for, or of whether the seven modes are about assertion, predication, or doxastic restraint, presented to me as the consensus view. American English, please.
