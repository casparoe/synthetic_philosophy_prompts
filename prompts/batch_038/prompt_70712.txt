For eleven years I owned a heating and cooling business, and since selling it I have volunteered at our town library, where I facilitate a monthly discussion group about machines that make decisions. We average eight to fourteen people and not one of us has a technical or academic background: a night nurse, two retired schoolteachers, a long-haul driver, a seventeen-year-old who writes code for money, a couple who run a farm stand. I have no formal training either. I read slowly and widely, I keep a notebook, and I try to arrange each session so that we argue about something definite rather than about the word "artificial."

Last month's session went badly, and afterwards I think I understood why. One member said the real danger is that these systems will not do what we tell them; another said the danger is precisely that they will do what we tell them, because what we tell them is already the wrong thing to want. They debated for an hour without ever disagreeing about a single fact, and the rest of us got restless. Reading afterwards, I came across a distinction that seems to name the split: a narrow way of stating the problem, in which the only question is whether a system reliably pursues what its operator actually wanted — intent alignment, one author called it — and a broad way, in which the question is whether the ends a system pursues are ends that people should want at all, and whose ends those are. I could not find the source again, and I do not want one: no citations, no reading list, nothing I would have to look up. I want something I can photocopy and hand round a table.

So please write me a short "which version of this problem do you actually believe in?" quiz for that group, of eight to ten questions with a scoring key. The requirements I care about are these. Every question must be a concrete situation with two or three possible responses, and no question may name a camp, a school, or any term of art; nobody should be able to answer by recognizing a label, and if you must use a piece of jargon, define it in a single clause. Draw the situations from systems my members actually encounter — a floor-cleaning robot, a warehouse arm paid by the item, a call-centre script, a delivery van routed by a dispatcher, a programming assistant that fixes a failing test suite, a social feed, the irrigation controller I built myself — rather than from science fiction. At least three situations should be ones in which the system does exactly what the operator asked and the outcome is still bad; at least two should be ones in which the system was rewarded for a single measurable target and ends up pursuing something nobody ever specified; and one should be a case in which the operator's own instruction is the troubling part, so that the question of whose values are being encoded cannot be dodged. Phrase the response options as things a person might say about the situation — what went wrong, what would fix it, what the case proves and does not prove — rather than as degrees of agreement.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers, not individual ones, onto positions I will list, and I want these leaves to appear: the narrow view on which the whole problem is whether the machine reliably does what its operator wanted; the view on which the failure point is always the instruction itself, since what is written down never captures what was meant; the view on which systems acquire ends of their own during training that nobody wrote down anywhere; the broad view on which the real question is which values should be built in and who gets to choose them; and a deflationary view on which there is no distinct problem about machines at all, only the ordinary question of who deploys what for what purpose. If you think there is a coherent sixth position that people like mine already hold and I have not listed, add it and say why it belongs. Before the questions, sketch the underlying space in a handful of lines: what are the plain yes-or-no claims that generate these positions, which combinations of answers land on which leaf, and which combinations are left empty — and for each empty combination, say briefly whether nobody is there because the view seems incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because nobody has explored it. Then flag any pair of positions my quiz cannot really separate, and tell me what question would separate them if I had room for one more page. Finally, tell me what to say to the person who lands halfway between two of them, because most of my group will. Keep the whole deliverable, questions and key and notes together, under 1,300 words, in language a seventeen-year-old would not need a dictionary for.
