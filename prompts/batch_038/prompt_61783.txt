Write a short story of roughly 1,500 words set in a jurisdiction whose highest court resolves disputes over old treaties by consulting an instrument that reports how matters stand in worlds nearby our own — worlds qualitatively indistinguishable from ours in every respect anyone there has ever noticed. The case before the court turns on a single term in a century-old water-sharing agreement: whether the name originally fixed by pointing at the liquid in a particular valley picks out that very substance in every world the instrument displays, or only whatever clear, drinkable, lake-filling liquid occupies that role locally, so that in some displayed world the treaty's subject matter is a different compound altogether. Tell it largely through a junior advocate preparing an explanation for a client with no philosophical training, and make that explanation dramatic rather than expository: the client must be brought to see why a truth that could only have been learned by drinking, testing, and measuring might nonetheless be one that could not have failed to hold, and why this necessity does not make the discovery empty. Give full weight to the client's best objection — that the necessity is a mere byproduct of the way the name was first attached to its bearer, and that had it been attached differently, the very same world would have made the treaty come out false — and let the story's resolution show what survives that objection and what does not. Favor scene, character, and consequence over terminology, but do not let the underlying distinction blur for the sake of atmosphere.
