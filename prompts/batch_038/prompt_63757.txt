I work as a pharmacy technician in a hospital, and I have been puzzling over something in the way we handle painkillers. When a clinician orders an opioid for a patient being discharged after a minor procedure, the computer has already filled in the details: the lowest effective dose, three days' worth. Changing it takes one click, and no explanation is ever required. The committee that built the system says the default simply makes it easier for clinicians to do what they already think is right, and our written policy says that overriding it carries no penalty and is a matter for the prescriber alone. Yet every month each department receives an email reporting the share of its clinicians who altered the prefilled order, and those near the top of the list are invited to meet with the medical director.

What picture of professional judgment does this whole arrangement presuppose? If the default really only expresses what prescribers already want, what has to be true about where their genuine preferences sit relative to what they actually click? And why would a system that confident need each deviation to be visible and countable? Please reconstruct the implicit view, identify the rival view of clinical knowledge that it quietly rules out, judge whether the parts of the practice presuppose consistent things or pull in different directions, and tell me what would have to change for the practice to match the policy statement.
