ok so I've been rereading the backward induction / centipede stuff again (I know, I know) and I keep snagging on the same thing. Basically every version of the paradox I run into — pop summaries, textbook chapters, most LessWrong-adjacent discussion — quietly assumes the last mover strictly prefers to take. And when people "dissolve" the paradox they always reach for the same small toolbox: random or unknown endpoint, uncertainty about the other guy's type, bounded rationality, reputation, infinite horizon, or "actually they're FDT-ish and just cooperate." Fine, but all of those change the game rather than answer the argument.

What the case is for, so you know what counts as cheating: I want to test whether the paradox gets its force from strict inequality at the terminal node, or whether it's rooted deeper than that. So a game that just relocates the temptation somewhere else, or where the unraveling fails because of something you stipulated about the players' heads rather than about the tree, does not answer my question — it fails even if the tree is internally consistent.

Build me a centipede-shaped game where the unraveling genuinely fails for structural reasons and with no new assumptions about the players whatsoever. Specs:

- Finite, alternating, perfect information, common knowledge of everything, both players are plain expected-utility maximizers over their own dollar payoff and both know the other is, all the way down.
- Recognizably centipede-ish: passing grows the pot, and at essentially every node taking is better for the person taking than passing is. It still has to feel like a temptation problem, not accidentally collapse into a coordination game or a one-shot PD.
- But: backward induction, applied honestly, does not tell player 1 to take at the first node. That verdict has to fall out of the payoffs plus the standard rationality assumptions and nothing else.

Banned: chance moves, incomplete information, infinite horizons, reputation effects, any altruism or other-regarding utility, discounting weirdness, "well they're not really rational," and payoff tweaks that make taking simply not tempting. If the only route is to make somebody exactly indifferent at some node, that's allowed — which i suspect is the only way — but then you have to say plainly that that's what you're doing rather than dressing it up.

Deliverables:

1. The game, node by node, with real dollar figures. Six-ish nodes: enough that the structure is visible, small enough that I can check your arithmetic by hand.
2. Walk backward through it explicitly, every step, including the step where the recursion stalls and exactly why it stalls there. If there's more than one subgame-perfect outcome, give me the whole set, not a favorite.
3. State what BI recommends to player 1 and to player 2, and flag every point where the recommendation is a set rather than a single action.
4. Now break your own case. Perturb the offending payoff by one dollar in the strictness-restoring direction, rerun the induction, and tell me which node the unraveling resumes at and how far back it gets. I want to know whether a one-dollar perturbation nukes the entire result or only part of it.
5. Name the single feature doing the work, and say what happens to the case if I delete that feature and leave everything else alone.
6. Steelman the orthodox pushback — the person who says "sure, but that's a knife-edge, nobody should care about a game with exact ties, and the paradox was never about tie cases anyway," possibly with some refinement argument about trembling hands attached. Give me their best two or three points, where my case has a real answer to them, and where it honestly doesn't.

Also, separately: what does a tie-based version do to the "if player 1 passes at node 1 then common knowledge of rationality is already blown, so all your reasoning about later nodes is counterfactual mush" objection? Does my version survive that better or worse than the stock centipede? I could see it going either way and I want your actual reasoning, not a hedge.

One more thing, please take it seriously: mark clearly which parts of this are settled and which are live disputes among people who work on it. There's a chunk of backward-induction material that reads like settled textbook doctrine and absolutely is not, and I don't want you presenting one side of an ongoing argument as "the standard view" just because it's tidier. If informed people disagree about whether BI is even the right normative requirement here, say so and say roughly what the disagreement turns on. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I can't check them and you'd just be guessing.

Length: whatever it takes, but keep the tree tight enough that I can hold the whole thing in my head at once.
