I'm a third-year philosophy student, and next week I'm presenting in a seminar on seventeenth-century philosophy of mathematics. My topic is Hobbes's long quarrel with Wallis: the claimed quadrature of the circle in *De Corpore* (1655), Wallis's demolition of it in his *Elenchus* the same year, Hobbes's retraction, the *Six Lessons* of 1656, and the decades of pamphlets that followed, with the young Royal Society — which never made him a fellow, though he had friends in it — increasingly cast as the institution behind the attacks. My audience includes two mathematics graduate students and a professor who is unusually sympathetic to Hobbes, and rather than open with a slide of dates I want to open with a poem of about two minutes' read-aloud length that makes the philosophically interesting point: that the scandal of Hobbes's mathematics is not that he was wrong, but that he was wrong inside a coherent rival account of what makes geometry knowable.

So: could you write me that poem, in Hobbes's own voice as an old man who has just been refuted again and is not going to stop? It needs to carry four things accurately enough that a specialist would recognize them rather than merely nod at the name. First, his thesis that we have demonstrative knowledge only of what we ourselves make — a circle is knowable because we generate it by carrying one end of a line around the other, while we cannot know the causes of bodies we did not construct. Second, his demand that a real definition tell you how to build its object rather than merely enumerate its marks. Third, his refusal to admit indivisibles and infinite quantities into mathematics, which he took to be the thin end of the wedge for a return of scholastic obscurantism. Fourth, the political parallel: the commonwealth is an artificial body we construct ourselves, and so civil philosophy is the only other subject of which we can have a genuine science. Please do not let him concede anything he could not have known — no allusion to the irrationality or transcendence of π, no hint that the problem was later proved impossible; the failure has to be visible from within his own standards, which is what makes it tragic rather than farcical. As for form, I'd like the poem in five sections of six lines each, headed the way a proposition is headed in a geometry text (something like Given, Construction, Demonstration, Corollary, and a closing section marked with the abbreviation geometers use to say that the construction has been carried out). The sixth line of every section must be the same line with exactly one word changed, and those changed words must trace a remainder that gets smaller at each return — the sliver of the circle the constructed square fails to cover — so that by the last section the reader is waiting for the line to declare that nothing is left, and the whole argument of the poem turns on whether Hobbes lets it. No thee-and-thou pastiche and no greeting-card couplets; slant rhyme and heavy enjambment are welcome, and the tone should be proud, furious, and lucid, not that of a comic crank.

After the poem, please add a short note of around two hundred words — no citations, no bibliography, no reading list, just the lay of the land. It should separate what historians of early modern mathematics treat as settled (that the constructions were invalid, that Hobbes retracted and then produced others, that the quarrel was as much theological and institutional as mathematical) from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material: whether his hostility to indivisibles and infinite quantities had real foundational content or was mostly a symptom of technical inadequacy; whether the "we know only what we make" thesis is a general epistemology or something narrower and more local to his nominalism; and how tightly his mathematical method is actually connected to the political writings. Wherever the poem leans on the sympathetic reading, or takes a licence with the chronology or the mathematics for the sake of the form, say so plainly, and do not present that sympathetic reading as though it were the consensus view.
