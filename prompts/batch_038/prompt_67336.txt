I want an essay on the raven paradox, but on a narrower and less-discussed question than the usual one, and I want it to end in a verdict rather than a survey.

Assume your reader knows the standard setup: the hypothesis that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to the claim that all non-black things are non-ravens; Nicod's criterion holds that a generalization is confirmed by its positive instances; the equivalence condition holds that whatever confirms one of two equivalent formulations confirms the other; and the two together yield the result that a white shoe, being a non-black non-raven, confirms the hypothesis that all ravens are black. Assume your reader also knows the three familiar responses: Hempel's own dissolution, on which the appearance of paradox comes from considering a single report in isolation from the total evidence available, and the discomfort fades once the background is restored, though the shoe does confirm a little; the Bayesian quantification, on which the report confirms but by an amount so small that our sense of its irrelevance is a confusion about magnitude, driven by background knowledge of how many non-black non-ravens there are relative to ravens; and the revisionary response, on which something in the machinery must be restricted — the equivalence condition, or Nicod's criterion, or the idea that confirmation can be read off the logical form of a report.

Here is the case I want adjudicated. It is a matched pair of stipulated situations, constructed so that both routes to the generalization are completed enumerations rather than samples.

World 1. A banding programme on an island has identified the species of all 1,000 birds there: exactly 40 are ravens, and their identities are known. No bird's colour has yet been recorded. The programme then inspects each of those 40 birds in turn and finds every one of them black. During the same season, a separate survey reports, for each of the other 960 birds, that it is not black. At the end of the season the hypothesis is established — not inductively, but by complete enumeration of the relevant class.

World 2. The same island and the same 1,000 birds, with the information arriving in the opposite order. An automated reflectance survey has classified every bird as black or non-black without identifying any species: 60 are black, 940 are not. Nobody has yet seen a raven. A field team then inspects each of the 940 non-black birds and finds that not one of them is a raven. Again the hypothesis is established by complete enumeration — of the contrapositive's instances this time. Nobody in World 2 has ever looked at a raven, and yet they end up knowing exactly what the team in World 1 knows.

Note that these are stipulated to be the same island with the same birds and the same final body of evidence; they differ only in what was known when, and hence in which reports did work along the way. That is the point of the pair.

The question: what should each of the three responses say about this pair, and which of them can actually say it? I take the data to be the following two intuitions, which I want you to test rather than assume. First, the 960 reports in World 1 seem evidentially empty — the census had already settled which birds were ravens, so learning that a known non-raven is not black appears to do no work at all, even though by the equivalence condition each such report confirms the hypothesis, and there are 24 times as many of them as there are raven observations. Second, the 940 reports in World 2 seem anything but empty: before the inspections, any one of those birds might have been a non-black raven, and the survey looks like enormous, tedious, genuinely probative fieldwork. If the intuition that non-black non-ravens are irrelevant is supposed to be a datum for a theory of confirmation, this pair suggests it is not a stable datum: same logical form of report, same hypothesis, same eventual total evidence, opposite evidential weight.

Your essay should work through the pair explicitly, including the probability reasoning, though no formalism beyond what a careful paragraph can manage is required, and it should answer at least the following. Does the asymmetry vindicate the Bayesian response, by showing that what does the work is background knowledge about how the classes are distributed rather than the form of the report? Or does it show that the equivalence condition needs restricting, since in World 1 the reports really do confirm nothing? Is Hempel's dissolution a distinct thesis from the Bayesian one, or an incomplete statement of it — in particular, is relativizing confirmation to total evidence anything other than fixing the background against which likelihoods are computed? And can your favoured response describe the intermediate cases, worlds in which the species of some but not all birds is known, and predict the resulting gradient in the weight of a non-black non-raven report?

Handle the obvious objection that both worlds are cheats, on the grounds that in each the hypothesis ends up certain and nothing can confirm what is already certain: say whether the objection generalizes, or whether it can be blocked by re-describing the cases so that genuine uncertainty survives at the end. Represent the strongest version of the response you reject rather than a caricature of it.

I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a map of the literature; argue from the structure of the positions, and invoke a name only where it is genuinely load-bearing. Aim for 1,200 to 1,800 words, with your thesis stated in the first paragraph.

Finish with a single line in exactly this form:

Verdict: <label>

where <label> is one of DISSOLUTION, BAYESIAN, RESTRICTION, or NONE, meaning respectively: that the paradox is an artifact of isolating individual reports from total evidence and nothing in confirmation theory needs changing; that both criteria survive intact and the paradox is a confusion about magnitude; that one of the criteria must be restricted because there are cases in which a non-black non-raven confirms nothing; or that no resolution succeeds and the question has no determinate answer without further specification of a confirmation measure. If you think the truth is a combination, choose the label that carries the most weight and explain the combination in the body.
