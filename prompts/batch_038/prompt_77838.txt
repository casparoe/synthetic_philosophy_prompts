Next month I'm giving a talk at a professional meetup for information designers: mostly people who build executive dashboards and news graphics, two academics who work in the Cleveland-and-Tufte tradition, and no philosophers. My thesis is that the pictorial statistics produced by Neurath's Vienna museum — charts assembled from rows of identical little figures, each figure standing for a fixed quantity, never a figure enlarged to mean more — are not a decorative or childish version of a bar chart but a distinct notation with real expressive limits, and that certain claims simply cannot be stated in it: negation, conditionals, degrees of confidence, the difference between a correlation and a cause. I was pushed into defending this last night, after a two-hour argument with a friend who edits graphics for a wire service; he holds that icons are ornament, that they buy emotional force at the price of precision, and that anything an icon chart can say a number can say better. I expect most of the room is on his side.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections this audience will actually raise, in their vocabulary rather than a philosopher's, ordered by how much damage each would do; for each, a reply I can deliver in under a minute and a frank note on where that reply is softest. Tell me what I should grant in my opening minutes so that I never have to defend it later, and which single response the talk stands or falls on. End with the objection I should be hoping nobody raises.
