1. Malthus's basic claim — population tending to grow geometrically while food can only grow arithmetically, so real wages get dragged back towards subsistence unless something checks them — was true of basically every pre-industrial society. England only got out of it by luck: coal, empire, industry. None of that was in his model.
2. If a theory can't be wrong about anything, it isn't a theory. I'm pretty hardline on this.
3. Two centuries of rising living standards don't refute Malthus. He had the mechanism right and the world changed the parameters. Right mechanism, wrong era.
4. That excuse does not extend to Ehrlich. He named dates — famines in the seventies and eigthies — and he was flatly wrong. People who defend him the way I defend Malthus are special pleading.
5. The difference is that Malthus's mechanism, diminishing returns on a fixed quantity of land, is a real thing that's still operating, whereas Ehrlich was mostly extrapolting a trend line off the back of a few bad harvests.
6. And I think the birth rate falling as people got richer vindicates him: he always allowed that the check could work through people's own choices rather than starvation, and that's exactly what happened.
