It's become weirdly personal, actually. So: write me a final-exam problem on that, pitched at final-year undergraduate / first-year MA level, for a three-hour paper where students should spend about 45 minutes on it, worth 25 marks. The constraint that matters most is that our students have done von Neumann–Morgenstern from the axioms up, the Allais pairs, Savage, and one week on prospect theory, but they have never seen rank-dependent or risk-weighted utility in any form — so the stem has to hand them the machinery rather than assume it. State the rule in words and in notation: start from the utility of the worst outcome, then for each successively better outcome add the increment in utility weighted by a risk transform applied to the probability of landing on that outcome or something better, where the transform takes those cumulative "at least as good as" probabilities, is increasing, and reduces to expected utility when it's the identity. Part (c) should go diachronic. Hand them the old Samuelson story, the colleague who wouldn't take a single 50-50 bet of winning two hundred or losing a hundred but said he'd happily take a hundred of them, and ask whether an agent with a global risk attitude is rationally criticisable for that pattern, what the answer implies about whether this kind of risk aversion is required by rationality or merely permitted, and the awkward follow-up about whether an agent who refuses the first bet has changed her mind partway through a sequence in a way the theory can or can't license.
