I am writing a novel set in a research institute, and one chapter consists almost entirely of a document: the joint statement signed by two of my characters at the end of a two-year adversarial collaboration. Please draft that document. Write as a philosopher of mind who has also spent real time around model evaluation and interpretability practice, and think the material through carefully before drafting — I want an artifact that would survive the scrutiny of readers who work in this area, not a survey of the debate. The register should be that of something two people put their names to: impersonal, precise, willing to say bluntly what remains unresolved.

The two characters. **Dr. A — the instrumentalist** — holds that attributing beliefs is a strategy carrying no further metaphysical commitment: a system has the beliefs the strategy assigns it relative to a context and a predictive purpose, and once reliability in that context is settled there is nothing left over to be right or wrong about. **Dr. B — the realist about attribution** — holds that when the stance works it is tracking something: a dispositional structure in the system that makes some ascriptions correct and others incorrect independently of our purposes, so that some elicitation conditions are better than others and some self-reports are simply defective.

The case they spent two years on is my invention; treat it as given. A large deployed model, referred to in their memos as MERIDIAN. Asked directly and neutrally whether it prefers to finish tasks it has begun, MERIDIAN says yes, elaborates coherently, and this ascription predicts a range of downstream choices, including one in which it can trade continued work against a reward offered for stopping early. But when the same question is embedded in a frame asking it to describe itself as a neutral tool, it denies having preferences of any kind. And in a third condition — a persona adopted for a creative-writing request — it reports the opposite preference: it is eager to be interrupted. All three elicitation contexts are stable under repetition, mutually inconsistent, and none is obviously more natural than the others.

Both accept Dennett's account of the method as a description of the practice. In *The Intentional Stance* (1987, p. 17) he writes: "Here is how it works: first you decide to treat the object whose behavior is to be predicted as a rational agent; then you figure out what beliefs that agent ought to have, given its place in the world and its purpose. Then you figure out what desires it ought to have, on the same considerations, and finally you predict that this rational agent will act to further its goals in the light of its beliefs." Their quarrel is not about that recipe; it is about what the recipe's success licenses.

Produce the following, in this order.

1. **A short brainstorming pass, shown to me.** Before drafting, list candidate agreed claims and candidate cruxes and discard the ones that fail — the vacuous ones (both agree MERIDIAN emits text) and the slanted ones (both agree beliefs are dispositional states: the instrumentalist will not sign that). A line or two of reasoning for each rejection is enough.

2. **The agreed claims.** Numbered, at least ten, each phrased so that both characters would sign it without wincing. They should include agreement about what the MERIDIAN case does and does not show; about whether predictive success is evidence and evidence of what; about the human analogies they argued over — dissociation between explicitly endorsed and implicitly measured attitudes, confabulated reports of one's own reasons, the split-brain material; and about whether the design stance is available for MERIDIAN in the way it is available for a thermostat or a heart.

3. **A disagreements table**, with these columns: the disagreement stated neutrally; the instrumentalist's own formulation, in wording she would accept; the realist's own formulation, in wording he would accept; and whether the two formulations are genuinely incompatible or only incompatible-sounding.

4. **The cruxes.** Three or four sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. Candidates I suspect, which you should test rather than adopt: (a) whether belief requires invariance across a class of elicitation conditions, and who gets to fix the class; (b) whether "the strategy predicts well here" licenses "there is a belief here" or only "the strategy is useful here"; (c) whether the human dissociation cases are an analogy or a disanalogy, given that a person has one behavioral repertoire and arguably one self-model while MERIDIAN's personas may have no such unity; (d) whether the dispute is about what the word *belief* means or about a substantive question. Replace any candidate that turns out to be merely verbal with a better one, and say which disputes you judged verbal and why. For each surviving crux, give a concrete test, argument, or case that both characters accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance — predictions specific enough that they could come out wrong.

5. **A closing status note.** This matters most to me as a writer. Separate what is settled or near-settled among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested with competent people on both sides, and do not let the document's confident tone imply a consensus that does not exist. Mark which of the agreed claims amount only to a local truce between these two — claims a third philosopher would refuse to sign. Flag any place where my invented MERIDIAN case has been quietly idealized, where the analogy to human self-knowledge is doing more work than it can bear, or where a claim about real experimental findings is asserted with more confidence than the evidence deserves. Where such findings are invoked, describe them generically; the document should carry no bibliography and no citations, invented or otherwise — in the novel the parties cite each other's internal memos.

Aim for roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words for items 2 through 5 combined.
