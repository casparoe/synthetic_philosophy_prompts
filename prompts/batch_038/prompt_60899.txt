ok so I need help turning a 2am argument into an actual proposal. I'm applying for my department's summer research grant — ten weeks, small money, and the committee has one person on it who thinks anything with a complexity class in it isnt philosophy.

Here's the thing me and my friend were fighting about. There's this increasingly popular move where you take an "ought implies can" style principle and make it computational. You can't be rationally required to believe all the consequences of what you believe if deriving them is intractable. You can't be required to maximize expected utility if finding the optimum is NP-hard. You can't blame an electorate for failing to detect a manipulation of a voting rule if detecting it is NP-hard. Complexity gets asked to do the work that "feasibility" or "reasonable effort" used to do, and it looks rigorous, which is part of why it's spreading.

My friend does complexity theory and says the whole move is a catgory error. Hardness results are worst-case statements. NP-hardness tells you nothing about the instances an actual agent faces — SAT solvers chew through enormous industrial instances all the time. So "it's NP-hard, therefore you're excused" is on the level of "it's cold somewhere, therefore bring a coat." I pushed back that average-case hardness is the fix: Levin-style distributional problems, smoothed analysis for stuff like the simplex method, Impagliazzo's five worlds, where Heuristica is precisely the world in which worst-case-hard problems are easy on the distributions that actually occur. And he said: fine, but now your excuse is indexed to a distribution, and where did the distribution come from? Pick a different one and the normative verdict flips, so you've traded a false principle for an unfounded one. That's where we got stuck and honestly its a good point.

So write the proposal. I want the question stated precisely enough that it could come out either way, and if you can frame it as: is the distribution-sensitivity fatal, or does it turn the principle into something interestingly context-dependent — I have a hunch this rhymes with the pragmatic-encroachment arguments in epistemology, where whether you know depends on what's at stake, but I don't want to just gesture at that, I want it earned or cut. Then: why the existing positions on both sides leave the question open rather than settling it, what I would actually do week by week, what a result would look like, and what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that it fails because there's no general answer, only a mess of case-by-case verdicts. Use the voting-manipulation debate as the running example since that's where the back-and-forth is most developed, and then say what would have to be true for the conclusion to carry over to something like intractable moral calculations.

End with a short honest note on what's too ambitious for ten weeks, because I always overscope and the committee always notices.
