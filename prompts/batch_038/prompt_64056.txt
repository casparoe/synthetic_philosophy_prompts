My flatmate Anya is halfway through a psychology PhD and we end up having the same argument roughly every fortnight. She isn't a general sceptic about people's own minds, she's just got very specific views about which first-person reports she'll accept at face value and which she won't. I've been keeping a list of her verdicts, because I think there's a rule under there and I want to catch her out:

- Someone says they've got a pain in their left knee. Anya: completely trustworthy, it makes no sense to ask them how they know.
- Someone's shown two faces, picks the more attractive one, gets handed back the other one by sleight of hand, and then explains at length why they picked it. Anya: the explanation is worthless, and so is their claim about which one they chose. (She is always going on about that one.)
- Someone says the room in front of them looked richly detailed all the way to the edges of their vision. Anya: no, they're wildly overestimating, don't believe it.
- Someone says they weren't thinking about anything at all during a twenty minute run. Anya: untrustworthy, there was plenty going on that they simply didn't notice.
- Someone says they felt an itch on their shoulder blade and scratched it. Anya: trustworthy.
- Someone says they knew at the time that they were furious with their brother. Anya: frequently false, people work out hours later that they were furious.
- Someone says the image in their mind's eye was a red door with a brass handle. Anya: untrustworthy, that's exactly the sort of case where imagining and seeing get muddled up.
- Someone says they reached for the blue mug because it was nearest. Anya: worthless, same as the faces.
- Someone says a sudden loud bang frightened them. Anya: trustworthy, obviously.
- Someone says they were in a perfectly calm, contented mood all afternoon. Anya: that one's harder, she says sometimes its fine and sometimes the calm was really suppressed anxiety and they should have spotted it.

My own theory is that she trusts anything about the body and nothing about the mind, but then the loud bang doesn't fit, and neither really does the calm mood answer, since she won't commit.

Here's the case I want to put to her. My cousin had a bad panic attack last month and afterwards said two things: first, that she hadn't felt anything whatsoever in her body during it, she was completely numb; and second, that she had been terrified the whole way through. I want to know what Anya will do with those. Which of the two will she take at face value, and will she treat them differently? Then, more importantly: can you work out what rule she's actually running? I suspect more than one rule generates all ten verdicts above, so if that's right, lay out the candidates, say what each of them gives you on my cousin's two claims, tell me which one you'd bet on and why, and name the single case I should bring her next week that would tell them apart. Bonus points if you think one of her ten verdicts is already in tension with the rest.
