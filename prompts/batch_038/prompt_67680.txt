I am preparing a graduate seminar session on the badness of pain and want a scored assessment rather than a survey. Below are eight candidate accounts of what makes pain bad for the person who has it, followed by the three data points I want them run against. Please treat my descriptions as complete and self-contained: I am not asking for references or a reading list, and if you attach philosopher-names to any of the views, do so only where you are confident of the attribution.

**The accounts**

1. **Intrinsic phenomenal badness.** Unpleasantness is a non-representational intrinsic property of the experience — what is sometimes called the mental paint view. Pain is bad because it feels that way, and nothing else does the normative work.

2. **Desire/attitude reduction.** The sensory component is normatively neutral. Pain's unpleasantness consists in the subject's occurrent intrinsic desire that the sensation cease, or in a displeasure-attitude directed at it. Pain is bad insofar as, and because, it is unwanted.

3. **Evaluativism.** The experience represents some bodily condition as bad for the subject, and its unpleasantness just is that evaluation. Pain is bad because it is a usually veridical presentation of bodily badness, not because it is intrinsically aversive.

4. **Imperativism.** The experience has imperative content — roughly: protect this part, stop doing that — and its unpleasantness consists in the experience's commanding. Pain is bad insofar as it issues demands that go unsatisfied while the damage persists.

5. **Awareness/higher-order accounts.** A pain is bad only insofar as the subject is aware of it as pain, or stands in a higher-order representational relation to it. Unnoticed, distracted-from, or dissociated pains are not bad at all, or are bad to a reduced degree.

6. **Two-component pluralism.** Pain sensation (location, intensity, quality) and pain affect (unpleasantness, suffering) are separable, and each can be a locus of badness in its own right. The dissociation syndromes are the central evidence for the split.

7. **Functional/teleological.** Pain is bad because it is the state whose biological role is to signal harm and compel protective action. Badness tracks functional role, so a state playing that role is bad even when it does not feel bad, and a state that feels bad without playing the role may not be bad in the relevant sense.

8. **Agency-disruption.** What is bad about pain is not its feel but what it does to the person: it monopolizes attention, narrows the space of available action, and degrades the capacity to pursue ends. Felt quality matters only instrumentally, as the mechanism by which the disruption occurs.

**The data**

(a) The classic asymbolia presentation: after bilateral damage to the insula and its connections with limbic structures, nociceptive stimulation is still correctly localized and graded for intensity and quality, and patients describe the stimulation accurately as sharp, deep, or burning. But they report that it does not bother them; they may laugh when pinched, fail to withdraw, and accumulate burns, pressure sores, and joint damage.

(b) The mirror case from cingulotomy: patients report that the pain is still present and unchanged in location and intensity, but no longer troubles them. They describe their lives as improved and are willing to undergo the procedure again.

(c) A case I have constructed for the seminar by extrapolation, so please treat it as a thought experiment rather than as a reported patient. Two years after the onset described in (a), the patient — call him M.K. — begins to report a new distress. It is not localized anywhere in the body, is not triggered by nociceptive stimulation, and does not feel like any pain he has had. It is triggered by seeing his own injuries and by thinking about the accumulation of damage to his limbs. He calls it dread, says it is worse than anything he felt before his lesion, and now protects and favors the damaged limbs reliably — but on account of the dread, not on account of the sensations, which he continues to describe as unbothersome.

**What I want**

1. Score each of the eight accounts from 0 to 10 on two dimensions: (i) fit with data (a)–(c), and (ii) normative adequacy, meaning its ability to explain why pain is a paradigm of harm, why it is rational to avoid it prospectively, and why it is bad for the subject even when the subject is not attending to it. Give both numbers for each account, with two or three sentences of justification, and be willing to separate accounts sharply rather than clustering everything near the middle.

2. Rank the accounts and group them into tiers — for instance, viable, strained, defeated by the data — stating explicitly where you place the cut-offs and why.

3. Adjudicate case (c). Does M.K.'s dread vindicate evaluativism, on the grounds that the evaluation of bodily badness survives the loss of unpleasant sensation and simply detaches from nociception? Or is the dread a distinct bad that no account built on pain can cover? For each of your top three accounts, say what it is committed to calling the dread: a pain, a second-order evaluation, an unsatisfied imperative, a desire, a disruption of agency, or something outside the theory entirely — and say which of those options costs the account the most.

4. Give me one number. Under your top-ranked account, what share of the total badness of a severe, sustained pain — express it between 0 and 1 — is carried by the sensory-discriminative component as opposed to the affective one? State whether that number is an empirical estimate, a conceptual consequence of the view, or a stipulation, and identify what kind of evidence would move it.

5. Close with two short paragraphs: first, the single strongest datum against your top-ranked account, stated as its proponents would want it stated; second, what the asymbolia evidence cannot settle no matter how many such cases accumulate — that is, where the dissociation may be a redescription of how we already carve up the concept of pain rather than a discovery about pain itself.

I would like the result to read as an argument with numbers attached, not as a review of the positions. Where you think a distinction in my list of accounts is bogus or two accounts collapse into one, say so and adjust the scoring accordingly rather than silently going along with the taxonomy.
