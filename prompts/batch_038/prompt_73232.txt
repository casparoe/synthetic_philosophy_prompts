For about seven years I have written a free fortnightly letter recommending one film and one novel to a few hundred subscribers. I have no training in philosophy — I left school at sixteen and have read unevenly ever since — but the letter has forced me to work out, slowly and in my own words, what I take myself to be doing when I tell people that something is worth their evening. Below are the commitments I have arrived at. I would like to know whether they hang together, because I cannot tell. Some of them feel obviously compatible to me, and I may be wrong about that too.

1. A judgment about whether a work is good has to come out of your own experience of it. Being told that something is beautiful is not a route to thinking it beautiful; it is at most a reason to spend two hours. When a friend says "trust me, it's a masterpiece," what I have received is an invitation, not a verdict I can take over.

2. All the same, I use aggregate scores constantly — the percentage on the review sites, the mean of the star ratings — and I do not regard this as mere scheduling. When a film sits at ninety-six per cent I form the belief, before I have seen a frame of it, that it is very good. I think that belief is often true and often reasonable for me to hold.

3. When I say a film is beautiful I am not stating something about the film that would be so whether or not it ever landed on anyone. I am reporting and endorsing my own response. This is why I never argue with a person who disliked what I loved: there is nothing there for them to have got wrong.

4. I nevertheless think some people simply have better taste than others, and that this is a fact about them rather than a fact about how well they happen to match me. It is why I read four or five critics and not just whoever is nearest to hand. I chose those four or five by track record: over the years their verdicts have agreed with my own reactions far more often than other people's have.

5. Twice now a critic has written at length about a film I had dismissed, pointing at things in it that I had not registered — the sound dropping out for a whole scene, a shot held well past comfort — and on a second viewing I found the film better than I had. I take my first opinion to have been mistaken, not merely different.

6. I do not think criteria could be written down that would settle whether a work is good. If they could, we would simply apply them and the experience would not matter, which is item 1 again.

7. I distrust professional critics as a group. They are courted and flattered, they get early access they would forfeit by being brutal, they review what the studios pay to screen for them, and they write for outlets carrying the advertising of the same studios. I assume a good deal of published praise is softened. This is part of why I prefer the aggregate to the individual review: a single critic can be bought or charmed, but a hundred of them averaged together is another matter.

8. I regularly tell friends they will love a film I have not seen myself — same director, same deliberate pace, same kind of ending — and I think that is legitimate and usually accurate.

Please go through these and tell me which of them genuinely pull against one another, which only look as though they do, and what the cheapest repair is in each case. Specifically:

- Open with a short bottom line: is the package tenable as it stands, and if not, which single change would do the most work.
- Then take the tensions in order of severity, worst first. For each, name the items involved, say why they cannot both stand (or why they can, if the conflict is only apparent), say what has to give, and say what giving it costs me — including in the letter, since that is where the practical stakes are. Do not tell me to drop something without telling me what I lose by dropping it.
- Be explicit about which conflicts are only apparent, so that I do not spend the next year repairing things that were never broken. I would rather have three real problems and a clear account of five non-problems than eight undifferentiated flags.
- Do not assume I will sacrifice any particular item. I have not decided, and I want the audit before the decision.

I would like the audit in prose, and then a table with exactly these columns, one row per pairing you have assessed, rows ordered by severity: Rank | Items in tension | Genuine or apparent | Severity (high / medium / low) | Why they pull apart, or why they do not | What has to give | What that costs | How sure you are of this particular call.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the audit as a whole, and single out the one point in it that you are least sure of — the place where someone who knew this subject properly would most likely tell me you had got it wrong.
