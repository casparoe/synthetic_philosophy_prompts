I am a philosophy PhD student serving as the ethics member of a teaching hospital's conscientious-objection panel. We receive two or three requests a month from clinicians to be excused from participation in something — assisted dying, enrolling patients in a trial, resuscitating a patient they judge to be beyond benefit — and we settle them by intuition, which the chair herself admits is arbitrary. Write us a decision procedure that clinicians, a hospital lawyer, and an administrator can run in a single ninety-minute sitting without having read any moral philosophy, built on Williams's integrity objection: ground projects, the doctrine of negative responsibility, and the claim that requiring an agent to justify their deepest commitments in impersonal terms is itself a kind of damage — held against the fact that Williams gives an objector no entitlement to transfer costs onto patients or colleagues. The panel is forbidden from ruling on whether the objector's moral view is correct, so the procedure has to do its discriminating work elsewhere.

Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a refusal, where it terminates in an accommodation, and where it must stop and record a value judgment it cannot discharge as a matter of fact — for instance, whether a commitment is identity-constituting or merely strongly held. Flag the steps that look like tests but are judgment in disguise. Address head-on the tension I cannot resolve: our intake form requires a written statement of reasons, which on Williams's telling may be the offending demand itself. Tell me how much of the objection survives that step and what the panel must not ask.

Then run the procedure on three requests: an anaesthetist seeking exemption from assisted dying; a research nurse refusing to enroll patients in a placebo-controlled trial for a condition with an effective treatment; and a senior cardiologist who will not authorize resuscitation of patients he calls beyond benefit, a stance formed over forty years at the bedside. On the third, give the answer the framework actually yields even if it is not the answer I want, and explain why it comes from Williams rather than from my own view of futility. Close with what the procedure cannot do: the requests it will systematically misclassify, and what it looks like when a panel uses it to dress up a decision it had already made.
