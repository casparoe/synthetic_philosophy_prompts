I have no academic background in any of this. For about fifteen years I have worked as an intake coordinator at a community mental health clinic, which puts me at the exact point where the words people arrive with meet the forms that get filed, and I have spent a long time trying to describe a pattern I keep running into and cannot find a name for.

The pattern, stripped down, is a circuit that runs through a measuring instrument. A diagnostic category comes with a screening questionnaire, and the questionnaire has a vocabulary. That vocabulary escapes the clinic. It turns up in school newsletters, workplace wellbeing material, magazine quizzes, and eventually in the mouths of the people sitting across from me, who describe themselves in phrases lifted almost word for word from the instrument's own items. Scores on the screener rise. Nobody is exaggerating and nobody is being coached; the answers are honest answers to the questions asked. Clinicians and administrators then read the rising scores as evidence that the condition is more common, or broader in its expression, than the official criteria suppose, and they cite those scores when they argue for widening the criteria or lowering an eligibility threshold. Once the category is wider, more people qualify, more material is written for them, and more of the vocabulary escapes. Around it goes again.

Two things I have seen make it concrete for me. First, an adult attention-difficulty screener: within a few years the specific phrasing of its items had become ordinary conversational English in our area, the mean intake score had moved substantially, and the referral sources and the population we served had not changed at all. Second, and in the other direction, a heavily stigmatised diagnosis from an earlier decade: once its name became widely known and instantly recognisable, people stopped volunteering it and started describing the same difficulties in different language, and our recorded rate fell while the caseload in front of me looked, to my eye, very much the same. That second case is part of why I don't think this is simply about labels being attractive.

When I try to explain it, I get told it is a self-fulfilling prophecy, or it is medicalisation. Neither seems right. A self-fulfilling prophecy needs somebody to hold a belief that is false and then make it true; here the statistics genuinely change, and the false step is the inference drawn from them — true numbers, wrong conclusion. Medicalisation is about a profession extending its authority over conduct that used to be handled elsewhere; here nobody is extending anything, and the professionals are being moved by their own instrument.

So: does this have a name, and if so what is it? If there are several near neighbours in the literature on classification, lay out how each differs from what I have described and which one fits best, and tell me plainly if I am running two separate mechanisms together and calling them one. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and give me its boundary conditions — what would have to be true for the circuit not to close, in categories that are not self-reported, or whose vocabulary never escapes the clinic, or that people actively avoid. And is my second case the same mechanism running backwards, or a different one that I have mistaken for its mirror image?

I am not after a label so much as the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges sit. Please be explicit about which parts of your answer are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested. My impression is that the general claim — that a classification changes the people it classifies — is not seriously disputed, but that whether it corrupts prevalence evidence in the way I have described, and whether anything can be done about it, is argued about bitterly. If you find one side of that dispute more convincing, say so, but do not let it come through as the consensus if it is not. Finally, if naming a particular thinker helps me locate an idea, do so, but please do not hand me a reading list; I cannot chase references and I am not asking for any.
