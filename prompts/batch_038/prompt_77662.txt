I sit in a decision-theory reading group. Whenever Parfit's hitchhiker comes up — a driver finds you stranded in the desert and offers a lift only to someone who can sincerely promise to withdraw a fixed sum from an ATM in town and hand it over, and who is extremely good at detecting whether such a promise is sincere — the member who leans causal cuts the discussion short with the proverb "don't cry over spilt milk." What he means, roughly: by the time you are in town the driver's judgment is behind you, nothing you do now reaches back to it, and paying does you no good going forward, so the rational act is to keep the money.

He uses the same sentence in two other places. On Newcomb-style cases: what is in the box was settled before you walked in. And when someone suggests that you ought to have acquired the disposition to keep such promises earlier in life: too late for that too, that milk is spilt as well.

Please take the sentence apart rather than defend a decision theory. List the distinct precise theses it could be expressing in these uses — a claim about what can be causally influenced, a claim about the point in time from which an act is to be assessed, a claim about the irrelevance of good or bad news, a claim about what regret licenses, and whatever else is genuinely in there — and for each say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it on its own carries the conclusion "do not pay once you are in town." Then say whether the three deployments amount to one thesis or to three, and which of them survives the rejoinder the evidentially inclined member makes: that what lies behind you in this case is not an ordinary settled fact about the world but a settled fact about you, produced by the very reasoning you are now engaged in. I want a menu with verdicts, ordered from the readings that carry no argumentative weight to the ones that carry all of it.
