I am drafting the specification section of a paper on what an adequate theory of confirmation must deliver, using the ravens case as the running test. Before I commit to a view of my own, I want the requirements list written out in full, because the literature tends to presuppose one rather than state it.

Fix the purpose first, since adequacy is relative to it. The account I am after must be usable by someone designing an automated evidence-triage system over a large observational database — a system that has to decide which observation to collect next. That imposes three things on any candidate account: its verdicts must be reproducible from an explicit evidence record rather than from the intuitions of whoever applies it; its magnitudes must be comparable across different hypotheses; and its verdicts must be stable when the same data are reported in different but equally accurate ways.

Write the desiderata. For each one, give the condition as precisely as you can state it; the case or consideration that motivates it — a concrete case wherever one exists, not a slogan; and your judgment as to whether it is non-negotiable or is instead a demand pressed by one camp in particular, and in the latter case which camp and what that camp gains by insisting on it.

At minimum, cover: the demand that positive instances confirm and negative instances disconfirm; whatever survives of the idea that logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by the same evidence; conditions linking confirmation to logical consequence, in both directions; the requirement that a consistent body of evidence not confirm two incompatible hypotheses; the treatment of statistical and existential hypotheses rather than universal conditionals alone; the standing of background knowledge and of the total evidence actually in hand; the question of what happens when the subject class is empty or when the population is finite and can in principle be exhausted; whether the list must accommodate cases in which, relative to certain background assumptions, a non-black non-raven disconfirms rather than confirms the generalization, and if so whether that is a datum to be explained or an artifact of a badly chosen background; and the requirement that the account explain the appearance of paradox rather than merely announce that it dissolves.

Then take up three conditions of my own, and treat them exactly as you treat the received ones — say whether each is genuine, which received condition it collides with, and what a theory surrenders in order to satisfy it.

First, auditability. Two analysts handed the same written evidence record and the same explicitly stated background assumptions must reach the same confirmation verdict. Nothing may turn on the theorist's unstated sense of which predicates are natural, which descriptions are salient, or which alternatives happen to feel live. Pressure case: one and the same observation recorded once as "this shoe is neither a raven nor black" and once as "this shoe is white leather."

Second, boundedness. Confirmation drawn from an unboundedly available and cheaply repeatable class of observations must not be accumulable, even in principle, into evidence as strong as confirmation drawn from a bounded class. A million white shoes must not outweigh a single black raven, however the arithmetic of small increments runs.

Third, calibration. Where the account assigns degrees, there must be a fact of the matter, in the long run, about whether assignments of a given magnitude are borne out: across many independent applications, hypotheses that received a large boost should come out true proportionally more often than hypotheses that received a small one. An account whose magnitudes could not in principle be checked by any such procedure fails this condition.

Finally, and this is the part I most want: identify every subset of the resulting list that you believe cannot be jointly satisfied. For each, say which condition has to go, what breaks in the account once it goes, and whether the collision is forced by the shape of the conditions themselves or is an artifact of my particular formulation of the three additions — if you take the collision to be one the literature already treats as standard, register that in a clause and move on. Close with a single sentence naming the one condition that, in your judgment, excludes the largest number of candidate accounts, and why it does more work than any of the others.

Keep the weight on the conditions and their motivating cases. Where you indicate briefly how the main families of proposal fare against the list, allow yourself no more than a sentence per family; I will assemble the scorecard myself. No bibliography and no citations — argue from the conditions, not from authorities.
