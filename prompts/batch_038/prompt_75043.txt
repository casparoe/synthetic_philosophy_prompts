I work as an assistant cataloguer in a university collection of printing history. Our curator, whom I will call Dr. Vos, has been on leave since the summer; her rulings on what does and does not count as a letterform have governed our catalogue for twenty years, and every entry we write either follows them or has to justify itself against them. A decision has come up that I cannot defer until she returns, and I want to work out both what she would say and what we ought to say to be consistent with ourselves.

First, her framework, because it constrains how the verdicts are to be read. She will not let anyone in the department use the word "shape" without specifying which of three things they mean: what a mark looks like, what it descends from, and what job it is doing for a reader at a particular time. She also insists there is a difference between *reading* a letter and *seeing* one, and she will not discuss a case until she has settled which of the two is in play.

Her verdicts, in the order the cases arose:

1. A single-story *a* from a geometric sans-serif and a two-story *a* from a book roman: both *a*, same letter, no qualification. "Different clothes, same body."
2. A Textualis *a* on a fifteenth-century leaf: an *a*. Asked whether it is an *a* for readers who were never taught blackletter, she said the entry should record that it is a letter for anyone who has been taught the script and a shape for everyone else, and that this is no defect in the letter.
3. The long *s* on an eighteenth-century page: an *s*, without qualification, even though almost every visitor to the gallery takes it for an *f*. "The letter is what the page was doing, not what we now do with it."
4. A texture produced by an image model in which three of our staff separately insist they can see a two-story *a*: not a letter at all. "They are seeing an *a* the way you see a rabbit in frost on a windowpane."
5. A corporate logotype whose designer volunteered a long account of how it is a stylised *a* — he began with a real *a* and removed strokes until it became a triangle with a nick in it: not an *a*; an emblem. She declined to discuss what he began with or what he meant.
6. A stencilled *a* whose bowl is separated from the stem by two bridges: an *a*.
7. A proof sheet on which a compositor has set a lowercase *b* in mirror image, a known press error: she catalogues it as a *d* in the wrong place, not as a damaged *b*. Her reason was that a reader meets a *d* on that line. Asked about the same operation performed on an *a*, she said that a *b* turned over is a *d* because the alphabet contains its own reflection there, whereas an *a* turned over is no letter at all.
8. A child's badly formed *a* in a school exercise book, identifiable only from the rest of the word: an *a*, "but on the strength of its neighbours."
9. A crude *a* cut into the plaster of a medieval stair wall, outside any text: an *a* — because whoever cut it was cutting an *a*, and because the form had already been carried to that wall by other hands.

The new case. Last spring a small metal type foundry closed and left us its remaining material. Among it is a revival face the foundry cut in the 1970s from an eighteenth-century model, in which the lowercase *s* is the long *s* throughout, with a separate and quite distinct *f*. The foundry's own stock books list the sort under *s*. There is also a promotional broadside the foundry printed to show the face off, set in ordinary modern English for whoever picked it up, in which the long *s* and the *f* occur in the same line. The designer's blurb on the reverse assures the reader that the archaism stops being noticed within a page. When we put the sorts in a tray in front of our reading-room volunteers, they could sort the two kinds into two piles without hesitation but could not tell us which pile was the *s* and which the *f*; when we put the broadside in front of them, they read every long *s* as an *f* and misread the words accordingly.

Our index is by letter, because students search it by letter, and I have to decide under which letter this sort and this broadside are filed: *s*, *f*, or something hedged.

What I would like from you:

(a) Set out the candidate rules that her nine verdicts leave open. State each so that it comes out right on all nine, and mark explicitly anywhere you had to stretch one to fit. Some of the verdicts kill single-factor answers outright; say which factors they kill and why.

(b) Say which of the surviving rules diverge on the foundry's long *s*, and in which direction. Where rules agree, set them aside rather than padding the answer.

(c) Give me your best guess at her verdict, and the reason for preferring that rule over its rivals. In particular: verdict 3 and verdict 7 both sound like the same kind of principle and yet they pull in opposite directions here, and I want to know which of them you think is doing the real work in her practice.

(d) Tell me which further case would settle it — one we could stage in the reading room rather than acquire, since we have no acquisition budget. Describe it precisely enough that I could run it, and say what each candidate rule predicts about it.

(e) Whatever she would say, tell me what we should say. Identify the closest of the nine precedents, and state plainly what principle the collection commits itself to by filing the sort under *s* and what it commits itself to by filing it under *f* — including what each choice would imply for the other items already in the index, since we hold more than one archaic form.

Please argue from the cases as I have described them. I am not looking for references, a reading list, or a survey of the literature on letterforms; I want the reasoning worked through on this material.
