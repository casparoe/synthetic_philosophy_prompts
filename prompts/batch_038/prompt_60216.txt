Our political theory reading group is run by a postdoc who has this one line he falls back on constantly: "there's no such thing as clean hands in politics." What's finally bugging me is that he deploys it in two opposite directions in the same evening. Someone asked whether a council member was actually wrong to promise a rezoning vote he already knew was dead in order to get a shelter funding package through, and he said don't be naive, that's just the cost of the job, there's nothing to feel bad about. Twenty minutes later, when someone else pushed on the slogan, he said an official who does that kind of thing and isn't bothered by it is the real problem — the feeling bad is the whole point. I'm fairly confident both of those can't come out of one sentence, but I can't say why.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making, ordered roughly from the platitudinous to the radical — stuff about foreseen side effects and compromise, claims about role-specific permissions, claims about the office rather than the person, whatever would be needed for an act to be both the thing you ought to do and still wrong, all the way to the amoralist reading where moral talk is just decoration? For each one, say whether its trivially true, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and then tell me which reading he'd need for each of his two uses and whether any single reading can serve both.

Two extra things. He insists the entire dirty hands puzzle evaporates if you define 'wrong' as 'what you have most reason, all things considered, not to do' — does it evaporate, or has he just relabeled the question, and what would survive the relabeling (residual duties, apology, compensation, self-punishment)? And I've read the Walzer paper and a chunk of the responses, so don't spend space on the basics — I want the menu of readings, not a summary of the debate. Flag clearly which bits are settled and which are genuinely live disagreements among people who work on this stuff; if his deflationary move is a minority position, say so rather than letting it stand as the default.
