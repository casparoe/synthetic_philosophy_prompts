I'm a second-year philosophy student, and for the creative option in my social epistemology seminar I would like to attempt a short story rather than a paper. Could you write one of roughly 1,200–1,800 words, in British English, on the dispute between conciliationism and steadfastness about peer disagreement?

To keep it from collapsing into a generic parable, please build it around the following apparatus. A public-health consortium's charter obliges two jointly responsible modellers, once they discover that they disagree, to publish the arithmetic mean of their credences rather than to argue; a symmetry auditor is employed to certify that the pair are genuine epistemic peers before the averaging is applied. I would like the story to turn on two complications I find pressing. First, averaging credences is not the same as averaging decisions, so the published midpoint may license an intervention that neither modeller would have endorsed alone. Second, the auditors disagree among themselves about whether the pair really are peers, so the rule must somehow be applied — or withheld — to its own criterion. Please let the ending remain genuinely unresolved rather than vindicating either side.

After the story, add a brief note identifying where you have taken liberties with the debate or leaned on a contested reading of it — for instance, if you have made one position look considerably weaker than its defenders would accept. And if you think my brief is itself confused — if the apparatus described would not in fact generate the tension I claim, or if a story is the wrong vehicle for it — please tell me so plainly rather than writing something agreeable.
