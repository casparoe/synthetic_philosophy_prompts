I am chairing this year's run of our department's graduate colloquium series, in which each session is a structured interview with a visiting speaker followed by open-floor questions. Next month's guest is a philosopher of science who defends a revisionist reconstruction of inference to the best explanation, and I want the questions to engage her version of the view on its own terms rather than circling the familiar realism-versus-anti-realism territory. Her position has three linked commitments. First, she holds that explanation is essentially contrastive — that a hypothesis is explanatory only relative to a question of the form "why P rather than Q" — so the standard formulation of the rule ("believe the hypothesis that best explains the evidence") is malformed, because "best" has no determinate sense until the foil is fixed. Second, she holds that the bad-lot objection, which charges that we have no reason to think the true hypothesis is even among the candidates we happen to generate, misfires against her view because it presupposes that the candidate set is given in advance; on her account the rule is not a procedure for ranking a fixed hypothesis space at a time but a procedure for growing and pruning that space over time. Third, and consequently, she argues that the charge of diachronic incoherence — that any non-Bayesian updating rule exposes an agent to a foreseeable sure-loss sequence of bets — is likewise beside the point, since her rule does not compete with conditionalization as an updating rule; where she thinks it makes a measurable difference, she points to computational simulations suggesting that explanation-favoring rules can outperform strictly Bayesian ones on average accuracy in the long run.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for the session, which runs about forty-five minutes before questions from the floor. The audience is mixed: several faculty are committed Bayesians who will be listening for concessions, and a fair number of first-years who have met the view only through the standard textbook presentation, so each question should carry enough of the setup to be intelligible without a prior lecture. I would like the sequence to be ordered so that the pressure increases, and I need at least one question on each of the following, plus one closing question about what result or line of evidence would count against her reconstruction as a whole:

- The foil-relativity worry: if "best explanation" is indexed to a contrast, then two investigators with the same evidence but different foils can be licensed to believe different things, which seems to make the support relation interest-relative. I want the question to force a choice between saying one of them is mistaken and saying the evidential relation genuinely differs, and then to press the case where the divergence is systematic rather than incidental — an entire research tradition organized around a different foil set from its rivals.
- The relocation worry: if the rule governs hypothesis generation rather than hypothesis ranking, the bad-lot pessimism does not disappear so much as move. The question should ask why a generative procedure tuned to explanatory virtues should be expected to yield candidates that track truth, rather than candidates we find readily conceivable or satisfying, and should ask what would distinguish her procedure from a merely psychological story about how scientists happen to think.
- The defanging worry: if the rule no longer competes with conditionalization inside a given space, it is not obvious what work is left for it to do. The question should anticipate her likely retreat to the claim that it determines priors or determines which hypotheses get entertained at all, and the follow-up should press the standard response that any prior differences are swamped as evidence accumulates, leaving the rule without consequences in the limit where accuracy is assessed.
- The falsity worry: in a good deal of physics the best available explanation is known by its users to be false or systematically idealized. What does her rule license an agent to believe in such a case, and does the answer differ depending on whether the idealization is one the agent could in principle remove?
- The normative force of the simulations: whether a result of that kind can bear on a normative question at all, given that it depends on a choice of scoring rule, a choice of which worlds are simulated, and an assumption about the agent's starting point. Ask her what the simulation shows that a purely armchair argument could not.

For every question, add a single follow-up sentence keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. She has a well-known habit of replying to objections by narrowing her claim — conceding that some phenomenon is outside the rule's remit, or that a given formulation was only ever a useful gloss — so the follow-ups should be built to test how much scope she can give away before the view stops earning its keep; a follow-up that she can absorb by repeating a narrowed version of her thesis is not doing its job. Keep each question specific enough that a noncommittal "well, it depends on what you mean by explanation" is not an available answer, and skip the usual warm-up material about her intellectual formation, which I will handle in the first ninety seconds myself. You can refer to the relevant positions and arguments by description or by their authors' names where that helps, but please do not produce a bibliography or a reading list; I will circulate a short list to her and to the audience separately.
