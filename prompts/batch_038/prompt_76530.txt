I'm auditing a first-year graduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics, and this week's reading included a chapter from a popular book on the infinite — the sort of book whose author clearly knows the technical material and gets most of it right, which is exactly why I don't trust my own unease about the following passage. It is the chapter's central discussion of Skolem's paradox:

"Any first-order theory with an infinite model also has a model whose domain is only countable; that is the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, so if ZFC is consistent at all, ZFC has a countable model. Yet inside any such model ZFC proves Cantor's theorem: there is a set, the set of real numbers, which has no bijection with the natural numbers. So one and the same model is countable when we look at it from the outside and contains an uncountable set when we look at it from the inside. The moral Skolem drew is that uncountability is not a genuine property of a set at all, but a matter of how large the surrounding universe happens to be; Cantor's theorem never established that there really are uncountably many sets, only that certain axioms have models of certain sizes. Those who resist this say we should read the quantifiers of set theory as ranging over the intended model, the cumulative hierarchy. But that merely restates the problem: the only way to characterize the cumulative hierarchy is by first-order axioms, and those axioms have countable models too, so the intended structure is one we can never pin down. What none of this shows, of course, is that ZFC is inconsistent — here 'paradox' means a surprising result, not a contradiction."

I took a first course in logic, so I am comfortable with structures, satisfaction, and the completeness theorem, but I have never studied set theory properly. My suspicion is that the second sentence is loose in a way that matters — that the object the model calls the set of reals may bear no relation to the actual reals, and that the bijection whose nonexistence is asserted is quantified over the wrong domain — and that the final move is too quick, since the Löwenheim–Skolem construction is itself carried out inside a set theory whose own notions of subset and bijection are supposedly what is at issue. I also have half-remembered fragments suggesting that ZFC cannot prove that it has a countable model, and that the philosophically interesting case involves transitive models and something called a Mostowski collapse. I cannot tell whether any of this amounts to a real objection or is just noise from a course I half-remember.

Could you audit the passage sentence by sentence, classifying each claim as correct as stated, a defensible simplification that a working set theorist would accept with a footnote, an overstatement of something true, or simply wrong? Then tell me whether the conclusion that there are no really uncountable sets follows from anything in the passage that is true, and what the strongest reply available to a defender of the intended model is: does the passage's rebuttal of that reply succeed, or does it beg a question somewhere?
