I copyedit a festschrift volume for a philosophy-of-physics department, and while I've been working on it I've been sitting in on their informal reading group, mostly because it meets in a basement seminar room with better coffee than the press office. Two of the regulars — Marta, a postdoc who works on causal structure in general relativity, and Julian, a visiting scholar who comes at these questions from the history of idealism — spent two sessions arguing about Gödel's rotating universe and whether it does what Gödel thought it did. Neither would concede anything, and they eventually asked me to look up the technical points afterwards, which I am not equipped to evaluate. Here is the exchange as I reconstructed it from my notes; I've tried to give each of them their best formulation rather than the version the other was reacting to, and I have no view of my own beyond noticing that each of them sounded decisive at different moments.

(1) Julian opened by saying the argument is usually presented as running from the frame-relativity of simultaneity to the unreality of time, and that this inference is invalid: that simultaneity is frame-relative shows only that no observer-independent hypersurface is picked out as the present, which is silent on whether events come into being at all. To get from the loss of a privileged present to the unreality of time you need the further premise that whatever is real must be invariantly definable across frames, and Gödel assumes that premise rather than arguing for it, so the argument begs the question at its first step. (2) Marta replied that this attacks the weaker half of the case: Gödel did not rest on simultaneity, and the rotating-universe solution was meant to do independent work, because in it there are closed timelike curves through every point and so the before/after ordering itself breaks down. That breakdown is a coordinate-invariant geometric fact about the model, not an artifact of a choice of frame, so even if the simultaneity argument is a non sequitur the second one stands. (3) Julian said that then it is an argument about a universe that is not ours: Gödel's model has rigidly rotating dust, a negative cosmological constant and no expansion, whereas ours expands and, so far as anyone can tell, is causally well behaved. At most the model shows that general relativity permits spacetimes in which objective time fails, which is a claim about the permissiveness of a theory rather than about the world; and there is a technical reason to think our spacetime sits in the well-behaved class, since a spacetime admits a global time function — a scalar increasing monotonically along every future-directed timelike curve — exactly when it is stably causal, and both the cosmological slicing and the complete absence of empirical hints of causal loops are consistent with our being stably causal. So the second argument, correctly scoped, yields far less than Gödel claimed. (4) Marta said there are two separate problems there. On scope: Gödel's point is precisely that if whether time is real turns on contingent features of the matter distribution, then temporal reality was never part of the structure physics describes, and a theory that leaves open whether the world has an objective temporal order has not described a world in which time is real in the sense at issue. On the time function: a global time function is a way of numbering events consistently, which gives an ordering but not a lapse, and since there are indefinitely many such functions with no fact about which is the right one, stable causality supplies only what Julian had already conceded and none of what is contested — an advancing present, or a difference in kind between past and future rather than a mere index along a curve. (5) Julian said that this equivocates on the reality of time, and that the equivocation is what makes Gödel's conclusion look stronger than it is: Gödel himself separates a continual coming into being of new nows from an objective difference in character between past, present and future as against a distinction we project, and since nobody, Gödel included, thinks a monotonic numbering is time in either sense, objecting that the time function is just a numbering wins by changing the subject. Once the two are sorted apart, the argument against lapse needs no exotic spacetime whatever, so the rotating universe does no work there, while the argument that does need the rotating universe is the one whose conclusion must be restricted to universes like it; either way the model is surplus to what Gödel concludes about our world. He added that there is an internal reason to discount the model as well: in Gödel's own spacetime no closed timelike curve can be a geodesic, so an observer cannot free-fall around a loop into their own past, and there is in fact a nonzero lower bound on the total integrated acceleration any such curve must accumulate — the time travel that is supposed to refute objective time is not available even to an inertial observer in the very model meant to exhibit it. (6) Marta said the acceleration point cuts against Julian rather than for him: Gödel's claim concerns what the geometry permits, not what is practically achievable, and a bound on total integrated acceleration is a statement about cost, which is irrelevant to whether the earlier-than relation is asymmetric — if it takes rocket fuel to reach your own past, the past is still reachable and the ordering still fails. Julian's demand that the argument concern our universe, she said, simply assumes the methodology Gödel rejects, since Gödel treats the question as conceptual: what would have to be true of the world for our ordinary notion of time to be vindicated, and the existence of a legitimate solution of the field equations in which it is not vindicated shows the notion is not secured by the theory's structure. Finally, she said, the disanalogy Julian relies on between the lapse argument and the ordering argument collapses: if a lapse requires a distinguished present and no invariant candidate for one exists in Minkowski spacetime any more than in Gödel's, then the rotating model adds no new premise — it makes visible in a single geometric fact a deficit that was already there.
