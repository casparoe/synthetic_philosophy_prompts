Marcus said that's the correct outcome and it proves something deep. Dev said its only correct if the donkey is dumb, and a rational one eats.

here's roughly how it went, move by move:

1. Marcus: the starving isn't a bug in the thought experiment, it's the payload. Choosing requires something that favors one option. No favoring, no choosing. So "the rational donkey just picks" is empty — picking without favoring isn't choosing, it's twitching.

2. Dev: then call it twitching, the donkey still eats. Also you're defending an impossible case. There's always some difference — which way its ears are pointed, a draft in the barn, one bale slightly yellower — so perfect symmetry never happens and your deep lesson is a lesson about nothing.

3. Marcus: that's dodging the experiment. Zero the differences out, that's what the thing is for. And your own examples help me: the second there's a draft, there's something to favor, and the favoring explains the eating. Every real case of choice you can name is a case where the preference came first.

4. Dev: not every case, because a rational donkey can manufacture the difference. Spin around once and eat whichever bale your nose ends up pointing at. Now there's a reason — "my nose points left" — and you still had total symmetry one second ago. Preference can come after the setup, not only before it.

5. Marcus: that's not a reason, it's a reason-shaped object. Your nose pointing left tells you nothing whatsoever about the hay. And you still had to decide to spin, and nothing favored spinning over standing there, so you moved the arbitrary step up one level and it just goes up forever.

6. Dev: fine, it goes up a level, and at that level the symmetry isn't perfect. Standing there burns energy. Thinking has a price. So the longer you try to break the tie, the less tied the options get — the tie eats itself. You don't need an infinite chain of reasons, you need to notice that "keep deliberating" is the one option that gets worse every second.

7. Marcus: so your rational donkey eats because it got hungrier, not because it saw anything. That's a timer, not an agent. And notice what you had to do — you built a real asymmetry in order to get the choice out, which is exactly what I said you'd always need.

8. Dev: you're also sliding between two different situations and treating them as one. "The two piles are exactly equal in value" and "the two piles can't be compared at all" are not the same claim. If it's the second one, there's no symmetry to get paralyzed by, because "equally good" isn't even the right description — they're just not comparable, and when stuff isn't comparable you're allowed to settle it however you like without pretending you spotted a winner.

9. Marcus: and where does that permission come from? You just said spotting a winner isn't required. That's my thesis with the sign flipped. If settling doesn't need a reason, then nothing needs a reason, and "rational" stops meaning anything.
