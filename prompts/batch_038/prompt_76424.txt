For the past three weeks my graduate seminar on the structure of well-being has worked through threshold axiologies using a handout that stipulates the following position, and I now need to turn it into the centerpiece of the three-hour written paper that ends the course.

**Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU).** Every life has a total quantity of pleasure, measured in hedons (Q), and a total quantity of pain, measured in dolors (P). There is a fixed threshold τ dolors. A life is *afflicted* if its total pain exceeds τ, and *unafflicted* otherwise. Betterness over populations is settled in two lexical stages:

1. *Affliction.* For a population X, let H(X) be the sum, over the lives in X, of the amount by which each afflicted life's pain exceeds τ. X is better than Y at this stage if and only if H(X) < H(Y). Any difference in H, however small, decides the comparison: no quantity of pleasure can offset any positive quantity of affliction.
2. *Net sub-threshold welfare.* If H(X) = H(Y), then X is better than Y if and only if V(X) > V(Y), where V(X) = ΣQ − c·Σ min(P, τ) summed over the lives in X and c is a fixed positive conversion rate.

Please draft one essay question built on this definition, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question must not be answerable by restating the definition or by rehearsing the familiar contrast between negative and classical utilitarianism. It should make candidates work on the view's internal structure and discover commitments that were never announced in seminar. At a minimum, it must require them to engage with all four of the following:

- **Exchange rates rather than incomparability.** As defined, LTNU holds that pleasure and pain are commensurable below τ at rate c and that the exchange rate becomes infinite above it. Candidates should have to say what is and is not lost by glossing this as a denial of the commensurability of pleasure and pain, and how the view would change if τ were treated as vague, or as marking a zone in which the comparison is neither better nor worse nor equal rather than a jump.
- **The unit to which τ attaches.** Nothing in the definition says whether the threshold applies to a moment, to an episode of pain, or to a whole life. Candidates should have to show that this omission is not harmless by constructing a case in which two natural completions of the view deliver opposite verdicts.
- **The summative character of H.** Because H adds across lives, LTNU is indifferent at the first stage between a billion lives each containing τ+ε dolors and a single life containing τ + (a billion × ε) dolors. Candidates should have to work out whether this collapses the view into negative utilitarianism fitted with a discontinuous weight function, what is left of its distinctive claim, and whether the ordering as defined is transitive. I want an answer that simply asserts, as a matter of course, that threshold views are intransitive to lose marks: transitivity here is something that has to be argued for or against.
- **Representation and decision under uncertainty.** Candidates should have to consider whether the ordering LTNU defines can be captured by a single real-valued measure of population value, what would have to be true of the space of populations for that question to have one answer rather than the other, and what decision rule an LTNU agent should use when she is uncertain how much pain her act will cause — including whether that rule requires a threshold of its own.

In addition, the question should ask candidates to rank a small set of stipulated populations, with concrete numerical values for Q, P, τ and c that you supply, and to defend the ranking against the charge that it is repugnant. At least one of the populations should be a case whose LTNU ranking a sympathetic defender of the view will find genuinely hard to swallow, and the question should press candidates on whether the charge has any force against a view motivated precisely by the claim that preventing severe suffering outranks producing happiness.

**Deliverables.** (a) The question exactly as it will appear on the paper, including the reproduced definition of LTNU — you may tighten the notation but not alter the view — the stipulated populations, the word limit (roughly 1,200 words) and the mark allocation (25). (b) A model answer of 1,100 to 1,400 words. (c) A marking scheme out of 25 that names each credited move, states the marks it carries, and says in one line what the characteristic weak response to that move looks like; it should end by stating explicitly what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one.

**Two constraints on all three items.** First, everything must be self-contained: no citations, no bibliography, no attribution of positions to particular philosophers, and no appeal to named theorems or papers that a candidate could not reconstruct on the spot. Technical vocabulary is welcome provided it is defined as it is introduced. Second, write the model answer so that the credited moves are visible at the seams; I will be using it to calibrate a second marker.

Close with a short paragraph telling me which part of the question you take to be hardest, which of the four issues above a strong-but-not-exceptional answer is most likely to fumble, and whether you think the paper should carry a hint. I will use that to decide how much scaffolding to print.
