Okay so I need someone to find the exit in this, because I've read it maybe thirty times and I still can't tell whether it's wrong or whether my job is impossible.

Context: I'm the engineer who owns the penalty term on a small household robot we've got piloted in ~a dozen homes. The task reward is normal, and on top of it there's an impact penalty that's supposed to be value-neutral — it looks at a short do-nothing baseline rollout and charges the agent for how far the world ends up from that baseline and how many futures it can no longer reach. We've tried all three flavors: baseline-distance, reachability-count, and a version that measures how much the agent has shrunk its own ability to pursue a grab bag of unrelated side goals. Last month the thing sat in a hallway in unit 4 for something like forty minutes because there was a mouse chewing a lamp cord. It wouldn't catch the mouse (huge change, mouse loses basically all its futures) and it wouldn't leave either, because leaving meant the cord keeps getting chewed and the kitchen loses futures. Same penalty both ways. I had to explain that to a customer.

Our new safety lead came out of a philosophy program and she says the mouse thing isnt a bug we can tune out, its the whole approach, and she wrote this up for the team:

(1) An impact penalty has to be a function from the agent's state-description to a number; there's nothing else for it to be a function of.
(2) If it's meant to be value-free it can't consult what states are worth — only structural features: distance from baseline, how many futures become unreachable, how much attainable utility across a sample of arbitrary side-goals has been lost.
(3) Structural features count, they don't weigh. Same arithmetic, same penalty, whatever the lost futures were worth.
(4) So a value-free penalty is in principle blind to the difference between crushing a beetle and crushing a person, whenever the reachability numbers happen to line up.
(5) And it's blind in the other direction too: causing a large off-task change and preventing one are both large deviations from baseline, so it charges them identically.
(6) An agent charged identically for preventing and for causing, and given no reward for preventing, sits still whenever the options are otherwise close. Doing nothing is the only zero-charge move.
(7) So value-free low-impact agents are inert. They're safe the way a rock is safe.
(8) Therefore you cannot define "avoid side effects" without a full specification of value. Any penalty that isn't inert is a value function in a disguise.
(9) Now run (1)–(8) on the task term instead of the penalty. "Clear the table" is also a function from states to numbers, and it also doesn't know that the thing on the table is the urn.
(10) So no task can be specified for an agent without a full specification of value either.
(11) Nobody has a full specification of value.
(12) So no agent has ever been given a task — which is false, I gave one to mine this morning and it did it, minus the mouse.

She's happy with (12), or at least unbothered: she says it's a reductio of the whole project of writing down objectives, and the practical upshot is that we should stop pretending and hard-code an explicit list of things that matter. I can't accept that, both because it makes my last year of work vacuous and because a hard-coded list is precisely the thing that doesn't generalize to the mouse.

So — where does it break? I'd rather give up (9) than (4), since (4) feels like the reason anyone works on this at all, but she'll just say (9) is (1)–(8) applied a second time and I don't have a reply to that. Tell me which premise or which step you reject, why it looked fine sitting there in the chain, and what I actually pay for rejecting it; and if you think it's valid all the way through, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and what happens to the mouse.
