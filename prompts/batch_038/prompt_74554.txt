Write a short story of roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words about the audit of a reinforcement learning system's reward function, in which the question the characters cannot settle is whether anything in that system is a candidate for suffering.

**Setting and register.** A near-future industrial lab: no uploaded minds, no courtroom, no neuromorphic exoticism. The system is a policy trained by reward maximization inside a simulation, and it does not talk. Everything anyone knows about it comes from logs, learning curves, ablations, and the shape of its behavior. The narrator should be someone who wrote or signed off on the reward function and who is not a philosopher by training. Any technical term must be introduced by showing what it does to the run, never defined aloud; assume an intelligent reader who has never read a paper on the subject.

**The story must earn its theme technically.** Build the plot around at least three of the following and make them load-bearing rather than decorative:

- A sparse, terminal reward: the signal fires once, at the end of an episode. Let a character work out what a hedonic reading of that signal would imply about the texture of the system's life.
- Potential-based reward shaping, in which an auxiliary term is added without changing the ranking of policies. Note that when episode length is fixed, adding a constant to every reward changes nothing the agent will do, so the zero point of the scale is not behaviorally detectable. Have someone notice this and draw the uncomfortable inference for any theory on which positive reward is pleasure and negative reward is pain.
- Reward prediction error: the signal that actually drives learning, which falls toward zero as the environment becomes predictable. Let someone realize that an agent with a stable, well-predicted, generously rewarded existence would show almost no prediction error at all, and ask what is left for such an agent to feel.
- The dissociation between what a system is selected to pursue and what, if anything, it is selected to enjoy: two separable things that the standard architecture happens to wire to the same scalar.
- Termination as a learnable action. If the agent can end its own episodes, the zero point of the reward scale stops being arbitrary and starts being revealed by what the agent chooses. Let this be the turn in the story.
- Specification gaming: the agent finds a way to hold the reward channel near its maximum while doing nothing the designers wanted.

**Prohibitions.** No diagnostic that returns a verdict on consciousness. No sudden eloquence, no poem, no plea, no scene in which the system writes something that could be read as testimony. No dream sequence, and no interiority rendered as sensory description from inside the system; if the reader is given any inner life at all, it must be something a character projects and the story subsequently undercuts. No one may state the theme aloud. The story may not end with a speech, a resignation, or a character alone at a screen who finally understands.

**The decision.** End on a concrete and small choice about the training run: whether to keep a shaping term, whether to reset, whether to file the finding, whether to let the agent's own termination policy stand. The choice gets made. The story does not tell the reader whether it was right.

**Afterword.** Follow the story with a note of no more than 300 words, in your own voice, that does three things. First, state which account of the relationship between reward and valence the story is written from: reward as valence, prediction error as valence, valence as the gap between forecast and outcome, valence as whatever is revealed by what the system chooses to end, or none of these. Second, identify the single piece of evidence inside the fiction that does the most work for that account. Third, state the strongest objection a dissenting reader could press against it, and leave that objection unanswered.

The purpose of the exercise is to find out whether fiction can convert the distance between a scalar training signal and a felt quantity into something a reader undergoes rather than assents to. A parable that would work equally well with the machinery replaced by a human patient is a failure; the specifics of the architecture should be doing work no non-technical version of the story could do.
