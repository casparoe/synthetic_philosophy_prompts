I'm a staff software engineer at a company of roughly two hundred people, and I own the process by which contested technical calls get settled: code reviews where reviewers disagree, incident diagnoses that are disputed while the page is still open, and go/no-go decisions on releases. I make or arbitrate about five of these a week. Each one goes into a written record that people read months later, and on the release calls the cost of being wrong ranges from a wasted sprint to a multi-hour customer-facing outage. I have no formal philosophy training. What I have is a recurring pattern I handle badly.

The pattern: the evidence in front of me — the diff, the logs, the test output — points one way, and then a separate class of fact points the other. That second class includes things like: a colleague I rate as my equal on this code disagrees with me; I have been awake for nineteen hours; I misdiagnosed something structurally similar last quarter; the one reviewer who agrees with me is known for agreeing with me; and in this company whoever asserts hardest tends to carry the room. I have no principled way to combine the two classes, so I improvise, and the improvisation is visibly mood-dependent. There is also a second failure I would be embarrassed to describe out loud: I work through a counterargument, conclude that my original read was correct, write that down — and then continue to behave as though I believed the opposite.

What I want is a decision procedure I can run in this role. Not an essay on disagreement, and no reading list; I cannot look anything up mid-incident, and I would rather you define any term you use than gesture at a literature. Specifically:

- Give the questions in the order they should be asked, and say what counts as a sufficient answer to each. State which questions are being answered by the evidence about the system and which by evidence about my own reliability, and keep those two separated all the way down — I suspect collapsing them is most of my problem.
- Include an explicit rule for known impairments (fatigue, time pressure, a recent confident error on a similar case, an incentive to be right). Say whether the discount applies to my conclusion or to my confidence in my reasoning about it, and why that difference changes what I do next.
- Address the circularity I keep running into: if the procedure says to defer when my peers disagree, and my peers are running the same procedure and deferring to me, the group converges on nothing. Give me a stopping rule and say what it costs.
- Address the state described above, where my judgment about what the evidence supports and my actual belief come apart. Say what the procedure requires of me there — revise the belief, reopen the evidence, or keep the belief and act against it — and say plainly whether a mismatch between my action and my own assessment of the evidence is always a defect I am obliged to repair, or sometimes the correct place to be.
- Separate what to believe from what to do. Most of my calls are forced and time-boxed, so the procedure must say when an unresolved epistemic state still yields a determinate action, and it must make the asymmetry of costs — not a manufactured confidence — the thing that settles it.
- Specify what I write into the record, in a form that lets me later check whether my discounting was calibrated. I already keep a log of calls I got right and wrong; the procedure should feed that log rather than merely appeal to it.
- Mark, step by step, which parts are genuinely mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed as procedure. I would rather have three honest judgment calls than twelve fake ones.
- Say what the procedure cannot settle and where it hands the decision back to a person. I expect at minimum that it cannot resolve disputes that are really about risk appetite or priorities, and that it cannot tell me whether someone is my peer on a question I understand worse than they do. If there are further blind spots, name them.

Deliver two variants: a full version for planned decisions where I have a day, and a degraded version runnable in twenty minutes at three in the morning. The degraded version has to work when operated by the impaired person it exists to check, which I realize is a design problem rather than a footnote — please treat it as one.

Then run the procedure on three cases.

1. A test in our payments service fails roughly one run in forty. A colleague who has worked on this code longer than I have, and whom I would rate as at least my equal, is confident it is a real ordering bug between two writers and wants the release held. I have read the relevant code three times across two days and cannot find a path that produces the failure; the signature also matches a known timing sensitivity in the harness itself. The release is scheduled for Thursday and two other teams are blocked on it.

2. I am paged at 03:10. I have been awake nineteen hours and skipped dinner. Error rates on one service are elevated but not catastrophic. Within twenty minutes I conclude the cause is a bad deploy by a downstream vendor and that rolling back our own service will make things worse. I then remember that last quarter I confidently attributed a similar signature to a vendor and it turned out to be our connection pool, and that the colleague who would normally check my read is asleep and would have to be paged.

3. Six weeks ago I arbitrated whether to adopt a new storage layer. After reading both sides I concluded, and recorded, that the evidence favored adoption. Since then I have kept commissioning analyses that would expose problems with it, I have under-resourced the migration, and I hedge in meetings. I have also noticed that the input I gathered at the time came mostly from people who were performing confidence rather than reporting independent readings.

At least one of these should come out against my preferences. Say so directly instead of softening it: I would rather learn now that the procedure costs me something I value than discover it during an outage.
