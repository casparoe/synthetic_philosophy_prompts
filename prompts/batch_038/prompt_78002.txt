**Question (20 points).** In the *Lectures on Religious Belief* (1938), Wittgenstein argues that religious belief does not work like an ordinary opinion or a scientific hypothesis. Explain what he takes the difference to be, drawing on his discussion of belief in a Last Judgement, and then state what you consider the strongest objection to his position.

**Student's answer.**

Wittgenstein's central claim is that the gap between a religious believer and an atheist is not a gap in how confident each of them feels about a proposition they both understand. When a believer says she believes in a Last Judgement, she is not putting forward a prediction about the future at a middling probability; if she were, her disagreement with the atheist would be an ordinary factual one, and the two of them would be arguing about evidence, with her confidence rising and falling as the evidence accumulated. That picture gets the belief wrong from the start. He contrasts saying that one believes in a Last Judgement with saying that there will be one, with some degree of probability: the second is a hypothesis answerable to evidence, the first is not an approximation to a hypothesis at all. What the belief amounts to is shown in what the believer does with it — it regulates her conduct, her fears, her sense of what matters — rather than in anything she could confirm or disconfirm. Wittgenstein accordingly describes it as closer to a passion, or to a commitment to a framework or system of reference, than to an opinion. Nor does he simply call the believer unreasonable: unreasonableness is a verdict issued inside a practice of offering and weighing reasons, and that is not the practice this belief belongs to.

This is why, on his view, religious utterances lack cognitive content in the ordinary sense. To say that one believes in a Last Judgement is not to assert that anything will happen, and so it is neither true nor false; like an ethical statement, it gives expression to an attitude or a way of living. The believer is committed to no checkable picture of the world, and nothing she says could be shown mistaken by the world turning out otherwise. The same point reappears in his later work, where propositions that are not themselves known or doubted function as the hinges on which all questioning and testing turn; religious beliefs are hinges of this kind. It follows that criticism cannot get a grip. Since believer and atheist share no evidential standards, no argument can pass between them, and any attempt to refute the framework from outside is question-begging, because the standards it appeals to are supplied by the framework it is meant to judge.

The strongest objection is that this leaves religious belief with nothing in it. If a Last Judgement is not asserted to be coming and cannot be gotten wrong, then believer and atheist are not disagreeing about anything, and it is unclear what the believer's life is organized around. A position on which nothing is claimed cannot explain what it was meant to explain — the intensity with which such commitments are held and defended. Relatedly, the account gives us no way to tell devout belief apart from mere habit or ritual, since neither answers to how things are.
