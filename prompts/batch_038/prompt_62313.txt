Where I live the city council has a written policy about naming public things after people, and I've been reading it closely for a project, and I can't make it hang together. Three relevant bits.

One: nothing gets named after a person — street, plaza, library wing, the lot — until they've been dead at least five years, and the stated reason is that a persons significance and reputation need time to settle into the record before the city commits. Two: once a name is up, it stays up. If it later comes out that the namesake did something genuinely awful, the remedy isn't renaming, it's a small suplementary plate bolted underneath the street sign or beside the statue saying who the person was — and the guidance is that the plate has to be factual and non-judgmental. It can state what he did; it can't say what we should make of it. I think a bunch of German and Austrian cities do something like this with their street signs rather than renaming. Three: actual renames are basically only permitted for brand-new streets or where the road is being dug up anyway, because changing everyone's address is a hassle for the residents.

Meanwhile out by the bypass there's a new subdivision with three streets named after the developers who built it, all still alive and well, and nobody raised an eyebrow.

So what theory of commemoration is this policy running? Is a street name a verdict on a finished life, a gift to somebody, an entry in the historical record, a teaching device, or a statement about the city rather than about the honoree? Which of those does the five-year rule actually need, and which does the plate rule need — are they the same one? The waiting period sounds like it assumes judgment requires complete evidence, but the plate rule says later evidence can never reopen the judgment, only annotate it, like a case that's been decided and can't be relitigated. Is that a coherent package, or two incompatible views sitting next to each other because nobody noticed? And what does the factual-not-judgmental requirement assume about who's being wronged and how — that the harm is residents not knowing the facts, rather than the city visibly honoring someone? The plate is at shin height where nobody reads it.

Tell me which parts the practice strictly presupposes versus which it just happens to fit nicely, where it's underdetermined so that several different theories would license the exact same rules, and what the policy would have to become if you swapped the presupposition out — say, if honor were revisable in light of new evidence the way a belief is, instead of final once rendered. Would the developers' streets then be a live problem, or is that the one piece every theory is happy with?

And please be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on commemoration — I don't want someone's disputed position handed to me as the consensus.
