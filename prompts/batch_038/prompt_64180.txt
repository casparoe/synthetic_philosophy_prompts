So I got talked onto the review panel for a small family foundation this year — five of us, we score grant applications against three published criteria and the money follows the scores. Last Thursday we hit something on one application that I still can't stop thinking about, and two of the other panelists, Naomi and Ben, ended up arguing about it for fourty minutes while the rest of us sat there.

The criteria, straight out of the foundation's guidelines: fund a project only if (1) it addresses a problem that actually matters, (2) its method could plausibly answer the question it's asking, and (3) this team can pull it off inside the grant period. All three, or no money.

The tally on this application: criterion 1, four yes one no. Criterion 2, four yes one no. Criterion 3, four yes one no. But when we voted straight on "do we fund this," it came out two yes, three no. The kicker is that the lone no on each criterion was a different person, and all three of those people voted no on the funding question. Naomi said she'd read about this on her phone and it has a name — the discursive dilemma, and apparently in the law reviews they call the same shape of thing the doctrinal paradox. Then they started going:

1. Naomi: the criterion vote is the one that counts. The foundation didn't ask for our overall feelings, it asked us to apply three tests. And look what the direct vote did — three people rejected this project each on the strength of one criterion that four of us rejected, so the panel's "no" is a verdict none of us arrived at by the reasoning we were asked to do.

2. Ben: and the criterion vote produces a "yes" that three of the five people voting actively think is wrong. Each of us weighed the whole application and came out no. If applying the criteria the honest way gives an answer most of the panel disagrees with, the criteria are broken, not our vote.

3. Naomi: then look at what we'd publish. We'd send out a report saying the project satisfies all three of the foundation's criteria and that we're not funding it. A body that has to give reasons can't give reasons that entail the opposite of its own decision.

4. Ben: you're treating the criteria like a logic machine. If we treat them as a checklist that structures the conversation instead of a conjunction that mechanically settles the outcome, there's no paradox: we talk, then each of us gives a considered overall judgment.

5. Naomi: if they're just prompts, why do applicants have to adress them one at a time and why do we score them one at a time? The point of published criteria is that a rejected applicant can see which test they failed. Under your version I can't tell them anything true.

6. Ben: then somebody flips. Whoever's lone no is doing the most damage reconsiders, or one of us votes a criterion against our own judgment so the two methods line up. People manage procedures all the time, it isn't a scandal.

7. Naomi: that's asking someone to assert to the group something they believe is false. The only thing that makes our verdict a judgment rather than a number is that each of us reports what we actually think. Flip a vote and the consistency you bought is counterfeit — the panel now stands behind a premise nobody on it believes.

8. Ben: then it's worse than you're admitting. This isn't the old voting paradox, nobody's preferences are going in a circle here. Your thing bites any time the questions are logically hooked together, which is every grant panel and every hiring committee and every appellate court. Naomi herself says there's a theorem behind it to the effect that you can't have everything you want at once once the agenda has that structure. So we aren't picking the correct procedure, we're picking what to screw up — and I'd rather screw up the paper record than the decision, because the decision is the one with money and a real applicant attached.

9. Naomi: and I'd say the decision is downstream of the reasons, so keep the reasons honest and the decision takes care of itself. If this keeps happening, the fix is to rewrite the criteria, not to overrule them case by case.

They both want me to take a side and I'm not going to, partly because we vote again Thursday and partly because I honestly can't tell who's right about what. (I'm leaving my own vote out of the numbers on purpose, though you can probably back it out — I just don't want it steering your answer.) Can you work through the exchange move by move and tell me where each of them is right and where they're wrong, not just who wins overall? Specifically:

- Is Naomi's incoherent-record point a real objection, or does Ben's "the group contradicts its own members" point beat it?
- Does move 4 actually dissolve the problem or just move it somewhere else?
- Is the flip-a-vote move as corrupt as Naomi says? Is there any version of it that's legitimate — like flipping because deliberation changed your mind versus flipping to keep the tally tidy?
- Is Ben right in move 8 that this is genuinely different from the cyclic-preference paradox, and right that it's therefore more pervasive?
- Is there a principled way to choose between counting premise by premise and counting the bottom line, or does it really just come down to the bylaws and what kind of body you are?

And please tell me what neither of them said that I should be weighing before Thursday.
