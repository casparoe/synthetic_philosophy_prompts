ok so this has been eating my brain since my stats teacher mentioned something called the reference class problem, and now I can't stop applying it to literally everything. The idea as I understand it: to predict anything you have to pick a group of past cases that are like yours and use their hit rate. But the group can be described a million different ways, and different descriptions give you wildly different numbers, and it feels like you can always find a description that gives you the answer you wanted. So instead of asking about it in the abstract I wrote down my actual gut verdicts, mostly on stuff happening to me and people I know, and I want to know what principle (if there is one) makes all of them come out right. Or which ones I have to throw out.

1. Tryouts, friend A. Says he's making varsity. Around 40% of kids who try out make it. I think 40% is the right number for him even though he's played club since he was 5. Feels lazy but right.

2. Tryouts, friend B. Also played since he was 5, but he also did a specific keeper camp and we badly need a keeper. I think his odds are clearly better than 40%. So I let extra details move the number here and I didnt let basically the same details move it in case 1. This is the pair I can't reconcile.

3. My uncle's restaurant. He says most restaurants die in year one but his won't because he spent 20 years in the industry. I told him that's the exact move everyone makes and I'm not buying it. Meanwhile in case 2 I bought it. I would call what he's doing flattery narrowing, but I need a definition that doesn't just mean "narrowing I disagree with."

4. College apps. Overall acceptance rate at the place I'm applying is like 12%, but the rate for kids from my high school who apply is closer to 55%. I'm confident I get to use 55%. But exactly two kids from my school who take AP Bio and play cello got in, and I do NOT think I get to say my odds are 100%. Where does the narrowing have to stop, and why precisely there instead of one step earlier or later?

5. Big n=1 stuff. Will two major nuclear countries actually fight a war in my lifetime. There is no reference class, zero. Everyone says you cant put a number on it. But I definitely have a number, I'd say something like 5%, and it doesn't feel like I'm pulling it out of nowhere. Is that a real probability or am I just fooling myself with a vibe?

6. My app. I want to know the odds it gets to a thousand users. Nothing from my school has ever done anything so there's no local class at all, and I ended up using "small free apps made by teenagers," which nobody in it is really like mine. That felt fine, closest thing available. But in case 4 I refused to use the general 12% even though it had way more data behind it. Why is far-away-and-thin okay in one and near-and-thick not okay in the other?

7. Stuff you can only know afterwards. "Most lottery winners go broke" — I can't use that on my aunt because she hasn't won anything. But the second she wins, am I instantly inside that class without doing anything? Same with "most people who drop out of med school regret it" versus "most people who start med school drop out." I want to say you're only allowed to describe the class using things knowable beforehand, but my teacher points out that entrance exam scores are knowable beforehand AND they predict dropping out, so the line isn't just about timing. What is it about?

8. Getting sorted into a class without agreeing to it. A study app I use flagged me as "likely to put off homework" based on kids like me, and it's right, and I hate it. I think its prediction is legitimate but I also feel like I get to reject the class when it's applied to me personally. Which flatly contradicts case 1, where I gave my friend no such option.

9. Classes that move when you use them. 90% of people who fail their driving test the first time pass the second time. I failed. Do I get to use that now? And if using it makes me practice three hours a week, then I've moved myself into a different group and the 90% was never about me. Almost none of the stats I use are about a world where everybody knows the stats.

My own attempt at a rule, which I want you to attack: the blindfold test. Narrowing is legitimate only if you would have named the same class before you knew which answer you wanted, and if you'd use the same class for a stranger with the same features. Case 3 fails it, case 2 passes it. I think that's the difference but I cant tell if the test is actually doing work or if I'm just dressing up my tastes.

So: is there a principle that gets all nine, or close? State it as something a person could actually follow at a desk with a spreadsheet, not just a diagnosis of why it's hard. Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop and which it saves. Then pick three new cases of your own that my list doesn't cover — make them genuinely awkward, not softballs — and give me the principle's verdicts on them so I can check whether I still believe it. And if the honest answer is that there's no non-arbitrary stopping point for narrowing and the whole thing is hopeless, say that plainly and tell me what people who say that are supposed to do when they still have to decide stuff. No need to point me at books or articles, I just want the reasoning.
