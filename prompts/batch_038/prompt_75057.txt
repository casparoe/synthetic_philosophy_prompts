I work in the collections unit of a natural history museum, and last week two visiting researchers had an argument I could not referee. The case: a frog genus with a lowland population spread across a wide river basin, and a second population confined to an isolated upland massif. Nadia, the field biologist, has just described the upland frogs as a new species, citing a fixed difference in advertisement call (two notes against three), a diagnostic dorsolateral stripe, and no interbreeding anywhere along a forty-kilometre transect between the two ranges. Owen, who sequenced both populations, objects.

(1) His gene trees place the upland frogs inside the lowland radiation, with no reciprocal monophyly and divergence on the order of two hundred thousand years; on any genealogy-based criterion, he says, these are one species, and a two-note call is exactly what one or two loci under selection look like inside a single variable species.

(2) Nadia replies that monophyly takes far longer to appear than isolation does, so making it a requirement builds a waiting period into the concept that misdescribes the situation on the ground; moreover, the gene flow he detected is confined to one stream, where the hybrids have visibly low survival.

(3) She adds that the word species does not name one criterion but a role — the largest units that evolution keeps apart — and that these populations fill it, which is why several equally respectable concepts converge on her verdict.

(4) Owen says that concession is the tell: if the term is satisfied by whichever set of criteria happens to deliver the conclusion you wanted, it is doing no explanatory work, and the two of them are not disagreeing at all but merely attaching the same sound to different definitions.

(5) Nadia says the dispute cannot be merely verbal, because she would abandon her position on discovery — were the hybrids shown to be fully fertile and freely backcrossing she would lump the populations tomorrow — and a purely verbal difference admits of no such evidence.

(6) Owen accepts that but says it turns against her: if what she is tracking is gene flow, then his non-zero estimate is the number that matters, and she owes him a reason to split rather than lump. He separately objects to her remark that the upland frogs are already being considered for protected status, calling that an attempt to settle a taxonomic question by its consequences.

Please go through the six moves one at a time and say who is right at each, rating them rather than just picking an overall winner. I would especially like your verdict on the charge about monophyly in (2), on whether (4) or (5) is correct that the disagreement is merely verbal, and on whether the appeal in (3) to a role rather than a criterion is a substantive position or an evasion. Close by saying what both of them are leaving out.
