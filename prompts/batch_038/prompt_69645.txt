I teach an upper-level undergraduate political philosophy seminar — 24 students, one 105-minute session, a single room with movable tables, one teaching assistant, no participant laptops, and no real money (points may be notional, or redeemable for something trivial). I want an in-class simulation of the interaction between freedom of movement and the welfare state. The standard way that interaction gets taught treats it as an empirical question about fiscal impact — how much newcomers draw down and contribute — and I want my students to leave seeing that this framing hides the more interesting structure. On my reading, the tension is generated mainly by the ordering of two decisions rather than by the size of any number: whether the electorate fixes the tax-and-transfer rate before or after people move, and whether movers get a vote at all. And underneath that sits a further problem, which I want them to hit rather than be told: at some point the group has to settle who is entitled to a vote, and the only people entitled to settle that question are the people who already hold votes. There is no neutral standpoint outside the electorate from which its boundaries can be fixed.

Please design the simulation so that both points emerge from play. Structural requirements:

- Each student is dealt a fixed productive position at the outset and keeps it for the whole session. Moving changes where a person is taxed and where they draw transfers, not what they earn.
- Run at least three rounds. In one, the electorate votes on a tax-and-transfer rate before anyone may move. In another, movement happens first and the newly arrived vote in that same round. In a third, some movers are admitted on a status that permits work and taxation but bars voting. Students must be able to compare outcomes across the three and see that the sequencing, not the magnitudes, is doing the work.
- Once during the session, the question of who gets a vote must be put to the group as a live decision, with no rulebook clause resolving it from outside and no facilitator intervention supplying a principle. Let them discover that whatever procedure they use to settle it is one they had no prior standing to authorize.
- Include one round in which students choose the rules behind a veil of ignorance over their own position, so it can be contrasted with a round in which they choose having already been dealt their type. I want the contrast to be visible in the numbers, not just in the rhetoric.
- Keep everything computable by hand on paper: small integers, no calculators, and a tally sheet each student maintains.

Deliverables: the complete rule set; a materials list including role and status cards; a payoff scheme stated explicitly — the production function, the tax-and-transfer formula, the transfer rule, and a table of point outcomes for each position under each of the three regimes; at least one fully worked example round showing the arithmetic; and a demonstration that the design is not degenerate, i.e. that no single strategy dominates, that the three regimes produce genuinely different winners, and that the guestworker round is stable enough to persist for a full round rather than collapsing immediately. Also give me a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact wording of the facilitator's scripted instructions for each round (deliberately thin — the less I say during play, the better), and a debrief that maps particular moments from the table and the tally sheets onto the conceptual points. The labels — including whatever the standard term is for the problem of a people setting its own boundaries — should appear only in the debrief, never during play.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I expect at minimum: a coalition of well-off students trying to shut the border in round one and then refusing to reopen it; someone who declines to move even when moving clearly pays; a round whose totals are dominated by the luck of the deal rather than by the rule under test; and the boundary vote being foreclosed too early, before students appreciate what they have done. Give me a handling strategy for each and for anything else you foresee, including how to run the debrief if a round collapses entirely.

Finally, be frank about what the simulation cannot produce. I already suspect it will render disenfranchisement as a bad deal rather than as a standing wrong, that it will present migration as a payoff-maximizing move by someone with no ties, history, or risk in leaving, and that it may make the third round look like a mere technical compromise rather than a distinct political status. Tell me which further features of the argument the activity can only gesture at, and write the two or three sentences I would have to supply myself to close each gap. Close with a stripped-down variant that runs with 12 students in 50 minutes, noting which of the three points that version has to sacrifice. No bibliography, no reading list — I want the design and nothing else.
