I'm a staff engineer working on developer tooling — mostly build systems and the plumbing underneath them — and for the last couple of years I've been reading philosophy on my own, badly and out of order, usually starting from something that annoyed me at work. A few months ago I read Susan Wolf's 1982 essay on moral saints, and it lodged. Since then I've been drafting a short essay of my own about it, which I'd like to post on my personal blog and then read out to a small writing group I've joined; the group is mostly engineers and one librarian, so the audience is intelligent but has no training in ethics. I'm not trying to publish anything academic. I want to know whether the thing holds up as an argument before I waste six strangers' Thursday evening on it.

Below is the current draft, cut down from something about twice as long. The one substantive idea I'm trying to make is in the third section; everything else is scaffolding.

---

**You Cannot Schedule a Love**

Susan Wolf's essay on moral saints asks a question that sounds almost frivolous and isn't: is it desirable to be as morally good as possible? Her answer is no. A person whose every act is as morally good as it could be would, she argues, be missing too much of what makes a life a life — he would have no time to read novels, no interest in music, no developed taste in anything, no capacity for the kind of humor that depends on standing slightly outside morality. Wolf gets at this through two portraits. The loving saint has had his desires reshaped: helping others satisfies him in the way that hobbies satisfy the rest of us, so he gives up non-moral pursuits without feeling any loss and without any inner conflict. The rational saint still wants the golf round, the lobster dinner, the afternoon with a novel — but he judges that moral reasons outweigh those wants, and so he systematically declines them. Wolf finds both unattractive, for different reasons: the loving saint is happy but thin, and the rational saint lives in a state of continuous self-overruling that she suspects is either unstable or makes him worse company than he could be.

The argument is usually summarized as a trade-off. Moral perfection crowds out other goods, so don't aim at moral perfection; a good life includes projects that aren't morally required. Summarized that way, it looks answerable, and the answer is one I've heard a lot in the last decade, because a great deal of practical advice aimed at people who want their careers to do good is built on it: protect your sleep, keep your friendships alive, take the holiday, because the person who burns out at thirty-two has produced less good and not more. On the trade-off reading, Wolf has simply identified a constraint the allocationist should already have been respecting. The saint needs a better scheduler, and there's no reason he can't have one — Saturday afternoons booked for the oboe, marked in the calendar as non-negotiable, justified by the fact that a rested saint is a more effective saint.

My claim is that this reply fails, and that the reason it fails is the most interesting part of Wolf's argument. Some goods can only be had by valuing their object for its own sake. I'll call them engagement goods. Playing the oboe badly and happily; being absorbed in a book; the particular texture of a friendship that exists because you called someone you wanted to talk to; cooking for people while actually thinking about the food. In each case the good is partly constituted by the attitude you take toward the activity, so that supplying the activity without the attitude supplies something else. If your reason for doing it is that it keeps you functional as an instrument of good, then whatever you get out of it, you don't get that. The shape of the point is familiar: Sidgwick's observation that happiness is characteristically missed by those who aim at it directly, and Frankfurt's account of the person as someone whose first-order desires are not merely managed from above but identified with — together with his recognition that a higher-order desire to have some first-order desire does not by itself produce the desire, since the regress has to terminate somewhere that is not itself a decision. You cannot decide to be spontaneous. You cannot intend to intend. And, I want to say, you cannot schedule a love.

If that's right, Wolf's conclusion is both stronger and narrower than the trade-off version suggests. Stronger: sainthood is not in competition with a schedule that could be optimized, because the saint who reserves Saturday for the oboe has not reserved anything. He has added one more instrumental activity to a life in which everything is instrumental, and the total is more crowded rather than less. Narrower: the argument does not touch the loving saint, who keeps no ledger and is therefore immune to it. Whatever is wrong with the loving saint, it is something else — Wolf's separate charge that his life is happy but uninteresting — and my argument doesn't reach him. That's a cost. It means the engagement-goods reply is an argument against the rational saint and against the modern allocationist who is the rational saint with a productivity app, and not an argument against moral ambition as such.

I should say where I'm standing when I say this. For about a year I logged my discretionary hours and scored them with a rough estimate of marginal good — money earned and given away, mentoring, writing, versus chess, versus cooking for friends, versus time in the mountains. The intent was the allocationist reply, implemented in Python. Within two months the categories I had built the thing to protect were the ones that decayed, not because I stopped doing them but because I started doing them with the ledger open. Cooking for friends became an entry that read, in my own notes, hosting as relationship maintenance, three hours, medium value. I stopped logging. The activities came back slowly, and mostly after I had stopped being able to say what they were for. I don't want to make much of one engineer and a bad spreadsheet, and I'm aware of the obvious problem: the anecdote teaches the lesson the essay is arguing for, so it is not independent evidence of anything.

Three things I have not resolved. First, I may be sliding from a normative claim to a psychological one — I say engagement goods cannot be had instrumentally, but what I have shown is that I couldn't get them that way. Is there a non-empirical argument available, something about the constitutive aim of an activity, or is the honest version just that instrumental valuing tends to corrode the thing it targets? Second, the Sidgwick analogy cuts against me: if aiming at happiness indirectly sometimes succeeds, why can't aiming at engagement goods indirectly succeed, at least for some people? I need a disanalogy or a weaker conclusion. Third, I might be under-describing the saint. A person with fifty years of practice at the oboe may genuinely love it and merely report that love in the language of usefulness, in which case the ledger is a symptom of how he talks and not of how he values — and then my whole argument is a claim about self-description.

What I'm not arguing: I don't think Wolf shows that we should aim lower morally, and I don't think the saint's life is worse by any standard the saint himself would accept. I think she shows that morality cannot be the sole master value, and that the reason has to do with the structure of valuing rather than the distribution of hours.
