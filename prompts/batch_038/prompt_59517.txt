I am guest-editing a special issue on the problem of evil, and I want the standard fixed before the call for papers goes out rather than left to individual referees' intuitions. Draft the specification an answer must meet to count as a theodicy rather than a mere defence: the phenomena it must accommodate (the actual distribution of suffering, not simply its existence), the cases it may not fudge, and the distinctions it must keep intact — among them, the difference between showing that a perfect being's permission of evil is compatible with its attributes and explaining why *this* evil is permitted; the difference between a victim capable of integrating a harm into a life she can still affirm and a victim who is not (the deeply demented, the anencephalic infant, non-human animals across deep time); and the difference between a justifying good that accrues to the sufferer herself and one that accrues to the world, to bystanders, or to some cosmic whole. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that generates it, and mark it non-negotiable or a local prejudice of one camp. I also suspect that two of the requirements cannot both be met: the demand for epistemic humility about the reasons a perfect being might have, and the demand that the answer be sayable to the sufferer, by someone who loves her, without insulting her. Tell me whether you agree; if you do not, identify the pair that genuinely cannot be satisfied. Close with a line beginning Verdict: naming the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate theodicies.
