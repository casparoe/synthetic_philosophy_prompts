I am a novelist, and I have been commissioned to write the introduction to a new edition of Mary Shelley's Frankenstein. Every previous introduction I have read reaches for the same three levers — hubris, the creator's responsibility, the monster as abandoned child — and I would rather write something that treats the novel as doing conceptual work, not merely illustrating a theme. The lever I want to pull is the special composition question, and I want your help making sure I do not pull it dishonestly.

Let me fix the terms as I understand them, and correct me where I have them wrong. The question is not whether composition ever occurs, but under what conditions some things compose a further thing. The candidate answers include the maximal view on which any plurality whatsoever composes something; the nihilist view on which only simples exist, and what we call tables, cats, and people are just simples arranged tablewise, catwise, personwise; and van Inwagen's proposal that composition occurs exactly when the activity of the parts constitutes a life — a view with the notorious consequence that there are no artifacts at all, no tables and no chairs, and hence nothing that is made rather than born.

Now the reading. I want to know where in this novel the question is genuinely being asked and answered, and where it is only being gestured at. The obvious pressure points are the assemblage from charnel house and dissecting room; the moment of animation; Victor's insistence that the parts he selected were individually beautiful and that it was only the whole that appalled him; the half-finished female torn apart on the Orkney island and her fragments sunk at sea; the Creature's final intention to build his own pyre in the ice and have his ashes carried away by the wind; and Walton's enclosing frame of letters. Take each seriously. In particular:

1. Is Victor's revulsion at the completed whole a genuine mereological insight — the nihilist's point that no new being has come into existence, only matter rearranged — or is it a confusion of aesthetic unity with ontological composition, the feeling that a thing is ugly mistaken for the judgement that it is not a thing? Pick one and argue for it.

2. On van Inwagen's view there are no artifacts, yet the Creature is precisely an artifact that behaves like a life. Does the novel treat him as a made thing that becomes a being, or as a being that was always merely a plurality? What does the novel's own language of creation, birth, and parentage commit Shelley to here — and is there a fourth option that neither nihilism nor organicism offers, which the book is in fact exploiting?

3. The destroyed female is composition interrupted. What does the novel suggest about whether a life is constituted by the parts' present activity alone, or also by their history — given that every part of the Creature was once caught up in some other organism's life, and given that Victor's act is described as an act of violence against something that had not yet begun?

4. The frame narrative and the two substantially different published texts raise the question at the level of the book itself: does this novel compose one work, and if so, what does that require of the letters, the editions, and the nested narrators? I want at most a paragraph on this, but I want it to be a real answer rather than a flourish.

5. Where does the reading break? Tell me plainly which scenes are doing something else entirely — theology, sympathy, gender, the anxieties of authorship — such that forcing the mereology onto them would distort the book, and say what an honest critic must concede.

6. Finally: what does Frankenstein show about composition that the arguments do not? If the answer is nothing, say so; if the answer is that the novel makes the reader undergo a question that the literature can only state, say exactly which question and how it does it.

Then give me a verdict, and give it to me committed: which of the three positions — universalism, nihilism, or organicism — does this novel end up siding with, and which is it most useful to read against? Do not lay out the case for each and leave me to choose; tell me what you actually think, with your reasons, and tell me where you are most likely to be wrong. And a note on liberties: where you are relying on a contested or idiosyncratic reading of van Inwagen, or on a disputed point of Shelley scholarship, flag it, because I would rather defend a reading I know is load-bearing than one that quietly collapses. No citations, no reading list, no bibliography — I want your argument, not your references.
