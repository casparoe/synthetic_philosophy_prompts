I cover cognitive science and artificial intelligence for a national magazine, and for the past year I have been reporting a series on debates that refuse to close — ones where two communities have been describing the same failure in incommensurable vocabularies for decades. The current installment concerns the frame problem, and I have persuaded two researchers to attempt an adversarial collaboration on it. One builds planning and retrieval systems for industrial operations and holds that the original puzzle about non-change was an artifact of an over-committed representational scheme, since absorbed on the formal side by successor-state axiomatizations (which buy tractability by assuming complete knowledge of what causes change, and thereby relocate the difficulty to the qualification problem) and on the engineering side by learned relevance — attention, retrieval, embedding similarity. The other works in the situated-cognition tradition and holds that what those techniques do is not determine relevance but rank similarity against a standing inventory of contexts, and that the deeper point, which no inventory can accommodate, is that the salience of a fact can be introduced by the situation itself rather than read off any prior description of it. Both have signed on, both want the opening instrument drafted by a neutral party, and because pre-registration is the entire point of the exercise, both want predictions recorded before any data is gathered. They have agreed to work from a single concrete case, and I want that case to do real work in the document rather than sit in a preamble: a large language-model-driven assistant is embedded in a municipal water utility's control room, reasoning over a formalized state description of the plant — valve positions, tank levels, pump statuses, pressures, and the causal and regulatory relations among them. During a shift change a maintenance crew re-tags a section of the plant as a temporary bypass, under a work order that exists only on paper and on a radio channel the assistant does not monitor. Nothing breaks and every reading stays in range, but the state description the assistant is reasoning in has silently ceased to pick out what it used to pick out, so the inference called for is not "what else follows from this reading" but "this reading no longer means what it meant an hour ago." The two disagree about whether that is a hard instance of the same old problem or a different problem the old formulations never managed to state.

What I need from you is the joint statement they could both put their names to. Open with a numbered list of claims both can sign — about what the early formal approaches to reasoning about action and change actually committed themselves to, about what the qualification and ramification problems are and are not, about what retrieval- and attention-based systems demonstrably do and demonstrably fail at, and about what nobody yet knows. Keep these substantive: if a claim is so hedged that neither side risks anything by signing it, cut it. Then give a disagreements table with four columns — the disagreement stated in the AI researcher's preferred wording, the same disagreement stated in the philosopher's preferred wording, what the disagreement is actually about, and whether it is substantive or a collision of terms. Where you judge a stretch of the dispute to be verbal, say so plainly and say what the two are really disagreeing about once the vocabulary is fixed. My own suspicion is that part of it turns on whether relevance is a two-place relation between a description and a situation, hence something that can be enumerated or approximated, or a property of a whole background practice in which descriptions get their sense at all, hence something for which every list is provisional — but test that suspicion rather than adopt it, and if you think the real fault line runs somewhere else, put it there.

Then the cruxes, which are the part I care most about. A crux must be a sub-question such that a definite answer would move at least one of them, and each must be attached to a test both accept as bearing on it — a designed case, an ablation, an operational scenario in a plant like the one above, a piece of analysis they would both recognize as probative — with each side's prediction written down in advance and, wherever possible, quantified. I want at least four cruxes, and one of them has to be this: whether a system whose relevance judgments are acquired from a fixed corpus can ever register a re-individuation of its own categories as a re-individuation, rather than as an anomaly to be smoothed into the nearest familiar case. If they think no experiment could distinguish a system with that capacity from one permanently without it, I want that impossibility claim on the record and argued for, not asserted. Close with the conditions of failure: what each researcher has agreed in advance would count as their own side having lost, phrased so that each would still accept it after losing. Two constraints on execution. Do not include a bibliography, a works-cited list, or named references to particular papers — I handle sourcing myself and an invented citation would ruin the piece. And write for two experts who have been reading each other for twenty years and are thoroughly tired of being caricatured, not for my readers, whom I will bring along separately in the reported text.
