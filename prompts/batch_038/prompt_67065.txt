I teach a graduate seminar deliberately split between a history department and a department of earth sciences, and the opening session is on when the Anthropocene begins. I would like your help building the worksheet for that session, and I want it cast as an evaluation rather than as a survey: the students will have already read the survey.

Take the following six candidate starting points.

1. The early-to-mid Holocene spread of agriculture, on the view that millennia of forest clearance, irrigation, and rice cultivation were already perturbing the carbon cycle.
2. The Columbian Exchange, with the drop of roughly seven to ten parts per million in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610 preserved in Antarctic ice.
3. The onset of industrial coal combustion, around 1800.
4. The mid-twentieth century, marked by fallout radionuclides in lake, marine, and ice archives — the marker on which the formal epoch proposal was built before it was voted down in 2024 and the rejection confirmed at the level of the International Commission on Stratigraphy.
5. The mid-1960s maximum in bomb-test radionuclides, as distinct from their first appearance.
6. The refusal of a date at all, on the ground that the term's extension is vague in the technical sense: there is a penumbra of onset that no point-boundary can honor, so the demand for a year is a request the concept cannot coherently satisfy.

Score each candidate from 1 to 10 on three criteria, which I want kept rigorously separate:

- **Stratigraphic performance.** How sharp, global, synchronous, and durably preserved in the rock record is the signal this candidate offers? This criterion cares only about legibility in an archive, not about meaning.
- **Causal centrality.** How close is the candidate to the point at which human activity became the dominant forcing on the Earth system, rather than one significant pressure among several?
- **Attributional legibility.** How well does the candidate make visible the agents, institutions, and processes that the evidence actually implicates, so that the name of the period assigns credit and responsibility roughly where it belongs?

In justifying each score, address the following asymmetry explicitly, since it is the hinge of the session: a marker may be a flawless record of a change it did not cause. The radionuclide horizon is an immaculate global signal produced by weapons testing, which is not obviously the same thing as the transformation of the Earth system. State for each candidate whether it is being offered as a cause or as a symptom, and whether its strength on one criterion is purchased at the cost of another. Where a score depends on your accepting a contested reading of the evidence, say so in the justification rather than letting the dependence hide inside the number.

Then produce two aggregate orderings, stating your weightings numerically: one under a weighting that reflects what a stratigrapher needs from a boundary, and one under a weighting that reflects what a historian needs from the name of a period. Report where the two orderings diverge, and say in a sentence or two whether that divergence tells us something about the world or only about the two conceptions of the concept.

Finally, answer one question with a number rather than with hedging: of the six candidates, how many could plausibly rank first under some weighting that a competent specialist might actually defend? Give the count, name them, and identify any candidate you regard as never able to rank first, explaining what would disqualify it.

Two constraints on the exposition. First, please give no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations; where you need to attribute a view, attribute it to a position rather than to an author or a paper. Second — and this matters more than the formatting — I want the settled separated from the contested throughout, and marked as such where the distinction is doing work. Several claims in this area are settled as matters of measurement: the radionuclide signal really is globally synchronous, and the seventeenth-century carbon dioxide dip is a real feature of the ice record. The inferences drawn from those measurements are not settled at all, and neither is the question of whether any of the candidates is the correct start of anything. Do not allow a settled measurement to lend its authority to a contested interpretation, and do not present the primacy of any one candidate — including the mid-twentieth century, which you may find yourself inclined to favor — as the consensus view among people who work on this. There is no such consensus, and the formal proposal that came closest to producing one was rejected.
