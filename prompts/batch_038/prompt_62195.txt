## The assignment as set

Module: Collective Decision-Making, Week 5 written assignment. Total: 100 points.

*A seven-member appellate panel must decide whether a defendant is liable. All seven accept that the defendant is liable if and only if all three of the following hold:*

- *P — the defendant acted as alleged;*
- *Q — the act breached a duty owed to the plaintiff;*
- *R — the breach caused the plaintiff's loss.*

*Every judge holds a complete and consistent view of the four propositions. Their votes are:*

| Judges | P | Q | R | Liable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1, 2, 3 | Yes | Yes | No | No |
| 4, 5 | Yes | No | Yes | No |
| 6, 7 | No | Yes | Yes | No |

*(a) [30 points]* *State the panel's verdict on liability under (i) a procedure that takes a majority vote on each of P, Q and R separately and then applies the rule all seven accept connecting them to liability, and (ii) a procedure that takes a majority vote directly on liability. Show the tallies.*

*(b) [40 points]* *Name the general phenomenon exhibited by the divergence in (a). Explain which formal conditions on an aggregation rule the first procedure satisfies and which it violates, and state what the relevant impossibility result says a group must surrender if it insists on collective verdicts that are always complete and consistent.*

*(c) [30 points]* *Explain what the second procedure buys and what it costs. Then say whether you accept the claim that the phenomenon in (a) shows majority voting to be an unreliable procedure for collegial bodies.*

## Student submission

Paragraph 1. Under procedure (i) the panel votes premise by premise. P carries 5–2, with judges 1 through 5 in favour. Q carries 5–2, with judges 1, 2, 3, 6 and 7 in favour. R carries 4–3, with judges 4, 5, 6 and 7 in favour. Since a majority affirms each of P, Q and R, and all seven accept that liability follows from their conjunction, the panel's verdict is that the defendant is liable.

Paragraph 2. Under procedure (ii) the panel votes on liability directly. Not one judge votes for liability: judges 1 to 3 reject R, judges 4 and 5 reject Q, and judges 6 and 7 reject P. The verdict is therefore not liable, 7–0. The interesting feature is that no judge is individually inconsistent. The contradiction is entirely a property of the aggregation rule, not of anyone's reasoning, and it does not need large majorities to arise, since R passes on the slimmest possible margin of 4–3.

Paragraph 3. This is the doctrinal paradox, which is the name given to it in the setting of collegial courts, and its generalisation to any group reasoning about logically connected propositions is the discursive dilemma. The relevant conditions concern whether the collective verdict on one proposition is allowed to depend on individuals' verdicts on other propositions. Procedure (ii) treats each proposition on its own, which is independence of irrelevant propositions; systematicity is the same requirement applied uniformly across the whole agenda. Procedure (i) violates independence, because the collective view on liability is settled by the votes on P, Q and R rather than by the votes on liability itself. The impossibility result in this area says that, for an agenda with the right logical structure, no rule can simultaneously satisfy universal domain, anonymity, systematicity and the demand that collective judgment sets be complete and consistent. So a group that wants complete and consistent verdicts has to give something up: either it drops independence and votes premise by premise, or it keeps independence and concentrates power, for instance by designating one judge whose view settles every proposition, which is consistent and independent but is dictatorship rather than collective decision.

Paragraph 4. It is worth noting that none of this is new. Political scientists identified the same difficulty a century ago as the Ostrogorski paradox, in which a majority of voters supports each individual plank of a party's platform while a majority nevertheless prefers the rival party overall. The Ostrogorski paradox is the doctrinal paradox in an electoral costume: both consist in a majority on each issue without a majority on the package, so both have the same diagnosis and the same repair, which is to stop voting issue by issue and vote on the package instead. In that light the judgment-aggregation literature is best read as the rediscovery, with better notation, of a result that was already in hand.

Paragraph 5. Procedure (ii) buys independence and guarantees that the group never contradicts the connecting rule it has itself accepted, and I see no formal cost attached to it: what it discards is the reasoning behind the verdict rather than anything structural. Its real cost is presentational and political. A court that acquits while a majority of its members believe each element of liability to be established will struggle to justify itself, since the published judgment cannot appeal to the panel's own findings on the premises. Procedure (i) has the opposite problem in addition to a strategic one: because the verdict is a function of the premise votes, a judge who cares only about the outcome can misreport a premise to move it, so premise-based voting is manipulable in a way that direct voting on the outcome is not.

Paragraph 6. Finally, the doctrinal paradox is usually presented as though it revealed something about majority rule, but it is really a corollary of Arrow's theorem. Preferences are one species of judgment, so once Arrow's impossibility is granted for ordinal preference profiles there is nothing further to prove: the discursive dilemma is simply what Arrow's theorem looks like when the agenda happens to be conjunctive instead of a ranking, and the impossibility results for judgments add no content beyond it. On the question actually asked, I would not say that majority voting is unreliable for collegial bodies. It is unreliable only for bodies that demand consistency and independence at the same time, and since that pair of demands is a design choice rather than a necessity, the phenomenon identifies a trade-off that an institution must settle deliberately, not a defect that disqualifies the procedure.
