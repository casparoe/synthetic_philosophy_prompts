So my friend runs a little AI-safety reading group over video chat, and last week the topic drifted into self-modifying agents, and two of the regulars — Ines and Marcus — got into it about whether the Löbian obstacle is a real thing or a myth. I was just there to listen and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, so I'm hoping you can referee. Here's the exchange roughly as it happened:

(1) Ines: Löb's theorem genuinely blocks an agent from trusting itself. Say the agent's policy is "if I can prove that doing X achieves my goal, then do X." Löb says that if a system can prove "if I prove φ then φ," it can already prove φ outright. So the check is empty — the agent never learns anything from its own proof search that it didn't already have. You can't bootstrap self-verification out of self-reference.

(2) Marcus: That's way overstated, since Löb's theorem is exactly what makes robust cooperation between modal agents work. Take FairBot: it cooperates iff it can prove the other player cooperates. Put two FairBots against each other and you don't get a deadlock where each one waits forever on the other — Löb's theorem, in its parametric form, hands you mutual cooperation. So it's a tool, not a wall. And the "you can't trust a system as strong as yourself" complaint is just the second incompleteness theorem with a new label, which any real agent steps around by going up a level: PA can talk about weaker systems, ZFC can talk about PA, and so on. A self-modifier just needs to reason in a theory slightly stronger than the version it's checking.

(3) Ines: Two things wrong there. First, FairBot isn't self-trusting at all — it proves things about the *opponent's* source code, a fixed finite object handed to it, and never proves anything about its own future output. And the mutual cooperation result is proved by us, from outside, in the meta-theory, about the pair. The trust is imported from the mathematicians, so that's not a counterexample to my point, it's an illustration of it. Second, climbing the hierarchy relocates the problem instead of dissolving it: the ZFC-level reasoner still isn't verifying its own reasoning, and when it builds the successor it has to build a stronger verifier along with it, which needs a stronger one above it. Infinite regress of theories, each one trusted on faith.

(4) Marcus: Then your obstacle proves too much. Actual bounded agents, humans included, revise their own reasoning constantly without proving anything, and they do it on a track record — I trust my future self because my past revisions mostly turned out okay, not because I hold a consistency proof. If your argument implies that no finite agent can ever rationally self-modify, it's reductio'd itself into irrelevance. And there are bounded versions of these Löb results where the agent only searches proofs up to some fixed length, which is what any real agent does anyway; cooperation still comes out with finite budgets. Even the technical literature points toward bounded trust being workable.

(5) Ines: Bounded proof search makes it worse, not better. The length bound has to be paid again at every level of self-reference: if I need a length-n proof that "my successor's length-m proof of safety implies safety," then m has to sit well below n, so each self-modification eats into the budget the next one needs. Bounded self-trust buys a finite number of revisions and then runs dry — that's the obstacle, quantified instead of just asserted. Also, as far as I know the bounded analogues of the robust-cooperation results were never actually established; I'm pretty sure the authors of the FairBot paper flagged that themselves in a later revision.

(6) Marcus: Fine, grant the decay. But a decay rate isn't a barrier. Make each modification smaller and each proof cheaper, and the number of safe steps available grows without bound, so in the limit of arbitrarily cautious self-modification there's no obstruction left at all. Which means the Lobian obstacle can't be what people claim it is — an obstacle to self-improvement in principle. At worst it's a speed limit, and speed limits don't come out of the second incompleteness theorem.
