I've been reading rationalist blogs and their comment sections for the better part of two decades, and the failure mode I've learned to distrust most is two well-informed people arguing for months, each coming away convinced the other never engaged the objection. My philosophy-of-language reading group is currently in that state. We've spent six weeks on vagueness and split cleanly into two camps: one member is an epistemicist of the Williamson line, holding that there is a precise last patch in the series to which the predicate applies, that this is a substantive metaphysical claim about how use fixes semantic value, and that our inescapable ignorance of it follows from margin-for-error reasoning rather than showing the claim to be absurd; the other is a supervaluationist of the Fine and Keefe line, holding that a borderline ascription is neither supertrue nor superfalse, that this is coherent because truth just is what survives every admissible precisification, and that the epistemicist is positing an unknowable sharp boundary for no explanatory gain. To my ear they have been answering different questions. What I want you to produce is the document this collaboration should have generated: a joint statement both parties could actually put their names to.

The statement should open with a numbered section of agreed claims, and I want these to be substantive rather than diplomatic — including shared claims about what the sorites paradox consists in, about what any adequate theory owes us on tolerance and on penumbral connections, about which classical principles survive on each account and which do not, about the excluded-middle versus bivalence distinction, and about what a competent speaker can know and assert at the first order. Then a disagreements table: for each live disagreement, the issue; the formulation the epistemicist would sign for their own side; the formulation the supervaluationist would sign for theirs; whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal; and what would move each party. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would change at least one party's mind — each paired with the argument or case both agree bears on it and each side's prediction written down in advance, pre-registration style, before anyone has seen the other's answer. Finally, an explicit verdict on how much of the dispute is merely verbal: my strong suspicion, which I want tested rather than assumed, is that at the first order the two views issue almost identical operational advice (neither lets you know where the cut-off is, neither lets you run the one-step-at-a-time argument, and once each fixes what "true" means for itself they diverge on little that anyone could detect), so that the real dispute lives at the second order — whether "definitely" itself admits of sharp cut-offs — and in the metasemantics of what it is that fixes an extension. If you think that suspicion is wrong, or half-right, say so and show where the non-verbal disagreement actually bites.

To force the metasemantic issue, run this case through the document as a shared diagnostic. A lab introduces a new predicate, "rish," over a series of one thousand colour patches shading by imperceptible steps from pure red at patch 1 to pure orange at patch 1000. The introduction rule is announced publicly: "rish" applies to patches 1 through 612 if a designated coin, flipped by a machine at the moment the study opens, landed heads, and to patches 1 through 611 if it landed tails. The outcome is written on a single slip, sealed in an envelope in front of everyone, and the envelope is then incinerated; no record, memory, or physical trace of the outcome survives or could in principle be recovered. Ask both parties to say what the extension of "rish" is, whether patch 612 is a borderline case, and why. Then give me the two variants that matter: the same setup but the envelope is merely locked in a vault to be opened in a century, and the same setup but the outcome was observed by exactly one person, now dead, who never told anyone. I want each side's account of what "admissible precisification" must mean for their answer to come out as it does — in particular, whether admissibility tracks all the facts, including inaccessible ones, or only the facts that publicly determine meaning — and I want the epistemicist to state what, if anything, distinguishes the ordinary sorites series from "rish" other than the mechanism by which the cut-off got fixed. Also tell me whether the two views ever diverge on a real decision: for instance, if a regulator must fix an operational threshold on a vague concept — a body-mass index above which a person is classified as obese, say — does epistemicism rather than supervaluationism, or vice versa, change what the regulator should do or how the rule should be drafted? If the answer is no, that belongs in the verbal-dispute verdict, with an account of what the disagreement is nonetheless doing.

Begin your response with the joint statement itself: no preface, no commentary, no restatement of this brief, and no bibliography, since both parties know the literature cold and the document has to stand on its own. After the document, add one short section, under its own heading, in which you tell me how confident you are in what you have drafted — ideally calibrated section by section — and single out the one claim in the statement that you are least sure a real partisan of that side would sign, together with what you think they would object to about it.
