I am a high school junior. I have been building and shipping small web applications for about four years, and I am considerably more fluent in the vocabulary of front-end engineering than in the vocabulary of philosophy: components and props, application state as distinct from the rendered view, the render loop, memoization and caching, lazy loading and virtualization, optimistic updates and client-side prediction, reconciliation, and rollbacks that the user is never permitted to see. The governing intuition of that work is that a view is a lossy, purpose-built projection of a store, not a picture of it. My philosophy reading is amateur and patchy; I have worked through introductory material on consciousness, but I have not read the primary literature closely.

The position I would like translated is the one sometimes called the user-illusion theory of consciousness: the claim that conscious experience is a simplified interface a system presents to itself, that the apparent unity and continuity of the stream of consciousness are artifacts of the rendering rather than facts about the underlying processing, and that there is no location inside the system where all the parallel work converges and gets viewed by something. Restate that position, as precisely as you can and claim by claim, entirely in the vocabulary above. Where a piece of it has a natural counterpart, name the counterpart; where you have to invent one, say so and justify the invention.

Then use one hard case as a stress test. In the color phi phenomenon, two stationary lights of different colors are flashed in quick succession at different positions, and subjects report a single light traveling between them that changes color partway along — which means the reported content depends on an event that has not yet occurred when the first half of the experience is allegedly underway. Proponents of the view I am describing take this to show there is no determinate fact about when, or even whether, the subject experienced the color change. Translate that argument into the language of optimistic rendering, prediction, and silent rollback, and tell me whether the mapping holds exactly or leaks.

After that, give me the residue report, organized in three buckets: claims that translate cleanly; claims that translate only with distortion, with the distortion stated explicitly; and claims that have no expression at all in my vocabulary. Please address these three questions in the report:

1. In software, an interface is always an interface for someone distinct from the machine that renders it. This view says the interface is for the system itself. Does my vocabulary have any way of representing a view whose only consumer is also part of the view, or does it silently reintroduce a little user watching a screen?

2. My vocabulary is realist about the backend: an interface hides a store that genuinely has the structure the interface omits. If the theory claims there is nothing further hidden — that the omitted processing is not experience either — am I forced to read it as an interface over a backend that is already non-experiential, which would be assuming the very point at issue?

3. Does UI vocabulary contain any analogue of what it is like to be in a given state? If it does not, is that because the vocabulary is third-person by construction and therefore simply lacks the resources, or because the concept fails to pick anything out?

One constraint on method: do not let the target framework's assumptions be presented as translation results. Every time you need to import a notion from the philosophy side in order to make the software side work, flag it as an import. Do not settle the debate by definition, in either direction, and tell me at the end which of the two readings of the residue you think is better supported — that my vocabulary is missing something real, or that the original talk of illusion and interface was less clear than it sounded. About 1,200 words, with the term-by-term mapping in a table and the residue report in prose.
