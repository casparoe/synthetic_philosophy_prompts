I have no academic training in philosophy. I left school at sixteen and have worked in freight logistics ever since, but for about three years I have been reading Donald Davidson on my own — a secondhand copy of *Inquiries into Truth and Interpretation*, plus whatever secondary material I could get hold of without an institutional login. I have also watched Denis Villeneuve's *Arrival* something like a dozen times, and recently reread Ted Chiang's novella "Story of Your Life," on which it is based. I am drafting a long piece for a small film-and-philosophy reading group I help organize, and my thesis is that *Arrival* is best understood as a drama about Davidsonian radical interpretation — and that it partly fails at being one, in a way that is philosophically instructive. I would like your help testing and sharpening that reading, including telling me where it is simply wrong.

A few things I would like the piece to do:

1. Set out the machinery I am leaning on, in your own words and for a reader without training: radical interpretation as the situation of an interpreter who must assign meanings to a speaker's utterances with no prior bilingual dictionary and no shared vocabulary to bootstrap from; the principle of charity and why Davidson thinks it is not optional but constitutive; the holism of belief and meaning; the later idea of triangulation; and the argument in "On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme" that a scheme too different to be translated is not a coherent idea at all. Please flag anywhere my amateur reconstruction is likely to be off.

2. Treat Louise Banks as an interpreter and judge her performance. I want particular attention to the early whiteboard exchanges and the naming of the two heptapods, and then to the point where the message rendered as "offer weapon" diverges between national teams. My hypothesis — the part I most want pressure-tested — is that Louise becomes a *bad* Davidsonian from the moment the visions begin. She starts letting private, unverifiable experience fix the meaning of signs the heptapods produce; she reads her own conclusion into their sentences instead of maximizing agreement with what they actually do. Is that a fair charge, or does Davidson's holism license it, since beliefs and meanings are assigned as a package and any local oddity can be absorbed elsewhere?

3. Decide what kind of case the weapon/tool ambiguity really is. Is it a genuine indeterminacy — several translation manuals equally compatible with all possible evidence — or ordinary lexical ambiguity that further evidence settles? In the film further evidence does settle it: Costello's explanation, the discovery that the writing occupies exactly one twelfth of the projected space, the disclosure that they will need humanity's help in three thousand years. If the puzzle is resolvable in principle, does the film thereby fail to illustrate the indeterminacy thesis it is almost always said to illustrate?

4. Assess a reading I find attractive but suspect may be a stretch. Louise suggests that the Chinese team's translation comes out hostile because of how they interact with the heptapods — a competitive game rather than open exchange. I want to read this as triangulation: it is shared activity in a shared world, not shared vocabulary, that fixes reference. Is that a legitimate use of the notion, or am I decorating a point about diplomatic framing with a technical term?

5. Take up withholding and deception. For most of the film the heptapods decline to state their purpose, and at least once they produce a sign they could reasonably expect to be misread. Charity maximizes truth and rationality in the interpreted speaker; how much deliberate opacity can a Davidsonian interpreter absorb before the framework strains, and what does the film suggest the cost of that strain is?

6. Be honest about where the reading breaks down. In the novella, Heptapod B is semasiographic — writing not tied to speech at all — and the pivot is Fermat's Principle of Least Time, which lets Louise infer a teleological rather than causal outlook. Radical interpretation is built on observed utterances and behavior in a common environment; does it even apply to a written channel detached from speech? Separately: does Davidson have anything to say about the strong linguistic-relativity claim the film leans on, or is that orthogonal to his project, so that citing him here is a category error? And there is a tension I cannot resolve: the film's premise that a scheme can be so alien that acquiring it rewires cognition looks like precisely the incoherent idea Davidson attacks, yet the plot requires the scheme to be learnable. Does the film refute itself, or is it making something subtler?

7. Close by saying what the work contributes that argument alone does not. My own suspicion is that *Arrival* shows a translation manual being adopted for reasons that are not evidential at all — Louise commits to the heptapod reading partly because it makes a life she has already grieved worth living, and partly because other people's survival depends on it — and that Davidson's picture of interpretation as answerable to evidence has no obvious place for a commitment of that kind.

Some constraints. Aim for roughly 1,600 to 2,000 words, organized under short headings. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; you have no way to check them and neither do I. Do not quote the film or the novella — paraphrase scenes and describe the logograms rather than reproducing lines of dialogue. If you are unsure of a detail of either work or of Davidson's position, say so plainly instead of inventing it. And please do not flatter the thesis: I would rather have one section that dismantles my triangulation idea cleanly than four that nod along with it.
