Question 4 (25 marks): A defender of the "quantum suicide" argument claims that an Everettian agent who repeatedly exposes herself to a lethal device triggered by a spin measurement should, from the inside, expect to observe herself surviving every round. Reconstruct the argument, and explain at least two reasons why the step from "some branch contains a survivor" to "I should expect to survive" is contested. Be explicit about the role of branch weight.

Student's answer:

"Everett's interpretation says the wavefunction never collapses. The whole universe is one state that evolves unitarily, and what we call outcomes are just terms in that state which have decohered from each other and so can no longer interfere. Nothing gets actualised to the exclusion of the rest: all the outcomes happen, in different branches. So if I set up a device that measures the spin of an electron prepared in an equal superposition of up and down, and releases a poison capsule on 'up', then after the measurement there is a branch in which I am dead and a branch in which I carry on, and the second is no less real than the first.

The quantum suicide argument runs like this. From the outside, an observer watching me repeat the experiment n times sees the frequencies the Born rule predicts, and the branches in which I survive all n rounds have weight (1/2)^n. But from the inside the situation is different, because a dead person has no experiences. Whatever it is that has my experiences after round n has to sit in one of the surviving branches, so the only observations I can ever make are observations of survival. Hence I should expect with certainty to find myself alive after every round, however improbable that is on the ordinary quantum statistics. That is quantum immortality, and it falls out of Everett together with the banal requirement that experience needs a survivor. It does not work in Bohmian mechanics or in a genuine collapse theory like GRW, since there only one outcome is ever real and so there is a straightforward chance of dying.

Two objections are usually pressed. First, decoherence does not carve the world up into a unique set of branches: the splitting is vague and depends on the basis, so it is not clear that 'the branches where I survive' even picks out a definite number of futures. Second, surviving is not the same as surviving intact, because in the very low-weight branches where I keep beating the odds I will have accumulated brain damage, and by round ten the survivor is a poor specimen.

Neither objection really bites. Deutsch and Wallace's decision-theoretic derivation shows that a rational Everettian agent ought to give equal credence to each of the branches decoherence produces, so the vagueness of the branching is not a threat; and since I am certain to be among the survivors, the chance that I am dead after n rounds is (1/2)^n, which tends to zero for large n, so in the limit death is a measure-zero event for me. If what an agent cares about is her own continued existence, and she knows she will have it, the prudential upshot follows: I should volunteer for the experiment. I grant that in the overwhelming majority of branches by weight there is a version of me who is dead, and that her death is a terrible loss, but the argument does not have to ignore that, because those losses are in branches I will not be in."
