Retired physicist here — did thirty-odd years in low temperature work, now reading philosophy full time, which has turned out to be more humbling than I expected. I spent a few weeks building an argument I was quite pleased with. Then my daughter's friend, who's a grad student in the subject, read it and said the trouble wasn't that it was wrong but that it proved far too much, and that I'd be in the same position if I applied it to my own field. I've been chewing on that for a month and I still can't tell who's right.

The target is the possible-worlds account of belief content: that what a belief says is fixed by the set of ways the world might be that it rules out, so to believe p is to have ruled out every possibility in which p fails.

(1) The content of a belief is a set of possibilities. Believing p is being in a state that rules out every possibility in which p is false, and nothing else does that job.
(2) If p entails q, every possibility in which q fails is a possibility in which p fails.
(3) So a state that rules out all the possibilities in which p fails thereby rules out all the possibilities in which q fails.
(4) Therefore anyone who believes p believes every consequence of p. Since everything entails every logical and mathematical truth, everyone who believes anything believes all of mathematics.
(5) But I've believed classical statistical mechanics since I was an undergraduate, and there are consequences of it I only came to see in the last decade and others I'd still miss if you put them in front of me. Nobody in 1900 believed all of it while believing any of it. And when they finally did grind out some of the consequences — the specific heats that equipartition commits you to — the numbers came out wrong.
(6) So (1) is false: belief is not a matter of ruling out possibilities.
(7) But (1) is the only account I've found that says what content actually is, rather than pointing at dispositions or at what sentences in the head do. Take it away and I don't see what's left for "S believes that p" to mean.
(8) So there's no determinate fact about what anyone's beliefs are about.

Her objection, as best I could follow it: (2) through (6) is exactly the argument that would finish off every idealizing model I ever used. There are no point masses, no frictionless planes, no ideal gases, and no perfectly rigid bodies; the models attribute to the world things the world does not have; if that's grounds for saying the model is not about anything, then physics has been producing nonsense for three centuries and nobody noticed. So the argument proves too much, and (6) to (8) collapse along with the kinetic theory of gases.

I don't think that's right and I can't say why. A frictionless plane is a simplification I choose knowingly, with a small parameter I can go back and expand in; the account in (1) is offered as an analysis of what belief is, and there's no correction term — it says I already believe the Riemann hypothesis, and there's no epsilon to fix it with. But if I insist that being an idealization is no excuse here, I owe a story about what makes the idealization harmless in physics and fatal in epistemology, and the only story I can come up with is that the agent hasn't done the work of seeing the consequence — which sounds to me like I've stopped talking about content and started talking about effort.

So, three things, if you can. Which step goes wrong, or if none of them does, which one should I drop and what does dropping it cost? I want to keep (1) — it's the only thing that makes "what a belief is about" feel like a real question — and I want to keep (5), because I'm about as sure of my own case as I am of anything. I keep circling back to (3) but I honestly couldn't tell you whats wrong with it, and if you pressed me I'd defend it. Second: is the idealization reply from my own field actually available here, or am I right that it isn't, and if it is available, what makes it non-arbitrary? Third: suppose the fix is to make the space of possibilities finer-grained somehow — I've seen the phrase "impossible worlds" used, and something about putting awareness into the formalism as an operator. Does that solve the problem or relocate it, given that I'd then have to say which of these finer-grained things I've ruled out, and I'm not obviously in a better position to know that than I was to know the consequences of my own theories?
