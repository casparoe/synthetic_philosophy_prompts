I work as a staff engineer on the data platform at a mid-sized company — dashboards, metric definitions, experiment infrastructure. I have no philosophy training; the last humanities course I took was a compulsory writing seminar twenty years ago. I picked up John Dewey's *The Public and Its Problems* (1927; the prefatory note says it grew out of lectures delivered in January 1926 and was then fully revised and expanded) after an argument at work in which I said that two teams looking at the same dataset could not rationally reach opposite conclusions about whether a project was succeeding, and a colleague said I was being naive about what data does. I am stuck on the opening sentences of Chapter I, "Search for the Public," which read as follows:

If one wishes to realize the distance which may lie between "facts" and the meaning of facts, let one go to the field of social discussion. Many persons seem to suppose that facts carry their meaning along with themselves on their face. Accumulate enough of them, and their interpretation stares out at you. The development of physical science is thought to confirm the idea. But the power of physical facts to coerce belief does not reside in the bare phenomena. It proceeds from method, from the technique of research and calculation.

I have read this maybe thirty times and I can paraphrase each sentence but I cannot say what the argument is. Please help me understand the passage rather than the book or the author. Specifically:

- What work is the scare-quote on "facts" doing in the first sentence? Is Dewey doubting that there are facts, or flagging that what gets called a fact in social discussion is already an interpretation, or something else? I want to know which misreadings that punctuation rules out.
- Who are the "many persons"? Is the opponent a philosophical position — some variety of empiricism, or a view about how evidence and belief are related — or is it a social habit of mind, the way newspapers and committees talk? Does it matter which, for how the rest of the paragraph should be read?
- The pivot confuses me most. He says the power of physical facts to coerce belief does not reside in the bare phenomena but proceeds from method. Is he conceding that physical facts do coerce belief, and merely relocating the source of that power? What does "method" cover here — controlled experiment, instrumentation, the mathematics, or the organized practice of a research community that checks and repeats? And is this a claim about how justification works, or a claim about what kinds of institutions produce belief?
- Then he turns to social discussion. What exactly does he think is missing there: a method, a public able to use one, or a mechanism for turning findings into belief? I cannot tell whether the gap he is describing is epistemic or political, and I suspect he is moving between the two. If so, tell me where the slide happens and whether it is a weakness or deliberate.
- Why would a book about the state and the public open this way? The contents entries for this chapter include "Theory in terms of perceived consequences," "Criterion of the public," "Function of the state," and "The state as an experimental problem." What does the rest of the chapter's machinery owe to these opening sentences — that is, what has to be true about facts and method for any of those later items to make sense?
- Does the passage commit him to any of the positions I would be embarrassed to be caught holding, such as relativism about evidence, or the view that all facts are constructions, or that more data cannot settle a dispute? Given the sentence about physical facts coercing belief, which of these is excluded and which is still live?
- I keep being told this book is a response to Lippmann's writings on public opinion. Tell me whether that actually helps with these particular sentences or whether it is background noise at this level.
- Finally, flag any word whose force depends on 1927 usage that I am probably misreading — "coerce," "social discussion," "interpretation stares out at you" — and flag where my questions above show me over-reading because I do not know the vocabulary.

One practical thing I would like settled, though it is secondary: my instinct on first reading was that this is an argument that dashboards are useless. I doubt that is right, and I would like to know what the correct conclusion is for someone whose default assumption is that enough well-collected data settles the question.
