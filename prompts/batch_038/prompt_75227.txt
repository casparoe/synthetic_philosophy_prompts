I've just come out of a three-hour argument with a friend after a screening of Citizen Kane, and I'd like a sober second opinion, because neither of us budged and I suspect we are each half right. Our reading group had spent the previous term on Plutarch's Lives — specifically on the methodological declaration at the opening of Alexander's Life, where he says he is writing lives and not histories, and that a slight thing, a remark or a jest, often discloses character more reliably than the marshalling of armies or the taking of cities; and, pulling the other way, the preface to Aemilius Paulus, where he describes reading the great lives as holding up a mirror, selecting out of each whatever is noblest and best attested so that the reader's own character is adorned in the process. My friend's position is that Kane is a Plutarchan picture that comes out right: the sled is precisely the slight thing, the discarded toy rather than the newspaper empire, that tells us the man more truly than any of his public deeds, and the film vindicates the biographer's faith that character is legible in small gestures. Mine is that the film is a systematic refutation of the project — that it shows the moral mirror breaking, since Thompson never learns what Rosebud meant and openly allows that no single word will account for a man, while we in the audience are handed the one answer the biographer is denied. On my reading, then, the film makes moral understanding of a life available only to someone who is not trying to learn anything from it.

Read the film through this lens and tell me where each of us has hold of something true. I'd like you to take its actual architecture seriously: the newsreel obituary as an official, commissioned, edifying life; the handful of witnesses whose testimonies Thompson assembles and who each hand him a Kane filtered through loyalty, resentment, or sheer incomprehension; and the final sequence in which the contents of Xanadu are sorted, crated, and burned. Is Thompson better understood as a Plutarch who fails, or as something else — for instance, as a figure whose own moral education is the real product of the inquiries, since by the end he has absorbed Bernstein's affection, Leland's contempt and Susan's humiliation without acquiring any verdict about his subject at all? I keep returning to a suggestion that is, so far as I know, my own rather than anyone's in print: that Kane is a synkrisis with the second term missing, so that the parallel lives in the film are not Kane and some Roman counterpart but Kane and each of his witnesses, and the ethical work happens on the witness's side of the comparison rather than the subject's. Does that hold up, or am I forcing a Hellenistic form onto a modernist film? And how much of the film's resistance to moral learning is resistance to hagiography in particular — the suppression of the unedifying detail in favour of an icon — given that Rosebud arguably gives the audience exactly the icon Thompson refuses to build?

I am not after a bibliography or a survey of what critics have said; I want your own reading, argued through the film's scenes and formal choices, and I want you to be honest about where the Plutarchan frame illuminates the picture and where it distorts it, including whether I have flattened Plutarch into a naive exemplarist he was not, since he freely records faults and discreditable episodes in the very lives he calls mirrors. Please also say how confident you are in the main claims you are advancing, and single out the point you are least sure of — flagging it especially if the shakiness concerns a detail of the film rather than the interpretation. I have seen Kane many times, but I am reconstructing some of the sequence from memory, and I would much rather you mark a doubtful recollection than build an argument on top of it.
