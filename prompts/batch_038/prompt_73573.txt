I am circulating a short statement of my own position on nuclear deterrence in advance of a workshop at which the other participants will be asked to attack it. Before I send it, I would like a rigorous consistency audit. I am not asking for background, history, or references of any kind; I want to know which of the following I can hold at once and which I cannot.

(1) If deterrence is justified at all, it is justified by what it prevents: the absence of great-power war that would otherwise have occurred. I give no independent weight to retribution, to keeping a pledge as such, or to the expressive value of standing by one's word.

(2) A threat restrains an adversary only insofar as the adversary believes it would be executed, and across long horizons and repeated crises that belief can be sustained only if it is true — if the threatening state really is disposed to execute. An announced intention one does not have may work once; it cannot serve as the settled policy of a state that expects to be tested. I therefore reject bluff as a foundation for deterrence, on both practical and moral grounds.

(3) Judged as a single act at the moment of decision, retaliation after a first strike that has already destroyed one's cities and one's chain of command cannot restore, compensate, or prevent anything. It can only add deaths. Such an act would be wrong.

(4) I do not think (1) through (3) amount to a paradox, because the proper unit of assessment is the standing policy adopted beforehand rather than the act performed afterward. Adopting and holding fast to a retaliatory disposition is what produces the good consequences, and the fact that executing it would later be pointless does not make holding it irrational. A disposition that dissolves at precisely the moment it is called upon was never worth acquiring, and part of what one buys in acquiring it is the undertaking not to reassess it later on grounds that were foreseeable from the start.

(5) It is impermissible to impose grave risk of death on people who cannot consent and who are not parties to the decision, as a means of securing one's own safety. I take intending harm and merely foreseeing risk to be morally different things, and I rely on that difference.

(6) What actually restrains an adversary in a crisis is seldom a deterministic threat. It is the deliberate generation of a shared and only partly controlled risk of catastrophe: the credible possibility that events pass the point at which anyone is still deciding. I think this is the best account of why crises have stopped short of war, and I think states do manage their crises this way and are right to.

(7) On the historical record: the decades of superpower deterrence produced no direct war between them, and I regard that as a structural feature of the balance rather than a run of good fortune. At the same time, I take it that the same period contained episodes in which catastrophe was averted only by chance, by a malfunction being correctly discounted, or by the judgment of one individual.

(8) A case verdict: a ballistic-missile submarine commander who has lost all communication, whose country has been destroyed, and who retains both standing orders and the means, should not launch. I also think that a posture in which commanders of that kind would reliably not launch is a posture an adversary can plan around.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Identify the pairs and clusters that genuinely cannot stand together, and rank them by severity instead of presenting them as a flat list of worries. For each, state precisely what has to give, then give the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and say what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Where two commitments only look incompatible, explain why they are not, and name the distinction that dissolves the appearance — I would much rather be told that a pair is sound than see it flagged alongside everything else. Finally, tell me which single revision would remove the most tension across the whole set, and whether any one of these commitments is doing unacknowledged work inside the others.
