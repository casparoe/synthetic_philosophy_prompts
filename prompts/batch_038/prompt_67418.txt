For a novel I am writing, I need a scene built almost entirely out of argument. The setting: a research hut above a fjord, containing an apparatus that has predicted the movements of a local seal population for forty years with a reliability nobody can account for. The builders are dead, the design notes burned, and the casing cannot be opened without destroying what is inside. What survives is the machine and forty years of ledgers. Two characters stand before it — the station's last technician and a former graduate student of the philosophy of science — and both know the vocabulary of the physical, design, and intentional stances well enough to be dangerous.

The technician is the realist about the patterns the ledgers disclose: for him the machine is genuinely tracking something, the seals have states that answer to belief-and-desire attributions in a sense worth taking seriously, and reliable predictability of the right sort is exactly what makes such patterns real rather than decorative. The ex-student is the sceptic: a pattern whose reliability no one can explain is a shadow cast by the way the ledgers happened to be kept, and realism about higher-level patterns is earned only where a design history or an accessible mechanism shows why the predictions succeed.

Write their dialogue. Neither may be a strawman, and neither may lecture. Each should locate and press the other's genuine soft spot — the sceptic on the fact that four decades of accuracy have never once been explained, only recorded, and that the categories in the ledger were chosen by the hands writing in it; the realist on the fact that his opponent has never said what a mechanism would contribute that the pattern does not already supply, and cannot say so without smuggling in a verificationist account of explanation. Keep them anchored to the object in front of them: the sealed casing, the columns of figures, the dead builders. I want the exchange to close with neither converted, but with an unintended concession from each.
