> Walzer argues that combatants on both sides of a war, whether their cause is just or unjust, have an equal right to kill one another, and that non-combatants on both sides are equally immune from attack. Explain this thesis and the argument Walzer gives for it. Then explain McMahan's revisionist objection to the thesis, and say whether accepting that objection gives us reason to change the law of armed conflict. Your answer should keep the moral claim and the legal claim distinct.

The response below was submitted anonymously as Candidate 14. I have transcribed it from the handwritten script; any infelicities of wording are mine rather than the student's.

> Walzer's moral equality of combatants is the claim that the permissions and prohibitions governing conduct within a war are independent of the justice of the cause for which one fights. A soldier defending his country against aggression and a soldier in the invading army stand in the same moral relation to one another: each may attack the other, and neither wrongs the other merely by doing so, provided each observes the same restrictions. The thesis has a mirror image, which is the immunity of non-combatants. Because the liability of combatants does not track the justice of their side, the immunity of non-combatants does not track it either: the civilians of the aggressor state are as protected as the civilians of the victim state. And since that immunity admits of no exceptions on Walzer's account, the only people who may be attacked in war are combatants, while every combatant may be attacked by any other.
>
> Walzer supports the thesis with two arguments. The first begins from the fact that combatants are dangerous. What makes a person a combatant is that he poses a threat of harm, and it is that threat which makes him liable to be attacked. Soldiers on both sides pose threats of the same kind and of comparable magnitude, so they are equally liable and equally permitted; the symmetry of the war convention follows from the symmetry of the danger. The second argument concerns responsibility rather than danger. The wrong of aggression is committed by the political leaders who decide upon and launch a war, not by the individual soldiers who fight it, since most soldiers neither choose their war nor have reliable access to the facts that would establish whether their state's cause is just, and many are conscripted or subject to intense legal and social pressure. A rule that permitted killing on one side only could not function as a shared rule of war in any case, since two armies fighting each other must be able to observe the same conventions. Aggression and conduct in war are therefore assessed separately, and a state may fight an unjust war while observing every rule of jus in bello, or a just war while breaking all of them.
>
> McMahan objects that the deeper premise of this picture is false. Liability to defensive killing is not generated by dangerousness alone; it depends on an individual's moral responsibility for an unjust threat. The justice of a combatant's cause therefore settles the question: whoever fights for an unjust cause is liable to be killed, and whoever fights for a just cause is not, so the two sides are not morally symmetrical at any point. An unjust combatant who kills a just combatant does something close to murder, while the just combatant who kills him acts permissibly. McMahan accordingly distinguishes the true, or deep, morality of war from the conventional view that the law of armed conflict encodes, and he holds that the conventional view is morally false.
>
> His conclusion is that the law should be brought into line with the deep morality: unjust combatants should forfeit their permission to fight and their prisoner-of-war protections, and just combatants should be legally entitled to strike at those responsible for the unjust war. Walzer's defence of the conventional view is, on this reading, a concession to practical limits, since he thinks soldiers cannot be trusted with the truth. The dispute is thus not about moral principle but about implementation, and both authors agree that the moral equality of combatants is false in itself and necessary in practice.
