For most of the past decade I have covered research institutions for a national magazine, which means I spend a great deal of time inside professional societies whose rituals look baffling from the outside. Two years ago, at a friend's request, I agreed to sit as the lay member of the programme committee for a mid-sized society in one of the biological sciences — roughly nine hundred members, a single annual meeting, some four hundred submitted abstracts each year. I would like your advice about a dispute I am now in the middle of, though I should warn you at the outset that I am not confident the dispute is about what the disputants believe it is about.

The meeting has kept the same shape since at least the early 1980s. Every accepted paper receives twenty minutes plus ten minutes of questions; talks run in parallel across two rooms; and the order of the programme is fixed so that the same four or five senior laboratories close each day's session. Almost everyone I speak to, senior and junior alike, tells me they dislike the arrangement. The twenty-minute slot is too short for methods-heavy work and absurdly long for a null result; the parallel tracks mean any given member hears at most half the meeting; and the closing positions effectively assign the largest audiences to a handful of groups. In each of the last three years a motion has been tabled to replace the whole thing — single track, poster-heavy, longer talks allocated partly by lottery — and in each of the last three years it has lapsed, notably without anyone rising to speak in favour of the status quo. Last year the committee attempted a compromise: an opt-in short-format session reserved for early-career speakers. It was oversubscribed the first year, half empty the second, and has now been quietly discontinued. The members who participated tell me they were not penalised so much as sorted: taking part seemed to communicate something about the kind of researcher one was.

The two camps now describe this history in incompatible terms, and because I am the one member with no stake in the field, I have been asked to prepare a short note saying which account is closer to the truth. The first camp holds that the format is simply how the society does things: nobody designed it, nobody polices it, each member is behaving sensibly given what every other member can be expected to do, and it is therefore a confusion to call the outcome unjust — the failed motions, on this view, show only how difficult it is to get nine hundred people to move at the same moment. The second camp holds that this is a consoling fiction: the format persists because those who gain most from it also decide whether it changes, the opt-in experiment collapsed because participation was costly to the participants and free to the people with standing, and all the talk about everyone merely adjusting to everyone else is doing ideological work. My difficulty is that both stories appear to fit every fact I have, and I cannot see what would count as evidence between them. Is there any way to tell, from inside an organisation like this, whether a durable practice of this kind is an arrangement that all of its participants are jointly trapped in, or a rule that some participants are holding in place against the interests of others — and if the distinction is not a clean one, what does the mixed case look like? I would also value your view on a narrower question that the first camp keeps pressing: suppose the whole format really is arbitrary, in the sense that some quite different arrangement would serve the society's stated purposes equally well. Does that fact alone give the committee any reason to keep the present one, or any reason to abandon it? Please treat this as a question about how to think rather than as a request for meeting-design tips; what I most need is a way of separating possibilities that currently look identical from where I sit.
