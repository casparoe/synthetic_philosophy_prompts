I am a third-year doctoral student in history. My dissertation chapter concerns a regional water authority that deferred maintenance on an aging spillway for the better part of a decade; when the structure failed, the flooding killed nine people. The chapter argues that those deferrals were not merely unfortunate but unreasonable — that the authority's own internal inspections had already surfaced enough for a competent administrator to act. My advisor returned the draft with a single marginal note where that argument appears: "Hindsight is 20/20 — you are narrating an ending you already know." He has asked me either to withdraw the claim or to rebuild it from the ground up. I would rather rebuild, but I cannot determine what exactly he is objecting to, and the specifics of my case are beside the point here; I am asking about the sentence he wrote.

The proverb seems to me to do several incompatible jobs at once. Taken literally, 20/20 denotes ordinary visual acuity, so the sentence appears to assert that retrospective judgment is clear and accurate; in practice it is almost always deployed to deflate that judgment, as a reason not to hold anyone to it. My own guess is that my advisor meant one of three different things — a psychological point (knowing the outcome distorts how I now read the earlier documents), an evidential point (what I cite was not available to, or not probative for, the actors at the time of decision), or a methodological point (my reasoning would not survive a change in the ending) — but I cannot tell which, and they call for very different repairs.

Please separate the distinct precise claims this proverb could be expressing, as a menu rather than a single favored reading, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis about retrospective judgment, or simply false, marking where the literal content of "20/20" and its ordinary deflationary use come apart. Then identify the reading my advisor most plausibly invoked, say whether under that reading his objection to my chapter succeeds, and describe what a historical claim about a past decision would have to look like in order to be immune to it — assuming any could be. If the objection, pressed hard, would condemn all explanation of past decisions rather than only mine, say so explicitly and explain why.

End your response with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of: objection succeeds, objection fails, objection unfalsifiable.
