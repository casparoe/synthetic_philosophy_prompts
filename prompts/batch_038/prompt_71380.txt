I am preparing a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship application in the philosophy of the social sciences, and the proposal section has a hard limit of 1,200 words. The readers are six people: four philosophers, only one of whom works on Popper, one historian of twentieth-century science, and one economist. I would like you to write the proposal out of a half-formed idea of mine — and to sharpen the idea where it is soft rather than simply expanding it.

The idea concerns Popper's case against historicism, in particular the argument that runs through the growth of knowledge: the course of history is strongly influenced by the growth of human knowledge; we cannot predict the future growth of our own knowledge by rational or scientific methods; therefore we cannot predict the future course of history. My worry is that the quarrel over this argument has been conducted at the wrong level of generality, because the phrase "a law of history" is doing at least three different jobs. Distinguish:

(i) sequential laws — lawlike generalizations linking conditions to transitions, of the form: whenever a society has features C, it passes into form F;

(ii) mechanism laws — invariant causal generalizations about processes at work inside societies at all times, concerning for instance how institutions respond to scarcity, which are silent about any ordering of stages;

(iii) developmental laws — generalizations about the trajectory of a society as a whole, entailing both a direction and a unique ordering of its stages.

Popper plainly attacks (iii), and his insistence that a statement asserting a trend at a time and place is a singular historical statement rather than a universal law seems aimed at (i). What I cannot see is whether the knowledge-growth argument has any purchase on (ii) at all. Nor is it clear that, where the argument does bite, it shows that such laws cannot exist, or only that they could not be known and used by inquirers who are themselves among the causes of the process they study. So I want the proposal organized around a second distinction of my own, which you are free to rename or restructure. Call a candidate law feedback-transparent if its coming to be believed by the agents over whose behaviour it generalizes leaves its extension unchanged, and feedback-opaque otherwise. My hunch: Popper's argument is a metaphysical result for feedback-opaque laws and merely an epistemic one for feedback-transparent laws, and the historicisms he actually argued against claimed laws of the first kind, while the piecemeal social science he was willing to countenance trades in the second.

The proposal needs to contain: a precise statement of the question, phrased so that it has a determinate answer rather than an agenda; two or three sentences in your own words giving the committee the argument I am targeting, since most of them will not know it; an account of why the standard readings of Popper on this point leave my question open, described as positions and arguments rather than as names and dates; the method, laid out in four three-month blocks, saying what I would actually be doing in each; the alternatives I rejected — close textual exegesis, the contemporary social-ontology debate about mechanisms, the literature on self-defeating and self-fulfilling social predictions — and why this approach rather than those; what a successful result would look like as a deliverable; and at least three concrete ways the project could fail, of the sort that would show the framing itself was wrong rather than merely that the argument resisted.

Give me no bibliography and no reference list; I will attach citations myself. Do not put any of Popper's words in quotation marks — if you need one of his formulations, render it as close paraphrase. Prose throughout, except that the timetable and the failure conditions may be lists.

Two further things. Tell me honestly whether a small formal model of feedback opacity — an agent forming beliefs about a law that governs belief formation — is worth building inside twelve months, or whether it is a trap that would consume the year and yield nothing the philosophers on the committee would credit; take a position rather than presenting options. And close with a section of no more than 150 words headed "Where this is most likely to go wrong," identifying the weakest step in the framing, saying which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about my central hunch, and — if you think that hunch is not defensible as I have stated it — saying so plainly and giving me the best nearby question that is.
