I have chaired the sickness-benefit appeals panel of a national research institute for two years; before that I spent thirty-five years directing a physiology laboratory, and I have come to the philosophy of medicine only recently and from that side. A case arrives next week that has split the panel, and I want a decision memorandum to circulate beforehand.

The appellant is a 43-year-old instrument engineer. Her thyroid panel, morning cortisol, HbA1c, actigraphy sleep measures, and peak oxygen uptake all sit inside the reference ranges, so the panel's medical adviser holds that there is no dysfunction and therefore no disease. Yet she cannot sustain more than about ninety minutes of concentrated work without a two-day collapse, and has been unable to work at all for fourteen months. Her representative argues the reverse: the loss of her ability to pursue the goals she has reason to want is itself sufficient for disease. The uncomfortable precedent is that the same adviser approved extended paid leave this year for two colleagues with asymptomatic hyperlipidemia and well-controlled hypertension — statistically deviant, subjectively unimpaired.

Please draft the panel's memorandum. It should decide the appeal; state the criterion of disease the panel ought to adopt for future cases, in a form members can actually apply to a file rather than debate in the abstract; show how that criterion disposes of the hyperlipidemia precedents consistently rather than by exception; and give the medical adviser's strongest objection — including his worry that an ability-based standard makes eligibility hostage to how the workplace happens to be organized, so that paid leave substitutes for accommodation — a full and honest answer.
