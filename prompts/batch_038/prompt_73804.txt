Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend — an economist by training — about whether there is any such thing as a market that is objectionable over and above the usual defects. His position, which he stated with real precision, is that a transaction is legitimate so long as both parties are adequately informed and neither acts under duress, and that every further objection is either a disguised version of those two or a bare recoil with no moral content: disgust at a trade, on his view, is a feeling to be explained rather than an argument to be answered. I do not accept this, but I could not produce a single case that he could not dissolve by observing that the seller was informed and free to walk away. I would rather test the question against you than against him.

Here are five proposals. For each, assign a number from 0 to 10, where 0 means the voluntariness-and-information defense is complete and the market is wholly legitimate, and 10 means something survives that defense and the market is seriously objectionable. Then give the ranking those numbers imply, from most to least legitimate, and for each case name the single residual objection doing the decisive work, choosing from: corruption of the meaning of the good traded; degradation of the seller's standing as an equal citizen; exploitation of a bargaining position the seller did not choose; crowding out of an intrinsic motive on which the practice itself depends; and opacity, meaning that the seller cannot now know the preferences they will later have. One or two sentences of reasoning per case is sufficient.

1. A municipality auctions the naming rights to individual classrooms in a public primary school. The winning bidder may rename the room annually, and the school must use the new name in all correspondence with parents.
2. A private fund pays a person holding a rent-controlled public housing unit a lump sum to surrender their place in the allocation queue to the highest-bidding applicant, with the municipal registry updated accordingly. Everyone still waiting moves back one position.
3. An employer pays a worker a monthly stipend in exchange for consent to a wearable device that delivers a mild shock whenever the worker's step count falls below a target or a cigarette is detected. Participation is nominally optional and does not affect pay or promotion.
4. A convicted person pays a licensed, screened operator to serve the custodial portion of their sentence in their place. The operator consents and receives a competitive wage.
5. A firm pays workers to complete a course of treatment that permanently lowers their ambitions and expectations, on the grounds that contented workers are more productive and report higher wellbeing.

If none of the five objections genuinely fits a case, say so and name the objection you would have to add. Finally, tell me which of the five you think is underdetermined by this taxonomy — the one where your number owes more to a prior you could not defend than to the reasoning you have just given.
