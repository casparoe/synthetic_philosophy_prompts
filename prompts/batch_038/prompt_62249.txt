I spent thirty-four years in analytical chemistry, the last fifteen of them running a calibration laboratory at a national measurement institute, and I have been retired for two years now, spending the time auditing seminars at the university. Lately I have been reading the literature on whether assertion is governed by a norm of knowledge, of truth, of reasonable belief, or by no constitutive norm at all. I find the debate absorbing but oddly unworldly: nearly every case discussed is a flat-footed binary proposition about weather, barns, or lottery tickets, and I cannot see how any of these accounts was designed to handle the kind of speech act my entire professional life consisted of.

Consider the certificate. A metrologist signs a document reporting the mass of an artefact as a value together with an expanded uncertainty, computed with a coverage factor of two, which on the usual normal assumption corresponds to roughly ninety-five per cent coverage. Her field's own governing guide characterises that uncertainty as a parameter describing the dispersion of the values that could reasonably be attributed to the measurand, and elsewhere glosses it as reflecting a lack of exact knowledge — a gloss whose epistemic, near-subjectivist reading is contested inside metrology itself. Now: what has this woman asserted? She does not know that the true mass lies inside the interval; on the standard reading it is not even correct to say she believes it, since the interval was built to admit a residual chance of failure. Yet issuing the statement is not merely permissible, it is obligatory, and a refusal to state the uncertainty would be the grave dereliction. Add a second case from the same trade: a colleague reports a trace contaminant as present because the measured signal exceeded the laboratory's decision limit, while knowing the false-positive rate at that limit is five per cent — a practice codified in the laboratory's procedures and treated by everyone as scrupulous rather than reckless.

What I want is the specification I cannot extract from that literature: the desiderata any account of the norm governing assertion must satisfy if it is to be good enough to adjudicate questions of proper scientific reporting, rather than merely to classify conversational exchanges. Please set out the requirements — the phenomena such an account must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it may not collapse, the established results it must remain consistent with — and give, for each, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a stylistic preference. I would particularly like to know whether an account must say what the content of a quantitatively hedged or probabilistically graded assertion is, and whether a hedge changes the content asserted or lowers the epistemic standard applied to it; if you think either of these is a genuine requirement, say so explicitly and show what it costs.

For each desideratum, mark it as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a prejudice peculiar to one camp — for instance, whether a norm of assertion must be action-guiding seems to me to be exactly the sort of demand that should be argued for rather than assumed. Flag any subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, since if the list itself is a result that matters more to me than the survey. Finally, and briefly, tell me how the four principal contenders fare against the list, with particular attention to the certificate and decision-limit cases.

Begin with your bottom line: one sentence naming the single requirement you take to be doing the most exclusionary work and why, followed by the full specification and the short scoring. No preamble, and no restatement of my question. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I want the reasoning laid bare, and where you are uncertain about a matter of fact from my trade, say so rather than papering over it.
