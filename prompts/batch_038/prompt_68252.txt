I work in infrastructure rather than philosophy, though a friend pointed me at Davidson's radical interpretation and the principle of charity a while back, and those ideas have attached themselves to a problem I keep running into on the job. I'd like a short story of about 1,200 to 1,800 words, written in American English, that dramatizes the problem instead of lecturing about it.

Here is the premise I'd like you to take as given; invent everything else. My team supports a legacy routing service, GATE-7, whose design document was lost years ago. The only handle on what any part of it does is what it does: the log lines it emits, the packets it forwards, the alerts it suppresses. Nobody has testimony, code comments, or original intent. Two engineers separately build complete readings of a three-year archive. Each accounts for every entry without a single failure, and the two are not cosmetic variants of one another. Under one reading, a heartbeat field is expected to return within 40 milliseconds, and GATE-7 has been silently compensating for a failure mode that stopped occurring long ago. Under the other, that field means something quite different, and the same behavior is the service correctly doing its job. One reading makes GATE-7 systematically wrong about its own environment; the other makes it right about everything and merely strange in its vocabulary.

Please let the story turn on the moment the distinction stops being academic — an external audit, a proposal to strip out the compensation logic, an engineer who has to page someone at 3 a.m. and pick a reading — and let the characters argue for real, including a forceful case that one reading simply is correct and the team is indulging in defeatism. I want a reader to feel why no further evidence seems able to settle it, and to notice that the interpreter's decision to keep the system largely rational is quietly doing labor that otherwise presents itself as discovery. Don't have anyone name the philosopher or state a moral at the end.
