**The question the student was answering** (take-home, roughly 700 words, seminar readings only):

> Call a situation a *genuine moral dilemma* just in case an agent in it is subject to two all-things-considered moral requirements that cannot both be satisfied. Some philosophers hold that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible; others hold that they are commonplace.
>
> (a) Reconstruct the standard argument for the impossibility claim, and identify every premise that a friend of genuine dilemmas might contest.
>
> (b) Explain how a Rossian account — prima facie duties, one of which becomes the agent's actual duty — handles tragic conflict, and how that account differs from accepting genuine dilemmas.
>
> (c) Does moral residue — compensation owed, apology, or agent-regret that survives a choice made in tragic circumstances — show that genuine moral dilemmas exist? Argue for your answer.

**The student's answer:**

> (a) The impossibility argument runs through the logic of obligation itself. Take a case in which the agent cannot both keep a promise he made and prevent a serious harm, and suppose he is subject to an all-things-considered requirement to do each. Obligations agglomerate: from "Jim is required to keep the promise" and "Jim is required to prevent the harm" it follows that Jim is required to do both. And ought implies can: if he is required to do both, he can do both. By hypothesis he cannot. So the situation as described is incoherent, and one of the premises must be surrendered.
>
> The premise a friend of dilemmas should surrender is "ought implies can." Nothing else has to move. Rejecting agglomeration — holding that the two requirements, though each genuine, do not combine into a requirement to do both — looks like the cheaper exit, but it buys nothing: an all-things-considered verdict is a single verdict, and an account that refuses to deliver one has not described a conflict between requirements, it has described a case that remains unresolved. Refusing "ought implies can" costs more, but it says what the dilemmist actually wants to say: that the agent is required to do something he cannot do, and that this is a tragedy rather than a contradiction.
>
> (b) Ross's strategy is to deny that both requirements in a tragic case are genuine in the relevant sense. On his picture, what we ordinarily call duties — keeping promises, not harming, promoting the good, repaying kindness — are prima facie duties: real and objective moral considerations, each with genuine weight, none of them an all-things-considered verdict by itself. In any situation several of them apply; the agent's actual duty is the one supported by the most stringent of the applicable prima facie duties, and the others are overridden rather than cancelled. The contradiction in (a) therefore never gets started: at the level of actual duty there is only ever one requirement, and one requirement cannot conflict with itself. Ross's account is not a theory of dilemmas but a theory of how to avoid needing them.
>
> It is worth noticing how much Ross gives up to get this. He concedes that we frequently cannot determine which of the applicable prima facie duties is the more stringent, and that our best judgment may be wrong. Where the agent cannot tell, there is no available fact of the matter about what he ought to do all things considered. Sartre's student is exactly this case: nothing in the moral landscape decides between staying with the mother who depends on him and leaving to join the Free French, and Sartre's insistence is that he must choose without the support of any rule. If a dilemma is a situation in which the agent has no determinate all-things-considered requirement to act on, then Ross has dilemmas in abundance and merely declines to name them.
>
> (c) Moral residue does show that genuine dilemmas exist, and it shows this by undermining the Rossian description of the case rather than by attacking any logical principle. After Sartre's student decides to stay, he is not quit of the Free French: he owes an explanation and an apology, and in the right circumstances a later contribution to the cause. If the requirement to join them had simply been outweighed and put aside at the moment of decision, as the Rossian description has it, there would be nothing outstanding to make up, since a reason that has been outweighed is a reason the agent has no further business acting on. That something is owed afterwards shows that it was never discharged. Two undischarged all-things-considered requirements that cannot both be met is precisely the situation the question describes, so the impossibility argument must fail somewhere — most likely, as in (a), at "ought implies can."
>
> Williams's agent-regret makes the same point from the other side. The lorry driver who kills a child through no fault of his own is not blameworthy, yet the response appropriate to him is not the regret any bystander might feel but a first-personal regret that registers his own agency in what happened. Williams takes this to show that faultless wrongdoing is possible. It follows that an agent in a genuine dilemma does wrong whichever way he turns, without thereby being a bad agent — which is just to say that the requirement he could not satisfy did not lapse when he failed to satisfy it.
>
> Act-utilitarianism has no room for any of this. Ranking every act, it always delivers a determinate verdict, and the residue must be reduced either to a useful sentiment or to a forward-looking duty to keep the practice of apology going. But a duty to apologise that is really a duty to maximise good consequences is not a duty owed to the person one has wronged, and the driver's distinctive regret is evidence against that reduction.
