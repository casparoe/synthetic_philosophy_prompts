I am in the second year of an MA in philosophy and am currently sitting in a seminar on the foundations of cognitive science; this week is devoted to the frame problem. Our instructor assigned, as background rather than as primary reading, a chapter from a well-reviewed popular history of artificial intelligence, and the chapter opens with a compressed account of the problem that several of us found difficult to reconcile with what the seminar leader had just said. Here is the paragraph, transcribed as closely as I can manage:

> The frame problem is the discovery that a machine can never know which of its beliefs matter. When the first AI researchers tried to describe a changing world in logic, they found that writing down what an action does is easy, while writing down everything it does not do requires a separate axiom for every combination of action and property — a task that grows without limit as the world gets richer. Dennett pressed the point home with a sequence of thought-experiment robots, each better equipped than the last to enumerate the non-effects of what it does; every one of them is destroyed while still reasoning. The lesson, which the phenomenological critics of AI had already reached by other routes, is that relevance is not a relation between a belief and a fact but a global property of a whole system of beliefs: to work out what matters, you must already know what matters. Because no finite body of rules can capture that, and because human beings manage it without rules, the frame problem shows that computational models of the mind are impossible in principle. The name itself is telling — a frame is a way of carving up the world, and the problem is that every carving hides what another framing would have revealed.
