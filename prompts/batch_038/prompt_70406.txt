I serve on the Integration and Housing Access Panel of a city of roughly 400,000 — a body of eleven lay members that administers our register for the 400 municipally subsidized tenancies let each year. Allocation is by points, and the largest single component, worth up to ten points, is 'duration of residence': one point per completed year in the city, reckoned from arrival. For eleven years that component simply used the date someone first registered with the municipality, whatever immigration status they held at the time, because the panel took the view that status is a matter for the national authorities and not something a housing body should re-litigate. Last spring the housing department brought forward a proposal to change that. Years spent in the city while the applicant's stay was legally contingent — specifically, while they held a tolerated-status permit renewed every six months and carrying no right to remain beyond the current period — should now count at half weight. The stated grounds are that a contingent stay is not the same thing as a settled one, and that counting such years in full penalizes the cohort who held continuous, unconditional permits throughout. The panel votes in three weeks.

I spent most of last night arguing about this with another member, who is also an old friend, and we made almost no progress, largely because I now see that we never agreed what a point on this register is even supposed to measure: need, strength of local ties, or a reward for having queued in the sanctioned way. Her case, put as strongly as I can, is this. The register is not a moral ledger but an administrative device whose whole credibility depends on being seen to treat the two routes differently; otherwise the panel is effectively announcing that arriving without permission and staying is equivalent to arriving through a channel, and that announcement is not ours to make. She adds the political point, which I find harder to dismiss than I would like: the register's standing with the households who did everything by the book is what keeps those 400 units funded, and a scheme perceived as indifferent to lawful status will not survive the next budget round — at which point everyone on the register loses. My reply was that half-weighting does not track anything about ties at all, since both applicants have worked, paid local taxes, and enrolled children in the same schools, and that the rule converts an accident of processing times into a permanent compounding disadvantage, because the people who spent years in contingent status are overwhelmingly there because the state was slow rather than because they chose to be. I also noted that a tenants' association has said it will publish the register's effects broken down by country of origin, and on any such table the discount will look like a nationality penalty wearing a legal-status costume.

Two complications need to be dealt with rather than deferred. First, the department proposes to apply the discount only to contingent years ending after the coming January, on the grounds that older files cannot practicably be re-adjudicated. That produces a genuine cliff: two applicants whose permits lapsed weeks apart would be scored differently, and I do not yet know whether the memo should reject that, accept it as an unavoidable transitional cost, or propose something in between. Second, the panel is split five to four with two members uncommitted, and tabling the proposal simply carries the old rule into the autumn round by default, which my friend insists is itself a decision with distributive consequences rather than a neutral pause.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the other ten members ahead of the vote — somewhere around a thousand words, written for readers who are busy and have never studied political philosophy. It must come down clearly on one option: adopt the half-weight rule, reject it and retain full counting, or adopt an amended version. If amended, state the rule precisely enough that the housing department could implement it without exercising discretion; I want a scoring rule, not an aspiration. Before the recommendation, set out explicitly what the panel should take a point to measure, and explain why the choice among candidate answers does most of the work in the dispute. Then give my friend's argument its full due, treating the queue-fairness strand and the budget-survival strand separately, since I suspect they stand or fall differently, and say what my own objection costs if her political point is right. Close with a short paragraph on how the recommendation should be explained both to applicants who lose standing under the old rule and to those who gain under the new one, so the panel is not saying two incompatible things in public. I would rather not see references to academic literature, court decisions, or other countries' schemes: the panel cannot act on any of that, and I am not in a position to check it.
