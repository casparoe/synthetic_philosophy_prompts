I have spent fifteen years conserving paintings on panel and canvas, and I think in terms of accretion, patina, minimal intervention, reversibility, and whether a later overpainting belongs to the work's history or obscures it. I have no philosophy training. Restate the historians' dispute over presentism — whether one can describe and judge the past otherwise than through the standards and categories of one's own time — in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: what in a historical account would count as overpainting, what would count as a reversible treatment, and what it would mean to leave an accretion in place because it has by now become part of the object. Keep two things apart as you translate, since they may not travel together: moral presentism, the condemnation of past actors by norms they could not have known, and conceptual anachronism, the imposition of our categories on them — period labels, say, or the modern separation of science from religion (the word scientist dates only from the nineteenth century). Then give me the residue report: which claims map cleanly onto decisions I actually make at the bench, which you had to distort to fit, and which have no expression at all in my vocabulary — above all, whether the reversibility of a treatment has any counterpart in the retractability of a historical judgment. Close by saying whether the residue exposes a limitation in conservation thinking or suggests that the demand to escape one's own time was never coherent to begin with.
