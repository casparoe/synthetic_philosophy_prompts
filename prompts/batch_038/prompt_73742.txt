I'm an engineer, not a philosopher — I own the permissions and consent screens in a health-tracking app (~4M users, US and EU). I've spent three weeks arguing with legal and design about a redesign, and at some point I realized I do have an actual position on what makes a consent valid, I just never wrote it down. So I wrote it down. Before I take it into the review I want someone to check whether it hangs together, because I can feel that a couple of these don't and I can't tell which.

Here's the list:

(a) Consent is only real if the person actually understands what they're saying yes to. Not "had a fair chance to find out" — understands. This is the load-bearing one for me.

(b) Continued use after we publish updated terms counts as consent to those terms, as long as we notified them somewhere they'd reasonably see it. We can't ship a blocking modal more than about once a year or churn won't survive it, so consent has to be able to update without one.

(c) I think of it as versioning. Consent attaches to a version of an act. Material change — new category of data, new third party who recieves it — means new version, old consent doesn't carry, you need fresh uptake. Immaterial change (wording, bug fix) means same version, old consent carries.

(d) Lying only kills consent when the lie is about what the act actually is. Lie about your motives or about something collateral and the consent still stands.

(e) Awful background conditions don't invalidate consent. Bad options are still options, and "you only agreed because you had no money, no insurance, no alternative" isn't by itself a defeater. But I also think a valid consent requires the person to have at least one reasonable alternative to accepting.

(f) Someone can consent to something they don't want in the slightest, and that consent is perfectly good.

(g) Competence is per-decision, not per-person and not per-age. Same user, same session, can be competent to agree to sharing step counts and not competent to agree to sharing continuous location.

(h) If they didn't understand because they didn't bother, that's on them. It doesn't automatically void the yes.

(i) Making the decline path cost two more taps than the accept path is fine, as long as decline is always available and equally visible. That said, once someone has said yes, pulling it back has to be as easy as saying yes was — same number of taps, no phone call, no retention screen.

Two bits of context. On (h): we log this stuff. Roughly 2% of users ever open the full terms and median time on the consent screen is about four seconds. I've been calling the gap between the yeses we've banked and the yeses we'd have gotten if everyone had actually read them our "consent debt," and my position has basically been that we're allowed to carry some of it. And the redesign actually on the table is: split the current accept-everything screen into granular toggles for three data categories, and stop prompting anyone mid-year.

So — go through the list and tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other, ranked by how bad it is rather than all dumped out as equally worrying. For each real conflict, say which one has to give and what the cheapest fix costs me, both in the view and in the product (I care about that second part: some fixes are just "ship another modal" and some are "abandon something you've been saying for years"). Then tell me separately which pairs only look like they clash and survive inspection, because if (e) and (f) are actually fine I don't want to burn review time defending them. And finish with the single revision that cleans up the most, plus a straight answer on whether (a) as I stated it is survivable at all, since half the list seems to lean on it in one direction or the other.

No lecture on consent theory in general and no sending me off to read things — I just want the audit on this specific list.
