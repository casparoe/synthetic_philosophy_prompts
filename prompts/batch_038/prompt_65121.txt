I work as a legal clerk to the amnesty panel of a truth commission in a country that emerged three years ago from eleven years of internal conflict. I have left the country unnamed deliberately, because everything you need is in this message and I would rather you work from what I give you than reach for sources you may be misremembering; please do not offer citations, and please write your answer in British English. Our enabling act grants amnesty only where four conditions are met: the act must be associated with a political objective; it must have been committed in the course of the conflict by a person acting on behalf of one of the three recognized parties to it (the state's security forces or either of the two armed movements); the applicant must make full disclosure of all relevant facts; and the means used must not be grossly disproportionate to the objective pursued. A grant extinguishes both criminal and civil liability for the acts disclosed, and once an incident has been amnestied our practice has been to treat it as closed for investigative purposes. What I want is not a general essay on transitional justice but a ruling in waiting: given our panel's track record, what should we decide about the application described at the end, which precedent is the closest analog and whether it genuinely governs, which principle the panel would be endorsing by deciding one way rather than the other, and what further hypothetical application would most cleanly separate the rival readings of our own practice.

Our recorded decisions so far, in brief:

1. A police sergeant who beat a detainee to death while extracting the location of a weapons cache, his unit commander present and approving: GRANTED — the act was directed by a superior, the victim was a member of an armed movement, and the means were not disproportionate to the objective of recovering the weapons.
2. A movement operative who, against explicit orders from his regional command, bombed a bar frequented by off-duty soldiers; four soldiers and a fifteen-year-old bystander died: REFUSED — outside the command structure, no objective identifiable beyond reprisal, and the bystander's death foreseeable rather than incidental.
3. A soldier who, without orders but in accordance with his unit's settled practice, shot a suspected informer who was running away and who turned out to belong to the armed wing: GRANTED — consistent with organizational practice, victim a participant, objective political.
4. A prison warder who tortured detainees under a general written directive from headquarters but described only some of the sessions and gave a false account of one: REFUSED — full disclosure not made; the panel expressly declined to reach the remaining criteria.
5. A member of a vigilante group armed and directed by the state's security police, who burned a house he had been told was a guerrilla safe house; the occupants were a grandmother and two children: GRANTED — acting on intelligence supplied by the state, honest belief that he was striking a combatant target, disclosure complete and corroborated.
6. A soldier whose unit shot two captured insurgents under a standing order; he disclosed the order, the burial site, and the names of everyone present except his brother, saying he would rather forfeit amnesty than implicate him: GRANTED — the disclosure was as full as he could make it and the single withheld name did not obscure the facts of the incident; the panel recorded the omission and referred the unnamed man to the prosecuting authority.
7. A police captain who assaulted a detainee under an interrogation memorandum and, during the same raid, took money from the detainee's house: GRANTED as to the assault, REFUSED as to the theft — personal enrichment not associated with a political objective.
8. A movement operative who poisoned the water supply of a military base, killing residents of an adjoining settlement as well: REFUSED — indiscriminate means; the applicant could not show the act was directed at a military objective rather than at terrorizing a population.
9. A foreign mercenary under contract to the state who carried out an execution for a fee: REFUSED — not a party to the conflict, and the objective was remuneration.

The new application is this. The applicant is a former non-commissioned officer in the state's special branch. In 1987 he was one of a four-man team that ambushed and killed a regional commander of one of the armed movements at a roadside, on written authority signed by his provincial commander. All four team members applied; the other three were granted amnesty two years ago on facts substantially identical to his. He has disclosed the planning, the written authority, the weapons, the burial site (which led to the recovery of the remains), and the name of the officer who signed. What he will not disclose is the identity of a fifth person: an informant inside the movement who supplied the commander's route and timetable, and who, the applicant says, was promised by the provincial commander that he would never be identified — a promise repeated to the applicant in his presence. Our own expert confirms that the informant's role cannot be reconstructed from any surviving document or from the other three applicants, none of whom ever met him. The applicant says the informant is now a serving member of the national legislature and a supporter of the government of national unity; he insists that his refusal is a matter of keeping faith rather than of shielding the powerful, and he states plainly that he accepts the refusal may cost him amnesty. The families of the dead commander have asked the commission specifically to establish how the ambush was made possible, on the ground that their relative was betrayed from within his own movement. The panel is divided. One member says precedent 6 covers the case and that a withheld name which does not alter the account of the incident cannot defeat an otherwise complete disclosure, least of all when three co-applicants have already been amnestied on the same facts. Another says precedent 6 turned on the immateriality of the omission, whereas here the withheld name is not peripheral at all: it goes to the one question the victims' families asked us to answer, and a grant would close the incident while leaving its central fact unestablished and the informant permanently beyond reach.

So: recommend a decision, and if you think the panel can do better than a flat grant or refusal — a conditional grant, a grant coupled with a referral to the prosecuting authority as in precedent 6, a grant limited to the acts for which the three co-applicants were already amnestied, disclosure taken in closed session or into a sealed annex, a postponement pending further inquiry — say so and explain what we would thereby be committing ourselves to. Alongside the outcome, I want your reading of the rule the panel has actually been running across the nine precedents: whether it is authorization and place in the command structure, contribution to the truth, proportionality of means, status of the victim, or some weighting of these, and where the new application sits with respect to that rule rather than with respect to the statute's bare text.
