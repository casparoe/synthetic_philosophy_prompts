I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about the socialist calculation debate — not who won it, but what my own verdicts on concrete cases add up to. Assume you know the literature cold (Mises's impossibility claim, Hayek's local-knowledge point, Lange's parametric trial-and-error reply and the market-socialist tradition that grew out of it), but treat that as background. The real job is finding the principle behind my list.

My verdicts, roughly in the order I arrived at them:

- A manufacturer with thirty-odd plants that sets its internal transfer prices by administrative rule rather than running an actual market: fine, no calculation problem in sight.
- The same firm outsourcing that component and paying a real market price for it: better, and better-informed about cost in a way I'd defend as real, not rhetorical.
- A wartime economy with rationing and price controls that out-produced its enemy: planning plainly worked there.
- Peacetime Gosplan hitting its steel tonnage targets while turning out shoes nobody wanted: planning plainly failed.
- A big-box retailer allocating stock across thousands of stores by algorithm, with no internal prices and no bidding anywhere: works, and I'd call it central planning without hesitation.
- An internal prediction market at a firm that beats the sales director's forecasts: this one unsettles me, because I want to say the market beat the plan.
- A big open-source project coordinating thousands of unpaid contributors: works, but it never allocates scarce capital, so I don't think it counts as a test case at all.
- A national health service allocating transplants by waiting list and clinical criteria: prices here would be both odious and worse at the job.

The pair I can't reconcile is the retailer and Gosplan — same structure, one central allocator moving quantities off reported needs, and its the opposite verdicts that get me. Second worst is wartime versus peacetime, which was largely the same country and arguably the same machinery.

Please think this through carefully and brainstorm candidate principles before you settle on one. I want: the principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I've never seen; which listed case motivates each clause; an explicit take on whether scale, the locality of knowledge, and incentives are doing distinct work or whether I'm conflating them; the principle's verdict on two cases I haven't listed — an economy whose main goods are non-rival and costless to copy, and a fully automated economy with a single computational planner; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it has to overturn. If saving everything forces something baroque, say so and give me the simpler version along with the cost of adopting it.

Also please separate what's settled among people who work on this debate from what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested reading dressed up as the consensus. British English, please.
