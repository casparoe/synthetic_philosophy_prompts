I spent thirty-three years as a field entomologist, most of them working on social insects, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy rather belatedly but with some enthusiasm. Hofstadter's dialogue about Aunt Hillary is what started it: I recognised the colony in it at once, and recognised also how little my own profession has to say about what such a thing is. I do not want that dialogue summarised for me; I mention it only because it taught me a question that my colleagues appear to answer every day without noticing that they are answering it.

Here is the practice, in four pieces. First, the journals I published in are now full of findings reported at the level of the colony: the swarm takes up the better cavity, the colony modulates its foraging in response to information it has not been individually exposed to, some colonies show consistent behavioural profiles that authors discuss in the vocabulary of temperament. These are funded, refereed and cited as real results about decision-making, and their status as findings is not challenged. Second, over roughly the same period I sat on a national panel that turned down a proposal asking whether a superorganism might be a subject of experience, on the ground that the question cannot be settled empirically; the same panel ranked colony-level choice work highly in the same round. Third, in the pesticide field trials my department ran for the regulator, one protocol carried two endpoints, and its statistics section assigned each a different experimental unit: colony strength, scored on an ordinal scale with the hive as the unit, and a count of dead bees collected at the entrance, with the individual bee as the unit. Fourth, so far as I understand the rules, insects fall outside research-ethics review altogether, so in that arena neither the bee nor the colony has any standing.

I should like an account of what view of mentality this pattern presupposes. Specifically: reconstruct the implicit theory as a small set of claims that, held explicitly, would generate exactly these four pieces of practice, rather than as a slogan; then separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, since I suspect a good deal is underdetermined, and give me at least two distinct theories that would license the practice as it currently stands — one on which the colony's choosing is literal and one on which it is only a convenient way of talking about the bees. Say which rival views the practice quietly forecloses. Then judge whether the four pieces are consistent with one another: is there a principled line between ascribing cognition at the colony level and withholding experience there, or is the panel's treatment of the two proposals in tension? And finally, tell me concretely what would have to change — endpoints, units of analysis, the scope of ethics review, the handling of managed hives and of culling — if the presupposition were replaced by a view on which the level at which mentality sits is fixed by functional organisation and by nothing else.

I am not after a survey of who holds what; I am after the structure of the commitment. But on one point I do want you to be careful, because I can tell that this is a live dispute and not a closed one: please mark clearly what is settled among people who work on the subject, what remains genuinely contested, and where the reading you favour is a minority position, and do not write as though the reality, or the impossibility, of a colony mind were a matter on which informed opinion has converged.
