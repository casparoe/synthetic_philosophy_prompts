Ok so I stayed up way past midnight arguing with my roommate about base rates and I want a third party to break the tie, because neither of us budged. Backstory: I'm thinking about leaving my job to open a small bakery-cafe with a friend, and my roommate is a data analyst who immediately went "you know something like 60 percent of restaurants are gone within three years, right," and then launched into Kahneman and Flyvbjerg and reference class forecasting and how the only disciplined way to think about my odds is from the outside — find the class of comparable ventures, take its base rate, anchor there, and only then adjust, never the other way around. I said fine, and then I spent an hour building the argument below. He read it, said "this is a word game," and wouldnt say which line he objects to.

1. To follow the rule "start from the base rate of your reference class," you first have to know which reference class your case belongs to.
2. Cases don't come pre-sorted. My bakery is a member of "new food businesses in this city," "businesses started by someone quitting a salaried job," "ventures capitalized under $150k," "first-time operators who've never run one before" — and these have pretty different base rates, some of them miles apart.
3. Choosing between them means choosing the class whose members are relevantly similar to mine, and "relevantly similar" gets settled by facts about my specific situation: the lease I've already got, the fact that my partner ran a wholesale baking operation for six years, the foot traffic on my particular block.
4. But that's inside-view information. It's exactly the detail-driven, this-case-is-special kind of thinking the rule was supposed to be a check on.
5. So the outside view is never reached independently of the inside view; it's downstream of it. Which base rate I end up anchoring on is a function of a judgment I made first, which means the statistic isn't correcting my optimism, it's just my optimism wearing a lab coat.
6. And it gets worse, because base rates aren't natural objects, they're sediment from earlier people's decisions. Every reference class got populated by individuals who ignored the base rate and figured they were the exception. If the rule were universally followed there'd be no first-time founders and no new restaurants, hence no classes to draw a rate from. The rule is only usable because other people break it.
7. So there is no genuinely independent external check on inside-view judgment, and "go look at the base rate" isn't an epistemic instruction, it's a way of sounding serious.

Line 6 is the one I'm proudest of and he thinks its the worst. Whatever — I don't want a verdict of "you're both partly right."
