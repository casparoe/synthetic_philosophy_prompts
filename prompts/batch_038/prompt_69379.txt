I spent thirty-six years as a materials physicist, most of it in the non-destructive evaluation of pressure-vessel welds: ultrasound, radiography, eddy-current inspection. Since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of science with some seriousness, and I have drafted the essay below for a reading group I attend. I would be grateful for comments rather than encouragement. Please tell me plainly where my memory or my reasoning has failed; I am not looking for a bibliography, and I would rather have your judgment than a list of things to go and look up.

FAULT-LOCATING: A RESTRICTION ON WHAT THOUGHT EXPERIMENTS CAN TELL US (draft)

1. In my working life, nothing counted as evidence unless an instrument registered something. This makes the standard histories uncomfortable for me. Galileo is credited with a decisive result about falling bodies obtained by imagining a light ball and a heavy ball tied together; Einstein is credited with a decisive result about electrodynamics obtained by imagining a magnet and a conductor in relative motion. No measurement was made in either case. Two accounts are currently on offer. On the first, associated with James Robert Brown, in the good cases we apprehend the law itself through an intuition that is quasi-perceptual, and no new empirical input is required. On the second, associated with John Norton, a thought experiment is nothing over and above an argument: it begins from premises we already have from experience and proceeds by ordinary inference, so nothing mysterious happens. My instinct is with Norton, but I think his account leaves out something specific, and I want to say what it is in the vocabulary of my own trade.

2. Non-destructive evaluation locates a fault without altering the object and without telling you much else about it. An ultrasonic scan of a weld yields an exclusion region: not here, not there. It does not give you the microstructure. I propose that thought experiments are non-destructive evaluation applied to theories. On this picture we should distinguish:

  (a) fault-locating thought experiments, which show that a set of commitments already held cannot all be true; and
  (b) fault-repairing thought experiments, which are credited with telling us which commitment to abandon and what to put in its place.

My thesis is that class (b) is empty. Every celebrated thought experiment is fault-locating, and in every case the repair arrived from somewhere else — from new theory, or from new data, or from technical argument that did not depend on the imagined scenario at all.

3. Consider the cases. Galileo's two falling bodies: the commitments that natural speed is proportional to weight, and that the speed of a connected aggregate lies between the speeds of its parts, generate a contradiction. What is established is that the Aristotelian package is untenable. What is not established is that all bodies fall alike; a determined opponent may deny the premise about aggregates, or hold that lashing bodies together changes the relevant weight. Maxwell's demon located a fault between the second law and the mechanical assumptions behind it; the repair came a century later, from Landauer's result on the thermodynamic cost of erasing information and Bennett's application of it to the demon's finite memory. Einstein's magnet and conductor located a fault between the relativity principle and the asymmetry with which the received electrodynamics treated the two cases of relative motion; the repair was a new kinematics, which required vastly more than the asymmetry. Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen located a dilemma between completeness and locality, and Bell's inequality converted that dilemma into a measurement, which is what actually decided it. By contrast, Newton's bucket looks fault-repairing — it appears to deliver absolute rotation — and I take it to be the clearest example of a thought experiment that produced no knowledge at all: Mach denied the premise of isolation, and the location of the fault has been disputed ever since without resolution. Schrödinger's cat, I would argue, is in the same position.

4. This suggests a criterion, which I shall call the uniqueness condition. A fault-locating thought experiment yields knowledge only where the location of the fault is uniquely determined: only where no comparably cheap revision of some other commitment in the set removes the contradiction. Where several exits are available at comparable cost, the thought experiment has narrowed the region in which the culprit may hide. That is still knowledge, but it is negative and it is weaker than the histories imply.

5. What makes uniqueness obtain? My proposal is that the commitments in play must differ in what I shall call their entrenchment differential: the degree to which each has been independently confirmed in a distinct regime of experiment. Galileo's target commitment was very weakly entrenched relative to the others; so, I think, was the asymmetry Einstein exploited. In philosophical thought experiments — Searle's Chinese room, brains in vats, cases about personal identity — the conflicting commitments are all of the same epistemic vintage. None has been confirmed in a separate regime. The uniqueness condition therefore can essentially never be satisfied, and the disputes persist indefinitely. I want to insist that this persistence is not a symptom of immaturity in the discipline but a structural feature of the material being tested.

6. On this account the yield of a thought experiment is a negative fact about the world: that the world is not such that all these commitments hold together. Since the commitments are about the world and not merely about words, this is a fact about the world, and the empiricist constraint is respected. No special faculty is invoked, and no new data are consumed. The information was already in hand; only its configuration is new. An exclusion limit in particle physics contains no detection whatsoever and yet genuinely shrinks the region where a signal could hide, and I think thought experiments work the same way.

7. Where I expect trouble. First, whether fault-locating is anything more than Norton's reconstruction thesis wearing a lab coat. Second, the Galileo dispute: my recollection is that one author argues the scenario cannot be reconstructed as a plain reductio without loss, because it depends on a conceptual reframing of what counts as a single body, and that Norton replies that it can be reconstructed once the premises about aggregates are made explicit. Third, I have characterised Kuhn as holding that a thought experiment can only disclose nature's failure to conform to expectations already held, and can never suggest the revision itself; if that is a misreading, my framing needs adjusting, since my thesis is close to it. Fourth, I have stated that Norton requires the reconstruction to be deductively valid. Fifth, I have said that the German term is usually credited to Mach, and I have kept the hedge deliberately.

On the basis of this draft, I should like your comments on the following.

1. Accuracy. Go through my characterisations of Brown, Norton, Kuhn, Mach, the dispute over Galileo's reconstruction, and the resolution of the demon, and tell me which are right, which are fair simplifications, and which are simply wrong. Flag anything a historian of physics would wince at, particularly in section 3, where I have compressed a good deal.

2. Independence of the proposal. Would a defender of Norton say that fault-locating is just his view renamed? Would a Platonist of Brown's kind object to section 6 on the ground that noticing the inconsistency is itself the intuition I claim to be dispensing with, so that my account presupposes what it denies?

3. The uniqueness condition. Is it well defined, or does the phrase comparably cheap revision smuggle in precisely the judgment I wanted to make objective? Apply it to Galileo: does his case actually satisfy uniqueness, or does the escape route through the aggregate premise show that it does not — in which event my own central example refutes my criterion, and I would rather learn that now than in front of the reading group.

4. The entrenchment differential. Is section 5 question-begging? Does it amount to saying that the thought experiments of physics work because I already accept their conclusions, while those of philosophy fail because I do not? If you know of a philosophical case that plausibly satisfies the condition, I want to hear it, because it would cost me the second half of the essay.

5. The analogy itself. Where does the comparison with non-destructive evaluation break down? My own worry is that in my trade the instrument is calibrated against standards of known composition, and I have no analogue for calibration here. Should the analogy be retained as the organising device, or reduced to a remark in a footnote?

6. Register and structure. Does this read as an informed contribution to a debate, or as an outsider's confident gloss on a literature he has only partly absorbed? Which paragraph should be cut, and which should be expanded?
