I have been auditing a graduate reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and one problem keeps resurfacing: every time a participant says "locality," a different principle turns out to be meant, and the argument then slides past the place where it could actually be evaluated. Some people mean no superluminal signalling. Some mean the factorization of a joint probability once the common past is fully specified. Some mean the absence of a preferred foliation, and some mean only that measurement outcomes are not fixed in advance of the measurement. Bell himself insisted that the principle be written in terms of what physically happens in spacetime regions — his "local beables" — rather than in terms of measurements or observables, precisely because the latter vocabulary is too vague to bear the weight of a theorem. I have come to suspect that a great deal of the disagreement in the secondary literature is not disagreement about physics at all, but disagreement about what job the word "locality" is being asked to do, and that the dispute would become tractable if one first wrote down a specification of that job.

That specification is what I want from you, and I would like it before any theory is assessed against it. Please write the desiderata for a locality principle in the Bell setting: the conditions a candidate principle must satisfy in order to be the sort of thing from which a no-go theorem about quantum correlations can be proved, and in the light of which the various escape routes can be compared on equal terms. This is not a request for an interpretation of quantum mechanics, and it is not a survey. The deliverable is the standard of adequacy itself.

To give you a sense of the register, and to give you material to argue with, here is a rough list of conditions people appear to have in mind when they demand locality of a theory. Treat it as raw material, not as an outline I want filled in: (i) the principle must be statable in terms of what physically occurs in bounded spacetime regions, without invoking measurements, observers, or observables as primitives; (ii) it must constrain the physical state-description a theory offers, and not merely the input-output statistics the theory licenses; (iii) it must require that the probability of an outcome in one region, conditional on a complete specification of what happened in its past light cone, be unaffected by either the setting or the outcome at a spacelike separated region; (iv) it must somehow rule out the possibility that the settings themselves are correlated with the relevant past in the right conspiratorial way; (v) violating it must not automatically amount to permitting superluminal signalling; (vi) it must be compatible with a Lorentz-invariant account of the world, or must state what it costs a theory if it is not; (vii) it must be strong enough to be violated by something and weak enough to be satisfied by something, so that the theorem is not vacuous; (viii) it must leave intact the possibility of giving ordinary common-cause explanations of correlations within a region; (ix) it must not be smuggled in through the choice of formalism — that is, it must not be a constraint that the Hilbert-space apparatus meets by construction and that only a hidden-variable reconstruction can fail. Your task is to triage this list and, where it is incomplete, to add to it. For each condition you keep, state it precisely enough that it could be checked against a theory, then give the consideration that motivates it: the phenomenon or intuition it is protecting, and what goes wrong if you drop it. A worked illustration helps enormously where you can supply one — for instance, a toy model that obeys a condition yet reproduces correlations no local theory should, or a way of satisfying it only by making every correlation in the world inexplicable. Please mark, for each, whether it is genuinely required for Bell's argument to go through or whether it is a metaphysical preference of a particular camp that has been folded into the word "locality." Where two conditions that look like one are really two — as I suspect is the case with the two halves of (iii) — split them, and note where the literature has been misled by keeping them together. Do not worry about getting attributions right, and please do not supply a bibliography; I want the analysis, not the sources, and I would rather you reason it through than reconstruct who said what.

Two things matter to me more than the rest. First, tell me which subsets of the conditions cannot be jointly satisfied. I expect the interesting result here is not that quantum mechanics violates some principle but that the principles people call "locality" conflict with one another, or that the plausible-looking ones collapse into something so weak that the theorem says nothing and something so strong that no candidate theory survives it — in which case the list is itself the finding, and I want that stated plainly rather than buried. Second, test whether your conditions actually discriminate. Run them against Bohmian mechanics, a spontaneous-collapse theory of the GRW family, the Everett interpretation, a retrocausal or time-symmetric account, a superdeterministic account, and instrumentalism about the quantum state, and tell me where each stands on each condition. If your list ranks all six identically, or if all six escape by denying some condition you had marked non-negotiable, the list has failed to do its job, and I would rather hear that than be handed a tidy grid. Finally, please address a claim one of my colleagues defends: that Bell's theorem shows only that measurement outcomes cannot have been fixed in advance, so that an Everettian escapes it entirely by denying that there are single outcomes at all. Say whether that response is blocked by any condition on your list, and by which.

Please organize the response under these headings, in this order. **Bottom line**: five sentences or fewer, identifying the one condition that does the most to exclude candidates and saying whether any subset of the conditions is jointly unsatisfiable. **The job of the principle**: about 150 words on what work a locality principle has to do in this setting before any theorem is proved from it, and why the usual formulations fail to specify that work. **The requirements**: the core of the response, roughly 100 words per requirement, each numbered, each stating the condition, the case behind it, and its status as non-negotiable, negotiable, or misplaced. **Collisions**: about 300 words on which requirements cannot be held together and what follows when they cannot. **Do the requirements bite?**: a table whose rows are your requirements and whose columns are the six views named above, with each cell recording satisfaction, violation, or inapplicability, followed by about 150 words drawing the moral. **The Everettian objection**: about 150 words on my colleague's claim. **What I would drop**: about 100 words naming the conditions from my list that you think should not be part of the specification at all, and what people are really asking for when they insist on them.
