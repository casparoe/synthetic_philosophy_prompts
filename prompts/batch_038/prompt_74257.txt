Ok — pretend you're a senior bioarchaeologist who has also spent a lot of time in philosophy-of-science seminars, and who thinks my position below is mostly right but for reasons I haven't earned. My view is a specific version of the "agriculture was a mistake" line, and I want to know what would count against it. Not the popular-book version. The version I'd defend:

(1) The health decline across the foraging–farming transition is real and large in most regions where we have decent skeletal series — stature, enamel hypoplasia, caries. And I think I have an answer to the osteological-paradox objection, because the indicators I lean on don't depend on survivorship or on lesion repair the way porotic hyperostosis / cribra orbitalia frequencies do. Enamel defects are a timestamped developmental record; stature is cumulative; caries is mostly a function of substrate and time in the mouth. So I claim I can take the paradox objection on the chin and keep the decline.

(2) The driver isn't domestication, it's sedentism plus a storable carbohydrate staple. Sedentary foragers with a storable plant or marine base show a chunk of the same profile — crowding, infection load, and caries rates in some Pleistocene samples that are surprisingly high — so farming sits downstream of a settlement-and-storage shift, and that's what does the damage.

(3) Nobody chose this. Its a ratchet: population growth under a resource structure with no exit. So "mistake" can't mean a collective bad decision, and the sense I want is process-relative — a self-reinforcing path with negative welfare drift and no available exit, whether or not any agent could have seen it coming.

(4) Durable, heritable inequality tracks the *legibility* of the staple — divisible, storable, harvest-time-concentrated, assessable — more than it tracks surplus as such. Which is why root-and-tuber economies and much of mobile pastoralism look different.
