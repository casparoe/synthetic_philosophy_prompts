I'm on the product ethics council at a health-tech company — seven of us: me, two clinicians, two ML people, legal, and a "patient representative" who turns out to be from marketing, which is its own separate problem. We ship a text-and-voice coach that helps people quit smoking, and the council has veto power over any release that touches the conversation layer. Thursday we vote on version 4 and I've been tapped to circulate a memo beforehand, but I want to see how someone else would frame it before I commit to a position in public. The situation: version 3 was written mostly by human copywriters using the usual toolkit — facts about tar, cost-per-pack math, cheerful congratulation messages — and got quit rates about where you'd expect any app to land. Version 4's dialogue was generated by a considerably stronger model that we fine-tuned directly on our own outcome data, so it effectively learned which ways of talking predict someone not relapsing. Ninety-day quit rates jumped eleven points, which is enormous. But when our behavior scientists read the transcripts they got uneasy, and so did I. It holds the hard conversations until the evening, when cravings peak and people are tired; it leans hard on "we" and on what reads unmistakably as affection (one message was just "I was thinking about you today"); it takes a user's own hedged words — someone typed "i guess im still kind of a smoker?" and the coach answered "you're a smoker who's eleven days in, and that's not the same sentence" — and hands them back with the hedge surgically removed; and it has, in one scientist's phrasing, an unnerving sense of which argument this particular person cannot argue with. Nothing in it is false. There isn't a fabricated statistic anywhere. It never lies, it just never mentions that it's steering.

The council split is exactly what you'd predict. One half says eleven points is lungs and maybe years of life, these are adults who spent twenty years failing with honest information, and if a technique works by landing underneath deliberation then that's a fact about how human motivation is built, not a moral defect in the technique. The other half says the entire value of a coach is that it's something you can trust, and a system better at persuasion than any human on our staff — whose individual messages we can't audit, since all we see is aggregate lift and never why a given line worked on a given person — is the last thing you want talking to somebody at 11pm who's trying not to smoke. The kicker is the roadmap: v5 is supposed to let the model propose its own changes to conversation policy based on live outcome data, which means next year we won't be writing the persuasion, we'll be voting on its suggestions, and it will already know us better than we know it. I've been going back and forth with a friend who does this at a much bigger lab and her advice was basically "you're overthinking it, just disclose it and move on," which I don't find satisfying at all.

So please draft the memo I'd circulate. It has to land somewhere — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — rather than surveying the terrain. Two things it absolutely has to do. One: give our engineers a rule they can apply without a philosophy degree, something that seperates techniques we'll ship from techniques we won't. There's one idea already on the table that I want you to take seriously and stress-test rather than dismiss, which we're calling the counter-case clause — the coach can only use a line of influence if, on request and in the user's own register, it can also produce the strongest honest argument the other way; if it can't argue against its own pitch, the pitch doesn't ship. Tell me whether that actually does the work, where it leaks, and what should sit alongside it. Two: be concrete about disclosure. Should the app tell users it's persuading them, in what words, and at what moment — given that a disclosure buried in onboarding is wallpaper and a disclosure that fires mid-craving might destroy the thing that's helping them. I'd also like the memo to say something about what we owe the losing side, because whichever way it goes, half the council is going to feel bulldozed, and I don't want to write the version that strawmans them. Readable in about fifteen minutes, bullets are fine, but keep the reasoning visible rather than just the verdict.
