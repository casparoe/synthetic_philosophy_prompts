I teach ethics and a bit of aesthetics — I don't do media studies, so there may be a whole literature here I've never opened, and I'd much rather you tell me that then have you invent a term for me on the spot.

Here's the pattern I keep running into and don't have a name for. A film or a series dramatizes a real, named, dead person, and it invents something: a line they never said, a meeting that never happened, an act of cruelty or cowardice the record doesn't support. Nobody disputes that it's invented — often the filmmakers volunteer it. What strikes me is that the moral weight of the invention doesn't seem to track how big or how damaging it is. It tracks how the audience sizes compare. The invented scene reaches forty million people; the article or the monograph that sets it right reaches forty thousand specialists, and reaches them years later. So the director can be completely candid — "we compressed, we invented, that's what drama does" — and be telling the truth, and still be leaning on the fact that the candor travels down a much smaller pipe than the invention did. The disclaimer is honest and does no work at all.

And it isn't only that the correction is smaller. It also seems to matter where the default belief sits. Before the film, most viewers had no view of this person whatsoever; afterwards they have a vivid one, and the historian correcting it is arguing uphill against a picture that's already installed. The drama gets to set the baseline, and the correction has to dislodge it — a much harder job than it would have been had the film never existed.

Two things make me doubt this is just dramatic licence or the tired artistic-freedom-versus-accuracy debate. First, the size of the invention seems nearly irrelevant: a small invented detail in a huge production does far more of this work than a wholesale invented plot in a film nobody watches. So whatever the wrong is, it isn't measured in departures from the record. Second, it doesn't require any deception. The credits can state plainly that events have been fictionalized, the director can repeat it in every interview, and the asymetry still runs. It's not lying, and it's not really even about whether the audience ends up misled.

I know the neighbouring terrain roughly: there's the general argument about whether the dead can be harmed at all, and there's psychology on why corrections fail to stick and on how absorbed audiences stop pushing back. But I'm after the structural thing — the mismatch between the reach of an invented portrayal and the reach of anything that could set it right, plus the fact that this mismatch is entirely foreseeable and is quietly doing justificatory work in how we excuse the invention. Does that have an established name, in media ethics, aesthetics, or anywhere else? Don't give me references, just the concepts. If the nearest ones only partly fit, tell me which they are and exactly where each stops applying. And if nothing fits, coin a term, define it tightly, and give me the edges — I especially want to know whether a wholly fictional character who happens to share a name with a real person falls inside it, and whether it still applies when the invention runs in the flattering direction.
