Since I hung up the lab coat — thirty-odd years in plant physiology, mostly drought responses in crop plants — I've been working through philosophy on my own, slowly and with no supervision, and lately I've got rather hooked on social choice theory. I also, to my occasional regret, sit on the management committee of a housing co-operative: 31 households, a shared roof, a shared garden, one meeting a month and an AGM in the autumn. Our constitution gives each household what it calls "reserved matters" — things the collective simply doesn't get a vote on, like what you plant in your own plot, whether you keep hens, what you do inside your own four walls, how you heat your own living room. The idea was to stop the tyranny of the majority over trivia. In practice we've had three years of situations where honouring everybody's reserved matters, plus adopting anything the whole membership would agree is an improvement on what we have, leaves us either deadlocked or landed with an outcome that everyone in the room says is worse than something else we could have had. Then I read Sen's little 1970 impossibility result about the Paretian liberal — the one with Prude, Lewd and a copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover — and I had the very uncomfortable feeling it was a description of our AGM. A member who did PPE in the eighties insists this is just Arrow's theorem with new paint; I don't think he's right, but I can't say why.

So here's what I'd like: a decision procedure my committee can actually run at the table, not a lecture. Assume twenty minutes, a whiteboard, a show of hands, and an audience of a retired nurse, a heating engineer, two primary school teachers and me — nobody with maths beyond counting and nobody who's going to read anything beforehand. It should take a proposal and walk us through, in order: how to write the choice down as a small set of alternative states rather than as a motion; how to identify who is decisive over which pair of states and how to spot the pairs that differ only in someone's reserved matter; how to test whether honouring all those decisive pairs plus unanimity generates a cycle or a Pareto-dominated pick (and how to tell that apart from ordinary disagreement, which is most of our meetings); and then a diagnostic branch telling us which assumption to relax and what each relaxation costs us concretely — give up the unanimity requirement, shrink or re-draw a reserved zone, restrict the profiles we're willing to treat as admissible because they're meddlesome, stop demanding a single best state and instead settle on a procedure with a tie-breaker, or hand the whole thing back to the membership. That last option is expensive for us: amending a reserved zone needs two thirds at the AGM, so please treat it as costly rather than free, and say which branch to prefer when more than one is technically available. Where a step is really a value judgement dressed up as arithmetic, label it as such, and mark the points where the procedure should stop and refer the question to the membership rather than decide it.

Please also run it on three of our actual cases, honestly and including where it comes out against me. First: a household wants to put a large solar array on its own south-facing roof section, which is currently a reserved matter, but the rest of us want a uniform look because the street is in a conservation area and it affects everyone's value. Second: two households want hens, several object on smell and fox grounds, and the hens are on one plot while the foxes and the smell are not — I want the procedure to cope with a reserved right whose exercise spills over, and to tell me whether that means the right was mis-drawn in the first place. Third, and the one I don't want to hear: I've been claiming that what I burn in my own living room is my reserved matter, and the membership would prefer I didn't, with a real health argument behind it. If the procedure says my claim doesn't survive, say so, and say which condition was the one that gave way. It would also help to have one short case where there's no unanimous improvement at all and two reserved rights simply collide — music in one unit against sleep in the next — so we can see what the procedure does when the Pareto branch is silent. Finally, be candid about the limits: what breaks with 31 households instead of two, what depends on people reporting their preferences honestly rather than strategically, whether it can handle reserved matters that come in degrees, and where it will misfire and produce a confidently wrong answer. And if the philosophers' quarrel over how to formulate a right in the first place — I gather some people think it ought to be something you can waive or trade rather than a fixed pair of states you're decisive over, and others think Sen's own example smuggles in an externality — actually changes what a committee should do, build the difference in as a branch; if it's just people arguing, tell me that so I can stop reading about it at two in the morning. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations please: I can't chase them and I'd rather you spent the words on the procedure. And write the whole thing in British English — organisation, behaviour, licence as a noun, that sort of thing.
