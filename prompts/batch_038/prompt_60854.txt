I lead a small behaviour-and-evidence team inside a national environmental agency. Roughly twice a month a policy colleague brings us a draft public appeal asking households to change some individual consumption habit — fewer flights, less beef, shorter showers — and asks whether we can stand behind it. Two things keep going wrong. First, the drafts routinely justify the appeal by asserting that each person's act has a measurable effect on the outcome, and our own analysts keep telling us that, given the size of the population and the way supply is organised, that assertion is either false or unverifiable; a citizen who does the arithmetic can reasonably reply that her own act changes nothing. Second, when we raise this, colleagues reply that such appeals are worth making anyway, and we have no principled way of saying when that is right and when it is wishful thinking dressed as strategy.

Please write a decision procedure my team can run on a draft appeal in about half an hour, usable by people who have not studied philosophy: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure returns "do not make this claim", the point at which it returns "make the claim, but on grounds other than the individual causal effect", and the points at which the question is really one of political judgement rather than fact and must be handed to a person. It must preserve the distinctions that actually do the work — that an act can make no difference to the outcome and still be one we can properly ask for, and that an act can make a difference only by helping to cross a threshold or by coordinating with others rather than by contributing smoothly — while stating plainly which distinctions a half-hour procedure has to drop. Flag the case types on which it will predictably misfire.

Then run the procedure on three drafts: (a) an appeal to eat less beef, where our procurement data show the retailer orders in fixed lots of several thousand tonnes, so a household's purchases matter only when they help tip an order across a lot boundary; (b) an appeal to forgo one return flight a year, where we can identify no such threshold and per-passenger emissions are close to linear; (c) an appeal to boycott a named supplier's palm-oil products, where the campaign has already been designed and publicly announced and a senior colleague will be very unhappy if the procedure says we cannot support it.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the procedure's central claims, and single out the step you are least sure of.
