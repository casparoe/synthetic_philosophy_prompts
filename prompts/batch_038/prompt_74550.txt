A standard recipe in present-day alignment work runs roughly as follows. Raters, typically hired contractors rather than the people who will eventually use the system, are shown pairs of candidate model outputs and asked to indicate which of the two is better with respect to some stated criterion. The pairwise judgments of many different raters are then fitted with a model in which every output receives a single real number, and the probability that a rater selects one output over the other is a smooth increasing function of the difference between the two numbers — the same mathematical device used to derive chess ratings from game results. The fitted numbers serve as the reward signal that shapes the model's behavior, and one such model is then served to everyone, so that all users inherit the same ordering of what counts as a better answer.

Two features of this arrangement strike me as philosophically loaded, and I would like a reconstruction of what they presuppose rather than a defense or critique of the method.

First: what theory of value, and of the aggregation of value across persons, is built into the scoring device itself? Please be precise about which commitments are carried by the functional form — a single dimension of betterness, cardinal magnitudes, comparability across raters, and the treatment of raters' disagreements as noise around a shared quantity — and which are carried instead by the decision to use the resulting numbers as an optimization target. Which rival views does this rule out: incomparability, genuine value pluralism, person-relative or culture-relative standards, or merely ordinal social rankings? For each, say whether it is excluded by the mathematics or only by how the output is deployed, and whether systematic misfit of the model to the data would count as evidence against the underlying view or only as a technical nuisance.

Second: practitioners who build these systems also warn that the reward must not be optimized too hard, since a model pushed far enough produces outputs that score extremely well while being plainly bad, and the customary remedy is to penalize departure from the model's earlier behavior. Yet the comparison data are never treated as measuring different things for different raters, and raters are rarely given an option indicating that a pair has no better member. What view of the reward makes it simultaneously a measurement of something real and a quantity that degrades when pursued? Is that position coherent, or does it concede that the scalar was a local proxy all along?

Throughout, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably, and mark where the practice is underdetermined — where, for instance, a jury-theorem confidence in the average rater, an aggregation theorem about combining individual orderings, and sheer engineering convenience would all license exactly the same pipeline. Finally, describe concretely what would have to change in the engineering if the presupposed view were replaced by one on which betterness is multidimensional or user-relative.
