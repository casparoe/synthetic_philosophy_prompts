For the past three years I have been the unofficial arbiter of a foraging circle of roughly two hundred amateurs: people post a photograph of a find and ask whether it is edible, and I am the one who either signs off on the identification or tells them to throw it out. Separately, I have been sitting in on an evening reading group that spent a month on the dispute over whether what is at stake for a person can bear on whether she knows something, and ever since then I have been unable to get my own verdicts about identification under uncertainty into any kind of order. What follows is my attempt to lay them out. In each case, please stipulate that the perceptual evidence is held exactly fixed — the same photograph, or the same specimen in the hand, the same diagnostic features present, the same subjective confidence in the person judging — and that only the surrounding practical situation varies.

First: a member posts a textbook chanterelle, blunt forked ridges rather than true gills, solid stem, apricot smell, and says she is going to photograph it and leave it where it stands. I am perfectly happy to say she knows what she has. Second: an identical find, but she intends to cook it for dinner that evening. Now I want to say she does not know, or at any rate that she is not entitled to treat the matter as settled. Third: the same dinner plan, but the person is a trained mycologist who has handled thousands of specimens of this species. Here I retract my hesitation entirely; she knows, and she would be right to eat it, and it seems to matter that her confidence is the product of a great deal of practice rather than of a field guide. Fourth: the same dinner plan, but the finder is a novice, the light is going, her phone has no signal, and there is no further check available to her at all. My verdict here embarrasses me, because I want to say she may go ahead and eat it while still refusing to say she knows — the asymmetry between being right and being dead has not changed, yet there is nothing left for her to do. Fifth, and related: yesterday, before she had decided to cook anything, she told me it was a chanterelle, and I would cheerfully have said she knew it then. Nothing about the mushroom or about her evidence has altered since; only her menu. I find it very hard to accept that her knowledge has been retroactively deleted, and equally hard to say she knows now, and I cannot see how to have both.

Sixth: a stranger asks me whether a find is safe to give to her toddler. I am confident of the identification, I am not eating it myself, and yet I feel that my own low stakes do not license my assurance — what governs is what is at stake for her. But now compare the mirror case: I take a specimen to a bored curator who has no interest in it whatsoever, and I am about to serve it to my family. His assurance seems entirely proper to me, and I would not dream of saying that his indifference prevents him from knowing. Seventh: my cousin, who is not going to eat anything, has foolishly agreed to forfeit a large sum to an acquaintance if the photograph does not show a chanterelle. I want to say he still knows perfectly well; I only want to say he should not have made the bet, and perhaps should not be asserting the identification so freely. Eighth, to show that this is not about mushrooms: my mechanic tells me the timing belt is still the original at ninety thousand miles, and on an ordinary week I would say I know the car is fine; but if I am about to drive across the country with my daughter aboard, I find myself saying that I do not know it, and replacing it anyway, though nothing whatever has come to light.

My rough first pass at a principle is this: a person knows that p only if, given the way she is actually planning to act, the expected cost of p's being false is small enough relative to the cost of checking further; and where there is no checking left to do, the condition is either vacuous or, more likely, a fudge I have added to save the fourth case. I would like you to test that formulation against everything above and either repair it or replace it. What I want back is a small set of candidate principles — perhaps two or three — stated precisely enough that I can see what each clause is doing and which of my cases forced it in, together with each principle's ruling on the cases I have not thought to list, including at least two that you construct yourself and that seem to me to discriminate among the candidates. I also want an honest accounting of which of my verdicts each principle cannot accommodate, and, if you think one of them must be surrendered, a recommendation about which single one gives up the least; I suspect it is one of the fourth, the fifth, or the seventh, but I cannot tell. Finally, apply whatever you settle on to the case I keep returning to: I identify a morel for my wife, who trusts me absolutely and will cook whatever I hand her, and I say "I'm sure" out loud while privately intending to go back to the key and re-check the spore print before anything reaches the pan. Does that unspoken intention to verify restore my knowledge now, or is it rather proof that I never had it? Please write all of this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bolded terms, no tables. I am not after references or a reading list, and you should not feel obliged to name books or authors; what I want is the reasoning.
