Background, briefly: forty years at a bench, now retired and reading about time's arrow for pleasure, and I've hit a wall I can't get past on my own. There are two accounts of what makes the arrow point the way it does. One says the arrow just is the entropic gradient — entropy rises toward the future, and memory, films, fossils and so on work because laying down a record is itself an entropy-producing business. The other (David Albert's line, going back through Reichenbach and Boltzmann) says entropy increase isn't self-sufficient: you need a special low-entropy boundary condition on the early universe plus a statistical postulate, and it's that package which licenses you to read a present state off as evidence about the past. There's also Reichenbach's business about branch systems, which I think matters here but can't pin down.

Build me a thought experiment that makes these two come apart. Specifications:

- The scenario is one paragraph and bench-top realistic: a sealed cell, a camera, a lab notebook, ordinary fluids. No demons, no reversed boundary conditions, no cosmology, no exotic materials — just a small system in which an entropy-decreasing thing genuinely happens by chance and gets filmed.
- The two accounts must deliver *different* verdicts on one definite question: is the film a real record, i.e. is the scientist entitled to retrodict the cell's earlier state from it?
- Don't rig it. The verdicts have to fall out of the accounts themselves, not out of something stipulated into the setup. The whole point of the exercise for me is to test whether the arrow is a local entropic gradient or a global boundary condition, so a case that smuggles in the answer doesn't count.

Then, after the scenario: walk through what each account says and why; tell me which single feature of the case is doing the separating work; say what happens if I delete that feature — does it collapse back into the standard everyone-uses-it example of ice half-melted in a glass, where both accounts agree? And finish with the strongest attack the entropic-gradient person can make on my case. My guess is they'll say my cell isn't really an isolated branch system at all, but make the objection sharper than that if you can.
