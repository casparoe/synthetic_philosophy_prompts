I retired four years ago from a national measurement laboratory, where I spent most of my working life in low-temperature metrology, and I have since acquired two habits that my former colleagues regard with mild amusement: reading philosophy in the mornings, and serving without pay as honorary curator of a municipal collection of historic scientific instruments housed in the old grammar school. The collection is small — some four hundred items, mostly nineteenth-century teaching apparatus, with a few genuinely fine optical pieces — and for the first year I did essentially everything myself: cataloguing, restoration, the correspondence with the charitable trust that funds us, and the Saturday sessions we run for the local schools.

Three years ago the trust agreed to fund a part-time assistant through a youth employment scheme. He was twenty-six, had left a technician's apprenticeship half-finished, and I expected to train him up over a year and let him go. He has instead stayed, and has proved considerably better with his hands than I was at his age. He now performs nearly all the restoration, has taught himself the delicate business of re-silvering and of repairing galvanometer movements, runs the schools programme end to end, and maintains the catalogue database, which I had kept on paper. I have drifted into what I would charitably call supervision: I approve his plans, sign the loan agreements, and represent the collection at trust meetings. Two consequences have followed that I do not know how to think about. First, I have become incompetent in my own museum. When he was ill last month I could not get the projection lantern working and had to cancel a school visit; I could not tell you which of the resistance standards on the second shelf is which; and I sign off on restorations whose steps I would be hard pressed to reproduce. Second — and this is the part I find least comfortable — the trust's chair now addresses her emails to him and copies me, and when a visitor asks a technical question everyone in the room, myself included, looks at him.

I resent him, which astonishes me, since our interests seem perfectly aligned and he has done nothing but serve the collection well. His praise has come to matter to me more than the trust's formal commendations ever did, and I cannot say why that troubles me; when he defers to me in front of visitors I feel a flicker of contempt for him, and then shame at the contempt. I have considered two remedies. One is to arrange things so that he takes up a paid post at a larger museum in the county, which I could manage through a word or two to the right people, and which everyone would call generous. The other is to reassert myself at the bench: to announce that from now on all restoration is done by my hands with him assisting, so that the knowledge and the authority sit in the same person again. Neither feels right, and when I try to say why, I run out of language. The moral philosophy I have been reading treats a conflict like mine either as a clash of interests or as a question of duty, but I do not see either thing here — nothing has been taken from him, and I have broken no rule I can name. Something has gone wrong in the arrangement itself, and I suspect it went wrong in a way that both of the remedies would merely repeat.

So: is my resentment an injury to which I am entitled, or a symptom of a mistake of my own making? And which of the two remedies, if either, would you advise? I should be grateful for a reply of no more than 450 words. Please open with your direct answer to those two questions in three sentences or fewer, and only then set out the reasoning behind it; I would rather have a short, considered judgement than a survey of considerations.
