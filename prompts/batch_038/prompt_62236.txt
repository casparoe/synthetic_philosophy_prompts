The student wrote: Cliodynamics is the program Peter Turchin launched in the 1990s. It grew out of cliometrics, the statistical history of the 1960s, but goes beyond it by building formal mathematical models of long-run historical processes rather than estimating equations from historical statistics. Its central claim is that agrarian societies pass through "secular cycles" of roughly fifty years, in which population growth, rising inequality, and fiscal strain accumulate until the state loses the capacity to contain elite conflict, after which breakdown and depopulation reset the pattern; several such cycles can be linked into "supercycles" of two to three centuries.

The engine of this is the structural-demographic model, which Turchin developed to explain state breakdown in early modern England, France, Russia, and China. It identifies several mutually reinforcing processes. Population grows faster than the resource base, so real wages fall and the peasantry is immiserated. Because elite families also reproduce, the number of people competing for a roughly fixed supply of positions of wealth and office grows — elite overproduction — which fuels intra-elite conflict and makes elites unwilling to support the regime. Meanwhile state revenues stagnate while its costs rise, partly because it must buy off or suppress factions, producing fiscal crisis. Turchin's contribution was to formalize these feedbacks as coupled equations and to fit them to long proxy series: real wages, grain prices, elite wealth, tax revenue, and counts of riots, assassinations, and rebellions.

The evidence is of two main kinds. Within single societies, the model orders the phases of long historical sequences well, in Roman, Chinese dynastic, and early modern European cases. Cross-culturally, large coded databases of polities show that the structural-demographic variables track measures of instability across many regions and periods.

As for prediction, the objection that the models are fitted to the same sequences they are said to predict is answered by the fact that they are calibrated on data up to an early cutoff and then run forward. The best-known case is the forecast, made well in advance, that the United States would enter a period of sharply elevated political instability around 2020, on the basis of wage stagnation, growing elite numbers and wealth, and rising public debt visible years earlier. Because the model produced this before the outcome, it is an out-of-sample success rather than a postdiction, and this establishes the general claim that large-scale political instability is forecastable at decadal horizons, even though particular timings remain uncertain.
