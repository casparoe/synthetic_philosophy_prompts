I chair a department that has been pulled into a university-level budget dispute, and what I need from you is not advice about the budget but an analysis of a piece of language that has been doing a great deal of unearned work in it.

The setting is this. Some eight years ago the university committed to constructing a specialized research facility. Roughly half of it was built before the principal donor died and the balance of the pledge disappeared with him. Completing the facility now would cost considerably more than the original estimate and would serve a steadily shrinking population of users; leaving it unfinished means a vacant shell on valuable ground, though the land, the foundation, and parts of the superstructure retain real value in other configurations. At last week's committee meeting a colleague objected that the earlier outlay could not simply be written off, and the provost answered him with the proverb: don't cry over spilt milk. The remark was received as closing the question — that is, as establishing that any consideration reaching back to what has already been spent is inadmissible, so that the only respectable decision procedure is a purely forward-looking comparison of the unfinished shell against the alternatives. I think that is too quick, but I would rather expose the quickness precisely than score a rhetorical point, and to do that I need the proverb taken apart first.

Please give me a menu of the distinct claims this sentence could be making, ranging from the near-tautological to the genuinely radical. For each candidate reading, state it as a precise thesis — being explicit about its subject matter (a claim about rational choice, about the appropriateness of an emotion, about the epistemic status of information, about the temporal structure of a decision problem, about individual agents or about institutions), about its scope (one-shot or repeated, recoverable or irrecoverable losses, personal or relational undertakings), and about its force (descriptive, normative, or merely admonitory). Then tell me for each whether it is a platitude that no one in that room would deny, a substantive thesis that could be argued for, or simply false, and in the latter two cases what would have to be true for it to hold.

Next, identify which reading or readings the provost's move actually requires in order to have foreclosed my colleague's objection, and whether any of those readings survives contact with the features of this particular case — the fact that the earlier spending produced an asset whose value depends on whether it is finished, that portions of the outlay are in principle recoverable rather than gone, and that the institution's visible willingness to abandon half-built commitments plausibly alters the terms on which future partners will contract with it. If it turns out, as I suspect, that the proverb's home case is a one-off, irrecoverable, purely personal loss and that each of those conditions fails here, say so plainly and say which of the readings, if any, still applies.

Finally, flag any reading on which the sentence is really a claim about feeling rather than about choosing, since I want to know whether an affective admonition has been smuggled in as a decision-theoretic principle.

Order the readings by how much argumentative work each can bear, rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list. I am not asking for the literature on this proverb or for citations of any kind; I want the conceptual map, and I want it in a form I can bring to the next meeting.
