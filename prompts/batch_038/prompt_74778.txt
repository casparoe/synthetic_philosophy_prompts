For several years I have been assembling a view about the taxation of inheritances by running cases past my gut, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell whether the resulting verdicts hang together into anything worth calling a position. I would like you to treat what follows as data rather than as a request for a defence of any of it, and to tell me what principle, if any, generates the pattern.

My grandfather left my father the house he grew up in, of little value even then: untaxed, and clearly rightly so. A very large estate routed into a trust for grandchildren who barely knew the man: taxed as heavily as the law can manage, and clearly rightly so. Parents paying a child's university fees out of current income: untaxed, and I do not want that to change. Parents handing a child two million dollars for a first apartment in the city where that child will work: this should be taxed, and yet it is not an inheritance at all, and I cannot articulate why my verdict differs from the fees case. A farm passed to the daughter who has worked it for twenty years: lightly taxed or not at all. The identical farm passed to a son who lives abroad and leases it to a neighbour: taxed. A bequest to a severely disabled child who will need care for the rest of his life: untaxed. A bequest to a charity: untaxed, and I am at peace with that. Two hundred thousand dollars split among the five children of a schoolteacher: fine. The same sum split among the five children of a financier: also fine, which troubles me, because I keep saying that what matters is where the recipients start out and not where their parents started.

Now the pair I cannot reconcile. I think a fortune built from nothing by a person's own labour carries a stronger claim to be handed on than a fortune that arrived by inheritance and was merely preserved. But I also think that from where the heir stands the two situations are indistinguishable, and it is the heir's position that I claim to care about. So either the origin of the money matters, in which case my talk of equal opportunity is decoration, or the heir's position is all that matters, in which case the distinction I just drew is doing no work whatsoever. Relatedly: I think it would be a kind of theft to tax a bequest consisting of the workshop the deceased built, since the heir will keep running it and nobody receives a windfall; and yet a child born into a business-owning family grows up with a fallback no other child has, and the heir's intention to keep working removes not one ounce of that fallback. Finally, I want large transfers taxed at the moment they pass, but I also think it would be a disaster if such a tax made parents stop saving for their children's futures, since that saving is among the best things parents do, and I suspect these two convictions are the same motive wearing two hats.

There is also the case I keep returning to and cannot settle: a transfer from one spouse to the surviving spouse, which my instincts want to exempt entirely, even though the survivor is often already the wealthiest member of the family and the exemption seems to do nothing whatever for the children whose starting points I say I care about.

What I want back is this. State the principle or the small set of candidate principles precisely enough that I could apply it to a transfer I have never encountered, and for each clause identify which of my cases above forced it in. Then tell me what the principle implies for kinds of transfer I have deliberately not mentioned, and flag the ones you expect I will find hardest to swallow. Give me its verdict on the spousal case, and on the two-million-dollar apartment. Above all, be honest about which of my listed verdicts it cannot accommodate: if preserving all of them takes enough epicycles that the result ceases to be a principle, say so plainly, tell me which verdict I should surrender, and tell me what I ought to say to the part of me that still feels its pull. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or for names and titles to chase down, only for the principle and its consequences. Please write your answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bolded phrases, or tables, in the manner of a letter rather than a report.
