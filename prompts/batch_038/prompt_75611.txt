I'm on the publication ethics committee of a mid-size biology journal — five of us plus the EIC, who doesn't vote. Six weeks ago we got an anonymous email I haven't stopped thinking about, and Thursday we have to actually decide something.

The email says the sender has documentation showing that a 2016 paper by one of our own editorial board members contains manipulated image panels. Their demand isn't money. They want an Expression of Concern naming him in the next issue, plus his removal from the board. If we comply, they say, the documentation is destroyed and nothing ever surfaces. If we refuse, it goes to two science journalists and a public image-forensics forum the same day, with a draft press release attached.

Here's the part that's got everyone spinning. The sender claims they've already acted: that before contacting us they ran a prediction of how our committee would vote, and if it predicted compliance they destroyed the files already, and if it predicted refusal they already shipped the package. So on their telling, whatever we do Thursday changes nothing about whether a leak happens, and the letter is just a measurement of what an institution like ours does. They say the same letter went to fourteen journals drawn at random from a list. They attached what they call a cryptographic commitment proving the action was fixed in advance; our one computational member spent a day on it and says it doesn't establish anything, its just a hash of a string they could have picked afterwards. Also they extended our deadline once when we asked for more time, which doesn't square great with the whole "already fixed" story.

Separately, and this is the part that keeps me up: the allegation isn't coming out of nowhere. Two years ago a postdoc emailed us about a failed replication of that paper and we never followed up. A reviewer mentioned something off about one of the panels at the time, in passing. We've never seen the sender's documentation and we can't request it without opening a channel to them.

One of my colleagues says this is a famous decision-theory puzzle, that the answer is obviously refuse on principle, and that we should be reasoning about the other thirteen journals rather than this one. I don't find that reassuring — it feels like he's enjoying solving a puzzle instead of deciding about a person's career and a body of literature.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate Wednesday night, two pages max. I need a recommendation on the demand with a timeline (including what we say publicly, if anything, and when), a recommendation on the 2016 paper treated as its own question rather than as a reply to the letter, a view on whether we should adopt a standing answer for the next letter like this one and what it should say, and an honest account of the strongest case for complying. That last one matters: if the leak lands in March and the allegations turn out to be right, "we refused on principle" is going to sound pretty hollow and I don't want a memo that pretends otherwise.
