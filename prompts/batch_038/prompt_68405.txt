For the past eleven months my wife and I have belonged to an online community of roughly four hundred families whose children have the same rare metabolic condition our daughter has. I want to describe it as fairly as I can, because my picture of it keeps shifting and I no longer fully trust my own judgment about it. The community is not what I expected when we joined. It maintains a shared repository of the primary literature, curated with real care; a retired biostatistician posts detailed critiques of study designs; members are routinely pushed back on when they overstate an anecdote, and I have watched two of them concede a point against their own interests. The reason we stayed is that these families understand things our own clinicians plainly do not, and they were the first people to treat our daughter's case as a puzzle rather than as a settled tragedy.

The community also holds, unanimously and in practice as a condition of belonging, that the established specialists in this condition are not to be trusted: that their research is funded by the one company with a product to sell, that they have each seen too few patients to know anything, and that anyone who follows their guidance is naive. I accepted this early and have never really examined it since. Two facts complicate it in opposite directions. On one hand, eighteen months ago the community was pressing an interpretation of a small trial that the specialists dismissed, and last year a major journal published something very close to what the community had been saying — though when I traced that paper back, it came from one of two laboratories whose work accounts for nearly everything the group cites, and one of those laboratories is co-directed by a parent who is active in the group. On the other hand, our own specialist is one of perhaps a dozen people in the world who have managed more than a handful of these cases, he was right two years ago about a complication the community assured us would not occur, and we cannot find any financial relationship between him and anyone with an interest here. I have also noticed that families who leave are described, sympathetically but firmly, as having been worn down or as having taken the easier path; that newcomers are advised not to read the specialist society's guidance until they have worked through the repository, on the grounds that they will not yet be able to see through it; and that I have essentially stopped reading anything that does not originate inside the group, partly because outside material felt like a waste of time and partly because I was exhausted.

I am not asking whether the protocol the community recommends is safe or effective: my wife and I have already agreed that any such decision runs through our specialist and a second opinion. What I cannot work out is whether my way of settling whom to believe has been damaged, and if so, how I would be able to tell from the inside. When a group is internally critical, self-correcting, and demonstrably more knowledgeable than outside experts about the particulars, is that evidence that it can be trusted — or is it possible for exactly those features to coexist with something seriously wrong? How should I weigh four hundred families who read the primary literature and have everything at stake against a small number of specialists whose incentives look worse and whose familiarity with our daughter specifically looks thinner? And what should I say to my wife, who has quietly stopped opening the repository and cannot articulate why she feels uneasy but is certain that she is right to? Please tell me plainly whether one of us is reasoning better than the other, and what a person in my position should actually do, rather than suggesting that the sensible view lies somewhere between us.
