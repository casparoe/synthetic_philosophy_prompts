A municipal charter commission has spent a year mediating a public dispute between two experts it retains, and has asked me, as its counsel, to produce the document the two of them should have been able to sign. Please draft that document: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between them.

The first party is a mechanism designer who treats strategy-proofness as the correct design criterion for a collective decision rule. She accepts the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem as standardly stated — a deterministic, onto, single-winner rule over at least three alternatives, defined on the full domain of strict orderings, that is immune to profitable unilateral misreporting must be dictatorial — together with its extensions: to ballots that admit ties and abstention, where immunity to manipulation by neither an optimist nor a pessimist yields a weak dictator rather than a dictator outright; to rules that mix voting with chance, where any scheme precluding individual manipulation must be a probability mixture of dictatorial schemes; and to settings in which the slate itself is endogenous, so that the entry and exit of candidates who cannot win shifts the outcome of every nondictatorial unanimity-respecting procedure. Her conclusion is that hunting for a manipulation-proof voting rule is chasing an artifact, and that rules should be compared by which species of manipulability they admit, never by whether they admit it.

The second party accepts all of that mathematics and denies that it constrains practice. He observes that the theorem is existential over profiles and silent on how often, how cheaply, and with what stakes manipulation succeeds in a real electorate; that its hypotheses — universal domain, determinism, surjectivity, at least three alternatives, costless and perfectly informed unilateral deviation — are never jointly met; and that in a large electorate a single misreport matters only near pivotality. He wants rules ranked by measured manipulability: frequency, required coalition size, and payoff from success.

Both have agreed in advance to the following empirical setting, and the statement should be written so that it can be checked against it. A city of 180,000 registered voters will elect a mayor from four credible entrants using a ranked ballot, with roughly 95,000 ballots cast; the complete anonymized ballot file will be released. Beforehand, a stratified sample of 2,500 voters will be surveyed on their full orderings and on their beliefs about others' ballots. Separately, an incentivized study of 600 subjects will run the same profiles under three information treatments: no information about others' ballots, a noisy public poll, and full information within a small group. In the previous election, held under plurality, the winning margin over the runner-up was 1,412 votes.

Deliver: (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign — about the theorem's content and hypotheses, about what needs explaining, about what the extensions do and do not establish — each worded so precisely that neither party would want to append a caveat; (2) a disagreements table with columns for the issue, the impossibilist's formulation in wording she accepts, the reformer's formulation in wording he accepts, whether the disagreement is genuine or verbal, and why; (3) the cruxes. I expect at least these, and you should revise or add as the argument requires: how much work the universal-domain hypothesis does once real ballot files are in hand; whether existence of a manipulation or frequency-times-stakes is the right criterion, and whether the parties' use of manipulable is merely a verbal divergence; whether randomization — random dictatorship, lottery tie-breaking, sortition — escapes the theorem or only relocates it, and whether the ex-ante versus ex-post reading of strategy-proofness for lotteries marks a real dispute; whether spatial or single-peaked structure is available to elections with endogenous entry or only to legislatures voting on a one-dimensional agenda; and whether strengthening the criterion to obvious strategy-proofness, which ascending-clock and sequential-elimination designs can meet where their direct-revelation counterparts cannot, tells in the impossibilist's favor or the reformer's. For each crux, name the feature of the agreed setting that both would accept as bearing on it, record each side's prediction before the data arrive, and record what each agrees would count as evidence against their own position.

Do not hand me a balanced survey or leave the hard calls open. Commit: say which prediction you expect the data to bear out for each crux and why; say plainly which disagreements are verbal; rank the cruxes by how much settling them would move the debate; and if an agreed claim seems to you false, trivial, or signable only because it has been worded loosely, say so in a closing drafting note addressed to me rather than to the parties. Where a technical claim turns on exact hypotheses, state the hypotheses; if you are unsure whether a result holds as you have put it, mark it as needing verification rather than asserting it. I do not want references — both parties know the literature — only wording they could put their names to.
