I am trying to formulate a criterion for deciding, when the temporal structure of a perceptual report fails to match the temporal structure of the stimulation that produced it, whether the mismatch is to be explained by retrospective revision of an experience the subject genuinely had — the Orwellian option in Dennett's distinction — or by the staging, in advance of the report, of content that was never present in experience at the relevant time — the Stalinesque option. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases, ordered roughly from those I hold most confidently to those I hold least.

1. The standard color phi display. A red dot at one location and a green dot at another, flashed at an interval in the range that produces robust apparent motion; the subject reports a single dot that traveled from the first location to the second and changed color at a point along the path whose position varies systematically with the interval and with the spatial separation. Verdict: Stalinesque. I do not think the subject had an experience of two stationary dots which was later rewritten. I think the motion-and-change content was in the experience, and that the experience simply took time to settle into that shape.

2. An ordinary memory error. I put my keys on the shelf; a week later I confidently recall putting them on the hook, and I recall it with the same phenomenology of reliving as the accurate cases. Verdict: Orwellian, and entirely unobjectionable. Nothing about this makes me want to say my original experience was altered. The report is false about the past, but the experience itself stays put.

3. The cutaneous rabbit. Taps delivered at the wrist and then at the elbow are felt as a series of evenly spaced taps marching up the forearm, including at locations where the skin was never touched. Verdict: Stalinesque. Those intermediate taps are genuinely felt, not inferred afterward and dressed up as felt.

4. The flash-lag display. A moving object and a briefly flashed object are physically aligned at the instant of the flash; the flashed object is consistently perceived as trailing the moving one. Verdict: Orwellian, and here I am content with that. I want to say that the positional content the subject had was subsequently adjusted in light of what came after the flash, and that this is a real case of the stream being edited rather than of a verdict reached backstage.

5. The attentional blink. Two items to be identified in a rapid serial stream; when the second falls a few hundred milliseconds after the first, it is usually not reported at all. Verdict: neither option applies. I do not think the second item was experienced and then overwritten, and I do not think an experience of it was staged. There was simply no experience of it, however much processing occurred.

6. Hearing a melody. I hear each note giving way to its successor; the succession is in the hearing, not reconstructed from two static hearings plus an inference. Verdict: again neither option applies, but for the opposite reason from 5. Ordinary temporal experience is not an anomaly in need of explaining, and any criterion I adopt must leave it untouched.

7. A probe variant of case 1, which I stipulate rather than report: the subject is asked for a report immediately after the first dot and before the second, and says a stationary red dot; then, after the second dot, reports the usual single traveling dot that changed color midway. Verdict: I want to keep the Stalinesque reading of case 1 even here. The mid-sequence report expressed something incomplete rather than a finished experience that was later overwritten — yet I admit this sits badly with 4, where the subject likewise cannot report the aligned position and where I am happy to call the outcome an overwrite.

My tentative principle has been this: retrospective revision is licensed only where the content revised had already been settled and was available for report at the time it is supposed to have changed; where the content was never available for report at the relevant time, the report must be taken at face value as disclosing a staged experience, so the revision, if any, happened before the record was made. I recognize that this gets 2 and 4 by different routes, may collapse 1 and 7, and appears to say nothing that separates 5 from 6.

What I would like from you is the following. First, state a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precise enough that it yields a determinate verdict on each of the seven cases — identifying the operative notion (settled content? reportability? presence in the stream? duration of the interval over which experience is extended?) rather than gesturing at it. Second, take the principle apart clause by clause, saying for each clause which of my cases forces it in and what it would cost to omit it. Third, tell me what the resulting principle says about cases I have not ruled on: the line-motion illusion, in which a briefly presented short line appears to grow outward from one end (treat the effect as stipulated if you doubt it); a subject who is never asked to report until long after the sequence, by which time intervening events have made one reconstruction far more likely than another; and the attentional blink case in which the missed item is later cued and the subject insists they saw it all along. Fourth, state plainly which of my seven verdicts the best candidate cannot accommodate, and, if no single principle saves all of them, which one verdict is cheapest to surrender and what the cost of surrendering it is.

I am not asking which published position my verdicts happen to resemble, and I would rather not be told that the question is ill-posed; I am asking whether a principle exists that entails them, and if not, how the set should be pruned.
