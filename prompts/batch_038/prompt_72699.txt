I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and in three weeks I'm presenting a chapter of my dissertation at an interdisciplinary workshop on levels of explanation hosted by a computational cognitive-science institute. The room will be mostly people who build and analyze neural network models of cognition — they think in terms of predictive accuracy, compression, model classes, and training distributions — plus two philosophers of science who work on scientific realism and one researcher who does mechanistic interpretability on large models. Nobody there is a Dennett specialist. I can follow the machine-learning discussion but I have never trained a model, so I cannot bluff my way through a technical question; where an objection turns on a technical claim, I need the claim to be exactly right, including where the objector has misstated how compression or model selection actually works.

The chapter argues for realism about the intentional stance by way of the compression criterion in Dennett's essay "Real Patterns," which I formalize and then push further than its author intended. Here is the argument the objections will be aimed at:

1. A level of description L tracks a real pattern in a system S just in case, relative to a fixed repertoire R of candidate descriptions and a fixed accuracy tolerance ε, L is ε-adequate for S's behavior and strictly shorter than every other ε-adequate description in R.
2. Once R and ε are fixed, the ranking is a fact about S rather than about us, so realism is compatible with the relativity in step 1 — much as the claim that a particular hypothesis best fits a given dataset at a given tolerance is mind-independent even though we chose the hypothesis space and the tolerance.
3. Applied to the three stances: for systems like us, the intentional idiom dominates the design stance on the length-versus-accuracy trade-off and utterly outclasses the physical stance, so beliefs and desires are real patterns and not merely useful fictions. The instrumentalist reading, on which the intentional stance is a heuristic carrying no ontological commitment, gets the criterion backwards: heuristics are permitted to fail, and the entire content of dominance is that failure at this level is not tolerable.
4. The corollary, and the part I am most nervous about: if a description in R ever became available that was both shorter and more ε-accurate — a cheap and exact physical simulation, say — the intentional level would cease to dominate, and beliefs would cease to be real patterns in that system. I want to defend this as a feature rather than a bug, on the grounds that a criterion of reality that cannot be lost is not a criterion of anything.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the eight or nine objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by the product of how likely each is to come up and how much damage it would do if I fumbled it. Phrase each one in the vocabulary its author would use: an interpretability researcher will not say that my criterion collapses into instrumentalism, they will talk about probes, features, superposition, and whether a posited belief is recoverable in the weights at all; a model-builder will talk about capacity, loss surfaces, and what it even means to say one description is shorter than another when both are implemented rather than written down; the realist philosophers will press on whether step 2 is anything more than constructive empiricism with an information-theoretic gloss.

Under each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes of speaking, plus one or two sentences stating honestly where that reply is soft. Mark which objections I should concede or defuse in the opening five minutes to buy credibility with a room that expects to be lectured, and identify the single reply that has to land for the chapter to survive the question period. Where an objection rests on a misunderstanding of what a philosophical claim about compression is doing, flag it — but tell me how to say so without appearing to accuse the room of a category mistake.

End with the question I am praying nobody asks: the one I believe I have no good answer to, stated as sharply as its most likely asker would state it, together with your assessment of whether there is any live escape or whether I should restructure step 4 before the talk. I don't want a bibliography or a reading list; I want the objections and what to say back to them.
