Two months ago I became the person at my university who decides accommodation requests. I'm the disability services coordinator at a public school of about 15,000, and during intake season I'm making eight or ten of these calls a day, students are promised an answer in five business days, and if I get one wrong there's an internal appeal plus a federal complaint route. It's personal for me: as an undergrad I was the kid who got turned down, and I still remember how arbitrary the letter sounded, so I don't want to write letters like that.

The standard I have to apply is lifted out of the regs — an impairment that "substantially limits a major life activity." I'm told to read it broadly, which does not help at 4pm when a file is on my desk and one adverb is doing all the work. Here's what I think is actually going on, and this is the part I want you to work with. One view of vague words like that is that there is a sharp line — some exact point where a limitation becomes substantial — but nobody is in a position to know where it is, so any confident call about a case near the line is a guess wearing a lab coat. The other view is that there's no single line at all, just a family of acceptable ways of making the word precise, and a borderline case is one that comes out true on some of them and false on others; on that picture the only thing you're entitled to flatly assert is what comes out the same on every acceptable way of drawing it. Those sound like they should produce completely different paperwork, and the paperwork is what I need.

So: write me the procedure I run on a file. Steps in order, executable in about ten minutes by someone with no philosophy background and no one to ask, and honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, where the procedure just stops at a no, and where it stops being a question of fact and hands the call to a human because somebody has to pick a way of making the rule precise rather than find one. Build it on whichever of the two views above actually changes what a coordinator does, and flag inside the relevant step what would be different under the other one.

Run it on two files as you go. Case A: sophomore, clinician letter documenting generalized anxiety that "significantly impairs sustained concentration," registrar shows a 3.4 and the student has never once used an extension we granted. Case B: grad student whose presentation is close to identical to one I approved in September, thinner documentation, worse academic standing — I want to approve it, and I suspect the procedure is going to tell me I can't just quietly do that, because either September was a mistake or this one comes out the same way, and choosing between those isn't reading the rule.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered steps and nothing else — no intro, no wrap-up, one or two sentences each. Wherever a step is the one that decides Case A or Case B, or the one that fails to decide it, say so inside that step.
