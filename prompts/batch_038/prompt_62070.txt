You're a political philosopher who does a lot of applied civic-ethics consulting — think of yourself as the outside expert an advice panel would hire to reverse-engineer its own intuitions. Context: I volunteer on the reader-advice panel of a nonpartisan get-out-the-vote nonprofit. Every fall we mail members a one-page guide, and year-round we answer letters from people asking whether some particular vote they're weighing is morally OK. We've been at it about six years. Nobody has ever articulated a theory — no one's ever said "we're consequentialists" or "we're doing the juror-not-customer thing" — we just vote on the replies. Which is why we're stuck.

Our record so far, roughly in order:

1. A reader in a district decided by 40 points asked whether they could skip the congressional race entirely and just vote the school board and the two ballot measures they'd actually studied. We said yes, no obligation to fill in every race, and casting a vote you can't defend is worse than casting none.
2. A reader whose preferred party was polling at 3% asked if voting for them was self-indulgent. We said: in that district, no. With a margin that wide there's nothing to spoil; vote for the platform you'd defend to a neighbor.
3. Swing-state reader, presidential year. One candidate's platform included something the reader considered a grave injustice, not merely a bad policy. The other was corrupt, cruel in tone, and would do real damage, but wasn't in that category by the reader's own account. We told them to vote for the second one and not agonize about it, and we wrote that in a state that close, staying home isn't a neutral act.
4. A reader in a total blowout wanted to vote for the lesser evil anyway, purely because it felt like doing something. We said fine, harmless, though we noted the vote meant something different there than it would have in a close race.
5. A nurse asked whether she could vote for a candidate who supported deep cuts to a program her patients depend on, on the grounds that the same candidate was much better on a foreign-policy issue she weighted more heavily. We said yes, and that we weren't going to second-guess her ranking of harms — but we asked her to write the ranking down before she voted.
6. A reader asked whether voting for a major-party candidate in a safe state, purely to pad that party's national popular-vote share, was a "wasted vote" rationalization. We said no, that share isn't causally empty, and it's a legitimate reason.
7. A reader wrote in asking permission to vote their heart and included a claim about the polling that they plainly didn't believe. We didn't answer the question. We told them to go check the actual margin first and write back when they could tell us honestly how close the race was.

Now the letter we can't settle, and it's going in next week's mailing either way.

A public defender in a midsize city. Mayoral race, plurality, no runoff. Front-runner X has run on criminalizing homelessness — sweeps plus prosecution. The reader considers that a grave injustice, not a bad policy; they've said so explicitly and they're not being dramatic about it. Candidate Y is also on the sweeps side but pairs it with a shelter-first commitment and no prosecutions; the reader calls Y "the same wrong, smaller." Candidate Z is clean on the issue and is, by the reader's own description, the best platform in the race; Z is polling around 11%, has said publicly they won't attack Y, and is drawing exclusively from Y's pool, not X's. Both the local pollster and the paper's board have said a Z vote most likely hands it to X. The reader writes: I've voted lesser-evil twice in my life and both times the lesser evil did the thing I voted against the other guy for. I don't want to be part of X's win, but I also don't want to be the reason X wins.

Here's the thing: in every one of rulings 1 through 7, at least one of the realistic options was, in the reader's own judgement, not gravely unjust. That's never been false before. Half the panel thinks ruling 3 governs and we tell her Y. Half thinks ruling 3 is about a two-way race with a clean-ish option and doesn't reach this, and that ruling 2 plus ruling 1 point toward Z or a spoiled ballot. Both halves think they're being consistent with the record.
