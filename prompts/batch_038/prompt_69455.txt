So — three weeks out from presenting a chapter of my dissertation at a translation studies work-in-progress group. Thirty minute talk, then a long seminar discussion, and the room matters a lot here: mostly people who came up through descriptive translation studies (norms, polysystems, that whole tradition), one very committed theorist of translator visibility and the ethics of domesticating versus foreignizing, two working poet-translators who've published constraint-heavy books and are openly alergergic to theory, and one computational linguist who does neural MT and thinks anything about form is a decoding problem. Nobody there does philosophy of language, and exactly one of them has read the Hofstadter book on Marot cover to cover.

The chapter's thesis, roughly: when you translate a poem under strict formal constraints — same syllable count per line, same rhyme scheme, the register warming partway through the way I read Marot's little poem doing — the loss isn't only in the target text. A constrained translation forfeits choices, line by line, that an unconstrained one keeps open, and I want to treat that forfeited range as a third axis of assessment alongside semantic fidelity and target-text quality. The visible weirdness we criticize constraint translations for (the filler word, the wrenched syntax) is mostly the surface trace of the forfeit, not a local defect. So the standard move of scoring the output against an unconstrained output misscores it, because it charges the translation twice: once for the awkwardness, and again for the alternatives it never had.

My argument runs in three steps. (1) At each line the translator faces a set of admissible options, and a global constraint shrinks that set before anyone ever looks at it. (2) The shrinking isn't uniform — some lines stay wide open, one or two are nearly forced — and the distribution of tightness is a property of the constraint-plus-source pair, not of the translator's skill. (3) So two translations can match on fidelity and on quality and still differ in what they cost the translator to produce, and that difference ought to show up in how we read them and how we argue about them.
