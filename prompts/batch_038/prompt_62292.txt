Six weeks ago I took a position at a small policy research institute, and my first assignment has been to draft the framing chapter of a report on how automation is redistributing work and bargaining power. My background is political theory rather than economics, so the chapter is meant to give the report some intellectual depth by tracing how earlier thinkers connected the availability of labor-saving devices to the justification of unfree labor.

The centerpiece I have been building around is a passage in the first book of the Politics in which Aristotle imagines that if every instrument could complete its own work at command, or in anticipation of its user's wishes, as the statues of Daedalus and the self-moving tripods of Hephaestus were said to do, and if the shuttle would weave and the plectrum strike the lyre unaided, then a master craftsman would have no need of assistants and a master no need of slaves. The reading I have developed, and which my co-author has accepted without argument, is this: Aristotle's case for natural slavery is fundamentally an argument from the scarcity of self-directing instruments. Because tools cannot act on their own, both bodily labor and, more importantly, direction have to come from somewhere, and slavery is the institution that supplies them; the people suited to it are those whose deliberative capacity is too feeble to organize their own lives. On this account, automation does not merely end slavery as a historical fact but removes the ground of the argument for it: once instruments can anticipate and execute a will, Aristotle's own premises stop picking anyone out as naturally fitted to be ruled, and the doctrine turns out to have been an artifact of a technological constraint all along rather than a claim about human inequality.

Two further features of the text have reinforced this reading. First, he describes the slave as a kind of animate instrument and as a living possession, and the discussion in which the shuttle appears is explicitly about instruments and about the craft of the master, so the whole doctrine looks like an account of who performs the work in a household. Second, he indicates that where slavery accords with nature the interest of master and slave coincide, which fits a functional story neatly: unfree labor is beneficial precisely where the instruments require it.

Before this chapter goes into print I want the reading tested rather than endorsed. Does the shuttle passage bear the justificatory weight I am placing on it, or is it doing something else in its context, and if so what? Does the animate-instrument language commit Aristotle to treating the slave as a source of productive labor at all, given that he elsewhere separates the things that make products from the possessions that serve action, and what turns on that separation? If my technological-scarcity reading does not hold, what is the actual structure of the natural-slavery argument: what does he take to make some human beings naturally suited to be ruled by others, how strong does he think that condition is, and does he believe the slavery practiced in his own cities tracked it? And once that is settled, is there anything left in the passage worth keeping for a report on automation, or should I cut it rather than misuse it?
