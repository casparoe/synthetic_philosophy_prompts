Six months ago I started volunteering for a small nonprofit that rates viral claims as true or false. We get maybe forty claims a week, each one goes to a panel of seven volunteers who vote independently, and the majority becomes our public rating. An outside reviewer recently checked a sample of our past ratings against primary sources, so for the first time we have real per-person accuracy numbers, and they're not flattering. Not one of the seven is above 62% on their own. Two of them are actually below 50%, and reliably so, in the sense that they're wrong in a consistent direction: roughly speaking, one subgroup calls anything sourced from an institution true, the other calls anything that sounds like an official line false. But the panel's majority vote lands at 84% — way better than any single member, and better than our best person working alone would have been.

Here's the part I have no word for. Last month we ran a short calibration training for the two institution-trusting raters, and it worked: their individual accuracy went from 47% and 49% up to 58% and 61%. And the panel's majority accuracy dropped, from 84% to something like 76%. I rebuilt the spreadsheet twice because I assumed I'd screwed something up, but it holds. With those two now leaning the same way as three of the others on borderline claims, the majority has started agreeing with itself in one direction, and the claims we used to catch by having people cancel each other out now sail through. So we have a commitee that's more accurate than any of its members, for a reason that gets destroyed when you make the members better. Our coordinator's instinct is obviously to train everybody, and I want to explain why that backfired here without sounding like I'm saying "please keep them bad."

I know the Condorcet jury theorem is the neighborhood, but the version I learned needs each voter to be individually better than a coin flip and to err independently, and we have neither — a big chunk of our accuracy comes from the fact that people are wrong in mirror-image directions. It also doesn't feel like the usual wisdom-of-crowds talk, where the point is that lots of independent guesses average out noise, because what's doing the work for us isn't noise at all, it's two stable biases pointed at each other. So: is there an established concept for collective accuracy that depends on the parts being wrong in offsetting ways, such that improving one part makes the whole worse? I'd want to know what the nearest concepts are and exactly where each one stops fitting, and how this differs from ordinary error-cancelling in measurement, where you basically assume the deviations are random rather than two deliberate-looking leanings. And if nothing names it precisely, what would you call it, and where are the edges — at what point does offsetting bias stop being a mechanism you can count on and start being a coincidence you got lucky with?
