For a seminar on the ethics of truth-telling, I would like a short satirical piece to circulate as an in-class reading. Write an advice column of roughly 600 words, set in a fifth-century North African diocese and composed by an invented bishop who has absorbed Augustine's teaching on lying whole: lying consists in having one thing in the heart and another on the tongue; every lie is a sin, though some kinds are graver than others; none is permissible; and the ruin of the soul is a worse thing than any bodily harm. Present four letters with their replies. Include a merchant weighing whether to disclose a hidden defect in his goods; a household sheltering a man whom a violent pursuer is seeking; a young man asking whether he may feign adherence to a sect in order to gather evidence against it from inside; and a fourth correspondent of your own choosing who appeals to a lying heroine of Scripture and must be handled the way Augustine handles such precedents. The comedy should come from doctrinal precision rather than caricature, and the target is not the bishop but the practice of holding an absolutist rule and then buying one's way out of it through the definition of a lie: every reply must run on the same resources — silence, the prudent withholding of what one knows, the refusal to answer at all — so that the bishop grants his correspondents nearly everything they actually want while never once conceding a lawful lie. Let the strain show through the ingenuity of the replies rather than through any admission on his part, and close with a letter he cannot answer without either lying or saying what he would very much rather not say. Keep the register warm, pastoral, and entirely in earnest.
