I'm TA-ing a 12-person upper-level seminar on formal epistemology, we meet once a week for 80 minutes, and next week is the session on credences and Dutch books. They've had the betting interpretation of degree of belief sketched in lecture and I did one worked example on the board — somebody with P(rain) = .7 and P(no rain) = .6 who gets sold both sides of the same bet — but I think they nodded along without feeling anything, and two of them left arguing that no real person holds "those" beliefs anyway. I want to run something where they walk into the book on their own and only learn what happened afterwards: don't name the argument until the debrief, and don't tell anyone up front that the point is to be incoherent. My professor sits in on the section and is openly sceptical that coherence norms have anything to do with actual believing, so she'll pounce on any place where the exploitability only shows up because of an idealization I smuggled in. I'd rather the activity hand her those idealizations explicitly than let her dig them out.

Room has one whiteboard, no projector, index cards, and a jar of ~200 poker tokens. No real money, but I want the currency to matter a little — I was thinking tokens that convert into a very small participation bump, though tell me if thats coercive, and if so what to do instead for a group containing two extremely sore losers.

The part I actually care about, because the board example glossed it: the design should make them feel that the theorem is about a *guaranteed* loss rather than about the size of the loss. So I want at least one student who prices everything perfectly coherently, takes a bet that's just genuinely bad or unlucky, and ends up down more than the student who got booked — and I want the booked student's loss to be bounded and knowable in advance from their own numbers. On top of that, please build in:

- a posting round where each student writes down prices on a fixed set of propositions and hands them in simultaneously, so nobody can revise after seeing someone else's slip, plus whatever rule you need so a student can't just refuse to trade and spoil it (somebody will try, and the refusal move is exactly the objection I want them to raise later rather than execute early);
- a variant, run on maybe three students in parallel, where the target isn't numerical but comparative — someone ranks A over B, B over C, and C over A. I honestly don't know whether the betting story goes through for that kind of inconsistency or whether you only get an ordering that looks silly, so design it either way and tell me straight which of those two things the activity will produce;
- an optional second half where they get a piece of evidence mid-game and re-price, set up so that a student who updates by something other than conditioning can be booked across the two rounds. Only if it fits in the time.

Deliverables: the rules and the payoff scheme in enough detail that I could hand it to a sub, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes, the exact slips/cards to print, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto specific steps of the argument — including a list of the idealizations (simultaneous acceptance, unbounded stakes, fixed prices, no learning, bets not changing what's bet on) saying for each one whether my activity enforces it, hides it, or ignores it. Then: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover in the moment, and a frank note on which feature of the argument the game simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply verbally.
