I write literary fiction and I'm stuck on a scene in my fourth book in a way that's turned into a philosophy problem, so put on your professional-philosopher hat — you're someone who works on the metaphysics of death and has graded a thousand argument-analysis essays — and think really carefully about this. Start with some brainstorming before you commit: give me three or four candidate diagnoses of where the thing breaks, and only then pick the one you'd actually defend.

The scene. A hospice chaplain, Dale, ex-grad student who never finished the dissertation, is sitting with Ilse, who is 34 and dying and furious about it, and he's trying to talk her out of the fury. He's not doing it with God, he lost that years ago, he's doing it with an argument. I want the argument to be good. I want a reader who knows the literature to nod along. But I also want Ilse to not lose, and I can't figure out how to write her pushback so it isn't just "but I'm scared," which is the line I've been circling for a month.

Here's Dale's argument as it stands in the draft. He lays it out slowly and numbers it, because he's the kind of man who numbers things.

1. Nothing is bad for a person unless there's a person for it to be bad for. Harm needs a bearer.
2. And a bearer at a time. Every bad thing is bad for somebody at some moment, and if you can't say when, you haven't described a harm, you've described a mood.
3. After Ilse dies there's no Ilse, so death isn't bad for her then. The old Epicurean move: where the death is, she isn't.
4. Before she dies she's alive and the death hasn't happened. Being alive isn't the harm. Dreading it is a harm, sure, but dread is a state of the living and it's contingent — Dale is in the room right now removing it, which he takes to be the entire justification of his job.
5. So death is never bad for the person who dies. Not Ilse, not anyone.
6. And the same machinery handles the case everybody already concedes. It isn't bad for me that I wasn't born in 1820 and missed the whole of the nineteenth century; there's no moment of my life at which that absence is bad for me. Lucretius's mirror. The premise that delivers (5) delivers this too, and nobody disputes this one, which Dale reads as confirmation rather than as a reductio.
7. Then he presses, because he's honest and he wants Ilse to see the shape of the thing. By (2), anything bad for me has to be bad at a moment when I exist, and what makes a moment of my life bad is how that moment is for me.
8. So no fact that never touches my experience can make my life worse. A friend's private contempt that I never find out about. A betrayal carried out behind my back. My books pulped and my name forgotten thirty years after I'm gone. None of it lands anywhere, so none of it is a harm to me.
9. Which means a life of perfect contentment that is entirely fabricated is exactly as good as the real one. He says this almost proudly; he thinks it's the consolation with the widest reach.
10. And it means that if somebody put Ilse under painlessly tonight, before the pain got bad, she would lose nothing that was ever hers.

Steps 1 through 6 I'm confident about, they're standard, and readers who've read around will recognize them. It's 7 through 10 that make me want to throw the chapter out, because Dale gets there without doing anything I can catch him doing, and Ilse in the current draft just goes quiet, which reads as surrender.

The thing I keep grabbing at and missing: somewhere between (2) and (7) he seems to trade one claim for a different claim, and I can't name the trade. And (4) has something in it I can't see clearly either — I keep feeling like there's a reading of "bad for her before she dies" that he's ruled out by fiat rather than argued against, but when I try to write the rival reading it comes out mushy.

I'd like to keep (1) if I can. It's the premise that makes Dale sound like a man who has thought about something rather than a man reciting a party line, and it's the one that sounds consistant with the rest of him. I'd give up (8) before (1), and I'd give up (9) and (10) before either, since those are the ones my gut says are already dead on the page. Tell me if that ordering is wrong.

What I need from you:

- Brainstorm first, then the diagnosis. Which step is the bad one, and what exactly is sitting in it — an equivocation, a scope shift, an assumption the premises don't support?
- Why does that step read fine at normal speed? I want to be able to plant it in the prose so it reads fine to a reader too, and then have Ilse step exactly on it.
- Price the alternatives. If Dale gives up (2), or keeps (2) and gives up (7), what does he lose? Does the Epicurean consolation survive in a weaker form, and is the weaker form any use at all to a man in a hospice chair at three in the morning?
- The asymmetry in (6). I want to know whether that step is support for the argument or a trap under it, and whether there's a respectable reply that keeps past nonexistence harmless and future nonexistence a harm without just insisting that we happen to care about the future. If there is one, describe it and say what it costs.
- And the deliverable I actually care about: one or two things Ilse can say back that engage the argument instead of routing around it. Not fear, not a story about her daughter. Something that hands Dale a real problem, in her voice, in a sentence or two, that he can't recieve as a symptom.

Take your time. Be concrete, and be willing to say a step is fine when you think it's fine — I don't want a hedged survey of every theory of well-being in print, I want a diagnosis and a bill of costs for each option. If your honest view is that the chain is valid and the only question is which premise is cheapest to abandon, say so and treat it that way instead.
