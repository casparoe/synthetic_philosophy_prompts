I teach a seventy-five-minute session inside a first-year lettering and type studio. The students have one term of letterform construction behind them — they can build a Roman capital on a grid with a broad nib and compasses, and they have been taught to describe a letter in terms of stem, bowl, counter, terminal, and axis — and they have no philosophy and no cognitive science. The subject of the session is a single question: what makes every 'a' an 'a', given that no feature seems to survive across all of them.

What I need from you is not an explanation of the issue but a plan for explaining it. Please deliver the following.

1. The order in which the pieces must be introduced, with the dependency made explicit at each step: which claim is unavailable to them until some earlier claim is in place, and why. I want a dependency structure, not a list of talking points arranged by interest.

2. The one specimen set that should carry the session — what it must contain and, just as importantly, what it must lack. Assume I have a whiteboard, a printer, and the ability to draw a letter deliberately badly. The contrast I keep reaching for is the double-storey 'a' of Times New Roman against the single-storey 'a' of Futura, plus a child's handwriting 'a'. I am also considering adding the italic from the Times family itself, on the grounds that italics commonly revert to the single-storey form even where the roman is double-storey, so that one typeface would be shown carrying both structures. Tell me whether that fourth specimen sharpens the point or merely invites them to conclude that a family contains two different letters; and tell me what a specimen set must exclude in order not to be solved on the spot by feature-matching, which is the first thing they will attempt.

3. The misconceptions they will bring into the room or form during it, in the order they are likely to surface, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than argued down afterwards. Three I already expect: that the answer is legibility, so that an 'a' is anything a reader recognizes as an 'a', which quietly converts a conceptual problem into a detection threshold; that style is a layer applied over a neutral skeleton they have already been trained to construct; and that once a feature list fails, the whole matter collapses into whatever a given writing culture happened to settle on. The third is the outcome I most want to prevent, because I grade their letterforms and plainly hold that some 'a's are simply wrong — the plan has to make room for a constraint that has no criterion standing behind it.

4. A decision I have been avoiding: whether the studio's working belief in skeleton-then-style should be dismantled during this session or left standing as scaffolding they will outgrow, and at which point in the sequence that decision must be made visible to them. If it should be left standing, tell me what I must not say while leaving it standing.

5. One question I can put to them at the end — a question about a particular letterform or a particular drawn 'a', not a request to restate a thesis — whose answer would show me whether they have grasped that the category is genuinely binding without being definable. Say what a good answer and a bad answer to it sound like.

6. What I should refuse to cover at this level, and should say out loud that I am refusing. For clarity: this question descends from a research project that tried to get a program to draw new letters in the spirit of a given set of letterforms. Strip all of that machinery out. I am not asking you to reconstruct it, and the plan must not depend on my knowing it.

Two further constraints on the plan. I do not want the phrase family resemblance doing any of the work: if it enters the room at all it will enter as a conversation-ender offered by a student who half-heard it somewhere, so tell me where in the sequence to expect it and what to do with it in the moment. And assume the students are intelligent, visually sophisticated, and half-suspicious that they are being handed philosophy in a studio costume; the plan needs to earn its first ten minutes without promising them that it will.
