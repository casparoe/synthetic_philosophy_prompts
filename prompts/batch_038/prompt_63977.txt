The passage:

> Suppose you have a reasoning system R that you trust completely: whenever R tells you that a statement is provable, you believe it. Formally, that means R's own theory proves, for each sentence B, "if R proves B, then B." But that is a soundness claim about R made inside R, and by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem a consistent R can never prove its own consistency — of which the soundness claim is simply the generalization. So R cannot trust itself. It must outsource its reasoning to some other system, and that system faces exactly the same problem, so the regress never terminates. The upshot for artificial intelligence is that a sufficiently intelligent system can never be certain about the output of its own reasoning: it has to treat its future thoughts as fallible evidence rather than as proof, which is precisely why an aligned AI requires human oversight instead of self-verification. Löb's theorem makes the point sharply: the only situation in which a system can derive "if I prove B, then B" is one in which it can already prove B outright, so trusting your own reasoning buys you nothing beyond what you could have derived anyway.
