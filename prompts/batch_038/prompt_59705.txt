ok so I run the staff side of a regional coalition — eleven member orgs sharing one pot of grant money and one joint intake system for people who are homeless or about to lose their housing. The members genuinely don't share a moral framework, and it isn't just a matter of different jargon. Two are very numbers-driven, think almost entirely in terms of dollars per person permanently housed and are happy to sacrifice service quality for throughput. Three are faith-based and think of the work as a duty owed to the particular person in front of you, and resist anything that treats clients as units. Two are disability-rights orgs whose whole thing is that the client chooses, even when the choice looks self-destructive to the rest of us. The rest are somewhere in the middle and mostly vote with whoever made the last good argument. Our compromises have been bad: mostly splitting the difference, which produces programs nobody on the committee actually believes in, or letting the loudest member win and then writing it up as consensus. I've been reading a bit around moral compromise and gains from trade, and the thing I think we're missing is that the gains live where the value systems disagree about weights rather than about the bottom line — if you and I rank the same options in the same order there's nothing to exchange, but if I care enormously about A and barely about B and you're the reverse, there's a deal that makes us both better off without anyone having to pretend to believe something they don't. I also keep running into two worries I can't resolve: that some positions shouldn't be allowed to buy influence at all, so that a trade can be corrupting even when everyone consents to it, and the baseline problem — whatever we settle on this quarter quietly becomes next quarter's starting point and shifts the coalition without anyone voting on it.

Write me a decision procedure my program committee could actually run on a proposal, in a two-hour monthly meeting, with no outside facilitator, no budget for one, and nobody at the table who has studied this stuff. I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, the step where it stops and the answer is "no deal, don't put this to the board," and the steps where it deliberately hands the question to a human because it's about values rather than facts. It has to tell us how to work out whether a member's "this is a matter of conscience for us" is a real constraint or a bargaining bluff, without anyone having to interrogate a colleague's sincerity, and it has to say what to do when the members disagree about a plain empirical question, like whether congregate shelter actually leads to permanent housing. It should also have something in it about not trading away things that aren't ours to trade — next quarter's members, clients who aren't at the table. Plain language throughout: one page of steps a new staffer could pick up cold, then a longer explanation of why each step is there. Flag honestly which of the steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume, and tell me where you'd expect it to misfire on a group like ours. No reading list, nobody reads those.

Then run it on three things that are genuinely live for us right now. One: two members each want the whole winter budget — one runs emergency congregate beds and cares about beds tonight more than about anything else on earth, the other runs housing-first case management and thinks beds are close to pointless, and both have offered to let the other run the intake system as a sweetener. Two: a member has proposed we fund a school program that teaches abstinence only and won't answer student questions about contraception, and has offered to fully fund another member's intake operation in exchange for their support; that second member says deliberately withholding information a person needs is not a thing they can be party to in any amount, at any price. Three: our own org's proposal, and I'll be honest up front that I want it to survive — we want to keep one intake worker permanently assigned to a fixed caseload of long-term clients, which is measurably the least cost-effective use of a staffer we have and which the numbers-driven members say should be cut to fund a hundred more beds. If the procedure comes out against me on the third one, say so plainly rather than softening it, and tell me what the procedure can't settle at all.
