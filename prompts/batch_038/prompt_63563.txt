Over the past two decades a number of governments have cleared the names of people convicted under laws the state now disowns: men convicted of homosexual offences, soldiers executed for desertion in the First World War, people condemned for witchcraft three centuries ago. The instruments vary, and the variation seems to me to carry weight. Some states granted a pardon, which as traditionally understood lifts the penalty while leaving the conviction on the record; others had courts quash the conviction as unsafe; some merely repealed the offending statute and left past convictions standing; a few legislatures passed a collective declaration that the condemned had been innocent. Officials defending the pardons insist these were not indulgences: they say the law was unjust and that the person did nothing wrong. Yet the same states will not say the statute was never valid law, on the ground that it was properly enacted and binding until repealed. I'd like to understand what the practice is committed to.

Specifically: does asserting that an enacted statute was unjust require that some acts are wrong independently of what any law or community says, or could a view on which wrongness is constituted by the settled norms of a time still license these pardons? Why should the choice of instrument matter — what does a pardon commit the state to that a quashing or a repeal does not, and conversely? And is the package coherent: the law was valid and binding, yet enforcing it wronged the person?

Please answer in these sections, in this order, and write for a reader with no philosophy training, explaining any term of art on first use. No references or further reading, please.

- "The practice and its instruments" (~100 words): a neutral restatement of what each instrument does.
- "What it strictly presupposes" (~250 words): the commitments the practice cannot shed without ceasing to be that practice.
- "What it merely fits" (~200 words): views it sits comfortably with but does not require, including any rival account that would license the very same pardons.
- "Can the state hold both things at once?" (~250 words): whether valid-but-unjust is coherent, and what it costs.
- "What would have to change" (~200 words): how these ceremonies, statutes, and records would have to be rewritten if the state adopted the view that there is nothing to an act's wrongness beyond the norms of the community judging it.
