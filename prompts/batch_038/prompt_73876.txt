I serve on the election-rules committee for a student-run lecture series at my university, and I am concurrently enrolled in a social choice seminar, so a dispute at last week's meeting hit rather close to home. For two years the series has selected its annual speaker by approval voting: each member marks any number of the nominees, and the nominee with the most marks wins. This year, with four nominees, the committee added a tiebreak — if two or more nominees tie for the most approvals, eliminate the nominee with the fewest approvals and re-run the vote among the remainder, repeating as necessary, which yields a full ordering of the slate. Two members then argued about what impossibility theorems actually say about this design.

Move 1 (Dev): Our TA told the seminar that Arrow's theorem makes procedures like ours arbitrary, but Arrow's theorem concerns social welfare functions that output a ranking. Approval voting outputs only a winner, so the theorem simply does not apply, and we have nothing to worry about.

Move 2 (Meera): Arrow is only the beginning. Gibbard–Satterthwaite says that any non-dictatorial rule over three or more alternatives that always selects a winner is manipulable. Ours has four alternatives and is not a dictatorship, so manipulability is guaranteed — and truncation is the obvious manipulation, since you should withhold approval from a strong second choice. Dev is safe only by mislabeling the theorem.

Move 3 (Dev): Gibbard–Satterthwaite concerns rules whose input is a profile of ordinal rankings. Approval ballots are not ordinal rankings: with four nominees I can express things no ranking permits, such as approving the middle two and rejecting the extremes. So the hypothesis fails there exactly as it fails for Arrow, and your objection collapses for the same reason my point stands.

Move 4 (Meera): That concedes my argument. If approval ballots are not a record of anyone's underlying preferences, the rule is not aggregating preferences at all; it is aggregating whatever people happened to report. Arrow shows the aggregation problem has no solution when the inputs are ordinal, and you have escaped it only by quietly changing the problem.

Move 5 (Dev): Changing the problem is the standard exit, not a cheat. The familiar criticism of Arrow's theorem is that independence of irrelevant alternatives discards too much information; approval and range voting give up independence and keep Pareto, non-dictatorship, and a transitive outcome. We took one of the legitimate routes out.

Move 6 (Meera): Then consider the tiebreak. Iterative elimination produces exactly the ranking you said we never produce, and it is plainly sensitive to who appears on the ballot — add a clone of a popular nominee and the ordering among unrelated nominees shifts. So Arrow does bite, at least for the ordering.

Neither would yield, and I preferred not to side with either in front of the committee.
