I work on the evaluation side of an animal-welfare charity assessment project. We need a way to compare interventions whose beneficiaries have radically different nervous systems — insects and crustaceans, fish, birds, mammals, and (increasingly, in the literature we're asked to cover) cephalopods. The framework we've converged on multiplies the number of individuals affected by the intensity and duration of suffering prevented, and then by a "moral weight" term meant to capture how much a given animal's suffering counts relative to a human's. For that third term I've been asked to build an index out of brain size, and the defense I've been drafting has three parts. First, brain mass is one of the few variables that can actually be measured across all of these taxa from museum specimens and published allometric equations, so unlike any candidate framed in terms of cognition or affective capacity it is not question-begging: it doesn't presuppose an answer to the question whose answer we need. Second, the standard objection — that brain size can only matter insofar as it tracks cognitive sophistication, which can only matter insofar as it tracks the capacity to suffer — doesn't defeat the index but merely restates what the index is a measure of; every proxy has a target, and naming the target isn't a criticism. Third, on that basis I've been telling colleagues that the live disagreement about brain size is a disagreement about where on the scale the cut-off for inclusion falls, not about the scale itself, and that the second question is the tractable one.

That framing keeps meeting resistance, and I can't tell whether the resistance is substantive or just squeamishness about numbers. The specific points raised against it in our internal review were: that brain mass and neuron number come apart across lineages, with elephants and some birds being the usual examples; that an octopus has the majority of its neurons out in its arms rather than in anything that would show up in a cranial-mass measure; that neuron density differs enough between bird and mammal brains of the same mass that a single allometric curve can't be applied to both; and that within our own species brain size varies substantially while our moral status does not, to which my reply has been that the index is only ever meant to be applied between species, never within one.

Could you tell me what is actually going wrong here, if anything? In particular: is there a principled reason a measurable variable can fail to be non-question-begging even when everyone in the dispute agrees it correlates with the property that does the moral work? What would have to be true about the relations among brain size, cognition, and the capacity to suffer for my first two claims to hold, and how would I know? Are the objections in the second paragraph pointing at measurement error I can absorb into a calibration step, or at something worse — that the index is not measuring one property across these taxa but different properties in different lineages, so that there is no single scale for the cut-off question to be about? And is my third claim right: is the threshold really where the disagreement lives? I'd also like an assessment of my reply that the index is never applied within a species, since that restriction seems to be doing more work than I've accounted for.

Please be explicit throughout about where the line falls between what is settled and what is genuinely contested, separately in the comparative neuroanatomy and in the philosophy of moral status; I don't want a contested view — mine or my critics' — handed back to me as though it were the consensus, and I'd rather be told that one of my three premises is doing unearned work than have the argument tidied up.
