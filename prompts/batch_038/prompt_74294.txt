I'm TA-ing a grad seminar on propaganda and political language this term, and the visiting journalist who ran our first session leaned really hard on an image he says comes from Orwell: prose is like a window pane, and the political writer's whole job is to keep the glass clean. He built an actual argument on it — roughly, that euphemism, jargon, and bureaucratic abstraction are smudges on the glass, so if you train people to write plainly and strip out the officialese, whatever is behind the glass becomes visible, and once it's visible people will resist it. His punchline was that clarity work is basically an anti-authoritarian technology all by itself: you don't have to touch institutions or incentives, just the style manuals and the copy desk, and the truth will show itself. A couple of students pushed back and I couldn't say anything more precise than "that feels too easy," which is why I'm asking.

Could you put on the hat of a philosopher of language who also knows the Orwell corpus properly and audit this analogy for me? Please think carefully and start with a short brainstorming pass where you list the ways the image could be read (window as medium, window as barrier, window as evidence of the writer's sincerity, whatever you come up with) before you commit to one. Then give me the explicit mapping — a table is fine — of what plays the glass, what the smudges are, where the light comes from, what the thing-seen is, who the viewer is, and what the cleaning cloth corresponds to. After that, walk through where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually threaten his conclusion and the ones that are harmless decoration. I especially want to know what the source domain quietly smuggles in: I'm suspicious that it presupposes a finished, pre-linguistic thought sitting behind the glass waiting to be seen, a viewer who is passive and equally positioned, and a slide from seeing to caring.

Finally, square the image against the rest of what Orwell says about language — the feedback-loop point that an effect can become a cause, so that slovenly language and sloppy thinking reinforce each other in a cycle, and the Newspeak idea in *Nineteen Eighty-Four* that narrowing the available vocabulary narrows what can be thought at all. If language is the pane you look through, is it maybe also part of what determines what's on the other side, and does that wreck the window picture or just complicate it? Give me a straight verdict on whether his conclusion (clarity alone is enough, no institutional change needed) survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it doesn't, propose a better analogy for the relation between plain style and political understanding — and say what that better analogy would and wouldn't license. Sorry if this is a lot; I just don't want a mushy "well, both sides have a point" answer.
