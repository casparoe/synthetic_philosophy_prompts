You are advising the selection committee of a small institute that funds conceptual work in the philosophy of biology. We have one two-year postdoctoral position, and its mandate has been fixed in advance: the appointee will work on the units-of-selection debate. They will have no laboratory capacity and no means of generating data of their own, but they will have two standing collaborators — an experimental evolutionist who runs selection experiments on multicellular clusters of yeast propagated by selecting for faster settling, and a cancer genomics group interested in somatic evolution within tumours. The committee expects a conceptual result that a working biologist would find worth citing, not a piece of internal philosophy.

We would like your recommendation on which open question the position should be aimed at, and on the ordering of the rest. The candidate questions as we currently have them:

1. Whether the deflationary reading is correct — that the standard covariance partition shows that any evolutionary change can be described at any level, so that level attributions are choices among equally adequate bookkeeping schemes rather than reports of a fact about a system.
2. Whether group-level fitness is a genuine causal property of a collective or a statistical aggregate of individual fitnesses, and whether there is any principled criterion for telling the two cases apart that does not collapse into an appeal to explanatory convenience.
3. Whether the familiar marks of evolutionary individuality — a single-cell bottleneck in the life cycle, a division of labour between reproductive and somatic parts, low within-collective conflict — are necessary, sufficient, or merely typical, given systems that violate them while still evolving as collectives.
4. Whether intra-genomic conflict and selfish genetic elements show that the gene fails to be a unit in the relevant sense, or whether they are instead the strongest evidence for sub-individual levels of selection that every account must accommodate.
5. Whether somatic evolution in cancer makes the organism a leaky or contested unit, and what a philosopher could add here beyond what modellers already supply.
6. Whether cultural group selection revives the group-level question in a way that no biological case does, and whether the same conceptual criteria can be carried across the biological/cultural boundary without equivocation.

Write the committee a memo of roughly twelve hundred words. Deliver a defended ordering, not a survey. For each question in your ranking, say explicitly what makes it worth two years: how much of the rest of the agenda turns on its answer, how tractable it is for someone with no data but with these two particular collaborators, how far it has already been worked over, and what a result would look like as against what would count as failure. Where these criteria conflict, say so and say which you are trading off against which — we would rather see the trade-off than a ranking that looks unanimous.

Treat question 1 as a special case and tell us whether it belongs in the list at all, or whether it is a background crux that conditions how the other five should be ranked: if the deflationary reading is right, does it moot the rest, or merely reframe what an answer to them could consist in?

For the top two entries, say what developments over the next three to five years — in the experimental evolution of collectives, in tumour phylogenetics, in the microbiome literature, or in the conceptual literature itself — would move them up or down, and what would persuade us that the project had stalled.

Finally, the committee is presently inclined toward question 5, on the grounds that it looks like the shortest route to a biologist audience. Several of us suspect that is precisely the wrong instinct. Address that inclination directly: either vindicate it or argue against it, and in the latter case name which entry you would remove from the list altogether as a dead end for a project of this shape and size, even if that disappoints us.

Refer to positions, distinctions, and arguments by their content rather than by author or title; please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind, since we will handle the scholarly apparatus ourselves. Assume your reader knows the standard framework well — the replicator/interactor distinction, the difference between group structure affecting individual fitness and selection among collectives as such, and the covariance decomposition — but has not thought seriously about the empirical systems named above.
