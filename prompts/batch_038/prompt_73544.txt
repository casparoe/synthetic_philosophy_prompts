I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level course in the philosophy of the social sciences, and I need an independent assessment of one script before I calibrate the rest of the marking. The question was set as a closed-book take-home, so students were told that page references and bibliographies were neither expected nor rewarded; please evaluate the answer on its content alone and do not deduct anything for the absence of citations. I have not marked it myself, and I would rather not have my own reading of it seeded in advance.

The question, worth 30 marks, was as follows:

> Popper opens The Poverty of Historicism by setting out the historicist position as a chain of claims and then attacking it. Reconstruct that chain, identify which link Popper thinks fails and why, and explain what his argument leaves standing in the social sciences. Close by saying whether you think the argument establishes too much, too little, or about the right amount.

The student's answer was:

> Popper defines historicism as an approach to the social sciences which assumes that historical prediction is their principal aim, and which holds that this aim can be met by uncovering the laws or trends according to which history unfolds. He notes that historicism appears in both a pro-naturalistic and an anti-naturalistic form: the first wants the social sciences to imitate the methods of physics, the second insists that society is too different from nature for that, yet both agree that the task is to foretell the future course of history.
>
> Popper reconstructs the historicist case as a sequence of steps. First, the course of human history is strongly influenced by the growth of human knowledge. Second, we cannot predict the future growth of our knowledge by rational or scientific methods. Third, we therefore cannot predict the future course of human history. Fourth, this rules out the possibility of a theoretical history that would correspond to theoretical physics. Fifth, historicism consequently collapses in its central claim to be a science of historical development.
>
> The link that fails, on Popper's diagnosis, is the historicist's implicit assumption that the growth of knowledge can itself be anticipated; Popper defends the second premise precisely in order to block that assumption. His reason is that a prediction of a future scientific discovery would have to contain the discovery: if we could predict in detail what we shall come to know, we should already know it, and what was predicted would not be a future discovery at all. Popper also warns against confusing trends with laws. A trend may be perfectly real — rising population, say, or continued industrialisation — but a statement asserting that a trend obtains at a given time and place is a singular historical statement rather than a universal law, and trends depend on initial conditions which may alter. The historicist, in Popper's telling, mistakes a tendency observed over a long stretch under particular conditions for a law of development, and then reads necessity into it. He adds that social predictions can influence the events they predict, an effect he names after the oracle whose prophecy helped bring itself about, though he treats this as a further complication rather than the heart of his case.
>
> Because the growth of knowledge enters into every social process and cannot be forecast, no statement about the future of any society can be lawlike; the social sciences are accordingly left with the description and rational reconstruction of particular events. Popper's positive alternative, which he takes over from J. S. Mill, is to explain social phenomena by deriving them from the laws of individual human psychology, so that sociology is ultimately grounded in a science of human nature.
>
> The argument seems to me to prove too much. If we cannot predict what we shall come to know, we also cannot rule out that some future discovery will make historical prediction possible; and Popper's own thesis that knowledge grows unpredictably is itself a thesis about the future growth of knowledge, which looks self-referentially awkward. A historicist could press both points.
