I've been reading decision-theory arguments on LW and its descendants for something like fifteen years and I've finally written my own Allais position down in one place for a little informal write-up. I want it stress-tested, not explained to me — assume I already know the standard moves and just do the audit.

Setup, textbook version: problem 1 is 1A = $1M for sure vs 1B = 10% of $5M, 89% of $1M, 1% of nothing. Problem 2 is 2A = 11% of $1M vs 2B = 10% of $5M, 90% of nothing.

What I claim to hold:

1. My reason for wanting EU-maximizing preferences is entirely the exploitability one. Linearity in probabilities isn't pretty to me; its just that if your preferences aren't representable that way, somebody can make money off you across a sequence of choices. The normativity comes from the pump, not from the axiom.

2. I make the Allais choices — 1A and 2B — and I don't think that's a bug in me. The certainty is doing something real, it isn't a presentation artifact.

3. Reduction of compound lotteries: yes, obviously. A 10% chance of a 50% chance is 5%. I have no patience for "well, the two stages feel different" reasoning.

4. Savage's sure-thing principle — your preference between two acts shouldn't depend on the outcome they agree on — strikes me as about as close to a priori as decision theory gets.

5. I want to be dynamically consistent, and I'm a plan-maker rather than a myopic chooser: if I'd take X off a menu, I want to still take X as the menu narrows. I explicitly refuse the sophisticated-chooser dodge where each step looks fine locally and the trajectory is a disaster.

6. Transitivity and completeness are off the table as casualties. Threads keep insisting that giving up independence puts you on a slope to intransitive preferences, and if that's genuinely what (2) costs me, I need to hear it said plainly.

7. If there's a repair for (2), I assume it lives in the probability weighting rather than the utility function — some inverse-S thing that overweights the certain branch, CPT-flavored. I get that concave utility of money doesn't do the work here.

8. Half-remembered from some thread and im not sure I believe it: if you're allowed to rewrite the sure $1M as two separate 44.5% branches, people's choices change, and no weighting-function model can absorb that, because such a model only ever sees the reduced distribution. If that's right it seems to hurt (7) harder than it hurts (2).

What I want: go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely can't coexist, ranked by how badly they hurt — don't flag everything as equally suspicious. For each real conflict, say which commitment has to go, what giving it up costs me, and then name the single cheapest revision to the whole set. Also point out the pairs that only look like conflicts, because I suspect at least one of mine is bogus and I can't tell which. In particular I want to know whether (7) is a real improvement on (2) with respect to (1) and (5), or whether it just relocates the problem somewhere I'm not looking. No reading list, no history lesson, no citations — I'm not going to look anything up, I just want the reasoning.
