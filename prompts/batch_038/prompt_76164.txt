I sit on the governance committee of a consortium that sets the technical rules for machine-to-machine commerce: some forty member firms whose software agents negotiate and settle transactions with one another automatically. Nearly every negotiation is one-shot. An agent deals with a counterparty it will never meet again, and because agents are re-keyed each session, nothing about the encounter attaches to a durable identity — reputation cannot do any work here. Each negotiation has a prisoner's-dilemma shape: the joint gain from settling on terms is real, but each side does better by taking a harder line than the terms it has itself proposed.

A member has proposed amending the protocol so that, before bargaining, agents may exchange an inspectable statement of the decision rule they will use, and may condition their own rule on what they read — cooperating, for instance, only with counterparties whose rule cooperates with cooperators. Proponents say this substitutes logical structure for the missing shadow of the future. Our technical staff report four difficulties: a disclosed rule cannot be authenticated, so nothing guarantees the running agent matches it; a rule can condition on features of the counterparty's rule, so trustworthiness can be programmed to switch on only for designated partners, and from outside it is indistinguishable from the general kind; rules that inspect one another have already produced unbounded simulation loops in our sandbox; and cooperation confined to like-minded rules would let the largest members form closed circles that simply exclude smaller firms. One faction now wants to mandate authenticated disclosure through a hardware attestation service only the largest members can afford.

We vote in three weeks among four options: prohibit rule-conditioning, permit it as optional, permit it subject to conditions, or mandate authenticated disclosure. Draft the written opinion I will circulate to the committee, recommending one option and defending it. Please use these sections in this order: "Recommendation" (about 100 words: which option, and the conditions attached to it); "What rule-conditioning changes about the game" (about 250 words); "The case for permitting it" (about 200 words); "The strongest objection, and what answering it costs us" (about 250 words); "Conditions and how we would enforce them" (about 200 words); "What would change my recommendation" (about 100 words). I want reasons the committee can argue with, not a survey of the options.
