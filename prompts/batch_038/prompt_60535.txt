I convene a small reading group on the governance of emerging technologies, and this term we have been working through the coordination problems that arise when a capability is spread across many independent parties. Our running example is a consortium of some thirty laboratories, each of which is able to publish a method that would lower the barrier to engineering a particular pathogen, and each of which forms its own view of whether publication would do more good than harm; the familiar difficulty is that what actually happens is decided by whichever laboratory thinks best of the matter, rather than by what the balance of informed opinion across the consortium would hold if the question were put to it as a whole. For next week's session I want to circulate an argument of my own construction which is meant to establish, as strongly as possible, that members of such a group ought to defer to its collective judgement. The trouble is that when I write it out it appears to establish that no group can ever do anything at all, and I have not been able to locate the slide.

Here is the argument. (1) Where a capability is available to n parties, each of which forms an independent estimate of the value of exercising it and acts whenever that estimate is favourable, the capability will be exercised if any one estimate is favourable. (2) Because the estimates are independent and noisy, the most favourable of them is likelier to be an overestimate than any other; so the chance that at least one party acts rises with n, and approaches certainty as n grows. (3) Consequently a capability whose true net value is markedly negative, and which nearly every party would decline to exercise if the question were put to the group as a whole, will nevertheless be exercised. (4) So the only way for such a group to prevent its exercise is for each member to treat the pooled judgement of the group as decisive whenever it conflicts with their own estimate. (5) A member who sets the pooled judgement aside on the ground that they are better informed in this instance is acting on a policy which, if adopted by all the members, would return the group to the situation described in (1); and a policy which cannot be adopted by all cannot rationally be adopted by one. (6) Therefore no member may act on their own estimate against the pooled judgement, however strong their reasons for thinking it mistaken. (7) The reasoning does not stop at the level of the whole group: a subcommittee that reports against the consortium's view, a member state that breaches a treaty body's decision, a researcher who overrides their laboratory's settled position are each doing the same thing, and (5) applies to each alike, whoever departs from the judgement of the group to which they belong being a unilateralist whatever the size of that group. (8) But a collective judgement does not arrive unmade: somebody convenes the meeting, a working group drafts the report, a motion is carried, and each of these is an act by some members which the group as a whole has not, at the time of the act, judged worth performing. (9) By (7) each such act is unilateralist unless the group as a whole has authorised it in advance; and since the authorisation would itself be an act by some members, it too would require an authorisation in advance, and so on without end. (10) No group can complete an infinite series of prior authorisations, so no group can ever act, and it cannot even adopt the norm in (6), since adopting it would be an act. (11) A principle of participation whose adoption would make participation impossible cannot be the correct principle of participation. (12) Therefore (4) is false: the difficulty described in (3) gives no reason for members to defer to the collective judgement, and each may act on their own estimate after all.

I cannot accept (12), and I do not think the case for deferral is as weak as (12) makes it look, so something earlier has gone wrong. What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a rebuttal. Tell me which step to reject — or, if you think several are defective, rank them by how much work each is doing — explain why that step looked innocent when I wrote it, and say what I pay for each of the repairs available to me. Then tell me whether the argument can be fixed rather than merely stopped: what is the strongest conclusion about deference that survives once the defect is removed, and does that conclusion still hold for a group of three, where the aggregate reasoning in (1) to (3) plainly does not bite? Where you say that a term is doing double duty, give me the two readings and say which premises come out true on each. For what it is worth, I would rather surrender (2) than (6), since (6) is the principle I actually want for the handout and (2) is only a heuristic about noisy estimates; I suspect that preference is carrying more weight than I can defend, and I am willing to be told so. One of the readers has already objected that (6) cannot be right because it would forbid a member from acting on evidence the group has never seen, and I should like to know whether that is the real problem or merely the most obvious one. Please do not send me a bibliography or a list of who has argued what; I will supply the references for the handout myself, and my interest is entirely in the structure of the reasoning.
