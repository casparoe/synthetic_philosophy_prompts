I'm putting together notes for a reading group on formal epistemology and I've hit a wall: I can't explain to anybody why the Dutch book argument for Probabilism and the accuracy-dominance argument for Probabilism are two different arguments rather than one argument with the word "money" swapped out for "inaccuracy." Both end up in the same place — if your credences aren't probabilities, something dominates them. Can you compare them properly for me?

I want the comparison driven by cases rather than by definitions, so here are two I cooked up.

Case 1. My friend Dev. Dev's credence that the council approves his building permit is 0.6, and his credence that it doesn't approve it is also 0.6 — he'll say both out loud if you ask him, cheerfully. He also has a phobia about wagering: he has never accepted a bet in his life, never will, and finds being offered one genuinely upsetting. Which of the two arguments actually gets any traction on Dev? Be precise about whether either one licenses calling Dev irrational, or only that he'd be in a bad spot if he were the sort of person who bets.

Case 2. Rani, whose credences are textbook-perfect probablities — nothing to complain about on the coherence front. But her utility for money is sharply diminishing: the first $200 she wins this month feels like a lot, the next $200 much less, and losses sting out of proportion. Because of that her price for buying a bet on X and her price for selling one come apart, and I'm fairly confident a sharp bookie can exploit the spread and hand her a guaranteed net loss without her being incoherent in any way whatsoever. Does the Dutch book argument condemn Rani? Does the accuracy argument have anything at all to say about her? If one of them bites and the other doesn't, tell me which one has got the right verdict and why.

Separately, a technical thing I keep hand-waving at: the accuracy argument needs an inaccuracy measure, and the usual one is additive — score each proposition, add them up. Suppose someone in the group digs in and insists on a non-additive measure instead, say the inaccuracy of a whole credence function is just the worst inaccuracy it has on any single proposition rather than the sum. Does the dominace argument still go through? If it doesn't, what does that tell me about how much of the work in the accuracy argument is being done by the scoring rule as opposed to by coherence itself?

Then give me an actual verdict rather than a both-sides shrug. Which of the two is the better argument, and does your answer flip if the target norm is Conditionalization instead of Probabilism? I ask because I suspect one of them generalizes to updating rules much more gracefully than the other, and I'd like to be pushed back on if that's wrong.

Wherever you have to stipulate something to get the argument moving — how credences hook up to betting behaviour, whether anyone is obliged to want accuracy, how payoffs combine — flag it as a stipulation out in the open instead of burying it in the setup.
