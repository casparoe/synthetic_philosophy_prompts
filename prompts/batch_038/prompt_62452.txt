I'm writing up the reasoning memo for our national bioethics council's next ruling, and I want to know whether we can stay consistent with ourselves. Our record so far, roughly:

- woman who delays pregnancy three years for her career and ends up with a different child whose life is just as good: fine, we didn't think it was a real case at all
- deaf couple who pick a deaf embryo at IVF so the kid shares their language and community: not fine
- woman who conceives now knowing a drug she cant come off yet will leave the child a moderate impairment, when waiting four months would mean a different, unaffected child: not fine, though half the council insisted she isn't harmed and has no complaint, since she wouldn't otherwise exist
- couple who have one child instead of the three they could easily afford and raise, all of whom would have had excellent lives: fine, and we said there was nothing to be said against it
- woman who conceives knowing the child's life will be dominated by pain and not worth living: not fine, and here we did say the child is harmed and wronged

The new file is a housing and infrastructure program that will support a much larger population two centuries out, every one of them with a life clearly worth living but noticeably less good than the lives of the smaller population who'd otherwise have existed. Nobody who'd otherwise exist is affected in any way.

Predict which way we go. Which rule is the council actually running — some impersonal comparison of outcomes, a complaint-style person-affecting view, something asymmetric about creating people, a duty you owe the particular child you'll have? Lay out which of those fit all five rulings, and whether you're reading our rulings as tracking worse outcomes or wrong acts, since those might come apart in the one-child-vs-three line. Then say what each surviving rule predicts for the new file, which one you'd bet on and why, and name one further case we could put in front of the council that would split the survivors cleanly.
