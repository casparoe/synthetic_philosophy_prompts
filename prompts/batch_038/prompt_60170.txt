Acting as a philosopher of economics with a genuine command of social choice theory, please help me settle an argument by reframing it. A friend and I spent two hours on whether interpersonal comparisons of well-being are ever legitimate, and we agreed the dispute is unproductive until we know what each side is demanding. So: write the desiderata — the full specification any account of interpersonal utility comparison must meet to be usable for a specific purpose, namely ranking two public programs whose benefits fall on populations that differ in their capacity for hedonic adaptation and in their norms of self-reporting (relief for people who long ago adjusted their expectations downward versus first treatment for people who have not).

Begin by brainstorming more candidate requirements than you intend to keep, and say explicitly which you discard and why. For each one retained, give the case or consideration motivating it and mark it as non-negotiable or as the local preference of one camp. Press hardest on: what fixes the zero point and the unit of a common scale, and which transformations of individual scales the theory's verdicts must survive; whether a subject's own report can be overridden, and on what grounds; what evidence the comparisons must be recoverable from — choices, avowals, or something else; and whether the account must yield a complete social ranking or may stop at local pairwise verdicts. If you invoke an aggregation result, state its conditions rather than its name.

Finish by telling me which single requirement eliminates the most popular accounts, and whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable — if the set of requirements turns out to be empty, that is the result I most want. One line each for hedonist, preference-satisfaction, objective-list, and capability approaches is sufficient as a scorecard; no references needed.
