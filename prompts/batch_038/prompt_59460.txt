I direct a small mutual-aid fund: roughly four thousand recurring donors, and between thirty and fifty requests for assistance circulated each month. We have no caseworkers and no triage committee. A request is written by the person asking, sometimes with a volunteer's help, posted to the donor list, and funded or unfunded entirely by whether donors respond. To keep the process legible and consistent we maintain a house style guide for how a request is to be written. In summary, it requires a first-person narrative of a few hundred words; a specific figure and what the money is for; one photograph; a tone that is calm and forward-looking; updates that thank donors and report progress; and a sentence or two on what the requester contributes to family, work, or neighborhood. It prohibits expressions of anger toward a landlord, employer, insurer, or relative; self-recrimination or extended complaint; graphic description of illness or injury; and figures above a set ceiling, on the stated ground that donors cannot picture a very large sum, so large requests are split into stages or informally discouraged.

Two findings came out of our annual review. First, requests that open in a hopeful or determined register are typically fully funded within days, while those that open in despair, and those involving chronic conditions — chronic pain, disability, addiction, mental illness — frequently fail to raise a quarter of the amount sought, even where the underlying need is by any measure greater. Second, a colleague on the board insists this is not a defect but the point: his view is that the style guide is Adam Smith operationalized, a set of instructions for producing concord between what a requester expresses and what a spectator is able to enter into, with our donors collectively discharging the office of the impartial spectator. I would like that claim examined rather than accepted.

(a) Reconstruct the theory the practice presupposes. What must it assume about how moral approval is generated, about what a spectator can know of another's need, and about the standing of the person requesting, for these particular rules to make sense as rules? Be specific about which rules carry which commitments: what work the prohibition on anger and the requirement of calm are doing, and what the "cannot picture it" ceiling assumes about the relation between felt response and moral claim.

(b) Is my colleague right that this is Smith's account, and specifically the impartial spectator? Distinguish the uncorrected sympathies of actual spectators from the judgment Smith thinks we reach only through general rules and by adopting a standpoint that discounts the accidents of vividness, nearness, and agreeableness — and tell me whether our practice presupposes the latter, or has built in the former while describing it in the vocabulary of the latter.

(c) Which rival accounts of why the needy are to be helped does the practice rule out, and which is it compatible with despite our self-description as need-based and impartial? I have in mind a utilitarian triage model, an entitlement or insurance model, a respect-based model that refuses to make assistance conditional on being an agreeable object of attention, and a character or desert model. Our prohibition on self-recrimination and our request for the requester's contributions look to me like a desert test we officially disclaim; say whether that reading holds. More generally, determine whether the gap between our self-description and our allocation mechanism is a contradiction in the practice's presuppositions or merely a failure of implementation.

(d) Identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which of its features would survive intact if the spectator story were replaced by one of the rivals, and which would have to go. Then describe concretely what a fund built on that rival would look like — intake procedure, allocation rule, tone requirements, the role of the photograph, the ceiling.

I am after the reconstruction rather than the scholarship, so gesture at Smith and his rivals by idea and argument; I will supply references myself.
