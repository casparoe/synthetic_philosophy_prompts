=== EXAM QUESTION (Essay 2 of 3; 45 minutes; 25 points) ===

Gregory Kavka's 1983 paper "The Toxin Puzzle" describes an eccentric billionaire who offers you one million dollars provided that, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink the contents of a vial tomorrow afternoon; the toxin will make you painfully ill for a day but will neither kill you nor cause lasting harm. The billionaire has a reliable means of determining whether you have the relevant intention at midnight, the money reaches your account tomorrow morning, and you are entirely free to change your mind afterwards.

(a) Set out the puzzle, making precise what the payoff depends on and what the agent knows at each stage.
(b) Identify the step that makes it impossible, on Kavka's diagnosis, for the agent to form the required intention, and state what more general claim about intention-formation that step is supposed to support.
(c) Explain how the toxin puzzle differs from Newcomb's problem, and say whether the difference matters for what each puzzle is supposed to show about rational choice.
(d) Evaluate this claim: the toxin puzzle establishes that an agent can never rationally form an intention on the basis of the benefits of forming it.

=== STUDENT ANSWER ===

The billionaire will pay you a million dollars if at midnight tonight you intend to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon. Drinking it makes you ill for a day but does no lasting damage, and you get paid in the morning, hours before the time for drinking arrives. So whether you drink has no effect at all on whether you are paid. The billionaire can tell whether you have the intention, so you cannot bluff.

(a) The payoff is keyed to your state of mind at midnight, not to your action. By tomorrow afternoon the million is already yours, and drinking would only make you sick, so at that point you will have no reason to drink. The agent knows all of this in advance, and knows that the billionaire knows it.

(b) The crucial step is that you cannot intend to do something you believe you will not do. Since the agent knows he will have no reason to drink tomorrow, he knows he will not drink, and therefore he cannot now intend to drink. The general claim is that intentions are not under our direct voluntary control. The million is a reason to have the intention, not a reason to perform the act, and intentions can only be formed on the basis of reasons for acting. Kavka's argument works because the reason and the thing it is a reason for come apart: the reward attaches to the state, while deliberation can only be about the act.

(c) Structurally this is Newcomb's problem run backwards. In Newcomb's problem a predictor fills the boxes on the basis of a prediction of your choice, so taking only the opaque box has the better expected payoff even though taking both boxes dominates, and the dispute between one-boxers and two-boxers is a dispute about whether to follow dominance or expected utility. Kavka's puzzle is generated by the same machinery: the billionaire's detection of your intention is a prediction of what you will do tomorrow, so it is once again a clash between dominance reasoning, which says keep the million and stay healthy, and expected-utility reasoning, which says become someone who drinks. Kavka's own preferred solution is the resolute one — once the intention is formed it is irrational to reopen deliberation, so the agent intends and then drinks — and he rejects it only on the ground that people can always change their minds, which simply assumes what is at issue.

(d) The claim in (d) follows from (b). If you cannot intend to drink for the sake of the million, you cannot intend anything for the sake of the benefits of intending it, which is also why promising is impossible in principle: a promise is just an intention that is rewarded by the advantages of having made it, and the toxin case shows that such a reward cannot produce the intention. The standard reply is that you could change the payoffs, for instance by signing before midnight an enforceable contract to pay a heavy penalty if you fail to drink tomorrow, which would give you a genuine reason to drink and hence make the intention possible. But this cannot work either. The contract has to be in place before midnight in order to affect what the billionaire detects, and the only reason to sign it is the million, so signing is itself an act performed for the sake of a state you cannot bring about; the same problem simply reappears one step earlier, and then again, without end. So the puzzle shows a general limit: we can intend only what we already have independent reason to do.
