For a graduate seminar on incompleteness and its philosophical uses, write a decision tree that sorts readers by their commitments about what the second incompleteness theorem can show about minds. Start from a node everyone in the seminar accepts — that no consistent, recursively axiomatized theory containing arithmetic proves its own consistency — and branch from there. The tree must include nodes on: whether a person's mathematical output is even the sort of thing a single determinate formal system could capture; whether a person could know her own soundness, and whether failing that kills the argument or only weakens it; how much work the idealization to unlimited output does; and whether the theorem is better read as disclosing a limit on us or a capacity we possess.

Phrase every node as a question a first-year graduate student could answer without knowing where it leads, using no person's name, no position label, and no technical term of art from the debate. Each leaf should be a position someone actually defends, identified by its commitments (you may supply the name only after the description), together with that position's most costly concession. Mark one combination of answers you judge unoccupied and say whether that is because it is incoherent, unmotivated, or simply unexplored, and flag any two leaves your questions fail to separate. Keep the whole thing under 900 words, with node questions of at most two sentences and leaves of at most three, and close with one line naming the question that does the most sorting work.
