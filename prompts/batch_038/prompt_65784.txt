For eleven weeks I have been trying to get five colleagues to put their names, alongside mine, to a joint claim for unpaid overtime against the company that employs us. Six of us work the same night line. The claim proceeds only if at least five of us are named; anyone named without protection is, in practice, first on the list at the next round of layoffs, and losing this job would cost me my visa. Three weeks ago a union lawyer told me, in writing and on the record, that the state board has already opened an investigation into the site, which makes anyone named on such a claim statutorily protected from retaliation. That one fact would dissolve the risk for all six of us. I have spent three weeks trying to get it to the other five and I have gotten nowhere.

The obstacle is that we cannot meet and we cannot use the group chat. Management reads the chat, phones are banned on the floor, and our shifts are staggered so that no two of us are ever in the break room for more than a minute. Everything travels as handwritten notes passed between shifts, and notes go missing — a locker is searched, a pocket is emptied, a page is dropped on wet concrete — with no way for the sender to tell whether it arrived. So we built acknowledgment into the practice: if you get a note you write back, and if you get the write-back you write back again. We are four rounds deep. All six have said we will sign. Nobody has signed, and the deadline is in nine days.

A friend who teaches economics told me last week that our problem is not nerve but the absence of common knowledge, and that a fifth round of notes would not help either. Marcus, who is one of the six, says she is an academic inventing a dignified excuse for cowardice. I want something to put on the table between them.

Build me a thought experiment, to the following specifications, that I can trace by hand in ten minutes. It should be our situation stripped down and made exact — the six of us, the fact, the notes, the failures — with the mess removed.

Specifications:

1. Six named people, ordinary stakes, ordinary technology. No demons, no omniscient predictors, no exotic machinery, no public announcement or broadcast of any kind inside the case, no meeting, no shared channel. The only thing wrong with the communication is that a message can fail to arrive without the sender knowing.
2. The payoff-relevant fact must be true, and at least one of the six must know it directly rather than from the chain.
3. Nobody may be slow, inattentive, or limited in how many steps of "she knows that I know that she knows" they can perform. The finite depth of knowledge has to come entirely from the message structure and not from anyone's head.
4. The payoffs, and everyone's full rationality, must be common knowledge among the six, so that the only thing missing is the higher-order status of the fact.

Deliverables, in this order:

a. The scenario, in no more than three paragraphs: the six, the fact, the protocol for passing and acknowledging notes, and the precise way a note can vanish undetected.
b. The payoffs, as a small table or as one sentence per state, showing three things: that if the fact is common knowledge all six sign and all six are better off; that if the fact is known by all six but not commonly known, signing is the rational choice for none of them; and that a small residual doubt about the others is by itself enough to tip any one of them against signing. The tipping has to come out of the numbers, not out of your telling me it does.
c. A level-by-level verification. Write out the first four levels of mutual knowledge of the fact for one named pair in full iterated form, and then exhibit a concrete world — a specific history of which notes went and which went missing — in which the fifth level fails for that same pair.
d. The induction itself: what each of the six does, in order from the one with the least information to the one with the most, with the reasoning that gets each of them there.
e. Two controls. Show that a fifth round of acknowledgments changes nothing, and that a single disclosure made to all six simultaneously changes everything. Then say exactly which property of that disclosure does the work that no finite sequence of pairwise notes can do.
f. Which single feature of your case is carrying the weight, and what the verdicts become if I remove it — specifically, if notes cannot go missing, or if the sender always learns whether the note arrived.
g. Marcus's best reply: that common knowledge is a philosopher's fiction, that we are simply risk-averse and dressing it up, and that the whole thing rests on an infinite hierarchy no real person ever climbs. Put it at its strongest and tell me whether it lands.

The case is meant to settle an argument between two people who have never studied any of this, so it fails if the verdicts are asserted rather than derived, and it fails if following the argument requires notation I cannot explain across a kitchen table.

Begin with your bottom line in a single sentence: whether a case meeting every one of these specifications can actually be built, and if two of them are in tension, which one has to give. Then the reasoning, in the order above. No preamble, no restating what I asked for, and no references or results named after people — I am on shift at six, and I want something I can read once and carry with me.
