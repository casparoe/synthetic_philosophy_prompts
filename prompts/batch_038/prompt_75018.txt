I have spent eight years in one valley and am now in the seventh draft of the novel it produced. Let me set out my position plainly, because I want an answer that engages with my judgements rather than with my credentials.

Carbeck is not on any map. It is my own composite of three real northern valleys; its mill, its failed embankment and its 1962 reservoir are inventions; the archive I describe below is therefore mine alone, and you will not be able to check it and need not try. I have no interest in sources, references or a reading list. I want reasoning.

Before I began, I read Nietzsche's meditation on history for life — the second of the Untimely Meditations, 1874 — and its three ways of holding a past, monumental, antiquarian and critical, have been loose in my head ever since, together with its talk of plastic power and of the beast that is content because it forgets. The essay gave me no rule. I have made my decisions one at a time. Here they are, with my verdicts, and I should like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. The embankment failed in two stages over nine hours, the second at first light. I make it fail once, at midnight. Fine. I have never doubted it and no reader has ever objected.

2. There were two owners, father and son: one signed the compensation cheques, one refused for eleven years. I merge them into a single man who signs and then refuses, and who is appalled by himself. Fine, though I flinch on each rereading.

3. The inspector of works was seventeen and kept his figures in a copybook; I have seen the hand. My editor asked me to age him by ten years, since nobody will believe it. I refused. Fine — I think I was obliged to refuse. The detail I could not have invented is the one I may not alter.

4. Of the strike committee's chairman four reported sentences survive, all of them flat. I give him eleven minutes on the bridge, in a rhetoric he could not have commanded, making an argument that the book goes on to dismantle. Fine. I will defend this to anyone.

5. The owner's cruelty is documented as intermittent, opportunistic and almost entirely lawful. I have made it a system: he keeps a book of grievances and he takes pleasure in it. Not fine. I know it is not fine, I have left it in, and I cannot account for the difference between this and 4. The distortions run in the same direction; only my sympathy differs.

6. A living great-granddaughter read the draft and was wounded, so I gave her ancestor a scene of repentance on the embankment. Not fine: the book went soft exactly where it had been hard. Yet I also think I was right to show the manuscript to the family at all and would do it again, and I cannot hold the two halves of that judgement together.

7. The bell that rang of its own accord survives only in a note written in 1931 by a man who was not present and was eighty years old. It is the hinge of my last section. Fine. Meanwhile I have dropped the coroner's inquest, documented down to the cost of the witnesses, because its findings belong properly to the next valley and would have confused the reader. Also fine, I think — though my evidential priorities have evidently been inverted without my authorising it.

8. I have shown that 1931 recorder falsifying his own notebooks out of love for the place: rewriting a figure, dating a funeral to a day it did not happen, so that the valley should have a tidier past. Fine; it is the chapter I am proudest of. In my first novel I did the same to my grandmother's letters and cut it as impermissible. Beyond kinship I have no account of why a town's dead may be revised and my own may not, and kinship strikes me as a poor reason.

9. A young woman in 1962 reads the report of 1874 aloud at the water's edge and then lets it go. Fine in draft; now I suspect it is a posture, the author clearing her throat. I cannot yet tell whether the suspicion is honest or merely the fatigue of a seventh draft.

My working principle has two clauses. The first I call recognition: a distortion is legitimate if someone who lived through the event could still have recognised it as theirs. The second I call the open future: a distortion is illegitimate if it forecloses a later reading of the event — if it settles what the valley was for, so that nobody after us can want anything else from it. Both clauses appear to bite backwards on case 5 and case 4. The chairman could hardly have recognised his own speech, and I keep it. The owner would very likely have recognised his ledger, since such men do keep such books, and I condemn it. So the principle either fails or wants a third clause.

What I am asking for is this. State the principle — or the two or three candidates you think are live — precisely, clause by clause, with the case from my list that motivates each clause. Tell me whether the three modes are doing any explanatory work: assign each of my verdicts to a mode, and say whether one mode is carrying the lot, in which case I want to know whether the triad is an instrument or an ornament I should stop reaching for. Repair or replace my two clauses. Then give me your principle's verdict on the cases I have not decided: whether to name the eleven children recovered from the water, whose names are all in the register; whether to reproduce three pages of the copybook in facsimile, as my publisher wishes, which I suspect is antiquarian bric-à-brac; whether to accept the family's offer of a bequest conditional on the repentance scene remaining; and whether the book may be titled with the date of the failure, which is the date the valley uses for everything, including the birthdays it has stopped celebrating. Add two further cases of your own devising where you think the principle is most awkward, and rule on them as well.

Finally, be honest about the cost. Tell me which of my nine verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which I must therefore give up; and if you can produce one that saves all nine, tell me what its complexity costs and whether I ought to prefer the simpler principle that overturns case 5 or case 8. And settle the last thing, which is not in the list: whether to include an epilogue in the present, in which a diver comes up from the drowned churchyard and cannot say what he saw, or to end in 1874 and let the reader out into forgetting. Tell me which the principle requires, not which you would enjoy reading.
