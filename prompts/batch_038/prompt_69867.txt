My brother was diagnosed last year with a hereditary adult-onset condition for which there is no treatment, and I am now deciding whether to have predictive testing myself. My genetic counsellor has been careful to insist the decision is entirely mine, but I want to know whether my reluctance survives scrutiny, so I turned to decision theory: Good's theorem on the value of information appears to establish that for an agent with coherent degrees of belief and stable utilities, free information can never have negative expected value. Two features of my case seem to block the obvious escapes — the test costs me nothing, and its result would bear directly on an act I have not yet performed, since my partner and I are undecided about conceiving and would consider embryo screening if I knew my status. That leaves me with the conclusion that declining the test is a preference I have simply failed to recognise as inconsistent with my own probabilities and utilities, and my question is therefore how to argue myself out of it. Before offering that argument, please assess whether the inference is legitimate at all: what does the non-negativity result actually assume, how strong is its conclusion, and which of those assumptions is doing the work in my case? I would also like to know whether it says anything about timing, since nothing I have read seems to distinguish testing now from testing in five years. If some assumption fails or the conclusion is weaker than I have taken it to be, tell me precisely where the reasoning breaks and what remains true about the rationality of choosing not to know; I would rather not settle a decision of this weight on a theorem that does not reach it.
