Our reading group keeps having the same argument every few weeks and I've finally realised it's three different arguments wearing a trench coat. It's a mixed room — two philosophers, some ML people who came at this via the AI-risk coherence argument literature, an economist who thinks the whole thing is trivially settled, and me. Whenever the topic comes up, half the room says they're 'sceptical about von Neumann-Morgenstern' and they turn out to mean wildly incoherent things by that phrase (sorry), so I want to sort people before we talk.

Write me a decision tree, maybe six to eight branch questions and eight or nine leaves, that takes someone from a starting point everybody in the room already accepts — something like: an agent whose choices across a sequential problem violate transitivity can be made to hand over a predictable amount of money for nothing — to a named position on what, if anything, coherence arguments establish about rational agents. Every branch node has to be a question someone can answer honestly without knowing which leaf it leads to, and phrased as a case or a claim rather than a label; no node should read 'do you accept the strong programme?'.

The leaves I want the tree to be able to reach:

- the constitutive view, where being coherent is part of what makes something an agent at all, so an incoherent thing isn't a badly reasoning agent but not an agent;
- the underdetermination sceptic, who points out that plenty of formally distinct numerical structures can represent one and the same preference ordering, so the representation theorems don't pin down anything as substantive as expected utility;
- the diachronic/money-pump pragmatist, who thinks the whole force of the argument is in repeated choice and vanishes if you deny that;
- the selection view, where incoherent agents simply get drained and what's left looks coherent — a claim about populations rather than about norms;
- the bounded/fast-and-frugal position, where the coherence norms presuppose capacities (a complete ordering over a rich enough gamble space, say) that no actual agent has;
- the 'preferences aren't the fundamental thing' position, which says the theorems smuggle in a preference ordering and that assumption does all the real work;
- the position that coherence is fine but expected utility specifically isn't the unique coherent option — rank-dependent and risk-weighted views live here;
- and the eliminativist who thinks the entire exercise is a philosopher's toy with no grip on real agents.

Two extra requirements. First, the axis my room slides past constantly, so please build it into the tree rather than leaving it as an aside: whether the conclusion of a coherence argument is supposed to be about what the agent is disposed to choose, about what the agent is already committed to by its own attitudes, or about what a describer is licensed to attribute to the agent. Those are three different claims and people swap between them mid-sentence. Second, flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely can't separate — I suspect the selection view and the constitutive view are indistinguishable from the inside, and if that's wrong tell me so — and mark which cells of the logical space come out empty and why. Is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because no one's bothered? I'd particularly like to know whether you can hold that preferences aren't fundamental and still think coherence is constitutive of agency, since that looks like a live option to me.

Then a short note at the end separating what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's contested — I don't want the tree quietly installing one side of a live dispute as the textbook default, so if a branch point is itself disputed, say so at the branch rather than only in the note. British English throughout, please.
