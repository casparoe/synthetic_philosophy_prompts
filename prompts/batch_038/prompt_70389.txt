I sat in on a graduate seminar whose reading was Maimonides on the sacrificial cult. Two students got into an argument I could not resolve, and the instructor moved on. Here is the exchange as best I can reproduce it.

(1) **Aaron:** In Part III of the *Guide*, Maimonides explains that God did not abolish sacrifice because the generation of the Exodus, raised among idolatrous nations, could not have accepted a Torah that gave them no sacrificial worship at all — he compares it to the education of a king's son, who must be fed meat before bread, and he treats the forty years in the wilderness as a kind of gracious ruse to produce a people capable of bearing the law. So the Temple cult is a concession to a historical condition, not an ideal. Since that condition is long gone, the concession must lapse; the detailed sacrificial halakhah in the *Mishneh Torah* is codification of the law as it stands, not a prediction about the future.

(2) **Devorah:** That is contradicted by his own legal works. He counts the sacrificial commandments among the 613, devotes an entire book of the *Mishneh Torah* to the Temple service, and in the laws of kings says the messianic king will rebuild the sanctuary and restore the commandments to their full practice. And a rationale that has lapsed does not repeal a commandment: for Maimonides the reasons for the commandments disclose divine wisdom, they are not conditions of validity.

(3) **Aaron:** Then the reasons do no normative work at all, which makes Part III pointless. Besides, he clearly ranks prayer — and above it the knowledge and love of God — as the higher worship. If sacrifice is only instrumentally useful and the instrument has been superseded, keeping it forever is keeping the scaffold after the building is finished.

(4) **Devorah:** You are running two questions together: what is the best form of worship, and what God commanded. His ninth principle is that this Torah will never be abrogated or exchanged. He can hold that sacrifices are a second-best institution and still hold them permanently obligatory — and he assigns them a function that has nothing to do with ancient idolatry: near the end of the laws of substitution he tells a person bringing an offering to regard the animal as standing in his own place, so that the rite produces self-recognition and repentance. That function does not expire with the Bronze Age.

(5) **Aaron:** There is an aggadic tradition that in the future all offerings cease except the thanksgiving offering. Maimonides knew that tradition, so your full-restoration reading is not the only available one. And a rite whose remaining job is to make you feel bad about yourself is precisely what a temporary concession looks like.
