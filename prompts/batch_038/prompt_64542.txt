I've been trying to pin down what I actually believe about whether meaning is in the head or not, and I've got a list of gut verdicts that won't line up. Individually most of them feel obvious. The problem is that two of them look like the same case with opposite answers, and I don't know which one to throw out.

Here's the list.

- Straight-up Twin Earth: Oscar and twin-Oscar both say water, they mean different things by it. Agree, no struggle.
- Generation-ship colonists: kids born on a ship that never carried a drop of free water, taught the word gold entirely out of old Earth archives, never seen or touched any. Later they land somewhere full of a shiny yellow metal that's iron pyrites and they call it gold. I think their gold still means gold, the real stuff, so they're simply wrong rather than talking about something else.
- Lifelong brain in a vat, hooked up from birth, no contact with anything outside: I think its water does NOT mean water. It means something like the watery stuff in the simulation. But notice this and the colonists are supposed to come out different, and the best I can do is "one of them inherited the word from people who actually touched the thing," which feels thin.
- Burge's arthritis guy who thinks arthritis can happen in the thigh: he still means arthritis. Agree.
- My own version, though: a friend who thinks 1 is prime and has always used prime that way, correcting his kids when they get it wrong. I do NOT think his prime means prime. I think he's got his own slightly different concept, and when he says there are infinitely many primes he's saying something near-miss, not what I'd say. Same structure as the arthritis case, opposite verdict from me.
- Oscar moves to Twin Earth, lives there twenty years, says water is wet. I think by then he means twater, but I also think he still knows without any investigation that water is wet, and I know those two are hard to hold together. I'd like to keep both if it's possible.

So: is there one principle about what fixes the content of a term, or a small set of rival candidates each with the case that motivates it, that gets these verdicts, or as many of them as it can? I want it stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself, and I want you to be upfront about which of my verdicts it has to break. If saving all of them costs real complexity, tell me what the simpler principle is and which verdict I should give up, rather than building me a Rube Goldberg machine with a clause per case.

Then tell me what your principle predicts for a case I haven't decided: the word gene as a geneticist in the 1910s used it, versus what a molecular biologist means today. Same content, or not?

Two housekeeping things. Say plainly which parts of this territory are settled and which are live fights among people who work on it — I don't want a contested position handed to me as the consensus. And keep the whole answer under 600 words, opening with your principle in two sentences before you get into the cases.
