Having followed the AI-risk conversation for something like fifteen years, from the old LessWrong sequences onward, I keep meeting a claim that is asserted far more often than it is exhibited: that a genuinely aligned system can still be an existential-risk vector, because the danger supposedly sits in capability and deployment dynamics rather than in the system's goals. I want that claim turned into a case rather than repeated.

Construct a scenario in which an AI system that is aligned in the strict technical sense — no goals we would disapprove of, honest, corrigible, faithfully responsive to what its operators ask, with no deception, no mesa-optimization, and no instrumental-convergence episode anywhere in the causal chain — contributes to an existential catastrophe: human extinction, or the permanent and drastic curtailment of humanity's long-term potential. Constraints: near-future and technically mundane; every individual step must be one its actor could defend on what they reasonably believe at the time; no one intends harm; and the alignment properties must hold all the way through rather than being quietly surrendered at the crucial moment. Scenario under five hundred words.

Afterward, show that the design works. Indicate where the alignment is load-bearing rather than incidental to the outcome. State which of the usual categories the case belongs to — misuse, misalignment, or multipolar and structural failure — and whether it resists that taxonomy, since the taxonomy is part of what I mean to argue about. Name the single feature doing the work, and describe what remains of the scenario if you delete it. Close with the strongest response available to someone who thinks all this establishes is that AI is a hazardous technology of an ordinary kind, not a distinctive existential risk. Stipulating that things go badly, or relabeling a misaligned component as aligned, voids the exercise: the question is whether the argument survives once alignment is granted.
