Next month I am presenting a paper at a one-day workshop in the history of economic thought, hosted by a history department, and I want to spend my remaining preparation time on the discussion period rather than on polishing the text.

The room will be about a dozen people, and I know roughly who is coming: two economic historians of eighteenth-century British public finance, who work in the idiom of the fiscal-military state and care about debt service, customs receipts, and the actual cost of defending the colonies; a historian of the East India Company and of Bengal in the years around the famine of 1770, who regards much of what Smith wrote about the Company as secondhand pamphleteering; a textual scholar who works from the Glasgow edition and the student notes of the lectures on jurisprudence and who is alert to what changed between editions; two or three political theorists working on property and dispossession, one of whom has written on the four-stages theory and is impatient with readings that convict Smith of a prejudice by his silence about it rather than by his arguments; and the remainder graduate students, mostly not specialists.

My thesis, in outline:

1. Smith's case against retaining the American colonies is not, in the end, a moral case, and it is not primarily a case about trade efficiency. It is a case about the incidence of a liability. The empire was an asset in the imagination of the British public and on the map, and a liability on the public account; the monopoly of the colony trade persisted because it concentrated gains in a small body of merchants and dispersed costs across taxpayers and consumers, and because statesmen had an interest in the grandeur of the project.

2. What the monopoly costs Britain, on Smith's own accounting, is not the colony trade as such — he thinks that trade genuinely profitable — but the diversion of capital it causes, the elevated rate of profit it sustains at home, and above all the debt incurred in defending a dominion that returned no revenue. The anti-colonial argument is thus a public-finance argument before it is a commercial one.

3. The proposal that the colonies be admitted to representation in proportion to their contribution to the public revenue is not a utopian appendix bolted onto the end of the chapter. It completes the argument: if the burden is to be shared, the sovereignty must be shared. What Smith offers is a fiscal federalism in which representation follows contribution, and his pessimism about whether any British government would ever adopt it is part of the point rather than an afterthought.

4. The controversial move, and the one I expect to be attacked: the gap in the argument is systematic rather than a failure of nerve. Smith nowhere grounds his case in the injustice of dispossessing the peoples of North America, and I argue he could not have done so consistently, because his account of property makes occupation and improvement the criterion of legitimate acquisition. On that criterion the settlement of the continent can appear as a legitimate passage from hunting to cultivation, leaving justice with nothing to say, whereas the Company's conduct in Asia is a clear case of injustice precisely because it is plunder by force against settled, cultivated societies.

5. Corollary: this asymmetry is doing the work that readers usually assign to Smith's moralism. The same framework licenses the American settlement and condemns the Bengal regime, so the right description of Smith on empire is not half-hearted anti-imperialism but a coherent criterion that we now find unappealing.
