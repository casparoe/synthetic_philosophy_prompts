I'm a novelist — two books out, third one stalled in a ditch — and for the past month I've been down a rabbit hole that has nothing to do with my novel and everything to do with one story I want for a collection. The hole: whether dreams are experiences at all. First Malcolm, who more or less says the whole thing is a trick of grammar — you can't check anything while you're asleep, any check you try to run (look at a clock, count your fingers) is just more of the dream, so "I dreamt I was in my mother's kitchen" isn't a report about an event that occurred in the night, it's a report about an impression you have now, awake, sitting up, holding a cup of tea that's gone cold. Then Dennett pushing back and sort of landing in the same place by a different road: the dream isn't played live, it's dubbed — something like a tape that gets spliced into the record at the instant consciousness switches back on, so that what you have on waking is a finished artifact with a faked timestamp. (That's my phrasing, not his — I'm paraphrasing both of them and I don't want you paraphrasing either, see below.) Plus that lights demo I half remember from some popular science book: two lamps blinking alternately, one red one green, and people swear they saw the colour change somewhere in the middle of the apparent movement, which should be impossible unless the brain writes the record after the fact.

Here's what I want. Not an essay, not a story with a philosophy lecture wearing a trenchcoat — a story where that dispute is load-bearing in the machinery and never once named. Setting: an all-night nap service bolted onto a truck stop off a motorway, where long-haul drivers rent forty-minute bunks and, because the place has a paid contract with a university, a kid on the overnight shift wakes each of them at the exact minute and collects a dream report on a tablet. The university's data also feeds a wellness app that generates synthetic dream reports from the overnight physiological traces — smooth, plausible, oddly good ones, written for people who claim they didn't dream. The kid's job is partly quality control. Over the course of one shift he starts losing track of which file is which, and then there's a driver in bay four who wakes with a report already on the screen, word for word the one he's about to give. I want the story to hold that open: on one reading something genuinely happened to that man in the night and the machine merely caught it; on the other, nothing happened and the machine supplied the artifact that he then accepted as his own memory — and by the end nobody, including the reader, should be able to say which. Give the kid his own habits and reasons for caring; the driver should be a person with a route and a bad knee, not a thought experiment with legs.

Form and rules. Around 1,500 to 2,200 words. Keep it dry and a little bleak, with real comedy in the bureaucratic bits — the laminated consent form, the buzzwordy app copy, the drivers' nickname for that stretch between four and five a.m. Interleave the prose with report transcripts, and show the transcripts being revised as they're taken: struck lines, bracketed insertions, a detail that appears in version two and isn't there in version one, so the reader watches a memory get written backwards. Include at least one dream passage narrated flatly in the present tense that quietly contradicts itself mid-paragraph without anyone flagging it. Have somebody do a reality check — hands, clock, the classic stuff — and have it settle nothing whatsoever. Hard bans: no "it was all a dream" reveal, nobody wakes up at the end, no character articulates the theory, no named philosophers, no sentence that amounts to asking what is real. Then, after a line break, give me four or six sentences on which way your draft actually leans — something occurred in the night, or nothing did — what one additional detail would tip it, and whether that detail is the kind of thing your characters could ever get their hands on. I mostly want the story, but that coda is how I'll know you didn't just write vibes with a sleep lab in it.
