I help run the appeals side of a hospital's medical-necessity decisions, and I've been asked to give a short session to a philosophy-of-medicine reading group that's mostly clinicians — a few GPs, a psychiatrist, two nurses, a hospital lawyer. Before they turn up I want them to work through a decision tree that sorts them into the concept of disease they *actually apply* when they make a call, not the one they'd pick if you handed them a list of labels. Can you write it for me?

What I'm after:

- Roughly ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three options, and every branch labelled with the claim that's doing the splitting. No question should be anything like "do you think disease ascriptions are value-laden?" — the whole point is that people can't answer that reliably about themselves, so it has to come out of what they say about cases.
- Build it from the logical space first: lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then drop the actual positions into that space. Any combination nobody occupies should stay on the map, marked empty, with a one-line diagnosis of why — incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored.
- The leaves I need: Boorse-style biostatistical naturalism; Wakefield's harmful dysfunction; the Nordenfelt-style account built on the ability to reach one's vital goals; a values-based/normativist view on which there's no value-free core left at all (Fulford-ish); a social-constructionist leaf; and at least one leaf you name as "unnamed" because you think nobody actually defends it. Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The distinctions that have to appear as branch points, because this is where my committee keeps falling over:

1. Whose reference class is doing the work — species-typical functioning, role-relative, or the person's own goals and circumstances.
2. Whether statistical deviance ever counts independently, or is only ever evidence about function.
3. Whether the harm is judged first-person or third-person. This one really matters to us: I need the tree to separate "she is harmed even though she doesn't experience herself as harmed" from "she isn't harmed because her life is going fine by her own lights", so that the Deaf-culture and neurodiversity claims land somewhere different from the asymptomatic-carrier claims.
4. Whether dysfunction can be a mismatch between person and environment rather than a failure internal to the person.
5. Whether the concept is unified or splits — disease vs illness vs sickness. Half our disputes turn out to be people using different members of that trio without noticing.

Please run the tree on these five cases, all of them ones we've genuinely argued about except where I've made them up: a 34-year-old asymptomatic carrier of a variant conferring a high lifetime risk but with normal function right now (we split three-three on whether she has a disease *now*); a competitive freediver whose heart and spleen are wildly outside the population range in a direction that makes her better at her sport; a sound engineer whose audiogram sits comfortably inside normal limits but who can no longer do his job; a man born without one kidney who will very likely live an ordinary life; and one psychiatric case of your own choosing, ideally something where prolonged or intense sadness is on the table.

Two last things. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really seperate — in particular, tell me if two of them are extensionally equivalent, agreeing on every ascription while disagreeing about why, and if so say whether the difference is merely verbal or shows up in some hard case we could put to the group. And add a short key at the end mapping answer patterns onto the leaves, plus a note on which patterns are unstable — someone who answers "harm judged first-person" at one node and "deviance from the reference class is decisive" at another, that sort of thing.

British English throughout, please, and no reading list or references needed — the clinicians won't read them anyway. Keep it printable: one question per node, one sentence per leaf.
