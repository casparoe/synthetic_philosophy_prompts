I'm helping run a half-day internal session for our alignment group on scalable oversight, and rather than open with an abstract survey I want to hand the room a worked case: a dialogue between two colleagues who have been disagreeing for months about whether the setup in which two models argue opposing answers in front of a judge survives the possibility of obfuscated arguments. Write that dialogue as if both of them were at their best. The audience will be a mix of ML engineers and two people from compliance who are technically literate but have never seen a proof assistant, so the register should be plain without being dumbed down.

Please treat the following as fixed stipulations of the case rather than as things the characters may argue about. A deployed autonomous scheduling system is under review, and a panel must settle one binary question: does the system ever violate a stated resource invariant? The correct answer is no, and this is a fact about the program's behavior rather than a matter of judgment. Supporting the correct answer there exists a machine-checked certificate of the invariant, produced by a proof assistant and roughly six thousand steps long. No one on the panel can read the proof language; the only thing that can check the certificate is the proof-assistant toolchain itself, which is a separate body of software that would in turn need to be audited before anyone could lean on it. Meanwhile the opposing side has a hand-followable argument for the wrong answer: a two-hundred-step trace through the system's states, written in ordinary prose with a few small tables, entirely within the panel's capacity to check by hand. One step in the middle of that trace is simply incorrect, but demonstrating this requires reconstructing a subtlety about the interleaving of two concurrent updates that nobody noticed until weeks of work had already gone into it. The panel has forty hours of attention in total, can execute the system only on toy-scale inputs that are too small to reach the relevant interleaving at all, and has no way to replay the deployment at real scale. So the asymmetry runs backwards from the usual story: the true answer's justification is opaque to the judge, while the false answer's justification is transparent, cheap to follow, and compelling.

The two characters should be, say, Ilse, who defends the protocol, and Nkem, who thinks that obfuscated arguments drain it of whatever guarantees it was supposed to have. Neither may be a strawman, and I want at least two moments where each is genuinely on the back foot. Every turn has to engage what the previous turn actually said; no parallel monologues and no pivots to a pre-prepared speech after a token acknowledgment. Neither character may appeal to published results, to benchmark findings, or to what the literature has shown — they must argue from the mechanics of this case plus general facts about search, verification, and bounded reasoning that a sharp person could reconstruct on the spot.

Give Ilse the resources a real defender has: that oversight does not require the judge to come to know the answer, only to be reliably steered toward it; that the disputed question can be decomposed until the atomic sub-claims are things the panel can check in minutes; that auditing a checker is a smaller and largely one-time job compared with auditing a proof; that a certificate is still better than the alternative of trusting a fluent narrator; and that Nkem's preferred routes — interpretability, direct inspection, iterated delegation to weaker assistants — inherit the same obfuscation problem, so none of this is an objection to debate in particular. Give Nkem real teeth: that in a game played against a bounded judge, the winning strategy is a property of the judge's checking habits rather than of the truth; that obfuscation does not require length, and can live in the choice of which claim to decompose into, in the distance between the formalized invariant and the invariant anyone actually cares about, and in premises the panel is positioned to accept without being able to evaluate; that demanding ordinary-language explanations of randomly sampled steps of a certificate only buys anything if the certificate has the right kind of redundancy built into it, which a raw machine-checked proof need not have; and that forty hours is a budget that does not grow as the systems being overseen do.

Somewhere in the middle, one of them should propose a concrete modification to the procedure — sampling steps of the certificate for explanation, or forcing the dissenting side to commit in advance to a witness it will stand behind, or splitting the panel's time between the certificate and the trace — and the other should show precisely what that change repairs and what it leaves exposed. End without a winner, but not with a shrug: the closing turns should have them converge on a statement of exactly where the disagreement lives, what each takes to be the load-bearing assumption the other is quietly making, and one piece of evidence they would both accept as decisive if it could be had. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words.
