Last night I got stuck between two friends of mine, Wren and Dev, who went at it for close to three hours over the procreation asymmetry and then — because I'm apparently the neutral party — asked me to say who won. I dont have the stomach to referee it and I've been turning it over all morning, so I'm handing it to you. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. Wren opened with the asymmetry itself: everyone agrees there's a strong moral reason not to bring a child into the world if you know its life will be dominated by suffering, but almost nobody thinks there's a comparable reason in favor of bringing one into the world because its life would go wonderfully. Her evidence is that we feel no duty to populate the earth with happy people, and there's no one we've wronged by leaving them uncreated, whereas the suffering case has an obvious victim. Her explanation was that harm and benefit aren't symmetric when the alternative is never existing: the absence of pain is a good thing even when there's no one around to enjoy that good, but the absence of pleasure is only a bad thing if there's somebody specific who's deprived of it. So creating a miserable person is bad both for them and impersonally, while failing to create a happy person is bad for literally nobody. Dev's first move was that this explanation is the very thing it's supposed to explain, and that it collapses under its own symmetry: if Wren says no one is deprived by nonexistence, she equally can't say anyone is spared by it, since the suffering child who's never conceived is no more a subject than the happy child who's never conceived. Both cases compare a life to a non-life, and if that comparison is illegitimate in one direction it's illegitimate in both. He said the impersonal good in her story is a switch she flips on only when it's convenient. Wren answered that it isn't a switch but a difference in who ends up existing: if you create a suffering person there is afterwards a real subject with a real complaint, someone who can say you did this to me, whereas if you don't create a happy person there is no subject afterwards and never was, so no complaint is available. The asymmetry tracks complaints, she said, not quantities of value.

Dev replied that then it isn't an asymmetry about reasons at all, just the ordinary person-affecting idea, and much weaker than she wants. For one thing it can't explain why creating a miserable person is wrong rather than merely worse for them, because at the moment of deciding both options are equally hypothetical and restraint benefits nobody — on her own account, the choice not to create the sufferer also lacks any subject who gains anything. For another, if the real content is "someone with a life worth living has no complaint about being born," the view has to permit creating a person who'll have near-unbroken joy plus one bad week, and has nothing whatsoever to say about creating lots of people — so it isn't population ethics, it's a remark about grievances. Wren said fine, that's all it was ever meant to be, and that his one-bad-week case supports her rather than him: that person is glad to exist, so no wrong was done; the miserable person isn't, so one was. She also accused Dev of assuming a moral theory has to be able to rank whole worlds, which she called a strange demand to make on a view that's specifically about what we owe to the people we make. Dev's last shot was that the complaint view can't handle the case that got everyone interested in the asymmetry in the first place, namely people with severe conditions who insist their lives are worth living and are glad they exist: if complaints are what matter and they have none, Wren's view calls creating them unproblematic, which most defenders of the asymmetry would reject. And he said she never answered the switch objection, she just relocated it — complaints are person-affecting, but absence-of-pain-is-good-anyway is impersonal, and she's still leaning on both. Neither of them would budge and I came away with a headache.

For each one I want to know whether it actually lands, whether it's just rhetorical pressure dressed up as an argument, and what the other side should have said back. Then tell me whether Wren has any version of the asymmetry left at the end that isn't trivial, and what both of them are ignoring — I have a nagging feeling they're each assuming that if the asymmetry can't be derived from a single principle it's dead, and that neither of them ever pinned down what a "reason to create someone" would even mean given that the person in question doesn't exist yet and might never.
