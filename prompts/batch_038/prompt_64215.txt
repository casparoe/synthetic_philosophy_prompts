I write novels, and for two years I have been building one around a rescue at sea. To make the climax honest rather than merely moving, I have had to work out what I actually believe about whether the greater number should decide, and I have found that what I believe is a heap of plausible fragments that do not obviously fit together. I would rather give you my verdicts on particular cases and have you tell me what principle they add up to than argue for a thesis I do not have. Please treat the cases as data, not as illustrations.

My tentative view, for what it is worth: a loss is a loss only for the one who suffers it; there is no shared ledger on which five deaths are added into one larger death; what I owe each person is not to treat them as indifferent, which is why choosing by lot sometimes strikes me as the only respectful thing available and sometimes as a dereliction. Test that against the cases and repair or discard it as you see fit.

The cases, with my verdicts:

1. There is one dose of a drug. A woman I have corresponded with for a year needs all of it to survive; divided, it will save five strangers. I give it to her. I do not think this is a failure I owe anyone an apology for.
2. Same five, and the alternative is one stranger I have never met and know nothing about. I save the five, and I do not think it is close.
3. Same five, and the alternative is one stranger I spoke with for twenty minutes on a radio. Now I am torn, and when I picture choosing the five I feel I have committed something like a betrayal, though I cannot say against whom.
4. Five strangers, one of them a cardiac surgeon who would go on to save many lives, versus one stranger. I save the five, but I insist the surgeon counts for exactly as much as the others and that his future patients are irrelevant to my choice here.
5. Four people are flooding into a sealed compartment. I can free them only by throwing a lever that will crush a fifth man's hand in the gearbox. I think it is impermissible to throw it. If the hand in the gearbox is my own, I think it is not merely permissible but decent to leave it there. I can explain this only by saying that I may spend what is mine, which does not sound like a principle.
6. The one man's death would be mourned by three children; the five are each alone in the world. In argument I hold that the children make his death no worse and the five deaths no less bad. In practice I have never once managed to write that, and every scene I have drafted treats the death with dependents as the heavier thing.
7. If the one man volunteers — genuinely, without pressure — I think it becomes permissible to divide his dose among the five. I do not think the five volunteering to be the one who loses changes anything at all, and I cannot say why the asymmetry holds.
8. Five versus four: I save the five and it feels like barely a decision. Five versus one: I save the five and it feels like something I will have to live with. A million versus a million and four: I still think I must save the million and four, and I do not think a coin toss is acceptable, but I feel essentially nothing. Size of difference and strength of feeling have come apart, and I no longer know which of them my verdicts have been tracking.
9. One case described two ways. As a tally — five lives, one life — I choose the five without hesitation. As a scene, with the one named, characterized, given a history and a particular way of laughing, I choose the one. I am inclined to call the second response a distortion, an occupational artifact of my trade. But I also suspect that knowing someone is a fact about the world and not merely a fact about my nerves, in which case the distortion story is too quick.
10. One stranger versus one stranger, and the choice is genuinely balanced: I toss a coin and feel I have done the right thing. One versus five, and tossing a coin feels like an evasion, though by hypothesis I have just denied that the five clearly win.

The case I keep circling is the climax itself. My coastguard can lift one more person out of the water before the boat is too heavy. He can take the woman he has been talking to by radio for two hours, whose voice he has come to know, or four people in a hull he sighted ninety seconds ago. I have drafted it both ways and I dislike both drafts.

What I want back:

- Your first sentence must be the bottom line: name the principle that best fits this pattern, and state in that same breath which of my verdicts it cannot keep. No preamble and no summary of what I have just written.
- Then state the principle precisely enough that I could apply it to a case neither of us has thought of. Use clauses if it needs them, and for each clause say which numbered case it is doing work for. If a clause exists only to handle case 5, or only to rescue case 9, tell me so; I want to see exactly where the machinery is bolted on to save an intuition.
- Then take up the question of felt weight. Is the divergence in cases 8 and 9 evidence about the situations, or noise in the instrument? If it is noise, how am I entitled to know that, given that the noise is the only apparatus I have? Answer this directly, because it determines whether I can use my own reactions as a novelist at all.
- Then give me the principle's verdicts on four cases I have not raised: where the alternative to the five is one person's sight rather than one life; where the one is my dog; where I can save five now or one now and four in twenty years; and where the five are five separate people I could each rescue instead of the others, who never meet and never form a group.
- Then offer the leanest principle you can that still gets most of my verdicts right, and tell me which verdicts it throws overboard and why I might prefer that loss to the complexity of the first. I would rather be told that two of my intuitions are false than be handed an epicycle for each of them.
- Finally: what ought my coastguard to do, and is the fact that the right answer reads wrong on the page any reason at all to doubt it?

No citations, no names, no bibliography, and no survey of the existing positions; I have read almost none of it and I want the argument to stand on the cases. Explain any term of art in passing as you go. About twelve hundred words, and no more.
