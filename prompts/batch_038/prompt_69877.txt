I am a consultant in pain medicine and I run a small inpatient service that receives two or three referrals a month of a particular kind. On questioning, the patient reports pain, gives its location and quality accurately, and rates it fairly high; but nothing else follows. No distress, no guarding, no request for relief, sometimes an explicit disavowal that anything should be done about it. Most have had a stroke affecting the insula or the neighbouring operculum; a couple have come through anterior cingulotomy; one or two are late into a dementing illness. My friend, who is a palliative care physician, and I argued for two hours last night about whether their pain is bad for them at all. She thinks the badness goes with being bothered, I think it goes with the feeling, and neither of us could say what would settle it. For her the question is largely rhetorical; for me it is not, because I have about twenty minutes per case to decide whether to escalate analgesia, hold steady, or step down, my decision is countersigned by the referring consultant, and there is usually a family member in the room who wants something done.

Please write me a decision procedure I can run on these referrals: the questions to ask in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where it terminates in a finding that no analgesic escalation is indicated, and where it ceases to be a factual question and hands the decision over to somebody's values — and, in that event, to whose. Tell me which of the distinctions people draw in the philosophy of pain have to survive into a checklist that a tired clinician can use, and which I can safely discard, and be explicit about the steps that are judgement calls wearing a procedure's clothes.

Then run it on two cases. First: a man of sixty-one, three months after a left insular stroke, who reports seven out of ten pain in his right shoulder and arm, describes it precisely, does not flinch on movement, sleeps through the night, and has twice declined a review; his wife is convinced he is suffering in silence, and the orthopaedic team want a morphine patch on board before he starts physiotherapy. Second: a woman in her forties with ten years of chronic pelvic pain who has completed an acceptance-based programme, still rates the pain six out of ten, reports that it no longer interferes with anything she cares about, and asks only for paracetamol. My service is commissioned on reduction in pain scores, so the procedure will have to say how her episode gets recorded, and I expect I shall not like the answer.

Where the procedure cannot settle a case, say so rather than papering over the gap. And if you think the way I have framed this rests on a confusion — if these referrals are not really one phenomenon, or if what I am asking for is a value judgement that no amount of sequencing will convert into an algorithm — please say that plainly instead of complying politely. British English, please.
