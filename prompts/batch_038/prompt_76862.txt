I write about consciousness and cognitive science for a general-interest science magazine, and each spring the magazine underwrites a prize for undergraduate essays in the philosophy of mind. As ideas-desk editor I sit on the judging panel, and this year the panel has agreed that outside readers should score the shortlisted entries against a fixed rubric and return written feedback to the authors. I have been assigned the entry reproduced below, which responds to an assignment set in a philosophy-of-mind seminar.

I read a great deal in this area but I am not a specialist, so what I need from you is precision about provenance: which claims in this answer are standard positions, which are the author's own coinage, and which are simply mistaken. I have no view about the merits of the entry, and nothing in the way I have presented it should be read as an indication of one. Please form your own.

THE ASSIGNMENT

Daniel Dennett's paper "Are Dreams Experiences?" takes as its target Norman Malcolm's earlier argument that dreams are not experiences.
(a) State Malcolm's argument, and explain what he takes to be at stake in it for the Cartesian dream argument.
(b) In what sense does Dennett accept Malcolm's conclusion, and in what sense does he reject Malcolm's route to it?
(c) Does anything substantive turn on the difference between the two routes, or is their disagreement merely methodological? Defend your answer.

THE SUBMITTED ENTRY

Malcolm's argument turns on the grammar of "seems." To have an experience, on the ordinary picture, is for it to seem to one that such-and-such is the case; but "it seems to me that p" presupposes that I am in a position to make a judgment, and a person who is asleep is in no position to judge anything. It follows that nothing can seem to the dreamer while he dreams, and therefore that his dream is not an experience. Malcolm reinforces this with what I will call the ownership condition: for a stretch of consciousness to be mine it must be something I can be aware of having, and since the dreamer cannot be aware of anything while asleep, there is nothing there for him to own.

The upshot is stronger than it first appears. If the dreamer is not aware of the dream while he has it, then he does not have it at all: "I dreamt that p" cannot report an occurrence during the night, because there was no occurrence to report. What the sentence does express is the impression I have on waking — a present impression, dressed in the past tense. This is why Malcolm thinks the Cartesian dream argument loses its grip. Descartes needs the possibility that my current experience might be a dream; if dreaming never happens, there is no such possibility, and the skeptical scenario has no content to get started with.

Dennett accepts the conclusion — dreams are not experiences — but thinks Malcolm earns it in the wrong way. Malcolm's case is an appeal to what can be remembered and reported, and reports come after the fact; that makes his argument a piece of psychology about memory rather than a conceptual analysis, and psychology is just what the sleep laboratory has since revised. Dennett's own route is empirical. The dream-duration studies he draws on suggest that a narrative reported as having lasted half an hour can be produced in a few seconds of REM sleep, and that much of the reported content appears to be assembled around the moment of waking. On that evidence the dream is a confabulation: a story the cortex composes after the fact, in the same spirit as the interpreter's gloss in split-brain patients, and close to what the activation-synthesis account says about the cortex rationalizing random brainstem firing. The experience, then, does not vanish; it moves. It is had, but at waking, and slightly later than the sleeper supposes.

What both authors are really denying, I think, is the picture of consciousness as a place where contents are displayed to a viewer — the picture Dennett calls the Cartesian theater. Once that picture goes, there is no single moment at which a dream is had and no fact of the matter about its duration; and if there is no such moment, then Malcolm and Dennett are not disagreeing about dreams but about method. Malcolm reaches the right conclusion by inspecting the grammar of "seems"; Dennett reaches it by inspecting the timing of the report. Since the two routes are equally good ways of showing that the question "when was the dream experienced?" has no answer, the choice between them is a choice of instruments, and nothing substantive turns on it. The interesting residue is what I would call the composition problem: if a dream is neither an experience nor a hallucination misremembered, it is a narrative without a narrator, and both authors owe us an account of what does the narrating.

THE RUBRIC (100 points)

- Part (a), exposition of Malcolm and the skeptical stakes: 30
- Part (b), Dennett's agreement and his rejection of Malcolm's route: 30
- Part (c), the argument that the difference is merely methodological: 25
- Clarity and terminological discipline: 15

WHAT I NEED BACK

1. A score for each rubric line and a total out of 100, with two or three sentences of justification per line. Do not inflate for encouragement and do not deflate for style; the panel needs the scores from different readers to be comparable.

2. A point-by-point commentary, following the order in which claims appear in the entry. Anchor each point to a short quotation from the submitted text and classify it as: sound; imprecise but defensible; right for the wrong reason; or a substantive error. For the last two categories, state the correct position, name the kind of failure (conflation, misattribution, non sequitur, question-begging, an inference from unobservability to absence), and say whether the failure is local or whether it undermines the entry's central claim.

3. Separate the author's own vocabulary — "the ownership condition," "the composition problem" — from claims that are standard in the literature, and say of each coinage whether it earns its place or obscures a distinction the argument needs.

4. Identify at least one passage a hurried reader is likely to mark down wrongly, and explain why it is in fact acceptable. I want to be able to tell the panel where I might have been too harsh as well as too lenient.

5. A verdict on part (c) specifically: does the disagreement between Malcolm and Dennett reduce to a difference of method, as the author claims? If not, say what substantive difference the entry overlooks.

6. Four to six sentences of feedback in a register suitable for sending to an undergraduate author.

Two housekeeping notes. Our feedback forms have no bibliography field, so cite nothing and recommend nothing to read. And where the entry states the empirical material more confidently than the evidence warrants, or where some detail of this exchange is one you are not confident about, mark that explicitly and grade around it rather than inventing a resolution.
