I sit on the collections committee of the Kestrel County Historical Society, which is a much grander name than what it actually describes: we're 1.5 FTE (one archivist, plus a front-desk person who is also our building manager), we run a two-room museum with a cannery diorama, our endowment is about $400k, and we've got roughly 900 linear feet of unprocessed backlog that we've been chipping away at with a minimal-processing approach since before I joined. I got on the board partly because my grandmother worked two seasons on the Talbot Bay line in the 50s and I wanted to see if her name showed up anywhere, which I've disclosed as a conflict and I'll recuse from the vote, but I'm still the one who has to circulate a memo before our meeting on the 19th so nobody shows up arguing cold.

Here's the case. The Talbot Bay Packing Company closed in 1989, the plant is coming down next spring, and the demolition contractor has told the family trust the warehouse contents get pulped if they aren't moved out before then. The trust is offering us about 200 linear feet: payroll ledgers and time cards, production logs, the owners' correspondence, plant photographs, and roughly 6,000 individual personnel files. Also in there, stored in the plant office, are the records of the Ladies' Auxiliary — a workers' benevolent society made up mostly of immigrant women and, in some seasons, Indigenous women hired for the line — and those ledgers are, as far as anyone can tell, the only place those names exist at all. The Auxiliary survives as a successor organization and has told us in writing that it considers the ledgers its own and will gift them to us separately and unrestricted if the trust doesn't claim them; the trust's counsel (a real estate lawyer, not an archivist) says anything sitting in the plant office is company property. The gift comes with four conditions: (1) personnel files and anything with medical or immigration content sealed for 75 years from accession, and counsel reads that broadly enough to sweep in the 1930s grievance books beacuse they contain names; (2) a 400-word family history statement the trust supplies, to be reproduced alongside the collection and linked from our finding aid; (3) a perpetual 90-day right for counsel to review any item before we digitize it or put it online; (4) the trust will fund processing, but only to collection level. A regional university library has separately offered to take just the photographs. Our options, roughly: accept as offered; counter on a subset or on terms; decline and redirect the money toward an oral-history and name-index project with former workers and their descendants, which would create new sources but leave the paper in the warehouse to be pulped; or some combination. Our archivist's case for declining is that we're producing a silence either way, and that under the seal plus collection-level description plus the trust's statement sitting in our own finding aid, what we'd be preserving is a collection where nobody can find the Auxiliary ledgers for 75 years and the donor effectively co-authors the description — so accepting is worse than declining and making a public fight of it. Half the committee thinks the opposite: paper in our climate-controlled room beats paper in a dumpster, and a restricted record is still a record.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate? I want a concrete recommendation with a time frame (there's a pulp deadline and a demolition permit in the background), the specific conditions we should push to trade and what we trade them for — is 75 years negotiable down to 25 with per-file review, can the right-of-review be narrowed to the finding aid instead of items, does the family statement get demoted to a clearly-labeled separate page rather than living inside our description — plus a decision on the Auxiliary ledgers, and criteria the rest of the committee can apply to the next poisoned gift, because if we accept a perpetual review right every donor in the county is going to ask for one too. The thing I most want actually engaged rather than handwaved is whether a sealed-but-preserved record is better or worse than a destroyed-but-argued-about one. I've read enough Trouillot to know he treats silences as produced at several distinct stages rather than as mere gaps — something going wrong when a record is created in the first place, when records get assembled into an archive, when they get pulled into a narrative, and later when we decide which of it counts as significant — and I think that framing cuts differently for each of our options, including the decline-and-do-oral-histories one. So please say plainly which stage each option operates at, and give the strongest case against your own recommendation a fair shake: both the privacy argument for the seal (there are real third parties in those personnel files, and a 1934 grievance book naming an informant could still land on a great-grandchild's kitchen table) and the don't-let-donors-buy-the-narrative argument. No citations or reading list needed, I couldn't check them anyway, and please skip the archives-are-never-neutral lecture — I want something the building manager and the two trustees who mostly care about the diorama can vote on.
