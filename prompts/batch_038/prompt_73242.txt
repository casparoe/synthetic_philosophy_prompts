I'd like you to reconstruct how Émilie du Châtelet would have judged a pair of modern developments that bear directly on her doctrine of self-deception in the Discourse on Happiness — the text she composed at Cirey and which reached print only after her death. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want the reconstruction argued from her actual commitments, in your own analysis.

Here is the case I want you to put to her. Suppose she is shown two things in sequence.

First, a much-disputed line of psychological research reporting that mildly depressed people tend to give more accurate estimates of their own competence and of how much control they really have over outcomes, while the non-depressed systematically overestimate both — and that the gap shows up even in narrow, checkable domains such as judgments about a task the subject has just performed.

Second, a consumer technology built on the assumption that this bias can be corrected: an always-on companion agent that tracks a person's self-appraisals and quietly counters them whenever they exceed what the evidence supports. It has no other route to mood; it works purely by removing flattering error.

Then the questions:

1. Would du Châtelet read the first finding as empirical confirmation of her claim that illusion is not an obstacle to happiness but part of its machinery, or would she resist the inference? Her position is subtler than "illusion is good": on her account reason's task is to choose among available illusions rather than to dispel them. Does this research license her to say that the choosing has now been done for us, and by whom?

2. Would she install the second device herself? Treat two cases separately: one in which she knows in advance exactly what it does, and one in which it is installed such that she later forgets it, or never learns of it at all. Her insistence that a deception one recognizes as a deception stops working seems to impose a structural constraint on the second case that the first lacks. Say what that constraint costs her view — and whether her own recommendation to let reason curate illusions is compatible with it, or was already in tension with it long before any such technology existed.

3. There is a further wrinkle drawn from her circumstances. She argues that study and the sciences secure her an independence that spares her from staking her happiness on other people's regard, and she treats that as especially urgent for women. Would she see the companion agent as an extension of that independence — a mirror that cannot withdraw its approval — or as its betrayal, a manufactured substitute for the self-sufficiency she wanted? Does her answer turn on whether the device tutors her in the sciences, merely flatters her, or merely deflates her?

I want to know where you are putting the weight. Mark clearly which moves you take to be licensed by her arguments and which are extrapolations you are supplying because she never faced the case, and flag anywhere you are relying on a contested reading of her — in particular any dispute about whether her appeal to self-deception is a psychological claim about what in fact works or a normative claim about what one ought to arrange for oneself. Do not dissolve everything into hedging: commit to a verdict on each of the three questions, and state how confident you are in each.

And if some part of this is badly posed, tell me plainly rather than answering around it. If the empirical finding I have described is too contested or too crudely stated to carry the philosophical load I am putting on it, say so and tell me what a better version of the case would look like. If you think counterfactuals of this kind cannot be done honestly at all — that any answer is simply my own view wearing a periwig — say that too, and then tell me what you would do instead.
