I'm TA-ing a 300-level seminar on aesthetics and politics and, because the professor is on leave, I've been handed the final to write. It's closed-book, no notes, and roughly half the seminar wrote their term papers on protest music, so they all think they know this material. Last year's question was "explain and evaluate Du Bois's claim that all art is propaganda," and two thirds of the scripts were the same three paragraphs of summary, obviously reconstructed from whatever study guide exists online. I want to close that route off entirely.

So: write one essay question on Du Bois and art as propaganda that is structured so reciting his view earns almost nothing. Here's the hook I'd like you to build on — rewrite the prose however you want, but keep the machinery. A municipal arts council adopts a grant rule requiring every funded project to file a one-page statement of purpose naming the political end the work is meant to serve, and the chair defends the rule by appealing to Du Bois's slogan. A critic (invent her, any name) then makes two moves: first, that if every artwork already is propaganda, the filing requirement adds no content and is pure bureaucracy; second, what she calls the transparency dilemma — a work that declares its end up front invites the audience to score it for compliance rather than to perceive it, while a work that hides its end is manipulative, so a Du Boisian funder can't have it both ways. The question should force students to say whether Du Bois is commited to the council's rule at all, i.e. whether "art is propaganda" is descriptive, normative, or doing both jobs at once, and what actually follows from each — and then to resolve or dissolve the transparency dilemma. The crux I'm aiming at is that the trivialization move only goes through if "propaganda" means the same thing in both of her premises (something like *rhetorically purposive* in one, *one-sided and covert about its methods* in the other), and that once you split the senses, Du Bois stops being a license for funders and starts being a claim about who gets to set the standards a work is judged against. They should also have to handle the fact that he was defending Black artists against a charge of didacticism while refusing art-for-art's-sake, and that those two commitments pull differently in this case.

Deliverables, in this order: (1) the question exactly as students would see it, 200–300 words including the two short passages (the chair's defense and the critic's dilemma), with the time and mark allocation stated up top — 55 minutes, 25 marks; (2) a model answer of about 700 words; (3) a marking scheme totaling 25 built around five named moves, with marks for each and a one-line note per move on the typical wrong way students execute it; (4) a three-or-four sentence note on the A/B boundary saying what separates a first-class answer from a competent one. No bibliography, no reading list, and if you want to gesture at Du Bois's own wording, paraphrase rather than quote — I don't want invented quotations floating around an exam paper, and nothing in the question should require students to recall page numbers or publication details either.
