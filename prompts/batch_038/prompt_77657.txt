Some background before the draft. I am a pianist. Fifteen months ago I crushed the tendons in my right hand, and I have spent the time since rebuilding both the hand and the repertoire, bar by bar. A festival has asked me for a short essay — 1,500 words, for the printed programme, to be read by people who care about music and have no training in philosophy — on the theme of freedom and constraint in performance. What I have actually written about is not music at all but the philosophy of free will, using the piano as the case. Below is the current draft, at about 900 words. I should be grateful for comments on it, of the sort specified after the draft.

---

**Working title: Sphexishness and the Density of Re-entry Points**

**1.** There is a story that has done a great deal of work in the philosophy of mind. A digger wasp paralyzes a cricket and hauls it to the mouth of her burrow. Before carrying it inside she goes in first, to inspect the nest. While she is in there, an experimenter shifts the cricket a few inches to one side. She emerges, finds her prey out of place, drags it back to the threshold — and now, because the prey is once more outside and she is once more at the beginning of that stage, she goes in to inspect again. Move the cricket again and she starts again; by some accounts the loop can be closed indefinitely. The tale reached philosophers by a curious route: it was taken up from a popular engineering book about the brain in the early 1960s, and Dennett turned it into a parable about the difference between doing something and merely being caused to do it. Hofstadter gave the vice a name, sphexishness, and it has since become the standard image of the unfree agent — not one who is coerced from outside, but one whose behaviour is locked onto a track it cannot leave.

The entomology is disputed. Fred Keijzer has argued that wasps do adapt after enough repetitions, and that re-inspecting a disturbed nest may be sound practice for an insect whose entire investment sits in one burrow. The parable may be doing rhetorical work rather than reporting a fact. I want to set that aside, because I think the parable survives its own zoology: something very like it happens to me every time I sit down to play.

**2.** A pianist who plays from memory does not remember a piece the way one remembers a list. Her memory is a chain of motor sequences, each one cued by its predecessor, and she has perhaps a dozen places — a bar, a phrase, a harmonic turn — from which she can start cold. If an interruption arrives anywhere else, the chain cannot be re-entered; the only recoveries are to jump back to the last good entry point or to stop. Since the injury my entry points have been fewer and further apart than they were, and I have become acutely aware that whether a performance survives an accident has almost nothing to do with whether I am in control at the moment it happens. It has to do with how many doors the routine has.

**3.** The familiar compatibilist reply to the wasp is that we are not like this because we have meta-levels: we can notice a routine, adopt a policy about it, and override it. Freedom, on that picture, is a function of the height of the hierarchy of control.

My worry is that height is the wrong measure, and that the meta-level story redescribes the problem instead of solving it. Consider the man who has taught himself to ask, at intervals, whether he is being rigid. The asking is itself a routine, triggered by cues, run to completion, and just as deaf to interruptions as anything below it. Adding a supervisor does not add doors; the supervisor has its own single entrance. What separates the pianist who recovers from the pianist who collapses is not that she possesses a controller but that her routine is perforated: there are many points at which it can be resumed, and many orders in which its material can be run without loss. So I want to say that flexibility, in the sense that matters for free will, is not the presence of a higher level but the density of re-entry points.

**4.** A second thought, which I hold less firmly. Not all the doors are inside the agent. My annotated score — the fingering pencilled at the head of a difficult passage, the small mark that means *you may begin here* — is a re-entry point stored on paper. A surgical safety checklist, the layout of a familiar kitchen, a colleague who refuses to let you start again from the top, are the same kind of thing. If the environment supplies resume points the head does not contain, then sphexishness is not a property of an agent but of an agent together with her surroundings, and the question *is she free?* is being asked of the wrong unit. Extended-mind arguments have been pressed for belief and for memory; I do not recall seeing the point made about flexibility specifically. The consequence is awkward. Freedom becomes something that can be installed or removed by a form designer, an interior decorator, or a teacher's habits of marking a score — which is exhilarating, and also licenses an indefinite amount of arranging other people's surroundings on their behalf, and leaves it unclear whom to praise when the recital goes well.

**5.** I want to end by saying that free will is not the absence of sphexishness. A wholly un-routinized agent could not act at all; technique is a stack of routines, and without mine I could not play a scale. Freedom must be something about the relation between an agent and her routines — whether the routine is *available to her for revision*, not merely interruptible. And here I stall, because "available for revision" looks as though it needs an account that does not itself appeal to re-entry points, and I suspect the whole essay is circular: I define flexibility in terms of doors, and then explain the doors by flexibility.

On the comments I am after:

1. **Accuracy.** Does my account of the wasp story, of how it entered the philosophical literature, and of the hierarchical compatibilist reply misrepresent anything? I am not asking for references or a reading list — I want you to tell me where I am simply wrong, where I am right but overstated, and where a hedge is needed. In particular, is it fair to describe the meta-level reply as measuring the *height* of a control hierarchy, or is that a straw man I have built for the pleasure of knocking over?

2. **The central proposal.** Is the density of re-entry points a genuine rival to the hierarchy-of-control view, or a relabelling of it — a hierarchy with many entry points at each level, described from the other end? If you think it is a relabelling, can the difference be made visible? Please give me a pair of cases that the two accounts score differently, or tell me that no such pair exists, which would be useful to know before the deadline.

3. **The circularity in section 5.** Is it real, and if so can it be broken without abandoning the proposal? A concrete suggestion would be worth more to me than a diagnosis.

4. **Section 4.** Does the distributed-flexibility move do any work that a compatibilist could not already accept — everyone concedes that circumstances matter — or does it genuinely change what free will would have to be? And is the paternalism objection the right thing to flag, or a distraction that will irritate a programme-reading audience?

5. **Interest and audience.** Which of my two examples should carry the piece: the wasp or the piano? Mark the exact place where an intelligent reader with no philosophy would come off, and tell me plainly whether anything here would be news to someone who reads popular philosophy regularly, or whether I have dressed up a commonplace.

6. **Cutting.** I must lose roughly a quarter. What goes?

7. **The strongest objection.** State the single most damaging objection an informed reader could make to the essay as it stands, and then one paragraph on how I might answer it — or on why I cannot.

Please write your comments in British English, and when you quote my draft back to me, keep my spelling as it stands.
