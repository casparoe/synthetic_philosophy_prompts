I chair the history department at a small college, and on Saturday I am giving a five-minute address at the rededication of a flood plaque on the river path. It was cast in 1953 and credits Elias Rook, a mill overseer, with hauling the sluice gate shut by hand during the freshet of 1846, saving the lower village. Ten years in the county archive has left me unable to stand behind it: the story first surfaces in a pamphlet written by Rook's son four decades later, and the two newspaper accounts from that week describe the gate as already carried away and never mention Rook at all. But those accounts were written by an editor who was then suing the mill company, and he had every reason to make the flood into a story about the company's neglect. The audience will include four of Rook's descendants, two of them trustees, the town historical society, a reporter, and a dozen of my own students.

Write the address, about 700 words. The disagreement between Ranke's ideal of showing how it actually was, Carr's insistence that the historian's facts reach us only already selected, and White's claim that the shape of a story is an imposition the evidence never licenses has to do the work: audible in what the speech says about Rook rather than announced as a survey, and at most one of the three names may appear. It may not use the sceptical side as an exit ("we shall never know, so the plaque stands"), since that hands the descendants the argument that the story is theirs to keep. It must also be speakable aloud to a reporter with no training in the subject. And it must end by saying what we are going to do about the bronze, and why.
