ok so a friend of mine runs a small media-criticism newsletter and she has been hammering on one image for months: a political euphemism is a fig leaf. There's a naked truth underneath, the leaf is draped over it, and the cure is exposure — pull the leaf off and everyone sees what was there all along. She invokes Orwell as her authority constantly. The practical version is a monthly "decoder" she publishes, translating org-speak into plain English ("capacity constraints" → we are not going to do this; "stakeholder alignment" → the boss already decided). And herths the thing thats bugging me: the decoder gets read by exactly the people who write the original documents, and within two or three issues the decoded phrasings started showing up as the new register. Nobody stopped writing euphemisms, the set in circulation just rotated. She says that's a failure of nerve, not a failure of the model.

I'm pretty sure the fig leaf image is doing more work then it can carry and I want it audited properly. You're a philosopher of language who has read Orwell closely rather than the t-shirt version — steelman her first, then break it. Think carefully, and before you settle on a structure, brainstorm a few candidate ways the analogy could fail and pick the ones that actually bite rather than listing everything.

What I want:

1. The correspondence table. What plays the leaf, the naked body underneath, the observer, the act of pulling, and the moment after the pulling?

2. Where the mapping breaks — and please separate the breaks that matter for her conclusion from the cosmetic ones. The one I care about most: a covering can hide something from an audience, and it can also hide something from the person wearing it, and Orwell seems to be after a third thing. He says he's concerned with language "as an instrument for expressing and not for concealing or preventing thought." Preventing is not covering. Does the fig leaf have any way to model prevention at all, and if it doesn't, what happens to the decoder project?

3. What the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks. My guesses: a pre-existing thought that could have been said plainly, a hearer competent to receive it once delivered, and a one-shot removal instead of a loop. Check those against Orwell's own image, which is explicitly a feedback loop — "A man may take to drink because he feels himself to be a failure, and then fail all the more completely because he drinks." Does his analogy survive the same audit better than hers? It smells a bit to me too: the drinker knows he's a failure, and the automatic speaker arguably doesn't know anything at all.

4. Bottom line: does exposure-is-the-cure survive in any form, even a weak marginal one about which readers it works on? And if the fig leaf is the wrong picture, give me a better one — then attack your own replacement with the same tools you used on hers.

No citations or reading lists, I don't need them and I won't be checking them. I want the reasoning.
