You are to act as a specialist in the epistemology and practice of supervising machine learning systems: someone equally at home with the philosophical literature on evidence, reliability, and methodological norms, and with the working details of how process reward models and outcome reward models are actually trained and deployed. Please think carefully before answering, and treat this as a design problem rather than an exposition problem.

Some background, since it determines what I am asking for. I am a retired analytical chemist. For some thirty-five years I directed a public-health testing laboratory, and a very large part of my working life consisted of deciding how closely to watch the people at the benches. I could inspect the finished result — a concentration, a positive or a negative — or I could inspect the bench record: the reagent blank, the spike recovery, the duplicate, the calibration curve, the instrument log, the order in which samples were run. The second mode of oversight is slower and more annoying, and yet I rarely regretted it. I am now writing a short book for an interdisciplinary audience about what supervision is and what it can and cannot certify, and I need a worked example that is not a restatement of what practitioners already repeat to one another.

The thesis I want pressure-tested is this: that the advantage of process supervision is not chiefly a matter of credit assignment under a sparse reward, but lies in compelling the system to compute and expose intermediate quantities that jointly over-determine the answer, so that the answer is pinned down by more than the correlations that happen to hold during training. I want a thought experiment in which outcome supervision is made as favourable as it can honestly be made — dense, immediate, ungameable — and process supervision nevertheless comes out ahead somewhere, for that reason and for no other.

**Design brief.** Construct a case satisfying every one of the following. If you judge two of them to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly and tell me which must be relaxed; do not quietly weaken a constraint in order to finish.

1. Identical task, identical architecture, identical training data, identical compute budget; the sole difference is the source of the training signal.
2. The outcome signal is dense, available immediately after each episode, unambiguous, and not exploitable: no specification gaming, no reward hacking, no sparse-reward credit-assignment story, no deceptive behaviour. I want the cheap objection foreclosed by construction.
3. The process rater is not an authority on the underlying phenomenon and never sees or scores the finished product. The rubric is the ordinary hygiene of careful bench work — the sort of thing a technician can verify from the record without knowing which hypothesis is correct. The advantage may not be attributed to the rater's global insight.
4. The rubric is not mis-specified. It is exactly what a scrupulous laboratory head would demand, and every item on it is genuinely good practice.
5. The process-supervised model is somewhat *worse* on the training distribution. This must be a real trade-off that a practitioner with a deployment deadline would feel, not a free lunch.
6. The process-supervised model's advantage appears under a shift that a working scientist would consider unremarkable — a new reagent lot, a different instrument vendor, a changed matrix, a different population — and not under an adversary deliberately constructing failures.
7. Realistic throughout: no superhuman systems, no exotic hardware, no science-fictional premises.
8. Concretely specified: name the task, the quantity or verdict being produced, the cue the outcome-trained system comes to rely on, and the precise condition that changes.

**Deliverables, in this order.**

A. *Design stage.* Before writing the scenario, sketch two or three candidate mechanisms by which the specifications might be met, note the weakness of each, and state which you have chosen and why. Keep this brief.

B. *The scenario.* No more than three paragraphs. An intelligent reader with no mathematics should be able to follow it; use no equations and no notation.

C. *Specification audit.* Numbered against 1–8, showing for each that the case meets it. For 3 and 5 in particular, show that the verdict is generated by the setup rather than announced: I should be able to see from the description why the outcome-trained system fails under the shift, without being told that it does.

D. *The load-bearing feature.* Identify the single feature of the case doing the work, then perform the removal test: describe what happens to the verdicts if that feature is taken out, and say whether the case then collapses back into the familiar sparse-reward argument I am trying to get past.

E. *The objection.* Give the strongest reply available to a defender of outcome-based supervision — I expect something along the lines of the rubric smuggling in causal knowledge, or of the gap closing once the outcome-trained system is given better coverage of the training distribution — and assess honestly whether it succeeds.

F. *Verdict.* One paragraph: what this case does and does not establish for my thesis, and what would have to be true in practice for the result to generalise beyond the example.

Two further requirements of style. Do not supply citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I cannot verify them and they add nothing here. If the phenomenon you describe has a standard name in your vocabulary, use the name once and proceed. Define any term of art on first use.
