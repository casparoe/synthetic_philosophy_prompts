Me and my friend got into a four hour argument last night about the statue and the clay and I want to weaponize it, becuase I TA the second-year metaphysics course and I've been given one of the two essay questions on the final to write.

Here's how the argument went. I ran the standard setup — the statue and the lump of clay it's made of, same matter, same place at the same time, the lump could survive being squashed into a ball but the statue couldn't, so by Leibniz's law they're two things rather than one. He was happy to grant all of it, and then he sprung this on me:

Suppose that over the course of an hour a prankster swaps out every molecule of the statue for a molecule-for-molecule duplicate, one at a time, so that at no instant is there anything on the plinth but a statue-shaped lump of clay. Meanwhile he keeps the originals in a box and reassembles them, molecule for molecule, into an exact duplicate statue on a plinth in the next room. End state: two statue-shaped lumps in two rooms, one made entirely of the new molecules, one made entirely of the originals.

His point: in the original case, shape and location were doing all the actual work of telling the statue apart from the lump, and the modal stuff about surviving squashing was just decoration on top. But in the swap case shape and location tell you nothing — both are statue-shaped, both are where a statue is — so the only thing left that could make one of them the original statue and the other a mere lump is its history. And if history is all that's left, he says, then you were never counting objects in the first place, you were just tracking them, and "there are two things in that place" was never a metaphysical claim, only a bookkeeping habit that gets exposed as soon as the tracking comes apart.

I think he's wrong but I couldn't say why without sounding like I was just defending my favourite textbook.

So write me the exam question. Second-year metaphysics final, in class, 45 minutes, worth 25 marks. It has to be built on a case like the one above — you can clean up the details or change them, that's fine — but the whole point is that it can't be answered by reciting the coincidence argument or by planting a flag on a named view. It should force them to say what work the claim "these are two objects" is doing once shape and location have been taken off the table, and to take a position on whether a purely historical difference can ground numerical distinctness. I'd like it to also make them notice what happens to the squashing intuition in the swap case, since that's the bit my friend thinks is decorative.

Then give me a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme that says specifically which moves an A answer makes that a competent-but-uninspired B answer doesn't. List at least four mistakes that should cost marks — the kind a student would make while sounding like they know the material, e.g. sliding from "we can't tell them apart" to "there's only one of them" as if that were a step. No reading list and no citations anywhere in the question or the answer: I want the marks to come from the reasoning, and I don't want them name-dropping positions they can't defend in an oral.

Start with your bottom line in the first sentence — say flat out whether the case above actually forces the question I want it to force or whether there's an easy escape route I've missed, and if there's an escape route, say what it is and show me how you've closed it in the version you write. Then the question exactly as the students would see it, then the model answer, then the marking scheme. No preamble and don't restate what I asked for.
