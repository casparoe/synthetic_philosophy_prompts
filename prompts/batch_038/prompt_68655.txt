I chair the examining committee for our PhD comprehensive in philosophy of science, and I need one essay question on causation for this year's paper. Candidates have ninety minutes and twenty-five marks. They will have read Lewis on counterfactual dependence and on causation as influence, and the interventionist programme from Pearl through Woodward, so the question cannot turn on their knowing who said what; it has to turn on a crux that I have become convinced the two programmes share and conceal.

Here is the crux I want built into the question. Take the interventionist's ideal intervention on X with respect to Y: it is supposed to change X and nothing else except what lies downstream of X, it must act on Y only through X, and it must not be correlated with any other cause of Y. Now ask what that characterization is made of — these are the surgical conditions meant to rule out fat-handed interventions. If they are made of non-causal modal resources, namely a counterfactual that holds fixed the past, the laws, and every variable not downstream of X, then the interventionist account looks like Lewis's account fitted with a restricted similarity metric, and the restriction is itself cashed in causal terms. If instead the intervention is taken as primitive, or elucidated only by its place in a family of interlocking concepts, then the account's content comes from our pre-theoretic grasp of what it is to manipulate a system, and the charge that nothing has been explained returns undiminished. I want the question to force candidates onto one of those two horns, to make them say what each costs, and then to confront a further point which I think determines whether the two programmes are rivals at all: for the interventionist, whether X causes Y depends on which variables are in the model — introducing a variable for an unmeasured common cause, or resolving one coarse-grained variable into two, can flip the verdict — whereas for Lewis it depends on what the worlds are like. Candidates should have to say whether that relativity is a defect, a virtue, or evidence that the two accounts answer different questions; and whether Lewis has an analogue of it in the relativity of causal claims to how events are individuated.

Please write three things. First, the question as it would appear on the paper: roughly 150–200 words, precise enough to be fair but open enough that it cannot be answered by summarising the two views. You may state the variable-relativity case concretely if you think that helps, but do not smuggle the answer into the wording. Second, a model answer of about 700 words that takes a definite position and argues for it, rather than surveying the options. Third, a marking scheme out of twenty-five that names five must-hit moves with the marks each carries, and gives, for each, one sentence on the particular way candidates typically get it wrong — for instance, treating the circularity worry as fatal without noticing that the interventionist never claimed a reduction, or reporting variable-relativity as an objection without saying what its Lewisian counterpart would be. Add a closing line stating what a candidate must contribute beyond those five moves to reach the top band.

Do not ask candidates for citations, and do not append a reading list or bibliography to anything you write: the question and the model answer should carry their content in the arguments rather than in attributions, so that a candidate who has absorbed the material but misremembers who wrote what can still earn full marks.

Finally, tell me how confident you are that the model answer's position is defensible rather than merely coherent, and single out the one claim in it that you are least sure of — the place where a sharp candidate could push back and be right.
