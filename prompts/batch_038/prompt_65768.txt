**The conceivability challenge as a self-defeat argument: a dilemma**

1. In the Second Dialogue, Hylas suggests that it is easy enough to think of groves and fields that nobody is currently perceiving, and Philonous responds by asking whether Hylas can conceive it possible for him to conceive any unperceived thing, drawing from Hylas's inability to do so the conclusion that supposing such a thing to exist is a manifest contradiction. (I have deliberately paraphrased throughout; the quotations will go in later.) Call this the conceivability challenge.

2. The familiar objection is that the challenge equivocates. Whatever Hylas manages to think about is, trivially, thought about; but the materialist thesis says nothing about the episode of thinking and everything about the item thought of. Conceiving an object under a description is not conferring the properties in the description on the object. Berkeley's inference requires that a property predicated within the content of a conception be transferred to the act that entertains it.

3. My proposal is that this objection, though sound against one reading, misses the reading on which the challenge has any bite. Consider that Philonous does not ask Hylas to evaluate a modal claim in the abstract; he asks Hylas to perform an act and reports that the act cannot be performed as described. On what I will call the pragmatic reading, the target is not the truth of the materialist's sentence but its assertibility by the person uttering it: the materialist is in the position of someone who cannot coherently do the very thing his sentence says can be done. The relevant structure is not that of a self-referential liar, where the sentence says something about itself, but of a self-undermining performance, where the utterance defeats the claim it is recruited to support. Something like this happens when I announce that no statement of mine is true, or when I assert that nothing can be singled out by any act of singling out while engaged in singling out.

4. This generates a dilemma for Berkeley. On the metaphysical reading — the challenge is meant to establish that objects cannot exist unconceived — the challenge needs the transfer of properties that objection (2) blocks, and so it fails, or at any rate it fails to establish anything beyond a claim about Hylas's imaginative capacities. On the pragmatic reading the challenge succeeds, but what it establishes is only that a certain sentence cannot be coherently asserted by a certain speaker in a certain context. That is compatible with the sentence's being true, and it is compatible with the materialist's retreating to every device by which an unassertible content can still be entertained: supposing it for the sake of argument, reporting it, embedding it in a conditional, or adopting a pretense toward materialist discourse while continuing to use its sentences in a spirit of make-believe.

5. Both horns hurt Berkeley's larger project in the same way. The pragmatic reading's conclusion is defeated by embedding, and Berkeley needs a conclusion that survives embedding, since what he wants is not to decline to assert the existence of matter but to deny it. Moreover, the continuity argument needs more than an assertion-theoretic result: it needs the premise that objects would otherwise perish whenever no finite mind perceives them, which is a claim about the objects, not about our sentences. So the argument that silences Hylas and the argument that motivates the appeal to God's perception cannot be one and the same argument, and Berkeley appears to need both.

6. A test case. Suppose a pebble existed for a million years on a planet that never hosted a mind, and was destroyed before any mind ever conceived of it. Under the pragmatic reading, the materialist can assert that the pebble existed unconceived without any self-undermining, because being conceived now is a different property from having been conceived then, and her present act of conceiving does not retroactively supply the earlier one. The temporal index absorbs the paradox. So the pragmatic reading bites only if the relevant predicate is read as unconceived-at-all-times rather than unconceived-at-the-time-in-question, and it is not obvious that this is the predicate Hylas is working with.

7. A residual worry about my own analogy. Moore-paradoxical assertions involve a mismatch between what is said and the belief-state that saying it expresses. But the materialist need not disavow any belief: she can cheerfully grant that she conceives of the pebble, that she believes what she says, and that her conceiving is entirely consistent with the pebble's having existed unconceived. If the mismatch is between the performance and the content rather than between the performance and a belief, the analogy to Moore may be decorative, and I may be buying rhetorical force at the price of precision.

8. One alternative I have toyed with and want judged. Perhaps the challenge is neither metaphysical nor pragmatic but concerns the content of conception: if conceiving requires some acquaintance with what is conceived, then the materialist's purported conception is empty, and Philonous's point is that Hylas has no content available to him at all. Whether this dissolves the dilemma or merely relocates it is something I cannot yet tell.
