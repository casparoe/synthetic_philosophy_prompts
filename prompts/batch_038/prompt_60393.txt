So — I've been asked to give a talk to a mostly political-science audience on what the prisoner's dilemma does and doesn't explain about cooperation, and I put a slide in the deck headed roughly "where I stand," and now I want someone to hit it before they do. Here's the package:

(1) In a one-shot anonymous PD between two agents who know the payoffs, I think there is nothing to say to a defector. I don't buy the Gauthier/Parfit line that having a disposition to cooperate is what pays, and I don't buy any of the redefinitional moves where cooperation gets declared rational after all. Instrumental rationality as standardly understood is the right normative yardstick and I'm not going to quietly water it down.

(2) What makes cooperation rational in the repeated game is the shadow of the future, and that's basically the whole story. Axelrod's tournaments are a demonstration of that point; tit-for-tat does well because it harvests the future.

(3) I also think the folk theorem is right: with enough repetition and a high enough discount factor, almost any feasible payoff profile that beats the minimax level is sustainable in equilibrium. So the interesting question is never whether cooperation can be sustained — it's which equilibrium actually gets played, and I think that's settled by learning dynamics and salience, not by rationality.

(4) Finite repetition under common knowledge of rationality unravels by backward induction, and I take that to be a lesson about idealization rather than a reason to think real players should defect from the first round.

(5) The cooperation I actually care about politically — large-scale, among anonymous strangers, in one-off encounters — isn't reciprocity at all. It's norm psychology plus cultural group selection plus reputational machinery. Dyadic iteration does essentially no work at that scale.

(6) Costly third-party punishment is what closes the second-order free-rider gap: since the punisher isn't the one who was exploited, punishing stops being a public-goods problem for the victim.

(7) Case I want to use in the talk: international climate negotiation is best modelled as an iterated PD, and the reason its going badly is that the shadow of the future is too weak — the effective discount rate is too high.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I half-suspect (2) and (4) can't both stand, but I may well be wrong about that and I'd rather you told me than agreed with me. Please rank the tensions you find by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say which commitment has to go or get weakened and what it costs me to weaken it that way. Then tell me which single revision would buy the most across the whole set. If something on the list isn't in tension with the others but is just wrong, say so separately — (6) and (7) are the ones I'm least sure about, but I might be wrong there too. No survey of the literature and no reading list, and drop names only where it's really unavoidable.
