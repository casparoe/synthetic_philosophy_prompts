Write a short story of about 1,500 words set aboard a generation ship some three centuries into its voyage, on which a certain class of claims — that the outer hull has never been breached, that the destination is where the charts say it is, that the engines have never stopped — has ceased to function as empirical hypotheses and now serves as the unmoving bed on which every other judgment the crew makes depends. Your protagonist is the officer whose post exists to maintain those claims: not by collecting evidence for them, since no one aboard would know what such evidence could look like, but by shaping the ship's inspections, rites, and schoolroom so that questioning them never becomes thinkable in the first place. The plot should turn on a genuine discovery that one of them is false, and on the officer's assessment of whether the crew can absorb the news without the rest of what they know losing its footing.

Dramatize the asymmetry rather than narrating it. Have a young engineer produce real data, and have her answered — plausibly, sympathetically, and not by anyone who is a liar or a villain — that her instruments are calibrated against the very thing she is measuring, that her arithmetic is trusted only because the surrounding picture is, and that what would count as a reading at all presupposes the bedrock she wants to prise up. Let the ship's own folklore carry a river image, hardened rock and shifting silt, without ever naming its philosophical source. Do not settle the question of whether the officer was right.
