My workplace announced a pilot this month: a 32-hour week at unchanged pay, with one catch — every fourth week you are on call for your team and expected to answer messages within roughly an hour, including on days you are nominally off. Two colleagues, Amira and Ben, have been arguing about it, and they asked me to write the exchange up neutrally so a third party could judge it without hearing either of them plead.

(1) Ben called it an obvious win: four fewer hours a week for the same money, and the on-call duty is bounded, published months ahead, and therefore something you can arrange your life around. (2) Amira said he is measuring the wrong quantity. The injury overwork does is not the number of hours consumed but the impossibility of ever being away — you cannot enroll in a course, take a long hike, keep a phone-free evening, or simply decide to do nothing, because at any moment you might be required. The relevant measure, she said, is how much of your time you can actually call your own, and a shorter week that leaves you permanently reachable hands back the hours while keeping the leash. (3) Ben replied that she is charging him for a purely psychological cost: if the actual demand averages an hour of email a day, total workload has fallen, and refusing to count that because it feels like captivity is just describing her own anxiety. (4) Amira answered that this is how unrecorded overtime has always worked — the hours on the books look modest while the availability expected is total, so the cost vanishes from every measure that was supposed to protect people. (5) Ben said her position now proves too much: it would condemn every nurse, doctor, and utility worker carrying a pager, who are generally thought to be managing fine. (6) Amira's last word was that those people are paid extra for the pager, their on-call is built into shifts with genuine relief, and no one expects them to be reachable on the days they are truly off; the pilot she has been given is all three of those deficits at once.

Please go through this move by move: rate each of the six steps from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification of each rating, and say which of the two is more right overall. Then tell me what both of them have missed — including whether they ever actually disagreed about the same question, since one seems to be asking whether the pilot beats the old schedule and the other whether reachability is a harm in itself.
