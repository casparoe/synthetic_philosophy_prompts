So I'm putting together a set of case studies for a governance-tech seminar and I need one on futarchy, done properly rather than as a thinkpiece.

Setup, so we're working from the same text: Hanson's manifesto says that in futarchy "we would vote on values, but bet on beliefs" — elected representatives define and manage an after-the-fact measure of national welfare, market speculators say which policies would raise it, and when a market clearly estimates that a proposal would increase expected welfare, that proposal becomes law. One of the three assumptions he offers in support is that speculative markets aggregate information well, and among his examples is that "betting markets beat opinion polls at predicting U.S. election results." He also concedes a long-shot bias where transaction costs are high, and notes that futarchy's actual task is harder than plain forecasting: it has to compare two conditional estimates, welfare conditional on adopting a policy and welfare conditional on not adopting it, which he suggests can be handled with devices like "called-off bets."

The episode: the 2016 US presidential election and the markets that traded on it. Facts I'll stipulate so you dont have to hedge — going into Nov 8 the big offshore exchanges had Clinton somewhere in the low-to-mid 80s, the US political markets (Iowa Electronic Markets, PredictIt) had her ahead too, and PredictIt was trading under a CFTC no-action letter that capped any single position at $850 and each market at 5,000 traders. Trump won, and the markets got written up as having failed alongside the polls, which is awkard given that the supporting assumption above leans on exactly this kind of case. If your recollection of a number differs, flag it and carry on.

Do the mapping before the argument, and I mean literally: what contracts were trading, who was plausibly on the other side of them, what a trader could have known that the poll aggregators didn't, and what the price was a price of. Then run that assumption — markets beat polls and expert judgment at forecasting governance-relevant outcomes — against the mapping.

Three things I want pressed, because the usual post-mortem skates over them:

1. Marginal vs conditional. 2016 was a bet on one event happening; futarchy needs policy-conditional counterfactuals. Is inaccuracy on the first any evidence about the second? What does the second add — who is informed about welfare under a policy that never gets adopted, what would the resolution criterion be, and does the called-off-bet device solve that or just relocate it?

2. Parasitic information. My hunch is these markets were largely re-pricing the same polls everyone else was reading. Hanson also writes that "it is hard to find information that is not embodied in market prices" — if that's right, the market wasn't an independent second opinion in 2016, it was a mirror with a price tag. Does that reading hold? Would it change if the position caps were gone and volume 100x bigger?

3. Reflexivity. Under futarchy the price doesn't just report the future, it triggers the decision that produces it. In 2016 the odds were front-page news, plausibly feeding back into turnout, donor money, and where campaigns spent. Is that loop a problem specific to letting markets govern, or just the generic cost of publishing any forecast?

Then say where the theory is silent — what the episode raises that futarchy has no resources for at all — instead of stretching it to speak. And argue the other direction honestly: does anything here count in favour of the premise? Two candidates I can see are that the markets degraded more gracefully than the most confident polls did, and that the whole thing happened under pathological conditions (position caps, thin liquidity, retail-heavy order flow), so it was never a clean test of the aggregation claim. Tell me whether either survives.
