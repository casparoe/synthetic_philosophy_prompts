ok so I've been asked to write down where I actually stand on uncertainty for a workshop, and before I say any of it out loud I want someone to tell me whether it hangs together. here's the package:

1. Keynes is right that rational belief isn't always a number. For some evidence-hypothesis pairs there just isn't a numerical probability to be had, and insisting there is one is a large part of what went wrong in macro and in risk modelling generally.
2. I also take on board his idea of the weight of the argument: over and above whatever the probability is, there's the question of how much relevant evidence is behind it, and that's rationally significant, not just a feeling. Two hypotheses can be equally probable with one much better supported.
3. The right formal home for 1 and 2 is imprecise credences — a set of probability functions rather than a point — and I think that's all people mean by Knightian uncertainty, nothing mystical about it.
4. Given 3, ambiguity-averse behaviour looks basically defensible to me: preferring the urn you know the composition of over the one you don't is what an indeterminate credal state should produce, and Savage's sure-thing principle is just an axiom that these cases give you a reason to drop rather than a discovery about rationality.
5. But at the same time: when you have to act, acting has to be a comparison. Whatever your representation is, the choice comes down to which option does best on it, and I don't buy decision rules that leave a clearly worse option alive or that just refuse to pick. The gamma-maximin family reads like a way of getting imprecision to do the choosing for you.
6. On markets: genuine uncertainty is why agents fall back on convention and animal spirits, and I think the General Theory's discussion of long-term expectation and the beauty contest is still the best thing written about asset prices. But I'm also pretty Hayekian about prices — they aggregate dispersed local information better than any central modeller could, and I don't want to give up on price signals.
7. Epistemically I'm a thoroughgoing empiricist. No a priori access to anything, everything comes out of evidence, and I think Keynes leaned way too hard on the principle of indifference and on intuition about logical relations in the Treatise. That machinery I want no part of.

So: which of these can't all be true? the ones I'm vaguely worried about are that weight in 2 sounds like it should be a second number, which would seem to undo 1, and something about 6 pulling in two directions. I have no idea how bad either is and I'd rather not be told everything is in tension with everything else. Rank the real conflicts by severity, say for each what has to go and what losing it costs me, then separately point out which of my worries are nothing, and why. Finish with the single revision that buys the most consistency for the least damage. And please say what would change your mind about the ranking — which consideration or argument would make you reorder it — plus where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to be off.
