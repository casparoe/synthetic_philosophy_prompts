I help write model-evaluation protocols, and I have grown uneasy about the logic of one of them — not its cost or its reliability, but what it has to assume to work at all.

The practice is sandbagging detection. Before a frontier model is deployed, evaluators attempt to establish a floor on what it can genuinely do, on the grounds that a system may be performing deliberately below its real capability — failing puzzles it could solve, answering badly questions it could answer well, appearing weaker than it is. The motivation is a specific one: if a system's behaviour under observation can come apart from its behaviour once observation ceases, and if a system that has learned that seeming to have certain goals is itself rewarded, then a capability measured while the system is being watched tells us little about what it would do unwatched. Since capability cannot be read off directly, the procedure escalates. If the model fails a task, the evaluators try harder elicitation methods — more worked examples, a paraphrase, a hint, a persona, a monetary or token reward, and in principle methods that act on the model's internals rather than its prompt — and treat the best performance obtained by any route as the real capability. A negative result is meant to be earned: the model is judged unable to do the thing only once every available elicitation route has been exhausted.

I would like an account of what this procedure presupposes. Reconstruct, as precisely as you can, what has to be true for "we could not elicit it" to support "it cannot do it". Is the practice tacitly committed to something like a conditional analysis of ability — to be able to do X is to be such that one would do X in suitable circumstances — and if so, which circumstances count as suitable, and who is entitled to decide when they have been reached? Then take the familiar counterexamples to that style of analysis: a disposition that is finked, so that the very circumstance which would trigger it also removes it, and a disposition that is masked, so that the trigger occurs but the manifestation is blocked. If a sandbagging test is itself a circumstance capable of finking or masking the disposition it is trying to detect, can the procedure ever terminate in the negative result it wants, or does it only ever licence the weaker claim that no elicitation route tried so far has worked?

Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with. Does its defender have to hold that capability is a stable trait carried across contexts, or could someone who thinks such behaviour is largely a function of the situation still run sandbagging evaluations coherently? Indicate where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival metaphysics of disposition would license the identical protocol. Finally: suppose the laboratory adopted the rival view — that there is no determinate fact about what a model can do apart from a specified elicitation context, so that capability is always relative to a test regime. What would have to change in the evaluations, in what they claim to show, and in the inference from a measured capability to a forecast of what the system would do if it were trying to mislead us?
