I'm in my final year of secondary school and I'm writing a 5,000-word essay for our school's academic journal about the claim that the linguistic data available to a child are too poor to explain what the child ends up knowing, and more generally about what 'innate' is supposed to mean when it appears in that claim. My supervisor's objection to essays of this kind is that they settle on a meaning for 'innate' on the first page and then win by stipulation. I think he's right, and I don't want to do that. So I'd like the requirements list first, and to choose an account afterwards, if choosing is even legitimate.

What I'm after is a specification: everything a theory of innateness has to be able to do before it may be put to work in an argument about language acquisition. For each requirement, give me the consideration behind it — a concrete developmental example, a recognisable way the argument can go wrong, a result the account cannot contradict — rather than a label and a slogan. Please cover at least the following, and add whatever you think I've missed while cutting anything you judge to be padding:

- The distinctions an account must not collapse: a trait being innate versus a capacity or disposition being innate; innate as present at birth versus innate as robustly developing; innate versus merely universal across the species; innate versus simply not learned.
- Non-vacuity. On some accounts nearly every cognitive trait turns out to be innate, and on others nothing does, so the requirement should be that an account must not make the nativist conclusion true by definition. If you think that demand is unfair, say so and tell me what to demand in its place.
- The developmental biology constraint. Every trait depends on genes and environment in interaction, so any account implying that some traits are carried by the genome independently of the environment is not merely contentious but mistaken. I want to know what is left of innateness talk once that is conceded, and whether what's left is enough to run an acquisition argument on.
- Relativisation. I suspect a poverty-of-input argument cannot even be stated until one says which alternatives the learner was capable of entertaining and what counts as the input. Please make this explicit, including how an account should handle input that is poor in one respect (no reliable correction of malformed sentences) and rich in another (abundant distributional evidence).
- Portability beyond language. If the account cannot be applied to infant number sense, face preferences, or object individuation, either explain why that is no defect or count it as one.
- Gradability and accessibility. Whether innateness comes in degrees, and if so whether those degrees can be assessed in principle for a species we cannot rear in deliberately varied environments.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of one camp; say which single requirement eliminates the largest number of popular accounts; and tell me whether any subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that a definition in terms of counterfactual environmental variation, non-vacuity, and testability on human beings cannot all be had together. If that's right, I want it stated as the finding of the exercise, not tucked away as a caveat.

There is one particular thing I need the list to be able to adjudicate. My supervisor considers the whole debate closed, because current large language models acquire usable syntax from text alone, with no correction and no embodied experience, which he says shows the input was never poor. I cannot tell whether that is an argument, a category mistake, or a genuinely relevant observation. One of the requirements should be strong enough to give a verdict: the account ought to tell me what would count as evidence for or against a poverty-of-input claim, and whether a system trained that way falls within the class of learners the claim is about in the first place.

Close with no more than half a page applying the list to three candidates — a universal-grammar style nativism, a usage-based or statistical-learning account, and one philosophical account that defines innateness in terms of developmental robustness. Keep that brief; the requirements are the deliverable, not the scorecard.

Two requests about manner. Please write in British English. And please keep a firm line between what is settled among people who actually work on this and what is genuinely contested — I'm not yet able to tell the difference on my own, and I'd much rather my essay said plainly that experts disagree than quietly adopted one side's assumption as the default. Where a requirement you list is your own rather than a standard one, flag it as such.
