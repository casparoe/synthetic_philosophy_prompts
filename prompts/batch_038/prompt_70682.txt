I write literary novels, and my working vocabulary is the vocabulary of craft: point of view, focalization, free indirect discourse, whose head the camera is in, what a character is entitled to withhold, the competing desires of narrator and reader for a given scene. I have no training in social choice theory beyond what a general reader picks up. Please restate Sen's liberal paradox in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can — the impossibility of combining the Pareto condition with a minimal liberalism condition under which each individual is decisive over at least one pair of social states, together with his example of the prude, the lewd, and the single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover. Where the proof needs a complete and transitive social ordering over states of the world, tell me what in a novel could possibly play that role, and if nothing can, say so plainly rather than reaching for a loose analogue.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the theorem map cleanly — my own guess is that the meddlesome preference, the person who cares what someone else does inside his own private sphere, is something fiction treats as an engine of plot rather than as a pathology to be excluded by assumption. Which parts you had to bend to make them fit, and at what point you caught yourself importing my craft assumptions and presenting the import as translation. And which parts have no counterpart in craft talk at all.

Finally, test a hunch of mine: that minimal liberalism is just the claim that a character is owed an interior life the other characters do not get to vote on, and that the paradox evaporates once one notices a narrator is not a society. Is that a genuine insight the standard presentation obscures, or a category error that misses what the proof is actually doing? Please write your answer in American English.
