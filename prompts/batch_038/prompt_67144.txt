I run a tiny philosophy-of-mind podcast and next month's episode is a deep dive on Dennett's "Where Am I?" — the one where the narrator's brain (Yorick) ends up in a vat while his radio-linked body (Hubert) goes off on the mission, and later there's a duplicate brain plus a swich that picks which one drives Hubert. My guest is a philosopher who's just published a book arguing the puzzle has been misfiled. Her view, which she calls the salvage criterion: "where am I?" is not settled by introspection or by psychology at all, it's settled by a counterfactual — you are where the thing is whose destruction would be your death. And in the duplicate case, where destroying either candidate leaves you running off the other one, you just have no determinate location at all. She thinks this dissolves the puzzle without taking Yorick's side or Hubert's.

Draft me eight to ten questions for the interview. I don't want her to spend the whole hour restating the criterion, so make them bite. I want at least:

- one on circularity — the criterion is spelled out in terms of *my* death, which seems to already presuppose the me it was supposed to locate — with the follow-up ready for when she says the circularity is harmless, like defining "the water in this glass";
- one pressing why the answer to a question about where I am should flip with engineering details nobody could detect from the inside, i.e. whether the duplicate is wired in locked step with the original or not, and the follow-up for her most likely reply, which is that location was never supposed to be a first-person-accessible fact;
- one on the threshold problem in ordinary life: I survive losing chunks of brain matter, so on a strict reading of "the thing whose destruction would be your death" my location looks like it should be smeared across a bunch of tissue, and the follow-up if she says the criterion is only meant for puzzle cases;
- one asking whether the view actually accounts for the pull of the original scene — standing there looking at the vat while everything seems to be happening out where the body is — or whether it just changes the subject away from that phenomenology;
- one on the moment where the switch gets flipped and, by hypothesis, nothing is different: does her view predict that indeterminate location should feel like anything, and if not, what work is the indeterminacy doing;
- one on the applied side — teletransporters and whole-brain uploading — where the criterion seems to give the answer "you're wherever the surviving copy is, or nowhere if there are two";
- and a closing one on what observation or case design could count against the salvage criterion, since right now it looks like it can absorb any counterexample by declaring the location indeterminate.

Also: she's very good at ducking with "that's an identity question, not a location question," so build in one question, somewhere in the middle, that makes that move progressively harder to use rather than letting her reset with it. No warm-up stuff about what drew her to philosophy or how long the book took.
