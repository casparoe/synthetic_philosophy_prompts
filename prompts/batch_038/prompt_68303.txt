My friend and I spent three hours last night failing to settle a question about superrationality, and I have come away convinced that the quarrel was never about superrationality at all: we were answering different background questions and congratulating ourselves on disagreeing. What I want is a diagnostic instrument that would have ended the argument in ten minutes.

The setting is a monthly reading group of about a dozen people, mostly non-specialists, who have read Hofstadter's two thought experiments — the Platonia dilemma and the luring lottery he actually ran by mail in 1983 — and who now use the word superrational to mean at least four incompatible things. The two cases the tree should be built against are these, stated so that a reader needs no prior exposure to the literature: (i) a group of addressees is told that if exactly one of them sends an affirmative reply that one person receives an enormous sum, whereas zero replies or several replies pay nobody anything; (ii) the lottery case, in which each respondent mails in a number and what any one respondent gains depends on what the others did.

Please write a decision tree of ten to twelve nodes terminating in roughly eight leaves, subject to the following constraints.

1. Every node must be a question the reader can answer from the cases alone, without knowing which position their answer leads to. No node may ask whether the reader finds superrationality plausible, and no node may use the vocabulary of any leaf.
2. The branch points must be substantive commitments rather than names. At a minimum, the tree should turn on: whether the reader thinks causal independence among the agents' choices settles what they themselves ought to do; whether they think their own choice is evidence about the others' choices even in the total absence of causation; whether the premise that all the agents reason alike is functioning as a stipulation about how the case is described or as an empirical claim about the addressees; whether the move from "if I reply, they will all reply" is being read as a counterfactual, as a conditional probability, or as the claim that one decision is being taken many times over; and whether the reader is prepared to carry the reasoning out of these artificially symmetric cases into an ordinary many-person dilemma among mutually ignorant strangers.
3. The leaves must be named positions where names exist, and must include at least: the causal decision theorist who says the symmetry stipulation does no work; the evidential decision theorist who reaches the same recommendation without it; Hofstadter's own superrationalist; the view that the many agents are literally one chooser deciding once; the view that superrationality is a universalizability principle dressed in decision-theoretic clothing; the view that the reasoning collapses as soon as we admit realistic uncertainty about the degree of correlation; and the view that nothing more exotic is going on than a salient strategy plus common knowledge of its salience. Any leaf no one actually defends should be marked unnamed rather than dropped.
4. Build the space first and locate the positions in it afterwards. Report which combinations of answers your questions permit but no leaf occupies, and say for each empty cell whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
5. Flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and state what further question would separate them, if any exists.
6. Attach to each leaf one sentence on the commitment it is most embarrassed by.

Finally, place me. My own answers, put without any of the labels: I do not think causal independence settles what I should do; I do think my choice is evidence about theirs; I treat the sameness of reasoning as part of how the case is set up rather than as something I have established about the addressees; I would still want to reason this way in an ordinary twenty-person dilemma among strangers; and I do not think the phrase 'we are all one decision taken many times' adds anything to the evidential point. Route that pattern through your own tree, say which leaf I land on, and tell me if it is a pattern your tree cannot resolve.

Close with a single line of the form \answer{leaf name} giving the label of the leaf I land on.
