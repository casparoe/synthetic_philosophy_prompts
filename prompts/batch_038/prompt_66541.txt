You are to act as a philosopher who works on action explanation and radical interpretation — the Davidson-and-Lewis tradition of making sense of an agent by assigning them beliefs and desires under constraints of charity — but who is also genuinely fluent in how machine learning infers human preferences from behavior: inverse reinforcement learning and learning from demonstrations, maximum-entropy formulations of the reward-inference problem, and comparison-based reward learning of the sort used to train assistants from human preference data. Please think carefully rather than quickly, and treat what follows as data to be explained rather than as an occasion for a survey of positions.

I am co-writing a workshop paper about reward learning for an assistive system that has to build a model of one particular user over many months, drawing on what the user does far more than on what the user says. The engineers keep running into a problem they have not recognized as philosophical: which behaviors are evidence about what the user values, and which are evidence about nothing at all. Arguing with two labmates, I noticed that my own intuitions are firm case by case but that I cannot state what unifies them. They are as follows.

1. A colleague has driven the same route to work for nine years; traffic data show an alternative that averages four minutes faster. When I mentioned the alternative he was surprised, tried it for a week, and then went back to the old route. Verdict on the nine years: they are evidence of nothing whatsoever, and I would resist encoding them as a preference. Verdict on the week and the return: now I can infer that he prefers the old route, and this one week outweighs nine years.
2. A friend orders the cheapest coffee on the menu every time, and I have watched her look at the board and audibly weigh a latte against it. Verdict: good evidence that price carries real weight for her.
3. My sister leaves every party within an hour, has never once stayed longer, and says she does not experience it as a decision — she simply feels bad and goes. Verdict: excellent evidence that she prefers quiet evenings; better evidence than case 1's nine years, despite the fact that she has never weighed an alternative.
4. A student rejected by her first-choice graduate program now says she never much wanted it, does not open mail from comparable programs, and has changed topics. Verdict: do not infer the preference. It formed under the rejection and should be discounted or at least quarantined.
5. A man who grew up very poor is now comfortably off, never considers expensive restaurants, and reports no interest in them. Verdict: take it at face value and infer that he does not want to eat at them.
6. A man has complained about his job every working day for twenty years and has never left. Verdict: infer that he prefers staying, but only weakly, since I do not know that leaving was ever available to him.
7. In vignettes I run for a project, healthy participants typically say they would rather die than live paralyzed; the paralyzed people I know describe lives they would choose to continue. Verdict: discount the healthy participants' stated preference, and take the paralyzed person's report at face value.
8. A woman with ample money and free time who never travels abroad versus a man who never travels abroad and cannot afford to. Verdict: infer a preference against travel in the first case, nothing at all in the second.
9. A neighbor who tells everyone she cares deeply about the environment and flies four times a year, versus a donor who gives away a substantial fraction of his income and has told no one, including the people who would have thanked him. Verdict: believe the flights over the words, and believe the transfers over the silence — in both cases the cost paid is what carries the information.

My working principle, such as it is, has two parts: an action is evidence of a preference only insofar as it was selected from options the agent actually considered, and the preference inferred should be the one that makes the choice most sensible given the agent's own beliefs about those options. It handles 2, 8 and 9 well and it badly mishandles 3, 5 and 7. There is also a technical version of the same worry. Our pipeline currently fits a soft-max choice rule, so every departure from the inferred optimum is treated as sampling noise; but my cases suggest that some departures should be read as expressing a preference, some as the absence of any preference, and some as the presence of a preference that ought not to be attributed to the person at all, and no single temperature parameter delivers those distinctions.

Here is what I want from you, in this order. First, a short diagnostic pass: for each of the nine cases, one sentence naming the feature of the case that you think is actually doing the evidential work, so that I can tell whether you read my intuitions as keyed to what I take them to be keyed to. Second, a single principle, or at most two competing candidates, stated precisely enough that a careful person could apply it to a new case without further interpretation, with each clause tied back to the case or cases that require it. Third, an honest coverage report: which of my verdicts the principle reproduces, which it reverses, and which it leaves genuinely open — and if the cheapest coherent version of the principle reverses one of my verdicts, say which one should be given up and why, instead of adding machinery to rescue it; if you can save all nine, itemize exactly what the saving costs. Fourth, the verdicts your principle delivers on three cases I have not listed, of which at least one should involve a system inferring preferences from click-through or dwell-time data, and at least one should involve an agent who knows they are being observed. Fifth, a short account of what the choice between the simple principle that overturns a verdict and the baroque one that saves them all actually turns on — is it a question about the nature of preference, about the purposes of the inferring system, or about something else? Cases 1 and 3 are the pair I most want pressure applied to; they look to me like the same structure with opposite verdicts, and I do not know whether the difference I sense is real or a prejudice in favor of anything with an affective flavor. Please do not tell me that all nine verdicts are defensible if they are not.
