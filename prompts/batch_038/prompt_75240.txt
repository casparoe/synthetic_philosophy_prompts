I moderate a reading group attached to our hospital's clinical ethics service, where we alternate between philosophy papers and the live problems in our scarce-resource allocation protocol. Last Thursday's session was on Taurek's 1977 article in Philosophy & Public Affairs about whether the numbers should count, and the discussion degenerated into a two-person argument that ran nearly an hour and that I have reconstructed from my notes. The two participants were a doctoral student in philosophy, whom I'll call Nadia, and an emergency-medicine resident with a public health background, whom I'll call Owen. Neither is a strawman, which is why I couldn't referee it, and I've deliberately kept my own views out of the transcript. The case under discussion was the one the paper turns on: six people exposed to the same toxin, and a single supply of treatment that will either save one of them outright or, if divided, save the other five.

The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Nadia opened by saying the paper's real insight is that there is no such thing as the loss of the five, because a loss is always a loss for somebody and no person stands to lose five deaths; the familiar principle that we must save the greater number therefore rests on a category mistake, and at most we are permitted to save the many rather than required to. (2) Owen replied that the permission isn't the interesting part, and that Taurek's own fallback — give every person an equal chance rather than choosing the group — makes the numbers count anyway, since with six people an equal chance for each means a five-sixths probability that the five are saved; over many such decisions his procedure and the maximizer's coincide exactly, so the dispute is about bookkeeping rather than outcomes. (3) Nadia said that coincidence in the equal-harm case proves nothing, because the two views come apart wherever the harms differ in magnitude: a pairwise approach to competing claims would forbid trading one person's death against, say, five hundred people's migraines, whereas summing outcomes permits it, so Owen has not shown a collapse. (4) Owen answered that this caricatures the aggregationist, who can perfectly well hold that claims must be of comparable strength before they may be added together — a restriction that yields saving the five in the numbers case without positing any aggregate sufferer. On his account the invalid step in Taurek's argument is the move from the person-relativity of badness to the irrelevance of numbers, and the lottery is just aggregation with extra steps that knowingly risks the worse outcome. (5) Nadia said the lottery differs precisely because it never announces that the one man's claim has been outweighed, and she added that Taurek's allowance for saving a friend rather than five strangers shows the view isn't about producing the best states of affairs at all; she also turned Owen's own demand for reasons that each person could accept against him, arguing that the man who dies under a maximizing policy has been told his death counted for less, whereas the man who dies after a fair lottery has been given everything he could ask for. (6) Owen closed with two charges: that Nadia's framework is unusable for an institution, which must settle on a procedure in advance and can run lotteries in bulk, at which point the long-run frequency of lives saved simply is aggregation; and that her premise proves too much, since if nothing is worse overall she cannot even say that a policy killing five hundred is worse than one killing one — something she plainly wants to say — which leaves her with an evaluative notion of worse that she denies at the level of deliberation and no account of how the two levels are supposed to fit together.

Could you work through this and tell me who is right about what? Rather than scoring each numbered turn, please first extract the genuinely distinct claims the two of them were fighting over — I suspect there are five or six buried in those six moves, including at least one place where they are talking past each other and one where their disagreement may be merely verbal. Then adjudicate each claim on its merits, saying which of them mischaracterizes the paper or the opponent's position and how. I'd particularly like your judgment on three things: whether the identity between the long-run frequencies of the weighted lottery and straightforward maximization is a real objection or a red herring, and what it leaves of the disagreement; whether Owen's restriction to claims of comparable strength genuinely escapes the migraine trade-off objection or simply concedes it under a new description; and whether the closing charge about needing an evaluative notion of worse is decisive or answerable. Finish by saying who was more right overall, and by naming at least one substantive consideration — about the difference between what an individual rescuer may do and what an allocator must do, or anything else — that neither of them raised.
