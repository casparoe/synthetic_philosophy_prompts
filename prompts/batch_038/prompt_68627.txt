Long shot, and fair warning: no degree here, I run a two-truck HVAC outfit and I read decision theory stuff at night because it scratches an itch. A few weeks back I finally ran into the absent-minded driver problem — the one where the guy is driving at night and has to decide at each of two exits whether to get off or keep going, but he can't tell the exits apart, and (as I remember it) getting off at the second one pays way more than getting off at the first, and blowing past both pays nothing. I follow the setup fine. What's got me stuck is the moral everybody seems to draw from it.

Here's my reading. Before he leaves, the driver plans to keep going at the first exit and get off at the second, and that's the best outcome available to him. But then he's actually at an intersection, foggy, no idea which one it is, and from inside that moment the math seems to tell him to get off right now — which works out worse overall. So the plan gets beaten by the judgment made in the moment, by a guy who knows less than the guy who made the plan. That's the part I want to apply, because my life is basically a stack of precommitments: the auto-transfer to savings that fires the morning after payday, the deal with my wife that I don't open the work group chat after seven, the standing rule that I don't buy a piece of equipment unless there's a job waiting for it. Every one of those was made by a calm version of me in order to constrain a tired, in-the-moment version of me who's standing in front of a shiny compressor. So: is the driver result a reason to treat those rules as advisory? Meaning, when I'm in the moment and something genuinely looks like a good idea, should I trust that read over the old rule, since the driver case seems to show the old rule is just a guess made by somebody who isn't actually in the situation? And if the answer is "some should hold and some shouldn't," what's the real test — I keep wanting to say it depends on how confused future-me will be, but I can't make that mean anything.

One more thing that bugs me. Every time this comes up it's bundled together with Ulysses tying himself to the mast, and with the claim that our preferences shift over time so present-me doesn't get to speak for future-me. Is the driver case just those two ideas with a math wrapper on them? Because if it is, I don't see whats worth all the fuss, and if it isn't, I'd like to know what's actually different about it — what makes the driver's predicament its own thing instead of a weak-willed sailor story dressed up.
