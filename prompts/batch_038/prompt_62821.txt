I am at work on a novel whose central character is a philosopher of mind. Her view of the self is Hofstadterian in lineage: the "I" is a symbol that models the system in which it sits, and the tangled loop between that symbol and the machinery producing it is what a self amounts to. I am not asking you to trace her position to any real philosopher's published view — she is mine, and the only constraints on her theory are the verdicts she has already delivered in the book:

- an adult human being: a self, fully;
- a newborn: not a self yet, a self under construction;
- a person in the late stages of dementia: still a self, thinned but not gone — she adds that it is held in trust by others;
- a thermostat: no self;
- two mirrors facing one another and the corridor of images between them: no self;
- a sentence that encodes a claim about its own unprovability: a genuine strange loop, but not a self;
- a narrator who is writing the novel in which he appears: a self, of another order of embodiment;
- a self-modifying program that maintains and revises a model of its own decision procedure: no self — a loop that runs for no one;
- a nation-state: no self, only a self-portrait.

In the chapter I cannot finish, she is asked about two people — a mother and a daughter, in my draft — whose self-models have become mutually constitutive: each carries an internalized model of the other's model of her, revises her own self-story only in light of it, and, when the two are separated, loses the ability to decide anything at all.

Predict her verdict. First, lay out the candidate principles her record supports — I expect at least three, and I expect at least one of them to be a conjunction of conditions — and say what each implies here, together with the precedent each has to strain or reinterpret to survive. Then commit: will she say two selves, one self, three selves (the two plus a joint one), or neither? Finally, judge which of the following would best separate the surviving principles, and whether her verdict on it would be forced by her record or genuinely open: (i) an immune system, which distinguishes molecular self from non-self with no symbol anywhere in the process; (ii) an archived copy of a mind, bodiless, with nothing it can lose, still writing its self-story; (iii) a pair whose mutual modelling runs one way only — one exhaustively modelled by another who is barely modelled in return.

I want the reasoning about principles, not a literature review: no citations, no reading list, no suggestions of where I might look. Close with your prediction on the final line in the form \answer{...}, containing exactly one of: two selves, one self, three selves, neither.
