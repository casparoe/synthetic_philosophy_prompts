**The question.** I work in laboratory medicine. Part of my job is maintaining the definitions our hospital network's systems run on, and I read philosophy of science on my own time. Over the past several years I have become stuck on a pattern I keep meeting and cannot name.

Here is the pattern. There is a term for some condition or group in the world; call it X. Everyone agrees that X is meant to be a real, worldly thing, the sort of thing that supports predictions: Xs have such-and-such outcomes, respond to such-and-such treatment, arise from such-and-such causes. But membership in X is decided by a rule — a checklist of tests, a numeric threshold, a set of criteria that a committee writes down and from time to time rewrites. And here is what I cannot get a handle on. When a case satisfies what everybody takes to be the underlying reality but fails the rule, the rule wins: the case is simply not X. Not "X that our test missed" — not X, full stop, in the records, in the statistics, in the guidelines. Yet when the committee later rewrites the rule and the new rule sweeps in cases the old one excluded, the same people describe the situation in the opposite register: we had been *missing* cases of X, our case counts were wrong, the reported outcome rate was wrong, the old rule was letting genuine Xs through the net. One community, one term, and in the first posture the rule settles what X is, while in the second posture the rule is a fallible window onto an X that was there all along. Nobody appears to notice that these two postures cannot both be right. And — this is what really puzzles me — the double use does not break anything. The term goes on licensing successful predictions right through several rewrites of the rule.

Some instances, from where I sit:

- A syndrome in critical care. Its standard definition has been rewritten two or three times within my career. Each rewrite changed which patients counted, and therefore changed the measured death rate for the syndrome, without anything whatever changing in the patients themselves. Reviews of the literature report figures from the earlier definition and the later one side by side without remarking that they are not about the same group. Throughout, the syndrome is treated unambiguously as a real clinical entity with a real pathophysiology, not as a bookkeeping convenience.
- Bacterial taxonomy. Whether two isolates belong to the same species is settled by a threshold on overall genome similarity. It is entirely possible for two populations to be ecologically and phenotypically continuous with one another and still fall on opposite sides of the threshold, and it is the threshold verdict that appears in the database and on the diagnostic report. At the same time, taxonomists argue about where to set the threshold in exactly the tone you would use about a measuring instrument that might be miscalibrated.
- The determination of death. There is a checklist; when it is satisfied, the patient is dead. That is not treated as the best available evidence that the patient has died but as what makes it so — the paperwork does not report a death, it constitutes one. Then someone points out that the checklist and the state of affairs it tracks come apart in certain cases, and the response is never "well, then our checklist is a poor test." It is either a defence of the checklist on the grounds that it gets the thing right, or a proposal to replace it with a better one. The two attitudes alternate freely.
- A smaller case in the same shape: a standing committee must approve a candidate before it counts as a distinct mineral species, and its decisions are reported both as naming conventions and as discoveries about the mineral kingdom.

The foil that makes me doubt this is just stipulation: when astronomers redrew the boundary of "planet," they said openly that they were drawing a line, and everyone understood that the line was a choice about how to use a word. In my cases nobody says that. The insistence is that the term is answerable to the world — and, remarkably, the insistence looks vindicated, because the term keeps working.

**What I want from you.**

1. Is there an established concept, or a small family of them, for the pattern I have described — in particular for the alternation between "the rule settles what X is" and "the rule can get X wrong"? I do not want a bare label. I want the carving: what exactly this phenomenon is, and what makes it a single phenomenon rather than two separate confusions standing next to each other.

2. For each of the nearest neighbours you can think of, say what it is actually called in the literature, what it covers, and where my description diverges from it. I have already half-considered several and found them unsatisfying, though I may have misunderstood them: the idea that a term simply means whatever procedure applies it, which would make our practice consistent but would then make the second posture a straightforward error, and it does not look like an error to the people performing it; the idea that a test can be a poor indicator of the quantity it is supposed to indicate, which describes the mismatch well but assumes the quantity is fixed independently, and so cannot accommodate the first posture at all; the idea that the boundaries of such terms are merely unsharp, whereas mine are precise, written down, and mobile; the pluralist idea that there are many equally legitimate ways to divide up a domain, which concerns competing schemes rather than one scheme held in two incompatible ways at once; and the idea that people are mistaking a construction for a thing in the world, which is a criticism and a one-directional one — my colleagues are not merely confused, they are doing something that demonstrably works.

3. If nothing established fits, say so plainly and coin a term. Give a definition, and then the boundary cases: two or three things that look like instances and are not, and two or three that do not look like instances and are. I especially want your view on whether the phenomenon requires that the kind be attached to a decision — a treatment, a legal status, a payment — or whether it can arise in purely descriptive science where nothing at all hinges on the classification, and if so, what it looks like there.

4. Give me your considered view on whether the double treatment is a defect or an achievement. If it is an achievement, what follows for the demand that the sciences carve nature at its joints? I would like to know what a theory of natural kinds must be able to say in order to leave this practice untroubled, and whether any theory that says it can also explain why the term's predictive success survives the rewrites of the rule that are supposed to have changed its extension.
