i TA a second-year early modern course and week six is Berkeley. the students mostly glaze over at esse est percipi — theyve heard the line, they think they know what it means, and the slides do nothing to disabuse them — but they always wake up at the bit where Philonous throws down his challenge: fine, he says in effect, if you can conceive of any sensible object existing outside the mind, I'll concede the whole dispute on the spot. Then Hylas tries, fails, tries again, fails again, and Berkeley treats the failure as decisive. Our prof calls it the master argument. I want to hand out a poem at the start of that seminar, something they can read in two minutes and then fight about for the rest of the hour.

Form: a pantoum, five or six quatrains, so around 20-24 lines — 2nd and 4th lines of each stanza becomming the 1st and 3rd of the next, with the last stanza closing the loop by bringing back the 1st and 3rd lines of the opening stanza, so the poem ends exactly where it began. That return is the whole point, i want the form doing the argument's dirty work. Each time a line comes back it should be carrying a slightly different sense of conceive: sometimes it's conceiving of the tree, getting the object in front of the mind at all, and sometimes it's conceiving that the tree goes unconceived, representing it as unperceived, which is a completely different achievement. The slippage between those two is where the argument actually breaks, and a reader should be able to put their finger on the stanza where it happens. Rhyme abab-ish if you can manage it, but the repetition matters more than the rhyme. Also don't use speech marks or dialogue tags — leave it ambiguous whose head each line is in, which is part of what makes the form work here.

Two things I want it to get right. First, Hylas can't be a dunce. He has a real objection, or the shape of one, and the poem should let him almost get it out before the form closes on him. Second, the missing premise — that whatever can't be conceived can't be — should be felt as a gap rather than announced. No moralising couplet, no narrator stepping in to explain the equivocation, and no de re / de dicto jargon in the verse itself, even though that's basically what's going on. Keep it inside 1713: a garden, a tree, two men talking, no modern references. I'll happily draw the connection to contemporary conceivability arguments in philosophy of mind myself afterwards — I think Berkeley is leaning on exactly the kind of move those arguments lean on, and that it's exactly as shaky — but the poem shouldn't wink at it. Give it a title.
