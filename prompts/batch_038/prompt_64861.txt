The question was worth 15 points:

**Question 3.** The usual way of telling the story of Buridan's ass goes like this: the animal is placed exactly between two equally attractive options, it can find no ground for preferring either, and so it starves. Two tasks. **(a)** Say why that story, told that way, is a bad argument rather than a genuine paradox. What exactly does the case put in doubt — that choice requires reasons, that exact indifference is possible, that a rational agent cannot act without preferring, or something else? Pick one and defend the pick. **(b)** Section discussed a variant. An automated disaster-response unit has one rescue drone left and ten minutes of usable window; its own utility model rates saving the occupants of building A and saving the occupants of building B as exactly equal, and it cannot do both. An engineer proposes hard-wiring a random bit to break the tie and says this dissolves the philosophical problem entirely. Evaluate that: what does the random bit settle, and what, if anything, is left unsettled?

Here's the student's answer, unedited except that I fixed the line breaks:

> (a) The argument is bad because it assumes that acting requires a preference, and that assumption is false. Decision theory doesn't require a strict ranking. When two options are tied, both are maximizers of expected utility, so both are permissible, and the ass is not rationally required to stand still — it is required to eat, since eating from either pile is at least as good as starving. Starvation only follows if you add a premise the story never states out loud: that an agent may act only when one option is strictly better than the others. Once you see that premise is doing all the work, the paradox evaporates. So what the case puts in doubt is whether reasons are needed for choice. It doesn't show that they are; it shows that a particular demanding picture of reasons, on which every choice must be determined by the stronger motive, is what generates the starvation. I'd add that the deeper issue is incommensurability. The two piles are not equally good in any positive sense; they are the kind of options between which no comparison is possible at all, so there is no fact of the matter for the ass to track, and that is what makes the case feel paradoxical instead of merely odd.
>
> Historically this is where Leibniz comes in. He used the ass to argue that the principle of sufficient reason fails for choices, since if everything that happens has a reason, and the choice of one pile over the other has no reason, then the principle must be false of the will. Buridan's own view was that under exact equality the will can only suspend judgment until circumstances change and the better option becomes visible, which is basically the ass dying, and it's why later writers made fun of him.
>
> (b) The random bit settles the practical problem and part of the theoretical one. Practically, the unit now always launches inside the window, so nobody dies of indecision and the engineers have an algorithm that terminates. Theoretically it also supplies the missing sufficient reason: the drone goes to A because the bit came up 1, and that is a real physical event with a complete causal explanation, so the choice is no longer arbitrary in the way the ass's would have been. Importantly this doesn't damage anything in decision theory either. Allowing ties doesn't break transitivity or any other axiom; it just means the maximizing set has two members.
>
> What's left over is mostly bookkeeping. Somebody has to explain to the families in building B why the drone went to A, and "a coin flip" is a bad explanation even when it's the true one, so there's a legitimacy cost the random bit doesn't touch. There's also the repeated-case problem: randomizing across a hundred ties in a year is fine, but if the unit meets the same tie twice it should arguably not randomize identically. Beyond that I think the proposal succeeds. The one thing I'd push back on is the temptation to conclude that the utility model is broken because it returned a tie. That's backwards — a tie is a perfectly good output of a well-formed model, and re-weighting the model until A and B come apart would just be smuggling in a preference the evidence doesn't support.
