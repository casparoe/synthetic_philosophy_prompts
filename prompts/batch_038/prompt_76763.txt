I run the awards program for a UK-registered charity (I'm American, which is why I'm writing this in one spelling and asking for the other — please answer in British English, everything we publish uses it).

Setup: about 300 nominations a year for 15 awards. The shortlisting panel is six volunteers with no philosophy training and four hours total, so roughly ten minutes per nomination they actually look at. The board ratifies whatever we send up and doesn't second-guess the form. The form is the whole process.

Right now it scores outputs — hours, money raised, people reached — and we hand awards to the top scorers. Twice recently we've honored someone and felt awful about it: enormous good done, but transparently as a networking exercise or a guilt tax. And we've passed over people whose work was smaller and whose reasons looked completely clean, and a panelist said afterwards that we'd had it backwards, that an award for this is supposed to be about why someone acted and not how much good fell out of it.

So I want to rebuild the form around the question of whether the nominee acted for the right reasons. I know this drags in a theory of moral worth — acting from duty versus merely acting in line with it, whether someone has to be moved by the thought that the act is right or only by whatever makes it right, whether sympathy, or a person's own recovery, counts as the right kind of motive at all. Fine. But my panel can't read theory and I can't make them. I need whatever that debate actually implies, translated into a scoring procedure six volunteers can run on a two-page nominaton in ten minutes, that produces something tallyable, because we do tally.

What I need:
- the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and what to enter when the nomination is silent — which is most of the time;
- which distinctions from the literature you're keeping and which you're dropping as unusable at ten minutes a head, and why;
- how the form handles the nominee who does a lot of good for bad reasons and the one who does modest good for good reasons, since they're competing for the same fifteen slots;
- the point where the form stops and a person has to decide, marked explicitly.

Two things I want you to be blunt about rather than paper over. First, motives aren't observable, and everything the panel sees is second-hand prose written by someone who likes the nominee. The procedure can't be a motive detector, so tell me what it can legitimately be instead and where it'll generate confident nonsense. Second, weve got a self-undermining problem: our guidance for nominators is public, so people increasingly write to the criteria, and some of the most motive-flattering nominations we recieve are obviously reverse-engineered from last year's winners. Build that in rather than treating it as an edge case — I want a form that behaves sensibly when the evidence about motives was produced by the form itself.

Then run it on three nominations I'll sketch: (1) a lawyer who's done a very large amount of pro bono work and whose nomination says frankly that it started as a way to build a client base and continued because he turned out to be good at it; (2) a woman who runs a small bereavement group after losing her own son, whose nomination is entirely about her warmth and says nothing about principles or duty; (3) a teenager who organized a big food drive because his youth leader told him it was the right thing to do and he wanted her approval. Give me the scores. I expect the procedure will produce a verdict I hate — probably ranking (1) under (3), or clearing (3) outright — and if it does, tell me whether the form is wrong or my instincts are.

And if the honest conclusion is that the form should stop pretending to read motives and measure something else entirely, say so and say what.
