Last weekend I had a four-hour argument with a close friend about Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, and I came away unable to say which of us had the better of it. He is sharper than I am and reads more widely, but he has never done academic history or social theory, and my own preparation amounts to the opening chapters of The Protestant Ethic in translation plus a couple of overviews. Neither of us was arguing from secure ground. Afterwards I set his position down as carefully as I could, in five claims, trying to keep his reasoning intact rather than reduce it to something easier to attack:

1. Weber's argument is that the Calvinist doctrine of predestination left believers with no way of knowing whether they were among the elect; that ministers responded by teaching them to read success in a worldly calling as evidence of election; and that this supplied the motive to work methodically, restrict consumption, and reinvest. That, on his account, produced the ethos Weber calls the spirit of capitalism, and it is how capitalism got going in the parts of Europe where it got going.

2. So the thesis is a claim that ideas cause material outcomes: culture is the independent variable, the economy the dependent one. Weber is offering a rival to Marx with the direction of the arrow reversed.

3. Weber understood that the thesis could not be tested, and that is why he surrounded it with the apparatus of ideal types and interpretive understanding. Ideal types are not hypotheses; they are instruments for organizing a picture of a period, and nothing organized in that way can be confirmed or disconfirmed by evidence.

4. This is not a contingent weakness but a necessary one. Beliefs and values cannot be causes. A norm or a meaning is not the sort of thing that can stand in a causal relation to an event; it can only be a reason an agent has, and reasons make an action intelligible from the inside rather than pushing it from behind. Any explanation that cites culture is therefore either a covert appeal to agents' reasons dressed up as causation, or an appeal to something non-cultural altogether — incentives, geography, technology, demography, institutions — that is doing the real causal work while culture supplies the vocabulary.

5. It follows that the Protestant ethic debate was never a genuine dispute. A century of argument over whether Calvinism produced capitalism has been an argument about whether something unintelligible happened: on one side people who think values explain conduct, on the other people who think they do not, and both confused about what would have to be the case for either claim to be true.

I want an accuracy audit I can take back to him, and I would rather it be blunt than diplomatic. For each of the five claims, tell me what a serious scholar of Weber would accept as a fair paraphrase, what is a defensible simplification for a general reader, and what is a distortion or simply false — and wherever the distortion consists of running together two things Weber kept distinct, say what the two things are. I am least confident about claim 3, since I have a recollection of Weber engaging empirical objections at length and revising heavily in response to named critics, which sits awkwardly with the idea that he designed the thesis to be immune to evidence; and about claim 1, since I suspect his account of the psychological mechanism is stranger and less instrumental than my friend's version, and that Weber was quite explicit about not offering a one-sided idealist replacement for a one-sided materialist history. Please also say which of the five is load-bearing: if he surrendered one, which surrender would cost him the rest. And where your own corrections rest on a detail of Weber's texts or of the secondary literature that you are not confident of, flag it rather than writing smoothly past it.

Then take up claim 4 separately, because it is the move that persuaded me in the moment and now strikes me as carrying more weight than it can bear. Is the thesis that reasons cannot be causes a settled result, a contested position, or a minority one? If it is contested, give me the strongest case on each side, and say whether claim 4 requires the strong version or whether a weaker one would suffice for the argument my friend wants to make. Finally, and this is the part I most want settled: suppose claim 4 is right. Does claim 5 follow? My intuition is that it does not, because the historical dispute has largely been conducted in ordinary empirical terms — the occupational and wealth distributions of Protestants and Catholics in confessionally mixed regions, literacy and schooling, the timing of commercial and industrial development in particular towns and provinces, and in recent decades large quantitative comparisons of regions that differ in confession while holding other things roughly constant — and I cannot see how a thesis about the metaphysics of reasons and causes would render all of that beside the point. Tell me whether that intuition is sound or naive. If claim 5 does not follow, say what is actually left in dispute once the misstatements are stripped out, and what it would take for one side of that residue to win.

I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I will chase anything down myself. What I need is an account accurate enough that I could defend it to him, and one that keeps three things apart that I think our argument kept collapsing: Weber's actual view, the philosophical question whether values or meanings can figure in causal explanation, and the historical question about what happened in early modern Europe.
