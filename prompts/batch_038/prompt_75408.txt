I prepare briefing material on democratic innovation for a parliamentary committee, and I keep being asked to explain why anyone thinks a body of randomly chosen strangers deserves a hearing. I would rather understand what the practice itself already assumes. Please reconstruct the implicit political theory of the citizens' assembly, as it is actually run.

Here is the device, described as concretely as I can. A legislature or head of government poses a question — constitutional reform, climate policy, an issue too hot for the parties. Invitations go out by mail or telephone to tens of thousands of residents; a few percent accept. From those who accept, somewhere between sixty and one hundred and fifty people are drawn at random, subject to quotas so that the final panel mirrors the population on sex, age, region, education and sometimes social class. Members meet over a run of weekends, are paid expenses or a stipend, receive childcare and travel, and are not individually named in press coverage. They hear from experts and stakeholders selected by an independent steering board, then deliberate at facilitated small tables, then vote in plenary on recommendations. The output is advisory: the government undertakes to respond publicly to each proposal, accepting, amending, or setting it aside, and may put the package to a referendum of the whole electorate. The body then dissolves; there is no re-selection, no recall, no accountability afterwards. Real instances include the Irish assembly that considered abortion, the French convention on climate, and a small German-speaking region of Belgium where a standing council of randomly chosen residents now sets the agenda for further assemblies.

One detail from the Irish case has stuck with me. The draw was carried out under contract by a polling firm; a substantial number of the original members withdrew before the first working weekend and were replaced; and when it emerged that several replacements had been recruited through the acquaintances of an employee of the firm rather than at random, they were removed. Nobody suggested that the substance of the deliberation had been affected. The randomness itself was treated as something that had to be repaired.

So: what view of political legitimacy does this apparatus presuppose? Distinguish at least three candidate rationales — that the panel is legitimate because it descriptively mirrors the population, that it is legitimate because cognitive diversity among non-experts produces better answers than expertise alone, and that it is legitimate because it is immune to parties, donors and re-election — and tell me which features of the design each rationale strictly requires, which it merely happens to fit, and which features none of them explains. Then assess coherence. The quotas treat members as standing for groups, while the anonymity, the facilitated discussion and the invitation to change one's mind treat them as individuals answerable to no one. The refusal bottleneck means the panel mirrors only those willing to surrender several weekends. Randomness governs the composition of the body but not the question, the witness list, the facilitation, or the government's response — all of which remain with the sponsor. If the microcosm's judgment is genuinely representative, why submit it to a vote of the macrocosm; and if the macrocosm's vote is what decides, why convene the microcosm at all? And how does the practice square with the same states' insistence that judges be appointed meritocratically and that anything with binding force be traceable to an election?

Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposition were swapped: what the assembly would look like if legitimacy rested on epistemic performance rather than resemblance, and what it would look like if the body were given decision-making power rather than influence. Mark clearly where the practice is underdetermined — where several rival theories license the same design — and where it quietly forecloses a view its sponsors would not endorse. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or a survey of who has argued what; use technical vocabulary only where it saves time, and no quotation marks unless you are certain of the wording.
