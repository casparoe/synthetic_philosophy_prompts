ok so I've been doing a kind of gut-check on the great man vs. structure question, writing down what I'd actually say case by case, and the list is kind of embarrassing. Would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler? Here's where I land:

- Hitler dies at the Somme, or of the flu in 1918: Germany still gets an authoritarian revanchist right in the 1930s. Versailles, hyperinflation then deflation, the stab-in-the-back myth, a völkisch antisemitism that was already all over the place — the slot get filled.
- But that regime doesn't invade Poland in '39, or not then, and not with that timetable.
- And it doesn't build extermination camps. This one I feel really strongly about.
- Except, and this is the awkward part: Germany had already carried out a genocide in South West Africa before the first world war, no Hitler required, so the capacity and even the template were sitting right there. I want to say 'no Holocaust without Hitler' and 'genocidal Germany was structural available' in the same breath and I can't see how both are true.
- Stalin feels different to me: forced collectivization and the end of the NEP happen without him, but the 1937–38 terror at that scale doesn't.
- The atomic bomb gets built by whoever has the industry to build it; the physics wasn't person-dependent. But the decision to use it on cities I do think tracks the particular occupant of the slot.
- Napoleon: if he dies at Toulon, France still gets a military expansionist republic-that-becomes-an-empire, so replaceable — but nothing replaces the actual 1805–1812 run.
- Meiji Japan: the domain coalition was structural, yet the sheer speed of abolishing the domains reads to me like a personal thing.

My tentative principle is roughly: structure determines *that* something happens and roughly when, individuals determine its form and content, so a person is irreplaceable only where form and content are doing independant causal work rather than just filling a slot. But when I apply it to Hitler it comes apart, because the form and content I want to call his — Lebensraum, racial rather than merely religious antisemitism, the party as a leader-movement — were all already in circulation; he just welded them together better than anyone else available. And I've noticed my verdict flips depending on how specifically I describe the thing I'm asking about: 'fascist Germany' looks overdetermined, 'the Third Reich exactly as it unfolded' looks like it needs the man. Which makes me worry I'm not tracking history at all, just my own choice of description.

Can you state a principle — or two or three rival ones — that actually delivers these verdicts, or delivers most of them without becoming baroque? For each clause, tell me which case above forces it. Then tell me what it says about cases I haven't touched: the Cultural Revolution without Mao, the New Deal without FDR, calculus without Newton or Leibniz, and something where the individual seems to matter for dumb reasons, like one engineer's decision to sign off on a design. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts it has to overturn. I'd rather give up one than end up with a principle that just says 'the individual matters whenever I feel like saying so.'
