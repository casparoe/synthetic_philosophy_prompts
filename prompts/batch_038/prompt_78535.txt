I run the modeling group at a mid-size city's water and wastewater utility. Twenty-five years of hydraulic models, capacity reservations, level-of-service standards, connection charge schedules, consent decrees, the whole kit. That's how I actually think, and I'm fluent in it — I'm not looking for a plain-English version of anything.

Our council is fighting a state bill that would override local single-family zoning near transit corridors, and I got tapped to write the utility's comment letter. Somwhere in that process I started reading the housing people and the right-to-the-city people — Lefebvre mainly, plus the more recent arguments about who has a claim on the city — and I hit a wall: I can't get any of it into my professional vocabulary, which is annoying, because I suspect a big chunk of the local fight is about things my vocabulary could express if I let it.

So here's the job. Translate the anti-density position, the pro-density position, and the Lefebvrian critique of both, into network terms — peak versus average load, firm capacity, marginal cost of the next connection, level of service, redundancy, rate base, who already paid in, design standards and who gets to set them. Existing residents as incumbent ratepayers. Neighborhood character as ... whatever it actually is in that vocabulary, which is part of what I want you to figure out. Do it one position at a time and as faithfully as you can, without softening the ones you happen to disagree with.

Then the residue report, which is the part I care about:

Which claims came across cleanly, without loss?

Which ones only rendered by smuggling in an assumption? I have a specific worry here. Every time I write the word capacity I'm quietly asserting that the thing being rationed is a fixed quantity with a technically determined limit — and that's exactly what the anti-density side asserts and exactly what the pro-density side denies, so my own word choice picked a winner before the argument started. Show me where the translation does that, in either direction.

Which claims have no counterpart at all? Lefebvre's idea that the city is a collective production rather than a delivered service — can a water utility represent that even in principle? There's no field in my model for the users rewriting the design standard, because in my world the standard comes down from a state agency and the users sit downstream of it, literally. Is that a gap in my framework or a sign the claim is vaguer than it sounds?

Finally, give me a verdict and be blunt about it. Either my framework is missing something real that the housing debate is tracking, or the debate is mostly a claim about who sets the standard and who bears the marginal cost dressed up in a way that makes it feel unresolvable. I can't tell which, and I'd much rather find out my letter is philosophically naive now than have it be naive and confident in front of the council.
