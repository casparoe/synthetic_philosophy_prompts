I teach an interdisciplinary seminar on collective action and environmental policy — eighteen students, mostly juniors and seniors, roughly half economics majors and half philosophy or political science — and I have one seventy-five-minute session in week five to spend on common-pool resources. Earlier in the term I ran the standard linear public goods game with them (private endowment, voluntary contribution, a 1.6× multiplier, equal redistribution) and it went smoothly but left the discussion bloodless: they solved it, free-rode in a principled way, and then talked about it like an arithmetic exercise. For this session I want something with considerably more grip, and I want the structure of the problem to be discovered rather than announced — nobody should hear the phrase "tragedy of the commons," or the names Hardin or Ostrom, until the final ten minutes. What I want them to carry out of the room is the sense that any collapse they produce was generated by the choice situation and not by their characters, since most of them will have sincerely wanted to conserve, and that whether it happens turns on institutional details: whether withdrawals are anonymous, whether the group is permitted to talk, and whether the rule they play under is one they wrote themselves or one handed down from outside. The payoff has to be felt but cannot involve cash; I am willing to convert tokens into a modest amount of extra credit or into some status prize, but tell me which you would pick and why, and tell me honestly whether the activity needs any conversion at all or whether the in-round stakes will do the work on their own.

Here is the shape I have in mind; I want you to supply the numbers, tighten the rules, and tell me where my design is wrong. Divide the class into three groups of six. Each group manages a shared renewable stock — a lake, a fishery, whatever you prefer — which starts at some level and regenerates between rounds by some fraction of what remains, subject to a hard ceiling. The feature I care about most is that regeneration is conditional on the stock staying above a threshold somewhere near the middle of its capacity: above it the stock recovers, below it regeneration ceases permanently and the group plays out its remaining rounds on a dead lake. Participants are told that a threshold exists and roughly where it sits, but not where exactly — give them a range rather than a point, and say how wide the range needs to be for the uncertainty to bite without turning the session into a coin toss. Add two asymmetries I think matter: players hold different extraction technologies, so that a unit of effort yields two, one and a half, or one unit of harvest depending on the role, and players' private valuations of the harvest differ, which should make an equal split of the take feel like a windfall to some and a robbery to others. Between rounds each group gets a short window in which it may do any of three things: talk freely; draft and ratify a rule of its own devising, which I will enforce if the group adopts it unanimously; or vote to buy an off-the-shelf enforcement package from me — a flat levy on every withdrawal plus public posting of each individual's take — at a price in tokens. I want the self-written rule to be expensive in time and the purchased package cheap in tokens but fixed in parameters nobody chose, so the two are in genuine tension rather than one obviously dominating. Six rounds or eight, you decide, with a justification for the number.

What I need back is a complete, runnable plan: the exact parameters (starting stock, regeneration rate and ceiling, threshold range, harvest technology by role, per-unit payoffs, price of the enforcement package, number of rounds, and phase timings to the minute), a materials list, the precise wording of everything participants are told at the outset including how you describe the threshold without giving it away, and a debrief that maps particular moments from the run onto particular concepts in the order the concepts should be named. The debrief should also work in reverse: once the phrase is on the table, I want them to see where the popular version of the idea is simply false about the case they just lived through — that a common-pool resource governed by communal rules is not the same object as an open-access free-for-all, and that the group which wrote its own rule may well have outperformed the group that bought mine. Say plainly what your design cannot capture about real commons governance that I will have to supply by talking; I suspect it has something to do with the length of the relationship and with the fact that my players know they are walking out of the door at the end. Then tell me how this will go wrong on a first run, in what order, and what to do about each instance — the group that colludes within ninety seconds, the group that collapses in round two and spends the rest of the session bored, the economist who reverse-engineers the threshold and announces it to everyone, the student who refuses to accept a sanction they voted against. Finally, identify which of my own assumptions the plan is resting on. I am assuming, for instance, that permitting groups to communicate raises cooperation, and that an externally imposed enforcement scheme can underperform a home-grown one; I want you to name what I would have to observe in the room, or what argument I would have to find persuasive, to abandon either assumption or redesign around it, and to single out the one load-bearing choice in the whole design together with what happens to the session if that choice is wrong.

For format: after the plan, give me a run-sheet table with the columns phase | clock (minutes elapsed) | what participants do | what the facilitator says or does | intended experience | most likely failure and on-the-spot fix; and then a short JSON object containing one entry per design parameter, each with the fields parameter, value, why_this_value, load_bearing (boolean), and what_changes_if_wrong.
