okay, kind of a weird request but here goes. I co-teach a small seminar on the history of letterforms, and the question we keep circling back to all term is what every 'a' actually has in common — there's the two-storey roman a, the one-storey a of italics and of most people's handwriting, the fat pinched blackletter a, the a in a cheap 8 pt newspaper face and the same a redrawn for a 96 pt display cut. Almost none of them share much in the way of shape, and yet nobody in the room thinks they're different letters. For the last day I want a poem to hand out that makes that point without arguing it.

Form: a pantoum, five or six quatrains, and I want the form to do the arguement — the lines that get recycled are what survives every re-rendering, the new lines are the variation, and the closing stanza finally pulling back the first stanza's two leftover lines should feel like the answer arriving only at the very end. Speaker is your call, but I like either the letter itself, tired of being redrawn, or a punchcutter cutting the same glyph again for a diffrent point size and knowing it has to change in order to stay the same.

I'd like each stanza to sit in a different moment of the letter's life — cut in stone, written by a scribe, cut as a steel punch and struck into a matrix, set in metal type, drawn on a grid for phototypesetting, hinted at low resolution on a screen — but feel free to merge or drop a couple; the history is mainly there to give the repeated lines something new to return into each time.

Keep the type vocabulary honest: counter, aperture, bowl, weight, x-height, serif, the roman/italic difference. A working type designer should be able to read it and not wince at a single line.

Hard constraints: no philosopher names, and no 'essence', no 'identity', nothing like 'platonic form' or 'family resemblance' — if someone who has thought about categories-as-roles-rather-than-shapes reads it, they should get it purely from the machinery, and someone who hasn't should just find it a good poem about letters. No moral, no wink at the end, no stanza that steps outside to explain itself. Around 24 lines total.
