Week six of my grad seminar on the ethics of autonomous systems, and I've got a CS postdoc auditing who has one line he wheels out every time we drift toward value theory. Roughly: 'you don't need to get the values right, you just need the thing to leave the world as it found it.' Its become his universal solvent. A fortnight ago a student asked whether you'd have to settle something about human flourishing before you could safely deploy an agent with real-world reach, and he waved it off — the point, he said, is that you penalise side effects and let the system chase whatever task you handed it, so the metaethics is beside the point. He's not stupid and and the line isn't nonsense, but I'm quite sure it isn't one claim, and I want to walk in next week able to say which claim he actually needs.

So: give me the menu. Take that sentence apart into the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — readings on which it's near-trivial, readings on which it's substantive and worth defending, readings that are simply false, and (I suspect this is the biggest bucket) readings that sound like they carry content and turn out not to. For each, say what kind of thing it's a claim about: a choice of baseline, a measure over states, a set of variables held fixed, a counterfactual comparison, a claim about what 'side' is doing in 'side effect', a claim about opportunity cost, whatever it is. Where a reading only survives thanks to some technical patch — reachability, attainable-comparison, offsetting, a default-policy baseline — say what that patch has to assume, and whether the assumption is itself value-laden. Then tell me straight which reading, if any, licenses his dismissal of my student's question, and whether the reading that does the work is one he'd want to own in a room full of philosophers.

No bibliography or references please, I know the literature; I want the analysis itself, in labelled entries I could hand out as a seminar sheet, with verdicts rather than hedged summaries of positions. And finish with the part people usually skip: which of these verdicts are you least confident in, what argument or consideration would flip them, and where do you think your reasoning above is most likely to actually fail rather than merely be incomplete?
