I spent thirty-four years running a mass spectrometry lab, mostly isotope ratios in groundwater, and since retiring I've joined a Tuesday-evening philosophy circle at the public library, which is a lot of fun and occasionally makes my head hurt. Last week we were on probability and rational belief, and it turned into a proper row between two of the regulars, Ruben and Dana, over our newest member Nils, who does landscaping and is not a numbers person at all. Nils said at one point he'd put it at about 60% that the town's new flood barrier gets finished before the autumn rains, and then, ten minutes later and without any irony, that he'd put it at 50% that it doesn't. Ruben, who is delighted by anything like this, immediately offered Nils two tickets at Nils's own prices: a $10 payout if the barrier is finished in time, for $6, and a $10 payout if it isn't, for $5. Nils took both. He's out $11 and collects $10 whichever way it goes, so he's a dollar down come rain or shine. Ruben said this is the real thing, not a thought experiment, and the two of them went at it for an hour. I have notes but I'm reconstructing the wording from memory, so treat everything below as close paraphrase, not quotation -- please don't go hunting for who said what in some published debate, and don't send me a reading list, I just want the reasoning worked through.

Here's the shape of the exchange, as best I can number it. (1) Ruben: there it is, a guaranteed loss, which proves the pair of numbers was incoherent, and shows that degrees of belief have to obey the probability calculus, additivity at minimum -- a demonstration, not a metaphor. (2) Dana: he'd have waved off the two tickets if you'd offered them together as one package; he only took them because you dealt them out separately. So the guaranteed loss came out of your sequencing, not out of his head, and there was never a moment when he was actually disposed to lose. (3) Ruben: sequencing is beside the point, same money ends up in the same place, and your own observation damns him further -- a man who refuses the bundle but accepts both of its parts has incoherent *dispositions*, and any two strangers who never meet could each take him for one component and jointly bleed him, so incoherent numbers and incoherent dispositions are one complaint in two hats. (4) Dana: money isn't utility and it isn't even linear for him -- Nils is careful with cash and judges each bet on its own. Take instead someone whose two numbers add up to exactly 1.0, perfectly coherent; because he evaluates each ticket in isolation and dislikes risk, he'll pay slightly *less* than the straight-line price for each, so you can sell him both at those slightly reduced prices and still bank a sure profit. Which means your trick works on coherent people too, so it can't be demonstrating incoherence -- all it demonstrates is that Nils is a cautious narrow framer. (5) Ruben: fine, run the whole thing denominated in utility instead of dollars and the objection evaporates; the book lands exactly when the numbers violate the axioms, and the dollar version was just a sloppy implementation of the argument. (6) Dana: no, you've smuggled the premise -- to switch into utility you already have to assume he evaluates bundles of gambles as a single integrated expected utility, and *that* assumption, not the probability axioms, is what produces the theorem; drop it and there are agents with impeccable probability assignments whom you can still take to the cleaners, so the argument is really telling us how to package decisions and only derivatively telling us about belief. (7) Ruben: and you keep leaning on the fact that a dollar is beneath Nils's notice -- if you're allowed to shrink stakes into the blur where he can't tell the difference, then no finite assignment is ever safe, since I can always scale the bets to sit inside his blur; so either the stakes go wherever his discrimination permits, in which case the theorem stands, or you keep the coarse discrimination, in which case you've abandoned the idea that belief has a precise numerical value at all, which is a far bigger concession than the one I'm asking Nils to make. One loose end neither of them ever picked up: nobody in the room pinned down what "finished" was supposed to mean -- signed off by the engineer, watertight, cranes gone -- and I suspect the two tickets weren't strictly on contradictories, though I couldn't get a word in.
