I am six months into the second draft of a novel whose third act turns on a prize draw. A failing literary quarterly, unable to pay its contributors, announces what its editor calls a Luring Lottery, openly borrowed from the one Douglas Hofstadter proposed in his *Scientific American* column in 1983: a reader enters by mailing a postcard bearing the number one, or by mailing a postcard bearing some larger number, in which case that number is the count of entries claimed; the purse — a million dollars, in my book as in the original — is divided by the total number of entries received and paid to the sender of one entry chosen at random. The recommendation the editor preaches from his editorial page is the superrational one: estimate how many readers are likely to respond, roll a die with that many faces, and mail a postcard only if it comes up one. As history had it, the thing dissolved; entrants mailed postcards claiming a googolplex of entries, the purse shrank to a fraction of a cent, and no winner could be identified. I have kept that collapse. What I cannot keep is the reasoning by which my protagonist arrives at her single postcard.

Vera is a proofreader, not a game theorist. She was taught ethics by her grandmother out of Kant, and the only moral vocabulary she possesses is the formula of universal law: act only on a principle you could at the same time will to hold for everyone. My philosophical consultant, reading the draft, told me not to worry on that account. Superrationality, he said, is the categorical imperative wearing a lab coat: universalize your maxim and you land where Hofstadter's superrational thinker lands, because both procedures require the agent to choose as though she were choosing for everyone in her situation at once. He believes this licenses two things. In the book, it licenses Vera's single postcard as not merely admirable but correct, so that the reader's sympathy for her is also an intellectual endorsement and her rival — the man who mails a card claiming ten to the sixth power entries — is not just greedy but wrong. In life, it licenses advice: a small press I publish with is running a homage contest on the same structure for a five-thousand-dollar purse, and my agent, who heard the lab-coat line at a dinner, has urged me to enter exactly once for exactly that reason.

So I should like the analogy audited rather than enjoyed. Begin with the mapping, entry by entry, and a table is welcome: what in Kant's procedure plays the role of the symmetry assumption that all superrational thinkers face the same problem and therefore reach the same answer; what corresponds to the payoff matrix, which Kant conspicuously never consults; what corresponds to the mixed strategy, the die roll that is the actual recommendation in the lottery; what corresponds to the standing requirement that one's opponents be superrational too. Then separate the breaks that matter for my two uses from the merely picturesque ones. I am especially curious about what the analogy imports from Kant that superrationality lacks — normativity, praiseworthiness, an ought that binds even when the others are fools or knaves — and about the places where the borrowing runs the other way, since the lottery's answer is stochastic and rests on an estimate of how many people will respond, which no formulation of the universal-law test seems able to accommodate. If the comparison reads better in reverse, with the lottery illuminating the imperative rather than the imperative justifying the lottery, say so and say what it then illuminates. Finally, and bluntly: does Vera's single postcard survive as correct rather than merely lovely, and does my consultant's advice to me survive? If the analogy cannot carry that weight, tell me what could — a better image for the relation between universalizing and superrational reasoning, or some other ground for Vera's card that a reader will accept without feeling cheated. Please argue from the structure of the two procedures themselves; I want the reasoning, not a reading list.
