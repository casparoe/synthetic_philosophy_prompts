So I'm reading a popular history of economic thought — the sort with chapter titles like "The Philosopher Who Hated Money" — and there's this passage on Aristotle that I want to sanity-check before I repeat any of it in a short review I'm writing for a department blog. It goes:

'Aristotle was the first thinker to ask why money exists at all. In the Nicomachean Ethics he argues that exchange presupposes commensurability: a house and a pair of shoes have to be measurable against one another before anyone can trade them, and nothing in nature makes them so. His answer is that money is a creature of convention, a token we agree to stand in for need — which is why, he points out, the Greek word for coinage is practically the word for law. Since its value is a matter of agreement rather than substance, Aristotle would have had no objection in principle to paper notes or to central-bank reserves; what he objected to was treating money as if it were worth having for its own sake, and above all to lending at interest, which he calls the most unnatural form of wealth-getting because it makes money breed money instead of doing what money is for. Everything else he says about commerce falls out of this: the merchant who tries to accumulate as much money as possible has mistaken the measuring rod for the thing measured, and a just exchange is one in which equal values change hands. Karl Marx, who studied this passage closely, took from it the seed of the labour theory of value.'
