I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a piece about a dispute that has outlived the data that started it: whether the human genome is mostly functional. I need an evaluation, not a survey.

Here is the background, so you have the shape of the fight without needing to go looking for it. In 2012 the ENCODE consortium reported that roughly 80 percent of the human genome participates in at least one detectable biochemical event — transcription into RNA, transcription-factor binding, a characteristic chromatin or histone modification. That finding was relayed, often by people who should have known better, as a claim that 80 percent of the genome *does something*, and the obituary for junk DNA was written several times over. A group of evolutionary biologists pushed back hard, on two grounds: that the consortium had quietly swapped an evolutionary notion of function for a biochemical one, and that population-genetic arithmetic — the mutation load a species can carry, and the substitution rates observed between primates — puts the genuinely functional fraction of the human genome closer to one nucleotide in ten. Underneath that exchange sits a much older argument in the philosophy of biology about what "function" even means, and the two camps in my story are, in effect, importing different answers to it.

I want to rank those answers by how well they would serve if a working biologist adopted one as the field's default definition of *functional DNA*. Score each of the six candidates below from 0 to 10 on that suitability, where suitability means all three of: (i) a biologist could apply it to sequence, comparative, and perturbation data without having already settled the question it is supposed to decide; (ii) it is non-trivial, in that it does not classify essentially the whole genome as functional nor essentially none of it; and (iii) it survives the two standard objections — the mutation-load objection, and the objection from clean deletions. Then rank them strictly, no ties, with the score doing the work of showing how close adjacent entries are.

The six candidates:

A. **Selected-effect function.** The etiological view associated with Larry Wright, Ruth Millikan, and Karen Neander: a stretch of DNA does Z if Z is among the effects of it that caused it to be retained in the lineage. Function is a historical fact about ancestors, not a present fact about the molecule. In practice this is operationalized by conservation and by signals of purifying selection in cross-species alignments.

B. **Causal-role function.** Robert Cummins's view: to ascribe a function is to slot a component into an analysis of some capacity of a containing system that an analyst has chosen to explain. Function is interest-relative and plural; the same element can have many, or none, depending on which capacity is being decomposed.

C. **Biochemical-activity function.** The operative ENCODE notion: a stretch is functional if it participates in a detectable biochemical event in at least one cell type or condition. No reference to history, no reference to fitness.

D. **Organizational function.** Reinhard Schlosser, and later Alvaro Moreno and colleagues: a component's function is its causal contribution to the self-maintenance of the organized system that sustains it. Selection history is not required, but neither is an arbitrary analyst's choice — the system's own closure fixes what counts.

E. **Dispositional / modal function.** John Bigelow and Robert Pargetter, refined by Bence Nanay: a stretch is functional if it has a disposition to contribute to the organism's reproductive success, whether or not that disposition is currently being exercised. Forward-looking where A is backward-looking.

F. **Functional credit.** This one is mine; please do not hunt for a source, there isn't one. Borrowing from the Shapley value in cooperative game theory: a stretch of DNA earns function credit in proportion to its average marginal contribution to fitness across all the ways the genome could be partitioned into retained and deleted parts. Redundancy therefore dilutes credit rather than destroying it, and function comes out intrinsically graded and comparative between elements rather than binary.

Three test cases I want every definition forced through, because they are where my readers' intuitions will actually be tested:

1. An ultraconserved noncoding element, perfectly identical between human and mouse, whose deletion in mice produces animals that are viable, fertile, and free of detectable abnormality on the usual panels of assays.
2. A low-level transcript produced from a young, species-specific repeat, present in no other mammal's genome, with no conservation signal and no assigned phenotype.
3. An enhancer assembled out of an ancient retroviral insertion that is now indispensable for gene expression in the placenta.

Deliver the whole thing as a single JSON object and nothing else, with exactly these fields:

- `ranking`: array of six objects, ordered by rank, each with:
  - `rank`: integer, 1 through 6
  - `candidate`: string, the letter and short name as I gave them
  - `score`: number, 0 to 10, one decimal place permitted
  - `estimated_functional_fraction`: number between 0 and 1 giving your estimate of what fraction of the human genome this definition would classify as functional, or `null` if the definition does not determine a number
  - `justification`: string, at most 60 words, explaining the score against criteria (i)–(iii)
  - `decisive_weakness`: string, at most 40 words — the single objection you think would be hardest for its proponents to answer
  - `test_case_verdicts`: object with the three keys `ultraconserved_clean_deletion`, `unconserved_young_transcript`, and `coopted_retroviral_enhancer`; each value a string of at most 25 words beginning with either `functional`, `non-functional`, or `indeterminate`, then a dash and the reason
- `closest_call`: string, at most 50 words — which two adjacent ranks you were least certain about, and what would flip them
- `collapsing_pairs`: array of objects, each with `pair` (two candidate letters) and `reason` (at most 40 words), for any candidates you think are not genuinely distinct definitions once they are applied to real data; empty array if none
- `one_i_would_defend_in_print`: string — the candidate you would be willing to have your name attached to
- `what_would_change_the_order`: string, at most 80 words — a specific result, distinction, or dataset that would reorder the list

Two constraints on how you work: give me no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations, since I cannot check them and they would not appear in the piece anyway; argue from the content of the definitions and the test cases. And where my candidate F is underdetermined as I have stated it — the Shapley construction is not obviously computable, and the partition space may be infinite — say so plainly in the relevant field rather than smoothing it over.
