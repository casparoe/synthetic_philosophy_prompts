I am a high school junior, and this year I helped run a prediction market for our student government. I have run into a pattern I cannot name, and I care much more about getting the carving right than about getting a label, so please do not stop at a term.

**The setup.** Our student council was deciding whether to replace our seven-period day with a block schedule of four longer classes. Rather than vote on the schedule, the council announced that it would adopt the change if, by a fixed deadline, the price of the yes contract finished above 65 cents in a market on a factual question: whether the share of students passing their end-of-year exams would be at least as high next year as it was this year. Trading used Council Credits, which students earn through attendance and club participation and spend in the school store on small perks. Credits could not be bought with real money, could not leave the school, and were also distributed in bulk to clubs and class organizations as part of their annual budgets.

**First observation.** For about two weeks the price drifted between 44 and 49 cents while people argued about evidence: what other schools had done, whether longer classes help or hurt students who struggle in the morning. Then, on a single day, it jumped from 47 to 71 cents. The only thing that had happened was that the senior class organizations released their credit endowments, roughly two thousand credits, and bought yes. Six days later it dropped to 58 cents when the freshmen and sophomore clubs pooled theirs and bought no. On neither day did anyone offer a new fact about exam results, and the students making the trades were candid that they were not reporting a belief. Several of them told me they had no idea what would happen to the pass rate and were simply spending what they had been given on the outcome they wanted.

**Second observation.** The same group, the same currency, and largely the same students ran a market a month earlier on whether our robotics team would place in the top eight at regionals. That price behaved completely differently. It fell from 70 to 52 cents the day a drive motor burned out, rose when a strong competing school withdrew, and closed within three cents of what the team's mentor had been estimating privately all season. Nobody had a stake in that outcome beyond bragging rights, and no organization held a large block of credits on it.

**What I cannot name.** Our council treated the first price exactly as it had treated the second: as a measurement, and as the reason for a decision. So a mechanism that was supposed to be forecasting the world ended up recording the strength and the budget allocation of the people who cared about the decision, and the decision rule then converted that record into policy. The troubling part is that nobody did anything dishonest. No one spread false information, no one traded to move the number in order to change someone else's mind, and every trade was sincere. The traders were, exactly as intended, expressing how much they wanted the outcome — and the mechanism read that as how likely the outcome was. I have been reading about futarchy, the proposal that governments should settle policy by letting people bet on outcomes and adopting whatever the favored market points to, and the distinction I keep seeing drawn there is between voting on what you value and betting on what you believe. Our market seemed to collapse those two into one channel without anyone noticing it happen.

**Near neighbors I have considered and rejected.** Goodhart's law, because nobody gamed the measure once it became a target; the measure was already contaminated before it became a target. Manipulation, because no one tried to deceive anyone and there was no strategic trading. The self-fulfilling prophecy, because the price did not bring about the outcome it predicted; if anything it may have made the outcome less likely, since passing depended on students' behavior, not their bets. The thin-market problem, because our market was thin in exactly the same way in the robotics case, and there it worked.

**The edges I want pinned down.** Would this have gone away if the credits were real money? If outsiders — students from other schools, parents, teachers — had been allowed to trade? If endowments had been equal, or capped, or non-transferable within clubs? And is the resulting price a forecast of anything at all — perhaps an accurate forecast of what a student vote would have produced, or of the intensity of support — such that the error lies not in the price but in the council's choice of what to read off it?
