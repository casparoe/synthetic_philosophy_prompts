A colleague in our philosophy-of-science reading group has circulated a short note defending scientific realism, and I want to audit its central analogy before we discuss it. Her argument runs roughly as follows. A criminal trial is inference to the best explanation with institutional scaffolding: the fact-finder is handed a fixed body of evidence together with two competing accounts of what happened, and she may convict only if the prosecution's account explains the evidence better than any alternative the defense can muster, better to a degree that leaves no reasonable doubt. The practice has a respectable track record, she says — its verdicts mostly track what actually occurred. Scientific inference, on her telling, is the same procedure applied to different subject matter: evidence on one side, rival theories on the other, and acceptance of whichever explains the evidence best. So if we do not doubt the reliability of the courtroom procedure, we should not doubt the reliability of inferring to the best explanation either; and the anti-realist point that our best theories have repeatedly turned out false, so that being the best explanation tells us nothing about being true, simply loses its force.

She anticipates the obvious objection — that the true theory may never have been formulated at all, so the best explanation on offer could be the best of a bad lot. Her reply is that the courtroom has a standing mechanism for precisely this: the defense, whose entire job is to raise the alternative nobody had thought of, and which cannot be silenced for having raised it. Science, she says, has the same mechanism in a competitive community of rivals, and it is that adversary structure, not any special virtue of explanatory goodness, that makes the inference safe. She adds that civil litigation makes the mapping even cleaner, since there the more plausible of the two stories simply wins.

I would like an assessment of the analogy itself rather than of her prose. Please work through the following.

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what in the trial plays the role of the evidence, the candidate hypotheses, the fact-finder, the threshold, the verdict, and whatever answers to appellate review — and flag the places where the mapping is already strained before any objection is raised. In particular, check whether the alternative to a prosecution narrative is really a rival narrative of the same kind, or something structurally different.

2. Say what the analogy quietly imports from the courtroom into science. I am most interested in structural features a trial has that a theory choice lacks: whether the set of verdicts a jury may return is exhaustive in a way that the set of formulated theories is not, what the rules of admissibility do to the notion of the best available explanation when the evidence is frozen in advance, and whether a threshold calibrated to distribute a specific kind of error is the same sort of thing as a claim to track truth.

3. Separate the disanalogies that do real damage from the decorative ones. Pointing out that jurors are laypeople while scientists are specialists, or that a trial runs for weeks while theory construction runs for decades, does not obviously threaten her conclusion; tell me which of the remaining differences genuinely block the transfer of reliability from the practice to the inference rule, and why the transfer was supposed to work in the first place.

4. Test her appeal to the adversary mechanism. Does an institutionalized opponent actually populate the candidate set with alternatives that would otherwise have gone unconceived, and does that role carry over to a rival research program, which has no duty to argue for hypotheses it thinks false? What happens, on her own picture, when the alternative nobody thought of is true?

5. Conclude. If her thesis does not survive, state the weakest claim about inference to the best explanation that the courtroom analogy can still carry — I expect there is one, but not the one she wants. And tell me whether a different analogy would do better work: someone in the group suggested that inferring to the best explanation is like a hiring committee choosing among applicants, where the committee's judgment can be impeccable and the pool wretched regardless. Does that capture the problem, or merely relocate it?

References are unnecessary; I want the reasoning worked out from the structure of the two practices, not from a survey of who has said what about them.
