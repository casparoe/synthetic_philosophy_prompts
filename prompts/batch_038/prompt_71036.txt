In about six weeks I'm giving a 30-minute paper at a small interdisciplinary migration-ethics workshop — fifteen people around a table, mostly political theorists, one empirical migration researcher who works on public attitudes, two from a law faculty, and a respondent who I've been told is pretty sympathetic to the Walzerian line. Sorry, this is going to be long, but I need the setup to be right. My paper reconstructs and defends something like the Dummettian position on admission, with a twist I should be upfront about: I don't ground the duty to admit in the interests of would-be migrants at all. My claim is that a democratic polity's duty to admit is first of all a duty it owes to itself — that closing the door is what corrupts the community, because it turns a people who profess to treat one another as equals into an association whose signature activity is policing a line drawn by birthright. And Dummett's diagnosis, as I'm reading it, is that most of the restrictionist case is racial and cultural anxiety wearing the clothes of prudence. I should say clearly that I'm doing construction here, not exegesis — I've never claimed to know what Dummett literally argued, so please don't spend the answer telling me what he really said; tell me what this room will say to me.

Here's the sketch the objections will be aimed at. First: a community's claim to exclude usually leans on the idea that membership is a good it made and can therefore withhold, but birthright membership isn't made, it's inherited, so the polity stands to it as custodian rather than owner. Second: custodianship of an arbitrary boundary, maintained over decades, is character-forming — it produces habits of suspicion, a legally two-tiered population inside the territory, and a self-understanding in which equal standing is conditional on an accident. Third: so the duty to admit isn't a matter of balancing migrant welfare against domestic costs; it's a condition on the polity remaining the kind of thing that can coherently claim to be a democratic community. The advertised upshot is that this duty is supposed to be robust exactly where the interest-based versions are fragile, since it isn't hostage to calculations about absorption capacity or wage effects. Weak spots I already know about: it looks like I'm admitting people for our sake rather than theirs, which reads as both paternalistic and kind of insulting; it's perfectionist, since the state is now in the business of shaping civic character; and I have nothing good to say about the refugee / economic migrant distinction, which my view seems to just swallow.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense of the paper. Give me the eight-ish objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each attributed to the type of person most likely to raise it and phrased in their vocabulary — the communitarian won't say "your argument is unfalsifiable," he'll talk about associative obligations, self-determination, and what makes a community more than a neighborhood; the empirical person will come at the racism diagnosis with survey evidence and ask how I distinguish xenophobia from ordinary worry; the Carens-flavoured liberal in the room will be the most irritating one, because she'll grant my conclusion and argue my premises do no normative work that the birthright-lottery argument wasn't already doing. For each objection: the one sentence I can concede without losing anything, a roughly two-minute reply, and a note on where that reply is softest. Then tell me what I should give away in the first five minutes so the room doesn't write me off as a zealot, and tell me the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the afternoon. Finally, name the question I'm praying nobody asks — the one I currently have no answer to. Two candidates I've already identified, so build them in properly rather than nodding at them: that a duty owed by the polity to itself gives the excluded person no standing to complain, so it isn't a right at all and can't ground anything a court or a minister could act on; and that my account can't tell you who to admit or how many, which makes it decorative for anyone who actually has to write policy.
