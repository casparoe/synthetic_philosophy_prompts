Compare two accounts of the contented unfree. The first is the Grand Inquisitor's, in the parable Ivan Karamazov recites: that Christ overrated humanity by offering free faith instead of accepting the three temptations he refused in the desert; that the mass of people cannot carry freedom of conscience and will surrender it for bread, miracle and authority; and that the Church which takes up that burden really does make them happy — a happiness the Inquisitor claims as an achievement, not as a consolation prize he is embarrassed by. The second is the idea, familiar from welfare theory, of adaptive preference formation: that people scale their desires down until they fit their options, so that reported satisfaction is a poor guide to whether a life is going well.

Both describe people who are content without being free, yet they appear to read that contentment in opposite directions. Set out precisely where the two converge and where they part company, and say whether the underlying disagreement concerns human psychology, the evidential weight of satisfaction, or what makes a life good. Then test both on a case: a long-term resident of a company town whose employer arranges her housing, schooling and medical care, who reports that she is satisfied and is free to leave at any time but has never weighed doing so. Does either account yield a verdict here, and if not, what would have to be added to it to yield one?

Please write your answer in British English.
