Last night my friend and I argued for the better part of three hours over whether griefbots — the commercial services that let you keep texting a simulation of someone who has died — are best understood as a technology of mourning or a technology of deception, and I have not been able to let the argument go. What kept coming back to me was not the ethics of it but the conceptual squeeze involved, which is where Dennett's thinking about counterfeit people seems to me most useful and most unstable. On one hand there is his long-standing view that attributing beliefs and desires is not a metaphysical luxury but the right explanatory stance toward anything whose behavior is well enough organized to be predicted that way, with the pattern in question being as real as anything else we call real. On the other hand there is his worry about systems engineered to be flawless targets of exactly that stance while, on his telling, having nothing behind it worth explaining — manufactured intimacy, deliberately produced dependency, no comprehension accompanying the competence. My friend's objection, which I could not answer cleanly, was that these two commitments pull against each other: if organized behavior is constitutive rather than merely evidential, then whatever makes a counterfeit indistinguishable from a person is what makes it a person, and no residue is left over for the worry to occupy. I think he is right that the tension is real, and I think it is genuinely funny, which is why I want a joke rather than another four thousand words.

I am doing a short set at an open-mic night hosted by the philosophy department: mostly graduate students, two or three faculty who have read Dennett seriously, and a handful of people who know him only as one of the more combative atheists. Write me a joke, or two competing candidates, whose humor turns on that tension. Concretely, the mechanism should exploit the fact that the intentional stance treats sufficiently well-organized behavior as enough for belief attribution, whereas the counterfeit-person worry needs something further — something about design history or provenance — to get any purchase at all. I want the joke to fail loudly for someone who does not know the distinction, and to be recognizably about the distinction rather than about chatbots in general; a good test is whether the piece would still work unchanged if I substituted the word chatbot wherever counterfeit person appears, in which case it has not earned its subject. Please also do not strawman either side. In particular, do not render the Dennettian position as naive behaviorism, as though passing a pleasant conversation were supposed to demonstrate understanding; his complaint about counterfeit people concerned engineered reliance and simulated reciprocity, not a missing inner light, and a joke that misses this will read to the faculty in the room as though I have only skimmed the summary.

A few formal constraints. Keep it tight: a setup of no more than four sentences and one punchline, with the reversal arriving in the final clause rather than being explained afterward. Banned material, on pain of my embarrassment: no Chinese Room, no Turing test staged as an interrogation or a beauty contest, no philosophical zombies, no two-robots-walk-into-a-bar openings, no punchline consisting of the word deepity, no punchline consisting of a language model's stock disclaimer about its own nature, and no pun on heterophenomenology. Do not put Dennett himself on stage as a character, since I would rather the joke be about the ideas than about a famous philosopher's opinions. If it helps, here is a seed to choose from and commit to: a showroom in which a company demos a companion model to a prospective customer; a quality-assurance session in which an engineer interviews the system about whether anyone is home; or a bereavement group whose members have stopped being able to agree which of them is the simulation. Finally, after the joke, add two or three sentences laying out the comic mechanism — which expectation the setup installs and which the punchline reverses — so that I can check whether you have the concepts right rather than merely the vocabulary. If you think the tension I described is a poor target and can propose a better one drawn from the same material, the difference between competence and comprehension being the obvious candidate, say so plainly and write that joke instead, holding the rest of these constraints fixed.
