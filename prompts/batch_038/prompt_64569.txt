Thirty-eight years of seawater chemistry behind me and now I'm retired with nothing but books, and I've fallen rather hard into the philosophy of history, which I know sounds odd for a man who spent his life titrating things.

Here's what's chewing at me. E. H. Carr, in What Is History? in 1961, was pretty withering about the whole might-have-been business — the old what if Cleopatras nose had been shorter sort of thing. He thought that was a game rather than history, and that real explanation came from the broad impersonal forces, with progress doing the steering. Reasonable enough, and he died in 1982 still holding it. But since then the counterfactual has more or less conquered the place. Fogel got a Nobel largely for working out what the American economy would have looked like with no railways at all; the statisticians now define a causal effect as the gap between the world that happened and the world where the treatment didn't; and the climate models I used to argue with are run as hundreds of counterfactual scenarios, and thats literally how the policy gets made. Nobody blinks at it.

So what would Carr make of that if he were sitting here now? I can see two answers and I can't choose between them. One: he'd say it's the same parlour trick with better arithmetic, because you still cannot observe the world that didn't happen, and dressing it in equations just smuggles back the individual-level storytelling he objected to in the first place. Two: he'd have to fold, because if every causal claim is a counterfactual claim, then his own talk of impersonal forces driving the outcome was counterfactual too, and his objection eats itself.

Which of those is truer to the man? And is there a third line available — something he could say that keeps the determinism and still lets the cliometrics through the door? Just the argument, please, I'm not after a reading list.
