I sit on the Vessen Transition Commission's Convictions Panel — three of us, and I'm the one who's supposed to circulate the first draft decision. Vessen is a made-up country, so nothing you've read about real transitions applies directly, but here's the setup. The old Directorate ruled from 1979 until it collapsed in late 2002. The enabling act the new parliament passed is deliberately narrow: we can annul an old conviction only if it "was unlawful under the law in force at the time it was entered." That's it. We have no power to declare old statutes invalid, and no power to apply new ones retroactively. Parliament gave us 180 days from our first sitting (we sat in March) and it's now month four.

Our first real case comes down Friday. In 1994, a radio repairman named Miro Halász told his neighbour over a fence that the Directorate's southern operation was going badly and that his own brother hadn't come back from it. The neighbour reported him. He was charged under Article 14 of the 1985 Public Confidence Act, which criminalised utterances "apt to undermine public confidence in the armed forces," and got five years. He served three, was released in the general amnesty of 1997, and died in 2019. His daughter is the applicant: she doesn't want the money, she wants the record wiped, and she wants the Commission to say in writing that her father broke no law that anyone was obliged to obey.

Two wrinkles in the file, and they cut in opposite directions.

First: Halász had been charged under the same article in 1988 and acquitted. The 1994 court invoked a "correction" clause in the Act permitting retrial where an acquittal was "obtained through concealment," and then said in its sentencing reasons that the earlier acquittal showed he had gotten away with it once and was therefore a standing risk. Nothing was concealed — the 1988 acquittal was for insufficient evidence and the file says so. So there's a straightfoward internal-legality argument available: on the Directorate's own published law the retrial clause didn't reach these facts, and the judge used a prior acquittal as an aggravating factor, which the sentencing provisions didn't authorise. On this route we annul without ever having to say Article 14 was not law.

Second: that route feels like a dodge to the applicant's counsel, and honestly to me. The 1988 acquittal shows the utterance was the same kind of utterance both times. What changed is that a neighbour reported it, and the reason a neighbour reported it is that section 22 of the same Act made non-reporting of Article 14 conduct an offence. Article 14 was enforced as written, on its face, against a man who said something true to one person in a garden.

Then there's the neighbour, Vera Kessler, now 81, who asked to file a statement. She says she reported because section 22 required it; that her son had been detained in 1989 for draft resistance and she was frightened for him; and that she thought the remarks were unpatriotic but not criminal. Her statement is oddly frank and does not ask for forgiveness. The daughter wants Kessler named in the decision. Two of my colleagues want her treated as a victim of section 22 and left alone; one wants her named so the record shows how the machinery actually ran.

What I need from you is the draft decision I'll circulate — recommendation first, then reasons, tight enough to read in twenty minutes. Specifically:

- Say whether we annul, and on which ground. If you use the internal route (the correction clause, the acquittal-as-aggravator), tell me honestly whether we can take it without implying that an Article 14 conviction where the trial was procedurally spotless has to stand. If you go the other way and treat Article 14 as not law at all, explain how that squares with the enabling act's own wording, which points us at the law in force at the time.
- Give me a rule the panel can apply to the ~2,900 applications behind this one. There are tax convictions and assault convictions from the same courts in the same years that nobody thinks should fall. Where exactly is the line, and does it survive contact with the 1988 acquittal detail?
- Address the strongest consideration on the losing side without strawmanning it: that a body like ours, annuling convictions because it disapproves of the statutes, is doing the thing we're criticising the Directorate for, and that the honest response to a wicked statute is compensation by new law rather than a fiction about what the old law was. Also the reverse worry — that money without annulment leaves an official finding standing that Halász committed a crime, which is precisely the harm his daughter is complaining of.
- Tell me what to do about Kessler, and whether the section 22 duty argument gets her anything.
- One paragraph on timing: the 180-day clock, and what we should say about applications where the applicant is elderly or ill.

Assume I know the general shape of the positivism-versus-natural-law quarrel and have read the famous mid-century exchange about grudge informers. Don't rehearse it at me — use it. The panel is split and my draft has to be something both camps can argue with rather than dismiss.
