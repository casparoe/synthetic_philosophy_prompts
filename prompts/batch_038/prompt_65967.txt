I retired three years ago after thirty-five years in experimental toxicology — dose-response curves, exposure windows, and the question of how much risk a person may properly be paid to accept — and I have since taken up philosophy seriously enough that my colleagues on the ethics board of our public fertility network now hand me the drafting. I would like your help with one such document.

Our jurisdiction permits oocyte donation against a capped compensation payment and prohibits any payment to a gestational carrier beyond verified expenses. Before the board is an application from a woman who has donated oocytes twice through our clinic under that capped schedule and who now asks to be paid the same capped sum to carry a pregnancy for an intended mother she knows. Half the board holds that the statute settles the matter and that we should decline without further argument. The other half holds that our own payment schedule commits us to the opposite result: we already pay a woman for a bodily procedure carrying real, documented risks, and if we decline to pay her for a pregnancy we are drawing not an ethical line but a bookkeeping one. For clarity, the board is also divided on whether the prohibition bars only payment or bars the clinic from facilitating an unpaid arrangement as well, and the document should say what we may and may not do on either reading.

Here is the thought I want the memo to test, and I should say plainly that it is my own and carries no authority. What separates the two cases, I suspect, is not commodification but whether the risk being sold is bounded. A donation cycle is short, quantified, terminates on a scheduled date, and can be abandoned at any point up to retrieval; a pregnancy is nine months of continuous bodily occupancy from which the carrier cannot withdraw without either terminating or surrendering a child. On that reading, the principle actually operative in our schedule is not that reproductive labour must not be bought and sold, but that only risks which can be priced and exited may be contracted for. If this is right it explains some of our intuitions and embarrasses others, and the honest upshot may be that we should either stop paying donors or stop describing the surrogacy prohibition as protective.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the board in advance of next week's meeting. It should reach a decision on the application — with conditions specific enough that the clinic could act on them, and with what happens next stated by when — set out reasoning that both halves of the board could actually argue against, and treat the strongest consideration on the losing side so fairly that its proponents would recognise it.

Two further requirements. First: no references, no bibliography, no list of authorities; I want reasons, and I shall be reading this on a train. Second, and this matters to someone trained to report uncertainty as uncertainty: mark clearly which of the premises you lean on are, so far as you can tell, broadly agreed among people who work on these questions — the descriptive claim, say, that a domestic prohibition relocates rather than reduces the practice — and which are live disputes with serious people on both sides. Where you are advancing a contested position, say so rather than letting it pass for settled. And if my bounded-risk proposal is weaker than I take it to be, show me exactly where it gives way.
