I work as a software engineer on a team spread across three countries, and I have come across a pattern for which I cannot find a name. I should like help working out whether an established concept covers it, and, if none does, settling on one.

Here is the pattern. In a design review, a colleague is asked whether the proposed architecture will hold up. He says that it will, that the approach is sound and the team should proceed. Everyone present knows it will not hold up: he knows it, the person who asked knows it, and each knows that the others know it. Nothing is concealed and nobody walks away with a false belief. Yet the exchange does real work. It records that the proposal has been heard, it allows the meeting to advance, and it spares the proposer a public loss of standing. Functionally the sentence is a protocol handshake — its payload is filler that both sides have agreed not to inspect, and its purpose is to acknowledge and to keep the channel open. For want of a better phrase I have been calling it a collusive acknowledgement, but that is my own coinage and I would rather know what the field calls it, if anything.

A second instance, from the cultural side of the same team. When I describe the exchange to my Dutch colleagues, the reaction is that the speaker lied, and that the right thing would have been to say plainly that the design was inadequate. When I describe it to my Japanese colleagues, the reaction is that he behaved properly and that saying so outright would have been gratuitously destructive. What puzzles me is that these do not look like two verdicts on a shared question. They look like a disagreement about whether the utterance was an assertion at all, or something else that merely has the shape of one.

I know a little of the neighbouring territory, and I may well have it wrong. I gather Kant argued that lying is impermissible even to a would-be murderer who asks after your friend, which suggests the strict view treats utterances like this as lies too. And I believe some philosophers hold that lying requires an intention to deceive, which would make my example not a lie at all, since nobody is deceived and nobody intends that anyone should be. I also know the everyday phrase for a harmless falsehood told to spare someone's feelings, but it seems to fit badly here, because the defining feature of my case is precisely its transparency: the falsity is out in the open rather than hidden.

So: does this configuration have a name of its own? Please treat the label as the least important part of your answer. What I actually want is the carving — what the phenomenon is, which established concepts come nearest to it, how it differs from each of those, and where its edges lie. At what point does a transparent, mutually collusive falsehood stop being this thing and become something else: a mere pleasantry, an evasion, an inside joke, a cover-up, or an assertion after all? If nothing in the literature picks out exactly this, say so directly, propose a term with a definition and boundary cases, and explain what work that definition does which the neighbouring terms cannot.

Two further requests about presentation. Please separate clearly what is settled among people who work on this material from what is genuinely contested, and do not let a contested position be reported as the consensus; where the field is divided, tell me which side you take to have the stronger case and why. And please write the entire response in British English.
