I've been working with a physicist friend who reads my drafts to keep me honest — the new book has a character who has to know in advance which of two things is going to happen, and the whole plot hangs on whether that's a fair thing for her to be able to do. Anyway, over a couple of beers last month he started rattling off verdicts about which processes he counts as random and which only look that way, and I wrote them down because I could tell they'd be useful. Here's the track record:

- A coin flipped by a really good flipper — same starting position, same force, trained to land it how he wants: not random at all, it's a deterministic machine that just hides its inputs.
- A casino roulette wheel: random for every practical purpose, but not genuinely random — get the release conditions to enough digits and the number's already written down.
- A double pendulum: same verdict, chaotic, but every bit of it determined.
- The weather nine days out: not knowable, and not just because we're lazy — not knowable by any finite observer.
- A deck right after a good shuffle: random, but only in the information-theory sense. It's ignorance, he said, not the world being unsettled.
- A quantum measurement of a spin along the axis you prepared it in: the outcome is certain, so not random at all.
- Same spin measured at right angles to that axis: this one he called the only genuine randomness there is.
- When a single radioactive atom decays: genuinely random, same category as the last one.
- The half-life of a big sample of those atoms: beautifully predictable, not random.

Two new systems I want to put to him, and I'd like to know what he'll say before I ask, partly to catch him out and partly because my character's situation is somewhere between them.

The first is a deterministic simulation he'd like — think a big array of coupled oscillators on a ring — where neighbouring states drift apart, but slowly: the gap grows like a power of time, not exponentially, so there's no butterfly effect in the technical sense. He's told me a perturbation of one part in a billion takes on the order of a million steps to become an order-one difference, and that to keep a forecast good out to a million steps you'd have to specify the starting state to a number of digits that keeps climbing the further out you want to go, without limit. No quantum mechanics anywhere in it, and it's cheap to step forward one step at a time.

The second is a little machine made of bits — rows of them, each row computed from the one before by a fixed rule. The starting row is written down exactly, all of it, no measurement error, nothing hidden, nothing quantum. But there's no compressed way to get at row one million except by actually generating all nine hundred ninety-nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine rows in between.

My hunch is that he'll call both of them random and I'll have caught him waffling, because the categories he's been handing out don't look like they can hold both. Maybe I'm wrong. So: tell me which of his past verdicts are actually doing the work, and lay out every rule you can build that fits all of them equally well — my guess is there's more than one, and that they've been hiding behind each other this whole time. Then say what each of those rules predicts for the two new systems, give me your best single bet on what he'll say about each one and why you'd bet that way rather than the other, and if his answers still leave some of the rules tied, describe one more system I could put to him that would break the tie.

Plain prose only, please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Write it like an email to a friend who's going to read it on a phone. And if it turns out my two new cases aren't actually the sharp ones I think they are, say that too and tell me which cases would be.
