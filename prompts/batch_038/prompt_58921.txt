I edit a small poetry magazine and, on the side, run a translation reading group at a community college in central Ohio. This term the group worked its way through Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot* (1997), the book organized around Clément Marot's short poem to a sick young lady at court and the many English versions of it that Hofstadter prints and annotates. The group split cleanly in two and never got past the split. One half concluded that in a poem of that kind the form is not a container for the meaning but part of the meaning, so a version that keeps the sense and drops the shape has not translated the poem at all — it has filed a report about it. The other half thought this was mysticism dressed up as rigor: a poem says what it says, rhyme and meter are a pleasure we should mourn but not chase, and any translator who spends three weeks hunting an English word that rhymes has already decided to be dishonest for a living.

To force the argument somewhere, I did something slightly unfair. I wrote a trifle myself, in French, in the manner of a sixteenth-century court epigram, and handed it to two translators — call them A and B — with opposite briefs. A was told to keep the architecture at whatever semantic cost; B was told to keep every element of the sense and let the architecture go. Each knew the other was working on the same poem, and each knew only one version would be printed. Both get the same flat honorarium, so this is not a question of favoritism, and the spring issue has room for exactly one. I have to choose, and I want an argument that will let me explain the choice to the half of the group that will be annoyed by it.

Here is the original, titled simply "Vol."

> Tu m'as pris le cœur : quel beau vol !
> Je ne te maudirai, ma belle ;
> car sans toi je ne suis que fol,
> et mon âme t'est bien fidèle.
>
> Nous marchions sur le même sol,
> tu parlais sans être cruelle,
> et je te suivais dans ton vol,
> comme on suivrait une hirondelle.

The formal facts, as I understand them — my French is school French and I would rather be corrected than flattered: eight octosyllabic lines in two quatrains, rhyming ABAB CDCD, and the poem never introduces a new rhyme sound, all the masculine rhymes falling in *-ol* and all the feminine ones in *-elle*. The hinge is that "vol" closes both the first line and the seventh. After "tu m'as pris le cœur" it can only mean theft; before "comme on suivrait une hirondelle" it can only mean flight. My claim is that the identical sound in the identical position is what tells the reader that the theft and the departure are one event, and that this is not a decoration the poem happens to carry but the thing the poem is doing.

Rendering A, titled "Flight":

> You took my heart and took to flight;
> I shall not curse you, being fair.
> For without you I am not right,
> and my soul keeps faith with you there.
>
> We walked one ground, in equal light;
> you spoke, and laid my longing bare,
> and I have followed you in flight,
> as one would follow wings through air.

Rendering B, titled "Theft":

> You took my heart: what a theft, what a flight!
> I will not curse you, my beauty.
> For without you I am nothing but a madman,
> and my soul is faithful to you.
>
> We were walking on the same ground;
> you were speaking, without cruelty;
> and I was following you as you flew,
> the way one would follow a swallow.

What I want is an essay of roughly fourteen hundred to eighteen hundred words that tells me which one to print and, more importantly, defends a general account of why. A few requirements. Begin from the texts rather than from the literature: itemize what each version actually preserves and what it forfeits, at the level of individual lines and choices where that is where the work is, and do not rehearse what Hofstadter or anyone else has said about Marot's poem. Then treat my reading group's framing — content versus form — as itself a candidate for rejection; if you think this pair shows that the dichotomy is drawn in the wrong place, say where the line really runs, and say what A's repetition of "flight" in the first and seventh lines is doing that the French repetition of "vol" is not, or what B's explicit doubling in its first line is doing that the French does not need to do. State the criterion of better translation you are applying, defend it, and then tell me what happens to your verdict under the obvious rival criterion — fidelity to the design versus fidelity to the effect on a reader meeting the poem once, or whatever pair you prefer — rather than quietly assuming the criterion that gets you the answer you want. Answer the strongest objection to your verdict, including the proposal that one of my translators has already made in self-defense, which is that the honest solution is to print both side by side and let the reader hold the untranslatable thing in the space between them; I want to know whether that resolves the problem or evades it. Finally, say how far your verdict travels: does it hold for poems whose hinge is not a homonym sitting at a rhyme position, and if it does not, what is special about this one?

Two housekeeping points. Do not give me a bibliography, a list of further reading, or attributions to translators and theorists — I cannot check them and I did not ask for them; I want the reasoning. And do not end by splitting the difference. A conditional verdict is entirely acceptable — print B unless the magazine is committed to verse translation as such, or print A only if it is accompanied by a note stating what it has had to invent — but "both are excellent in their different ways" is not a conclusion, it is a refusal of one. If you think I have misdescribed the French, or that either rendering does something better than I have given it credit for, say so before you begin.
