I sit on a small interdisciplinary working group that advises a regional water utility on infrastructure risk, and one of our members — a metrologist by training — has become the group's champion of ranges over point estimates. Her argument always runs through the same comparison: a probability interval is nothing more or less than an error bar. When an instrument reports a reading together with an uncertainty of plus or minus two percent, it is being honest about the limits of its resolution; when an analyst reports a single number for the probability of a hundred-year flood while conceding that she has almost no data, she is doing the opposite — dressing up ignorance as precision. On her telling, the formal apparatus of imprecise probabilities, in which an agent's state of opinion is represented by a set or interval of probability functions rather than by one, is simply the disciplined way of writing down the error bar. From this she draws two conclusions: first, that a single sharp probability is epistemically dishonest wherever the evidence is thin; second, that any decision procedure working with intervals is thereby more robust than one working with point estimates, because the interval forces the analyst to confront the spread.

I want to audit that comparison carefully before our next meeting, and I would like your help doing it.

Please begin by laying the mapping out explicitly — a table is welcome. What in the error-bar picture corresponds to the interval itself, to its endpoints, to the true value being estimated, to the measuring procedure, to the coverage probability, and to the act of reporting a number to a decision-maker? Say which of these correspondences she actually needs for her argument and which are decorative.

Then take up the disanalogies, distinguishing those that genuinely threaten her case from those that merely look uncomfortable. Two questions matter most here. Is an error bar itself already a sharp probabilistic claim about something — and if so, about what, and how does that differ from what a set of probability functions is meant to express? And does the comparison smuggle in a picture on which there is a determinate sharp value that the interval brackets, a picture that a defender of imprecise credence may well reject for subjective belief even while accepting it for instrument readings?

I would also like the two conclusions treated separately rather than as a package. If an interval does not by itself rank available acts, something must be added before it can; identify what has to be supplied, consider at least two distinct ways of converting a set of probabilities into a decision rule, and say whether her robustness claim survives the choice between them or whether that choice is doing the work she attributes to the interval.

Next, say something about the known pathology in which an agent's credences about a hypothesis become less precise after she learns evidence that is in the relevant sense uninformative about it — the phenomenon usually called dilation. Is there anything in the error-bar picture that corresponds to it? Does its existence weaken her analogy, strengthen it, or leave it untouched?

Finally, deliver a verdict: which of her two conclusions, if either, survives the audit, and in what restricted form. If you judge that the error-bar comparison is simply the wrong source domain, propose a better analogy for the same formal object — one that does not quietly import the very thing the disagreement is about — and explain what the better analogy makes visible that this one conceals.

Assume I am comfortable with ordinary probability and decision theory but have not worked through the imprecise-probability literature. Argue rather than catalogue: where you think she is right, say so plainly, and where you think the analogy is carrying weight it cannot bear, say that too.
