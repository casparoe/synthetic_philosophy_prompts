I am preparing the final examination for a graduate seminar on modal metaphysics and the classical arguments for theism, and I would like help drafting one essay question on the modal reformulation of the ontological argument, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The paper is sat in class under a two-hour limit, the question is worth thirty marks, and I would expect a strong model answer to run to roughly a thousand words.

The reconstruction my seminar works from is as follows. Writing Pos(P) for "P is a positive property," G(x) for "x is God-like," and □ for metaphysical necessity, with properties treated as intensional predicates over individuals:

(A1) ∀P [Pos(¬P) ↔ ¬Pos(P)]
(A2) ∀P∀Q [(Pos(P) ∧ □∀x(Px → Qx)) → Pos(Q)]
(A3) Pos(G)
(A4) ∀P [Pos(P) → □Pos(P)]
(A5) Pos(NE), where NE(x) ↔ ∀P [ess(P,x) → □∃y P(y)]
(D1) G(x) ↔ ∀P [Pos(P) → □Px]
(D2) ess(P,x) ↔ P(x) ∧ ∀Q [Q(x) → □∀y (Py → Qy)]

The argument then runs through the lemma that God-likeness is an essence of anything God-like, and the step by which a God-like being, having necessary existence among its positive properties, is such that its essence is necessarily exemplified; it closes with the inference from ◇∃x G(x) to □∃x G(x). If you think this reconstruction diverges from Gödel's own notes in some respect that would affect the question, please say so in a sentence and then work with the system exactly as I have stated it.

What matters to me is that the question cannot be answered by recitation. A candidate who reproduces Anselm's formula about that than which nothing greater can be conceived, or Gaunilo's island, or the slogan that existence is not a predicate, and stops there, should earn little more than a bare pass. I want to test whether candidates understand the architecture of the axiomatisation. Specifically, the question should compel them to engage with four things:

(a) The structural consequences of A1 and A2 taken jointly — that they make the class of positive properties a maximal consistent family, an ultrafilter in the Boolean-algebraic sense, rather than a ranking of degrees of greatness, and hence that "positive" is doing logical rather than evaluative work in the proof.

(b) Where the burden of the proof consequently falls. My own reading, which I want candidates to evaluate rather than inherit, is that the interesting premise is the possibility premise: given (a), the claim that something possibly exemplifies all the positive properties has the shape of a realisation or compactness step, a move from a family that is nowhere internally contradictory to the claim that it is jointly instantiated. On this reading the premise is the modal descendant of Leibniz's insistence that the ontological argument requires a prior demonstration that a being possessing all perfections is possible. I want candidates to say whether the analogy holds, and to state precisely what would license the possibility premise if it is not derivable from A1–A5 as given.

(c) Which modal principle carries the argument across the final step, and what is lost if the system is weakened — candidates should be able to explain what becomes of the proof in a logic validating only reflexivity, or only reflexivity together with transitivity.

(d) Whether treating necessary existence as a positive property answers Kant's objection or merely relocates it, given that NE is no ordinary first-order predicate but one defined by quantifying over essences, and that what D1 attributes to a God-like being is the necessary exemplification of properties rather than existence simpliciter.

Please supply: (i) the question as it would appear on the paper, phrased so that candidates can see what is being asked without my signalling points (a) through (d) too transparently; (ii) the model answer, of about a thousand words; (iii) a marking scheme out of thirty built around five must-hit moves, giving the marks each carries and a one-line note on how candidates typically botch each one; and (iv) a short statement of what separates a first-class answer from one that is good but not excellent.

Two further requirements. First, please keep everything self-contained: I am drafting away from my library with no access to reference works, so I want no bibliography, no reading list and no citations to secondary literature, and the question should not presuppose that candidates have read any. Second, and more important, please keep a firm line between what is settled and what is genuinely disputed among people who work on this argument. I take it to be settled that the reasoning from the possibility premise to the conclusion is correct in the appropriate modal system, and that the philosophically live questions concern the axioms and that premise; but there are real disagreements about whether the possibility premise is licensed by A1–A5 as stated, about whether "positive" can be given determinate content at all, about whether the axioms carry a collapse consequence on which every truth turns out to be necessary, and about how to read the historical relation between this formalism and Anselm's argument. Wherever the model answer touches one of these, it should say which position is better supported rather than presenting one as the consensus, and the marking scheme must not penalise a candidate who defends a minority position competently and flags it as contested. Finally, if any of my four points rests on a reading you consider idiosyncratic or simply mistaken — the compactness analogy in (b) is my own and may well not survive scrutiny — tell me so plainly and propose a replacement crux of comparable difficulty.
