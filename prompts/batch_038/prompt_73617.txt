I am a second-year graduate student in philosophy, and this term I am auditing a seminar on comparative epistemology for which I have no Sanskrit. One of the assigned readings was a chapter from a general-audience history of logic — well written, obviously not a hack job, and the only thing on the list I had the background to follow easily. The chapter's treatment of the Nyāya school struck me as confident in a way I could not check, and when I mentioned it afterwards, a classmate who works on Indian philosophy said only that the passage was "the same story people have been telling since the nineteenth century," without saying where it goes wrong. I would like to know exactly where it goes wrong, and where it does not.

Below is the passage, transcribed from the section on Indian logic.

> Long before the European tradition caught up, the Nyāya school had worked out a complete theory of knowledge resting on four *pramāṇas*, or means of knowing: perception, inference, analogy, and testimony. Perception comes first, and it is simply a matter of the senses being in direct contact with the world, which is why the Naiyāyikas held that what you perceive cannot be false. Their confidence is remarkable: on their account, when you take a glint of shell for silver, you really do perceive silver — it is merely silver that is somewhere else, and the mistake lies in where you put it. Error, for Nyāya, is always a matter of misplacing or misdescribing something that was genuinely perceived; the senses themselves cannot go wrong. Inference is a five-step syllogism. You state a thesis (there is fire on the hill); you give a reason (because there is smoke); you cite the rule with a familiar instance of it (wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen); you apply the rule (the hill is like that, it has smoke); and you restate the conclusion (therefore there is fire on the hill). Note that the fifth step repeats the first. This is a sign that the apparatus was designed for prevailing in public debate rather than for establishing truth, and it is also why the system cannot be counted a genuine logic: its conclusions are never guaranteed by their premises, since the universal rule connecting smoke and fire is only ever a generalization from repeated observation. Later Naiyāyikas piled condition upon condition to close the gaps in these rules, which is itself an admission that they knew the whole edifice rested on a guess. Testimony is the fourth *pramāṇa*, and here Nyāya shows its true colours. Because it treats the word of an authority as a source of knowledge in its own right, Nyāya is at bottom a religious epistemology, a technical machinery built to show that scripture cannot err and that God, who makes the world as a potter makes a pot, is its guarantor. Analogy — *upamāna* — is the softest of the four, and the Naiyāyikas conceded as much, since it plainly reduces to inference: if you have been told that a *gavaya* is like a cow, and then in the forest you encounter one and think "this is what the word *gavaya* means," you have simply reasoned from an observed similarity to a conclusion. What is genuinely distinctive about Nyāya is rather what it refused: two *pramāṇas* that other Indian schools accepted, postulation and non-apprehension. It refused them out of parsimony, on the principle that the fewer sources of knowledge you admit, the harder it is for an opponent to smuggle in something unobservable. The upshot is a system more cautious than Greek logic, less rigorous than modern logic, and in the end a scholastic defence of orthodoxy wearing a technical veneer.

I would be grateful for an accuracy audit rather than a general appreciation. Specifically:

1. Go through the passage claim by claim, and for each classify it as something a Naiyāyika would accept, something they would accept only as a lossy simplification, or something they would reject outright. For the second and third categories, say what the correct position is and what turns on the difference — not merely that the chapter is imprecise.
2. Where the chapter attributes to Nyāya a position actually pressed by its rivals, identify the rival and explain why the difference is philosophically significant rather than a matter of school labels. I have in mind especially the claims that the Naiyāyikas conceded the reducibility of *upamāṇa*, and that perception cannot be false.
3. The chapter offers a reason for Nyāya's rejection of postulation and non-apprehension — fear of admitting the unobservable. Is that the reason Nyāya actually gives? If not, what is, and does the substitution change how one should read the four-*pramāṇa* list?
4. Assess the two larger conclusions separately: that the five-membered form shows Nyāya inference is not genuine logic and its conclusions are not guaranteed, and that the framework as a whole is essentially a defence of religious orthodoxy. For each, tell me whether it survives the corrections, survives in a weakened form worth stating, or collapses. If a weakened form survives, state it precisely enough that I could defend it in seminar.
5. Finally, name two or three features of the *pramāṇa* theory that this framing would lead a reader to miss entirely — the questions the chapter never raises because its narrative has no room for them.

Two constraints on the answer. I am working offline and want no citations, reading lists, or references of any kind; I want the reasoning I could reconstruct myself. Sanskrit technical terms are welcome where they do real work, but please gloss each one in English on first use.
