Our AI ethics reading group is doing The Remains of the Day next month, which is a weird pick for that group but I have reasons. There are no machines in it, so I want the reading to be upfront about where the analogy holds and where it snaps -- not a costume party where we just relabel Stevens as a language model and feel clever.

The thing I want to read it against is eliciting latent knowledge: getting a system to tell you what it knows rather than what it's learned that its audience wants to hear. The hard part, as I understand it, is that you can't supervise the reporting against the truth, because if you had the truth you wouldn't need the system -- the usual toy case is something watching a vault and answering whether the diamonds are still in there -- so the best you can do is train it to say what a human observer would say, and those two targets come apart exactly when you care most.

Stevens seems like that problem with the difficulty turned up, and I can't tell which of three things is going on with him. Maybe he does know -- about Miss Kenton, about the two housemaids he sent away when Lord Darlington asked, about what Darlington actually was -- and he won't say it, so it's concealment and the fix is a matter of incentives. Or he knows it in a form the question can't reach, so no amount of asking gets at it, because it was never stored as anything he could endorse or deny. Or there isn't a determinate fact of the matter at all and the answer gets manufactured by the asking, in which case the whole project is just measuring the questioner. Which one does the book support, and can it even tell the difference?

Hang the case on scenes rather than summary: the night he keeps serving guests while Miss Kenton is trying to tell him about a marriage proposal, the meeting with her years later as Mrs Benn, and the bench on the seafront at the end, where he breaks down a little and then pivots straight into planning how to get better at small talk for his new American employer. Is that last scene a successful elicitation or a failure dressed up as one?

Then the part I actually care about: what does the novel give this debate that an argument can't? My suspicion is that its about the elicitor. I want Stevens to confess badly enough that I'm probably willing to overread him to get it, and I worry that isn't a flaw in my reading specifically but a standing hazard for anyone building a reporter -- you get the answer you were hoping for and you can't tell from the inside.

Please seperate what's settled from what people who know this stuff genuinely argue about, on both sides. I assume almost nothing is settled on the AI side, and I know there's a live fight about how much a reader of an unreliable narrator is entitled to infer. Don't hand me your favorite account of latent belief, or of unreliable narration, as if it were the common view.
