I teach a 110-minute session of an interdisciplinary seminar on legal interpretation — eighteen students, roughly half of them law students and half philosophy graduate students — and I want a classroom exercise in which the contest between fixing meaning in the enacted text and fixing it in the legislature's apparent purpose is encountered through the mechanics of the exercise rather than announced by me. Please design the whole thing: full rules, printable materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief, and a candid account of its failure modes.

I have drafted the statutory materials myself and I would like you to keep them, tightening the wording where you like but preserving the four defects I built in. The instrument is a state statute I will call the Corridor Quiet Use Act. Section 1 recites that the Greenway Corridor is used by pedestrians, cyclists and persons with mobility impairments, that the growth of motor-driven devices has increased collisions and noise, and that the purpose of the Act is to keep the Corridor safe and quiet for persons travelling under their own power. Section 2 defines "motor-driven device" as any apparatus propelled by an engine or motor, expressly including internal-combustion engines and electric motors, and says nothing about battery size, pedal assist, or medical equipment. Section 3 forbids any person to operate a motor-driven device on any segment listed in Schedule A. Section 4 imposes a fifteen-mile-per-hour cap on bicycles and motor-driven devices "on the Corridor," which is a wider domain than Schedule A and creates a redundancy the teams must fight over. Section 5 exempts vehicles used in construction, maintenance or repair of the Corridor and emergency vehicles responding to an emergency on it. Schedule A lists five segments, one of which — Segment 4 — has hosted gasoline-powered guided tour vehicles continuously since 1974 and which the record twice describes as the motorized-use segment; the list as engrossed does not match the segment descriptions the committee approved.

The four cases for argument are: a seventy-one-year-old woman cited under Section 3 for riding a physician-prescribed electric-assist tricycle to the community garden she cannot otherwise reach; the Segment 4 tour operator cited under Section 3 while driving at twelve miles per hour; a delivery contractor on a 250-watt electric cargo bike going eighteen miles per hour, cited under both Sections 3 and 4; and a search-and-rescue volunteer cited under Section 3 for flying a gasoline-powered drone that launched from, and returned to, Segment 2 without touching down anywhere inside the Corridor.

Please build the exercise around a scarce resource. Give each counsel team a fixed budget of tokens and price access to the legislative record, with prices set so that no team can afford everything: the Senate committee report, which says the ban targets gas-powered mini-bikes and off-road motorcycles and adds that nothing in the Act limits access for persons with disabilities; the sponsor's floor statement, which says the ban reaches anything with a motor and specifically answers yes when asked about electric-assist bicycles; the agency hearing transcript, containing the tour operator's counsel stating that his client was assured Segment 4 would be excluded; the nonpartisan drafting consultant's memorandum noting that Schedule A was renumbered in the second printing and that the engrossed list does not match the approved descriptions, a memorandum never read aloud, never voted on, and written by someone who is not a legislator; and a one-paragraph endorsement from a disability-rights organization praising the bill for preserving access. Award points to teams that argue within the four corners of the text, and score the briefs on a published rubric whose dimensions cannot all be maximized at once.

Two sealed envelopes should be opened only at the end. The first discloses the subcommittee's actual state of mind: four legislators, four incompatible understandings of the operative term, one of whom voted for the bill without reading Schedule A, with the compromise being to use a broad word and let the agency sort it out. The second states, in one sentence, that the document which would have settled the meaning is the consultant's memorandum, and asks the room what status that gives it.

The session must run without my saying the words textualism or purposivism, and no student may be assigned a school of thought; participants play counsel and judges, and any methodological commitment has to emerge from the incentives. Tell me what will predictably break the first time I run this — I expect it to be the budget, and that every team will try to buy the memorandum — and give me a mid-session patch. Give me the objection the most articulate purposivist in the room will level at the design, namely that I rigged the record to be expensive and self-contradictory so that the text would win by default, and tell me whether that objection lands. Finally, be explicit about which features of the real disagreement the exercise cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
