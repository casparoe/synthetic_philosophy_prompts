I am drafting a novel set in a small polity that has abandoned elections for sortition. Its assembly is four hundred members drawn by lot from the register of residents; each serves a single three-year term and cannot be reselected; any of them may decline to serve without giving a reason; and the body may veto legislation but cannot propose any.

My difficulty is one of emphasis rather than invention. I have perhaps a hundred pages in which the assembly's authority can be contested, and I must decide which of the standing objections to rule by lottery deserve them. The candidates, as I see them: whether a body nobody chose can represent anybody; whether accountability survives at all once reselection is impossible; whether the right to decline quietly converts a cross-section of the polity into a gathering of volunteers with something to prove; whether a veto-only power makes the assembly a check on government or merely an obstacle to it; and whether the proper standard for judging it is the wisdom of its decisions or the impartiality of its composition.

Which two or three should bear the weight of the book, and which am I overrating because they are agreeable to argue rather than consequential? Please defend the ordering rather than survey it. I want to know which of these questions the others quietly presuppose, which can be felt in a scene instead of explained in one, and which have already been argued to exhaustion in the press, such that a novel built upon them would read as a pamphlet. Tell me also what would rearrange your list — a change in the assembly's powers, in the manner of its selection, or in how often its vetoes are honoured.
