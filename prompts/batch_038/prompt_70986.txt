I am in the second draft of a novel whose middle section turns on John Locke, brought forward without explanation into present-day London and confronted with money that has no body at all: bank balances, a retail central-bank digital currency, a stablecoin redeemable in nothing but itself. My working understanding — correct me where it is wrong — is that in the recoinage dispute of the 1690s Locke opposed Lowndes' proposal to raise the denomination of the clipped coinage, holding that the value of the pound lies in a determinate quantity of silver and that no act of the legislature can make it otherwise; and that in the Essay he nonetheless treats the boundaries of the species as work of our own making rather than as joints discovered in nature. Tell me, as responsibly as the material allows, how Locke would receive a monetary system resting entirely on collective acceptance and state credit: which of those two commitments he would press, how he would explain or explain away the tension between them, and where he would be forced into error simply by having no apparatus for thinking about fiat. Finish by separating the reactions that would carry dramatic weight from those that would be anachronism I ought to strike.
