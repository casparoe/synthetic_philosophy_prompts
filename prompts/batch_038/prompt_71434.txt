I work in distributed systems, and for the past few years I have been reading the theoretical literature on how hard computational problems are, on my own time and without any formal training in the subject. There is a pattern I keep running into that I have no word for, and I would like to know whether one exists.

Three instances first, since the abstraction only makes sense once you have seen them side by side.

One. The best published exponent for matrix multiplication is better than the one you will find in an undergraduate text, and each improvement gets reported as progress on a question people used to consider closed. As far as I can tell, nobody runs those algorithms: the constant factor only pays off for matrices too large to be written down in this universe. Both facts are accepted at once — the improvement is genuine, and it will never execute — and nobody thinks the second undermines the first. The unreachable result is treated as the deeper of the two, because it says something about the shape of the problem rather than about any particular size of it.

Two. A problem is proven intractable in the strongest way anyone knows how, and the conclusion binds only for inputs beyond any size that will ever exist in practice, yet the result is cited, unqualified, as the reason to stop trying to solve it.

Three. At a talk, someone observed that for any single fixed input length there are only finitely many inputs, so you can hardcode the answers, and therefore every decision problem restricted to that length is solvable in constant time — meaning, they suggested, that the whole edifice says nothing about any computation anyone could ever perform. This was dismissed as a cheap trick inside a minute, and correctly dismissed, I think; nobody took it seriously. Yet as far as I can see it is literally true, and it is the same observation as the first two: a claim quantified over a range with no reachable members.

What I want a name for is the asymmetry. That the quantifier ranges over sizes no one can ever get to is counted as a virtue in the first two cases and gets you laughed out of the room in the third, and I have never seen anyone state the rule that decides which is which. It is not that the first two are positive results and the third is a deflationary one, because I can build variants where that flips. And it is not simply a theory-versus-practice complaint: in the first case nobody is claiming the work is useful, and in the third nobody is asking for a faster algorithm.

Is there an established concept for this? Please tell me what the nearest existing notions are — from either the complexity literature or the philosophy of science — how the thing I have described differs from each of them, and which, if any, actually covers it. If none does, coin a term, define it, and give me its boundary cases, including at least one clear non-example. And separately: has anyone stated a principled criterion for when an appeal to a regime that cannot be reached is legitimate rather than evasive? I am not asking for references, just the concepts and the distinctions; a bare label would not help me. The interesting part is where the line goes.
