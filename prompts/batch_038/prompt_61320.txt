Our MBA strategy seminar had a consultant come in this week and hand out a two-page "quick history of decision theory" primer, and the Herbert Simon section has been bugging me ever since. It goes roughly like this — I'm typing it from memory of the handout, not quoting it word for word. Simon's big idea was that because human brains have limited processing power we simply can't maximize, so instead of hunting for the best option we settle for the first one that clears our bar; he called this satisficing. The primer then says this is basically laziness wearing a Nobel Prize, because if you set your bar low enough anything counts as a success, and a satisficer can never tell you why they stopped where they stopped. After that it says economists rescued the theory by redefining satisficing as optimal search — you keep looking as long as the expected payoff from one more look beats the cost of looking, and you stop when it doesn't. From which the author concludes that bounded rationality was never a rival to rational choice theory at all, just rational choice theory with a budget line for thinking, and that the practical upshot is this: a satisficer is simply somebody who has priced their own attention correctly.

I half want it to be true, because it makes satisficing sound respectable and gives me something to say in meetings when people accuse my team of shipping the first OK option instead of the best one. But a classmate who did an econ undergrad says the middle part is a mess, that "optimal search" and "satisficing" are supposed to be opposed rather than identical, and I cant tell whether he's making a real point or just being territorial about his discipline. So could you go through the primer's paragraph piece by piece and tell me what a decision theorist would nod at, what they'd write off as a tolerable simplification, and what is actually wrong or backwards? I especially want to know whether that rescue move works — can satisficing really be restated as a cost-benefit search rule without smuggling back in the very capabilities bounded rationality was invented to stop assuming, like knowing the distribution of options you'll encounter or being able to compute an expected gain from looking one more time? And what about the claim that a satisficer can't explain their own stopping point — that strikes me as the opposite of how an aspiration-level rule works, but I don't trust my instinct here. Also tell me whether the closing line about pricing your attention is something in Simon's tradition would endorse, or something they'd reject, since it seems to require a second round of the same optimization the whole theory says we can't do.
