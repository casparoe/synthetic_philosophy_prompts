**Question (10 marks, ~350 words):** Two sprinkler timers are both set to water the lawn at 6:00. Timer A's valve works; timer B's valve would work too, but the system has a float switch such that once the lawn is wet B is disabled. In the actual sequence A waters the lawn at 6:00 and B stays disabled. (a) Explain how Lewis and how Woodward would each assess the claim that A caused the lawn to be wet, and in particular how each account is supposed to understand the counterfactuals it relies on. (b) Would either verdict change if the float switch were removed and both timers fired at exactly 6:00?

**Student's answer:** Lewis holds that causation is counterfactual dependence between distinct events, where the counterfactuals are read off nearby possible worlds: the closest A-less worlds, ranked by a similarity metric that preserves the actual laws at the cost of a single small local miracle. The counterfactual "if A hadn't fired, the lawn wouldn't have been wet at 6:00" comes out false in our case, since in the nearest such world the float switch never trips and B does the watering instead. So Lewis can't get direct dependence and falls back on the ancestral strategy: causation is the ancestral of counterfactual dependence, and there's a chain here running through B's being disabled, so A still counts as a cause.

Woodward's account differs in that it's explicitly non-reductive — he doesn't analyse causation in non-causal terms, because an intervention is itself a causal notion — but the underlying idea is close: you have variables and structural equations, and A is a cause of the wetness insofar as the equation for wetness is invariant under interventions on A, i.e. insofar as "if an intervention set A = off, the lawn would not be wet" is true. Woodward reads these counterfactuals the same way Lewis does, only restricted to worlds in which the change in A is produced by an intervention rather than by a miracle; so interventionism is roughly Lewisian counterfactual analysis plus a constraint on which worlds are eligible. And on Woodward's view A causes the wetness only because an intervention on A is something we could actually perform — we can turn the valve off — which is why interventionism is vulnerable to the charge that it's an account of manipulability rather than of causation.

(b) With the float switch removed both timers fire at 6:00 and neither is counterfactually necessary. For Lewis this is just the same situation as before, so he reaches for the ancestral again, and since there's a chain from A to the lawn's being wet, A is still a cause. For Woodward it also goes through: an intervention on A alone, with B held fixed by stipulation, changes the value of the wetness variable, so the invariance claim holds. Both accounts therefore give the right verdict in the symmetric case, which is a real advantage over a crude but-for test.
