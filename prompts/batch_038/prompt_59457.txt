The standard story about the deserving and undeserving poor is a moral one. Polities sort claimants by whether they're thought to be responsible for their own plight, and conditionality — work requirements, activity tests, sanctions, time limits — is the institutional expression of that judgment. I want to argue the causality runs the other way far more often than we admit. Conditionality attaches to a category of claimant not because the category is morally suspect but because the fact that determines eligibility is hard to verify. Where the state can read the relevant fact off a record — age, contribution history, a certified impairment — assistance flows with few behavioral strings attached. Where the relevant fact is an interior disposition (are you really looking for work, do you really not have a partner, are you really incapable of this job) the state has to build a proxy, and the proxy is always a behavioral requirement: sign the agreement, attend the appointment, take the offer, keep the diary. The moral rhetoric of desert then gets recruited to justify the device, because a device with visible victims needs a story to tell about them.

Take 1834. The workhouse test is usually read as pure moral theater, a deliberate degradation ritual marking the able-bodied pauper as undeserving. But look at what it actually is: a screening technology. The commissioners could not observe whether a man asking for outdoor relief could in fact have earned a wage; what they could do was offer relief on terms that only someone with no alternative would accept. Less eligibility is not only a punitive doctrine, it is a self-operating test, and that is very nearly the phrase the commissioners reached for themselves. Note what happened next: where the test was expensive to run, it decayed. In much of the industrial north the unions kept paying able-bodied people outside the house, decade after decade, because the machinery of verification was unaffordable and the cyclical unemployment of mill towns broke the assumption that a refusal of relief conditions meant a job was available. Moral opinion in those districts did not move; administrative capacity did. The Speenhamland-style wage supplements that the 1834 report attacked are a useful control case, since the allowance was set by family size and the price of bread — facts you can read off a table — and attracted the most ferocious moral condemnation of the era while being comparatively unpoliced in operation.

The second case is the contrast between insurance and assistance. Contributory schemes — Bismarckian insurance, British national insurance, American social security — are famously unconditional and nearly unstigmatized, and the standard explanation is that contributors have earned an entitlement, so no moral test is needed. But look at the paperwork. An insurance record is the most legible fact in the entire welfare state: a list of payments, held by the state, unambiguous, essentially impossible to fake. The eligibility question is settled at the counter in seconds and there is nothing left to police. Means-tested assistance has to establish a negative — that you have no other resources, no other household, no other income — which is the paradigm of a hard-to-verify fact, and it gets policed with capital rules, cohabitation investigations, and repeated re-attestation. The cleanest way to see the point is that the same person, with the same moral standing in the eyes of the same community, is treated as deserving under one program and as a suspect under the other, and what differs between them is not judgment but the epistemics of the eligibility test.

Third, the American reform of 1996, which is where the moral reading is most dominant: the welfare queen figure, the argument that benefits corrode character and family formation. Yet the instruments actually chosen are epistemic ones. Time limits are what you impose when you cannot observe whether a spell of dependence is temporary or permanent; you substitute duration for disposition. Work requirements built on counted hours and participation thresholds are what you impose when you cannot observe search effort; you count motions instead. And the sanction, the thing that bites, is overwhelmingly imposed for a procedural failure — a missed appointment, an unsigned form, an unmet contact — rather than for evidence that this claimant was employable and idle. Read sanction statistics as an audit trail of verification failures rather than as a moral ledger and a lot of the pattern falls into place.

The obvious counterexample is disability, and I should say so myself. Disability attracts both enormous stigma and enormous conditionality — medical assessments, functional tests, reassessment cycles, appeals — and it is precisely the category that moral desert theory says should be exempt, the impotent poor of the old classification, blameless by definition. My response is that the case supports the thesis rather than refuting it: what makes disability conditionality-heavy is that the relevant fact, capacity for work, is genuinely unobservable and permanently contestable, so the state builds an elaborate proxy machine, and the visible failures of that machine then manufacture the stigma. The apparatus comes first and the moral category grows around it. I'll admit this is the weak joint, because it starts to make the claim look unfalsifiable: heavy conditionality proves verification difficulty, and its absence proves verification ease.

If the legibility account is right, though, the boundary of the undeserving should move with verification technology rather than with moral opinion, and that gives me two testable claims. One: as data matching, biometric identification and real-time earnings reporting get cheaper, conditionality should lighten in exactly the categories those tools can settle — in-work benefits, self-employed income — and stay heavy or get heavier only where they can't reach. Two: moral panics about fraud should be decoupled from measured fraud rates and coupled instead to administrative weakness, so the loudest fraud discourse should come from the states least able to verify anything. I think the first one holds. I'm much less sure about the second, and I may drop it.
