I work as a software engineer on scoring and ranking systems, and I have no formal philosophy training. So I would ask you to state your diagnosis in plain terms rather than assuming I have read the ethics literature; if there is a standard name for a move I have made, give it, but explain it as if to a colleague who writes proofs and ships models and nothing else.

Last year I was asked to review a risk-scoring pipeline, and to prepare I worked through the impossibility results showing that calibration and equal error rates cannot generally be had at once. I follow the mathematics and I reproduced it by simulation on synthetic data. What I did next, on my own initiative, was write out the following argument, and I now think it proves considerably more than I can accept. I would like to know where it breaks.

(1) Call a score calibrated if, for every score value s and every group g, among the individuals in g who received score s, the proportion who in fact have the outcome is s.

(2) Call a score separated if, at every threshold, the false positive rate and the false negative rate are the same in every group.

(3) What a well-trained model estimates is the true conditional probability of the outcome given the available features, and that quantity is calibrated by construction. Calibration is therefore just the property a score has in virtue of being accurate; it is a purely statistical property with no normative commitment built into it. Call any model that has it accurate.

(4) The impossibility results establish that if base rates differ between groups, no score that is not perfectly predictive can be both calibrated and separated.

(5) So in any population with differing base rates, every accurate model fails separation.

(6) A model that imposes a higher false positive rate on one group than on another burdens members of that group with errors that identically situated members of other groups do not bear, and that is what it is for a classifier to be unfair to a group.

(7) So every accurate model on such a population is unfair to some group — including the Bayes-optimal model, computed from flawless data, with the group variable and every proxy for it removed.

(8) The Bayes-optimal model is the upper limit of accuracy, and (7) holds at that limit. Therefore no improvement in data quality, feature engineering, or model capacity can move a classifier away from unfairness. The violation is not an artifact of sloppy models; it is present in the best model there is, which is to say it is present in all of them.

(9) Therefore fairness is not a property of a model. At most it is a property of a population — a fact about base rates and how they came to be — and the engineering program of building fair classifiers is aimed at the wrong object. Worse, (7) shows that the choice among competing fairness criteria is not a technical choice either, since which criterion a team adopts has no bearing on whether its model turns out to be unfair.

Two notes on where I stand before you answer. I verified (1) through (4) numerically, so I would like to keep them. And I am attached to (6), because it is the only step in the whole argument that mentions anybody being harmed; if (6) goes, I am not sure what the remaining steps were ever about.

Here is what I want. First, identify the step at which the argument first fails, and say what kind of failure it is — an equivocation on a term, a hypothesis quietly dropped from a theorem, a scope or quantifier shift, a contested normative claim dressed as a definition, or something else. Second, explain why that step looked unobjectionable to someone with my background, since I want to know which habit of mine produced it. Third, price the alternatives: taking (3), (5), (6), and (8) in turn, tell me what I lose if I reject that step instead of the one you identified, both formally and in terms of what I could honestly say to the team that owns the pipeline afterwards. I can check anything you assert against small examples, so if a step is simply false rather than merely unearned, give me the concrete case — a tiny population, a specific score, and the numbers — instead of a general assurance.
