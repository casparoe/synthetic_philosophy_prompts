After 35 years in isotope geochemistry — provenance work, so basically a career spent arguing about how much a single measurement licenses you to claim — I've retired and fallen down an archaeology hole. The leaps give me whiplash. Six inference patterns I keep meeting, in popular books but also in serious ones:

1. A little female figurine turned up on a house floor → household worship of a mother goddess.
2. Ration lists or account tablets in a building → that building was an arm of a state redistributive bureaucracy.
3. Same vessel forms and near-identical weights across many sites in a region → centrally controlled production or trade.
4. Healed fractures and embedded projectile points in skeletons, plus a wall and ditch → organized warfare.
5. Some children buried with as much gear as the richest adults → hereditary, ascribed status.
6. The same horned figure showing up in art over a wide area for centuries → a shared religion with a real pantheon.

Give each a 0–10 score for how much of the distance between the observation and the conclusion gets filled in by assumption rather than by the object itself (10 = nearly all of it), then rank them best to worst on that score. For each one, the strongest rival reading a practioner would offer, and what extra find would actually earn the big conclusion rather than just suggest it. And be straight with me about which of your scores you'd least want to defend in public, and what argument or piece of evidence would move it.
