I convene a graduate reading group in decision theory and formal epistemology, and this term we are recording a long-form interview with a philosopher whose work defends the position sketched below. I want the questions aimed at her actual commitments rather than at the textbook version of the money-pump argument, so please read the sketch closely.

Her position, in brief. Money pumps against transitivity are usually presented as follows: an agent with cyclical preferences, A over B over C over A, is offered a sequence of trades, each with a small premium attached, and is led around the cycle until she is back where she started and strictly poorer. She thinks this framing concedes too much to her opponents, because it makes the normative work depend on the premiums. On her account, what a pump reveals is a defect of diachronic self-governance: the cycling agent is such that, having completed the sequence, she would prefer to have declined the first trade, even though at every node she preferred to accept it. She holds that this defect is dispositional rather than consequential — it obtains even in a world where no trades are ever offered — and that the standard replies all miss it. Transaction costs, precommitment devices, and crediting the agent with backward-induction foresight bear on whether she can be exploited, not on whether she is pumpable, and it is pumpability that carries normative weight. She also argues that the sophisticated-choice and resolute-choice strategies make the verdict on an intransitive agent turn on her foresight and planning capacities rather than on the structure of her preferences, which she regards as the wrong place to locate the requirement.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each followed by a single follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — a specific rejoinder to the predictable reply, not a generic prompt to elaborate. I would like at least one question on each of the following:

1. Whether an agent with full foresight, who declines the first trade and thereby secures her most preferred reachable outcome, is still defective in her sense, and how she answers the intuition that foresight ought to be rewarded rather than punished.
2. The worry that a preference over sequences of choices is simply a higher-order preference, so her account relocates the demand for acyclicity from outcomes to policies instead of grounding it — and whether her norms over policies could themselves cycle.
3. Whether the argument begs the question against an agent who understands the pump and is content to pay to cycle, or who declines to treat money as an adequate proxy for her good.
4. The costless case: a sequence of free trades that leaves her at an outcome she prefers less than her starting point. Is being led to a dominated position, with nothing lost, a genuine normative failure or merely a description?
5. Precommitment and the unity of the diachronic agent — what her account says about agents whose temporal parts have divergent preferences, including deployed learning systems that exhibit cyclic behaviour under specification change.
6. The familiar impossibility of holding, in dynamic settings, to dynamic consistency, separability of choice at a node from what is unreachable, and the standard axioms all at once. Which horn does she take, and what does that cost her?

Fold in anything else you judge important, such as whether her treatment extends to violations of independence rather than transitivity, or whether the account has a counterpart for incoherent credences.

Three constraints. The questions must be short enough to be asked aloud, with the technical setup compressed into a sentence or two; where the literature has a standing name for a move, use the name once and gloss it in a clause. No biographical softballs, no questions whose only possible answer is a restatement of the view, and no request for a reading list or citations — our students will have the relevant material already. Close with one question designed to elicit what she would accept as a counterexample: a case in which an agent is pumpable by her lights yet, she agrees, is not irrational, or a case in which an acyclic agent fails by the same standard.
