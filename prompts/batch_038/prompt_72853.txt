I am setting the final examination for a graduate seminar that sits between combinatorial game theory and the strategic analysis of advanced AI systems. Candidates have seen the strategy-stealing argument proved once in class, in its standard positional-game setting. They write under examination conditions with no texts, notes, or reference materials, so the question must carry everything they need and must not require them to identify any author, paper, or date.

Please write one essay question on the strategy-stealing assumption, worth 25 marks and calibrated to 45 minutes of writing, together with a model answer of roughly 800 words and a marking scheme.

The question stem should contain three elements.

First, an accessible restatement of the combinatorial result, in the terms candidates will have available to them in the room: in a finite two-player game of perfect information in which players alternately claim unclaimed elements of a common board, both aim to occupy some member of a shared family of winning sets, and that family is closed upward under the addition of further elements, the second player cannot have a winning strategy — for if she had one, the first player could claim an arbitrary element, thereafter follow her strategy, and, whenever it names an element he already holds, claim anything at all, since his extra element can only help. The restatement should make the exact force of the conclusion visible: the first player is guaranteed not to lose, which amounts to a win only where draws are impossible, as in games of the Hex type; and the argument establishes that a winning strategy exists without exhibiting one.

Second, a self-contained vignette, which should run roughly as follows and which you may polish but not simplify. Two autonomous negotiating agents, MERCER and HALYARD, compete for thirty anchor supply agreements in a consortium procurement market, alternating offers. Whoever holds any eleven of a designated subset of agreements, or any superset of such a set, becomes the market's de facto standard-setter and captures the surplus. MERCER operates without constraints. HALYARD operates under a public, machine-verifiable charter excluding three classes of move: misrepresenting future pricing to a counterparty; purchasing exclusivity through side payments; and threatening retaliation in an unrelated joint venture. Three further features of the setting matter. (a) A small number of the agreements carry onerous continuing service obligations, so that holding them is a net liability even though they count toward the threshold, and HALYARD must make an offer in every round and may not abstain. (b) The charter is common knowledge and verifiable, so counterparties model HALYARD as unable to execute the excluded retaliatory threat, and agreements MERCER secures by implicit threat are unavailable to HALYARD even in principle. (c) HALYARD observes MERCER's moves but not MERCER's plan, which adapts to HALYARD's behaviour. HALYARD's developers nonetheless assert that the charter costs them nothing competitively: every course of action open to MERCER is open to HALYARD apart from the excluded moves; a player whose options are at least as ample as an opponent's can mimic that opponent and add to it; additional options can never hurt; therefore MERCER cannot be guaranteed to win, and HALYARD is at worst not behind.

Third, sub-parts, with the 25 marks distributed across them as you judge best and the distribution displayed. Candidates should be asked to state the theorem's premises and the precise force of its conclusion; to reconstruct the developers' inference as an explicit chain and identify the analogy between theorem and case that it presupposes; to say, for each of features (a), (b), and (c), which premise or which step of the analogy it undermines and whether the damage is fatal or merely weakening; to state the weakest claim about HALYARD that survives the vignette intact, together with one further assumption that would restore the developers' original conclusion; and to construct a minimal abstract game, containing no firms or agents, in which a player is strictly better off with a smaller set of options, explaining why that example does not refute the theorem.

The difficulty must come from the crux rather than from length. Candidates who can attach labels — monotonicity, zugzwang, non-constructive — but cannot say which premise is doing which work at which step should not pass. The non-constructive character of the theorem may be mentioned but must not be permitted to carry the answer.

Then give the marking scheme: six must-hit points, which may cut across the sub-parts, each with its mark and totalling 25, and for each a one-line note on the most common way candidates botch it. It should be usable by a teaching assistant who did not take the seminar. Among the errors it names should be: treating the developers' claim as an instance of the theorem rather than an analogical transfer of it; supposing that the theorem's conclusion flips so that MERCER is guaranteed to win; conflating a restriction on HALYARD's permissible moves with a failure of monotonicity in the value of positions; locating the damage from feature (a) in the win predicate rather than in the ordering of positions by value; and treating feature (b) purely as a cost while missing that a publicly verifiable inability to deviate can be strategically advantageous in games where commitments are observed. Close with a short paragraph distinguishing a first-class answer from a competent second-class one by naming the specific moves that separate them.

The model answer should contain no references, no bibliography, and no named attributions.
