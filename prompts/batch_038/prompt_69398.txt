I teach a second-year undergraduate ethics course and have one 80-minute session in the unit on character. Twenty-four students, a flat room with movable chairs, no budget, and no equipment beyond paper, pens, and a projector I may or may not use. They have done the reading on virtue ethics and have been told only that this session concerns character and its critics.

Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the situationist critique from the inside: their own confident dispositional predictions about other people's conduct should, in the room and in real time, turn out to have done no predictive work, while some trivial feature of the setting that they would be embarrassed to cite as a reason appears to have moved them instead. I want them to arrive at the discomfort before anyone supplies the vocabulary for it.

The design must satisfy all of the following.

1. The class generates its own data. Students record predictions, in a form you specify, before the target situation arises, so that the debrief can set a live table of forecasts beside a live record of what happened. Keep the arithmetic to something a non-statistician can work through on the board in three minutes.

2. The manipulated variable is mundane. No shocks, no authority figures, no staged emergencies. The standard student escape — that the classic studies the critique leans on (the seminarians in a hurry, the found coin) produced effects only because their situations were extreme or artificial — must be structurally unavailable to them, not merely argued against. If at most one confederate is required, say who that is and exactly what they do; if none is required, so much the better.

3. Sequencing of disclosure. Nothing is named in advance: not the critique, not its proponents, not the experiments behind it. Tell me the precise order in which names and results are released during the debrief, and what must already have happened in the room before each release can land.

4. Robustness to a null result. With twenty-four students, my in-class effect will very likely fail to appear. Give me a version of the design and a debrief in which the force of the critique still lands when the class's own numbers show nothing — and tell me candidly whether such a debrief risks teaching the opposite lesson, namely that a failed replication vindicates the trait view. If it does, repair it.

5. The ethics of running it. To get the intended effect I will have to mislead my own students about at least part of what the session is for. State what advance notice or consent language I need, what I owe them afterwards, what would make the activity indefensible, and a no-deception fallback version, together with an honest account of precisely what the fallback costs in what the students feel.

6. Anticipate the philosophically competent pushback that arises mid-debrief: the student who re-describes the trait at progressively finer grain until it is guaranteed true of whoever acted as they did, and the student who insists that a mass of small situational effects remains perfectly consistent with global character. Give me a response to each that does not depend on assertion.

Deliver a materials list; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes; the exact wording of anything read aloud or handed out that has to be worded carefully; the debrief as an ordered sequence of questions rather than speeches; the three or four things that predictably go wrong on a first run and how to handle each; what changes if enrolment drops to twelve; and a frank statement of which feature of the critique this activity cannot reproduce, and which I will therefore have to supply by exposition.
