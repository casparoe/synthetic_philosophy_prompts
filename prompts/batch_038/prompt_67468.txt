Our institution has adopted, as the framing text for a staff training day on the colonial provenance of our collections, an essay that originally appeared in a heritage-sector magazine. The paragraph our handout quotes runs as follows:

> The archival turn made one simple point: archives are not neutral containers of evidence but instruments of power. Foucault showed that what may count as a record is settled by the archive itself, so that every document already carries the domination that made it possible. Trouillot, in *Silencing the Past*, located the decisive moment of silencing in the archive: because the powerful decide what is preserved, the powerless leave no trace, and the honest historian has only two options — mark the gap openly, or write into it. His treatment of the Haitian Revolution makes the point vividly: the revolution was censored out of the Western record because it was unthinkable. Derrida pressed further, arguing that archiving is itself a species of repression, the substitution of a dead trace for living memory, so that the archon's authority to house the records is in truth an authority to forget. Two practical consequences follow. First, the principle of provenance — treating a fonds as a coherent whole because it issued from a single creator — is a residue of the same logic and should give way to re-assemblage around the communities the records concern. Second, the objection that all this collapses history into opinion answers itself: if evidence is produced rather than found, no account can be better evidenced than any other, and that is precisely what makes the argument radical.

I find the passage compelling, but two colleagues do not. One is a professional archivist who insists that provenance is the very feature that makes an institution's power visible in a fonds rather than a disguise for it. The other is a historian who thinks the closing sentence is self-refuting and says the essay would not survive contact with actual practice in the reading room. I lack the background to adjudicate between them, and I would rather know where the passage is sound than discard it wholesale.
