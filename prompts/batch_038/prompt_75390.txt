I'm in the final year of a law degree, and I've run into a phrase that seems to migrate between two very different conversations without anyone noticing. I first met it in criminal law, where it was presented as a settled common-law maxim: ignorance of the law is no excuse. Our instructor was clear that its justification is partly institutional — allowing a defence of legal ignorance would invite fabricated claims and would reward people for never acquainting themselves with published rules — and that a defendant can be convicted even where their ignorance was entirely reasonable. Then, in a reading group on ethics that I attend for pleasure, the same sentence (and its cousin, moral ignorance is no excuse) turned up constantly, but doing work that had nothing to do with courts, evidence, or statutes. What troubles me is that the sentence is treated as though it expressed a single claim, when I suspect it is being used for at least three, and I can't articulate the differences well enough to object.

Three instances. First: a friend's great-grandfather ran a firm with openly discriminatory hiring practices and, by all family accounts, never once thought there was anything wrong with it; my friend invoked the maxim to insist the man is blameworthy regardless, since "not seeing it doesn't wash." Second: a member of the reading group is genuinely torn about whether eating animals is seriously wrong — call it close to even odds, on his own report — and continues to eat meat; when he voiced his unease, another member replied with the maxim, apparently to mean that sitting at fifty-fifty doesn't let you off, and that he is obliged to act as though the stricter view were true. Third: a guest speaker, asked about a doctor in a much earlier era who performed procedures we now consider atrocities while sincerely believing them beneficial, said that whether the doctor believed it was right is beside the point, because the question is whether the act was right. These three feel like different theses wearing one sentence — the first seems to be about blame, the second about what to do under uncertainty, the third about whether an act is wrong at all — and yet nobody in the room distinguished them.

So: can you lay out, as an explicit menu rather than a single gloss, the distinct precise claims this slogan could be making? For each, I'd like the exact proposition stated (whose ignorance, of what, and excusing from what), a verdict on whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and an account of what it would take to refute it. I particularly want to know which of the readings are artifacts of the legal setting — resting on considerations about proof, deterrence of ignorance, or the publicity of enacted rules — and whether those considerations survive translation into a claim about moral blame. Please also separate, carefully, the claim that a wrong act stays wrong under ignorance from the claim that the agent stays blameworthy, and that in turn from any claim about how much blame is appropriate; tell me whether there is a reading on which the slogan comes out true but for a reason too weak to be worth repeating, and whether the half-remembered idea that blame for a bad belief must be traced back to some earlier failing in forming it is one of the readings or a separate move entirely. Since the second instance is the one that has actually changed behaviour among people I know, say explicitly which reading, if any, licenses telling someone who is merely uncertain that they must act on the more demanding option, and what would have to be true about credences across competing moral views for that to follow. I'm after the distinctions and the verdicts, not references — you needn't name sources, and please don't just tell me the phrase is ambiguous and leave it there.
