I'm TA-ing a first-year seminar called Lives and Their Uses, where we read Plutarch's Parallel Lives next to some modern biography and a couple of films, and this week's screening is Citizen Kane. My seminar leader thinks I'm overdoing the Plutarch angle but I want to hand the students an actual reading rather than the usual unreliable-narration summary, so help me treat the film as a work of biograpy and specifically as a Plutarchan one. The angle I want you to push on: in the proem to the Alexander, Plutarch says he's writing lives rather than histories, and that character shows up more reliably in trifles — a remark, a joke, a small habit — than in the big set-piece deeds like battles and sieges. Rosebud is the most famous trifle in cinema, so the obvious question is whether Welles is running Plutarch's method or parodying it. The film's whole last movement hands us a small object that seems to explain the man, but it explains him only to us, since the reporter who spent the picture collecting testimony never finds out what it means and ends up concluding that no one word can sum up a life. So is the moral lesson landing somewhere the biographer can't reach? And if it is, what does that do to Plutarch's confidence that a life can be held up as a mirror for the reader's own self-improvement — a mirror Kane, who surrounds himself with statues and reflections and never once sees himself in one, seems to be a deliberate counterexample to.

Then there's two other things I want worked in. First the portrait passage in the Lucullus, where Plutarch asks biographers to behave like painters dealing with a slight imperfection in a face: neither omit it entirely nor dwell on it, because the first course makes the portrait ugly and the second makes it unlike the sitter; he goes on to say we should treat a man's transgressions and follies as shortcomings in some particular excellence rather than as the vile products of outright baseness, partly as a way of defending human nature against the charge that it never produces a wholly good character. Kane is a film that does essentially nothing but dwell. Does that make it a failed Plutarchan portrait, or is it making the point that modern audiences want the ugliness and have decided to call that honesty? Second, the hagiography thread, which is the part students miss. Treat the News on the March newsreel as a piece of public hagiography running in reverse — an obituary that can't decide whether to canonize Kane or damn him — and treat Xanadu as a self-built reliquary, a man crating up the material evidence for his own cult. Plutarch pairs a Greek with a Roman and then closes with a comparison chapter that weighs the two against each other; Kane gives us six competing testimonies from people who each loved or resented him for their own reasons and then no comparison at all, nobody who adjudicates. What does a life look like when the synkrisis is missing and the only synkrisis on offer is the audience's own? And does the film distinguish praising someone from understanding them, a distinction I think hagiography constantly collapses?

Two cautions, and please take them seriously. Don't just turn Kane into an illustration of Plutarch — tell me where the film resists the reading and where an honest critic has to admit the fit is bad; for instance, maybe Thompson's failure is really about privacy and the limits of testimony rather than about moral education at all, and maybe the sled is a joke aimed at viewers who need a moral spelled out for them. I'd also like you to finish by saying what the film contributes to the argument about the uses of biography that a philosophical essay couldn't: what does watching a life get assembled, piece by piece, and then fail to add up teach that just stating the problem doesn't. Point me at the specific scenes you'd screen clips of — the newsreel, Thatcher's manuscript, the breakfast-table sequence, the opera debut and what follows it, the hall of mirrors, the sorting of the junk in the great hall — and say what each one is doing in the argument, because I have to justify the clips to someone with a ninety minute slot. No bibliography and no quotations please, I don't need a reading list and my students won't have the Greek, just the reading argued out.
