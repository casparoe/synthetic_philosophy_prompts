A general-interest magazine essay I read sets out Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility as follows: 'Strawson's point is that you can only be morally responsible for what you do if you are responsible for how you turned out. Everything you do flows from your character and your circumstances, and you chose neither. To be responsible for yourself you would have to have created yourself, which would mean an endless chain of prior choices, each made by an earlier self you never picked — precisely what determinism rules out. Since nothing can be its own cause, no one is ever truly morally responsible. The consequence, Strawson says, is that we should abandon blame and resentment altogether and replace them with something like the therapeutic management of dangerous people, a shift he concedes we could never actually pull off, because, as Hume showed, our reactive attitudes are natural and inescapable.'

I suspect the determinism sentence is wrong — isn't Strawson supposed to insist that his argument works whether or not determinism is true? — but I can't tell what else is off, and I don't want to dismiss a passage that may be mostly sound. Please work through it claim by claim, separating what Strawson would endorse, what is a fair simplification, and what is a genuine distortion. In particular: how wide is the demand that you be responsible for "how you turned out," given that Strawson restricts it in some way? Does the argument really need an infinite regress of choices, or is there a more basic point about what choosing presupposes? Does Strawson anywhere recommend reforming blame, punishment, or resentment, and how does he distinguish his conclusion about true moral responsibility from other notions of responsibility he is willing to keep? And is the closing appeal correctly attributed? End by assessing the argument's real dialectical force against a compatibilist who objects that Strawson has simply defined moral responsibility out of existence.
