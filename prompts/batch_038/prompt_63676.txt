ok, I need a second opinion because I got steamrolled last night and I'm not at all sure I deserved it. My friend Marguerite (everyone calls her Marg) runs a small homeschool co-op — nine kids, six to fourteen, meets in a rented church hall three days a week — and the whole program is self-consciously built out of Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education. Not "inspired by." She sat down with the book and worked out a syllabus from it, and shes the worst possible kind of person to argue with because she'll say "that isn't consistent with what I actually did in March" and then be right.

Her track record, numbered so you can point at it:

1. A parent proposed paying his kid in gaming time for good maths marks. She refused to run the scheme, said it would ruin both the maths and the kid.
2. A boy who flat out refused to read. She never made reading an assigned task. She put letters on dice and they played a game with them for a term; he reads now.
3. Cold foot-washing, thin coats, outside in winter regardless. A parent asked to exempt his son on days when he had the sniffles; she said no, and the kid didn't get sicker.
4. A boy who was genuinely good at verse and loved writing it. She discouraged the verse and steered him toward bookkeeping and some joinery.
5. She has never caned anybody. A kid who stole from another kid got no punishment whatsoever, but she made sure the whole co-op knew about it, then let it die down quietly over a week.
6. French before Latin, both by conversation with hired speakers, no grammar drills for either.
7. A six-year-old screams at the table naming one particular sweet: doesn't get it. The same kid silently wants to go outside: goes outside.
8. "Recreation" means digging in the garden and whittling spoons. She won't let them sit idle, on the grounds that idleness isn't recreation, it's just idleness.

New girl, twelve, starting in a fortnight. She was at a normal school, is well behind on arithmetic, and is genuinely frightened of it — she cries if you put a workbook in front of her. Against that she has this ferocious self-taught astronomy thing going: own notebook, moon phases logged nightly through a cheap telescope, and she's quietly teaching herself the arithmetic she needs for it without noticing that's what she's doing. The parents want four things from Marg: (a) two hours of workbook arithmetic a day at the co-op, because she has to catch up; (b) they've offered her a much better telescope for getting through it, though the mother also let slip that they were probably buying the telescope in the spring anyway; (c) the girl wrote poems about the planets at her old school, had a poetry club, is desperate to keep it going; (d) she's slight and was anaemic last year, so the mother wants her indoors on cold days, in warm clothes, with warm milk and sugar. We ended up shouting, mildly. My claim was that her real rule is the one behind 1 and 2 — never turn the thing a child already loves into the currency for the thing they hate, and never let learning become a task. Her claim was that her real rule is harden and habituate and use the child's own interests as the vehicle, which she insists is a different thing. We both think the other's rule can't survive 4.

So: what does she decide on a, b, c, d? Give me the bottom line first — three or four sentences with your actual prediction for each of the four and a confidence level, and tell me which of the four you're least sure about. Then lay out at least three candidate rules that her eight precedents are jointly consistent with, and be sure to include the deflationary one, namely that she doesn't have a general principle at all and is just following the book item by item. For each rule say what it predicts about all four sub-decisions. Identify the closest analog in her own precedents for each of the four, and if the precedents genuinely underdetermine one of them, say plainly what principle she'd be adopting by going one way rather than the other — I suspect the poetry question is where that bites, because either she has an anti-fancy rule that overrides her own inclination-following practice, or she has an inclination-following practice with one arbitrary textual exception. Also name the single further case that would best separate your top two candidate rules, and what each of them predicts about it.

Format: bottom-line prose first, then a table with these columns — rule_name (short label); statement (one sentence, in her own idiom, that could generate the precedents); explains (which numbered precedents it accounts for and which it strains or flatly contradicts); a_drills; b_telescope; c_poetry; d_cold_days (one short verdict plus a clause of reasoning each); locke_grounding (how much of the rule is actually in the book versus extrapolated by her); splitting_case (the one case that separates it from the runner-up). If nine columns is unmanageable, split it into two tables sharing rule_name.

One thing I want you to be careful about: separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually know this book. I don't want the anti-verse advice, or the status of the hardening rule when a kid is delicate or unwell, or whether Locke's last-resort permission for the rod in cases of outright defiance is a real exception or a rhetorical concession, presented as though everyone agrees. And if you're fuzzy on whether the text says something, say you're fuzzy — no reconstructed quotations, no invented section numbers, no page refs. Flagging your own uncertainty about the book is more useful to me than sounding sure.
