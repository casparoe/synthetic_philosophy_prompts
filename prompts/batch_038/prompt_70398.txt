I am auditing a graduate seminar on the philosophical prehistory of artificial intelligence this term, and one of my reading-group partners — who now works on reinforcement learning from human feedback — pressed an objection over dinner that I have not been able to shake, and that I would like you to argue against as rigorously as you can. She calls it, and I will adopt her label, the *teacher-dependence objection* to Turing's proposal for building a thinking machine out of a child machine and then educating it. The objection has three components. First, on Turing's own picture the educational process consists in a teacher applying signals of approval and disapproval to a system whose behavior is not yet settled, so every disposition the finished machine acquires is one the teacher's signals selected for. Second, the imitation game is an evaluation of verbal performance, and verbal performance is precisely what the teacher's signals were calibrated to shape; therefore a passing performance is at bottom a measurement of the teacher's judgments about what a thoughtful adult would say, transmitted through the machine as a medium. Third, the objection is supposed to be structural rather than empirical — no amount of extra training data, extra compute, or a better-behaved teacher dissolves it, because it concerns who, or what, the evidence ultimately supports. If it is right, then passing the test licenses no inference about the machine at all, and the child-programme strategy does not answer the question it was offered as an answer to.

Please make the case that the teacher-dependence objection fails, and fails on resources Turing himself supplies rather than on imports from later philosophy of mind or from contemporary machine learning. The argument should be laid out as an argument: numbered premises, an explicit conclusion, and no rhetorical padding in place of inference. I want you to work hard with the specific features of his proposal — the deliberate choice to simulate a child rather than an adult mind, the analogy he draws between that teaching process and the ordinary punishment-and-reward education of a human child, the fact that the space of possible modifications to the child machine is vastly larger than anything the teacher can survey, the role he assigns to a random element in the machine's behavior, and his observation that a teacher cannot foresee how a child under education will turn out, any more than a parent can foresee what their child will become. Draw on those features as the premises of your argument rather than as background decoration, and be explicit about which single one does the load-bearing work. Then ask yourself the harder question: is that load-bearing claim something Turing actually asserts, something he is merely entitled to given the rest of his picture, or something you are supplying on his behalf and attributing to him by reconstruction? Say which, and say whether the argument still holds if you downgrade the claim to the weakest version he is clearly entitled to. Finally, treat the objection as an opponent would: steelman a restatement of it that survives your reply — for instance, one that concedes the teacher cannot predict the outcome but insists that unpredictability is not the same thing as independence of the teacher's standards — and tell me honestly whether that restated version is fatal, merely damaging, or answerable.

Structure the response under these headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths: "The objection restated" (about 150 words, in your own words, as charitably as you can); "The argument" (about 600 words, numbered premises to conclusion); "What carries the weight" (about 250 words, on the load-bearing premise and its evidential status); "The objection rebuilt" (about 300 words, on the strongest surviving version and your verdict about it); and "Settled, contested, and merely my reading" (about 250 words). Two constraints on execution. Do not put anything in quotation marks: I cannot check quotations against the text and I would rather have a faithful paraphrase, and wherever your argument leans on a specific claim of Turing's, mark your confidence that he actually makes it as distinct from your confidence that the claim is true and that he is entitled to it. Give me no bibliography, no page numbers, and no reading list. In the final section, separate what I can take as settled among people who work on this paper — the general content and purpose of the child-programme proposal, the punishment-and-reward analogy, the appeal to a random element — from what is genuinely contested, including whether Turing's test is best read as an operational criterion of thought or as a merely suggestive heuristic, and whether he should be counted a behaviorist at all; do not let a contested reading of the paper do quiet work in your premises and then present itself as consensus at the end.
