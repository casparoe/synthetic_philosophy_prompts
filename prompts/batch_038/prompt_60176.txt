I'm marking scripts for a second-year political philosophy exam and I want a second pair of eyes on one of them. The question was:

> "Conditionality in the modern welfare state is just the old distinction between the deserving and the undeserving poor in new clothes." Critically assess. (20 marks. You may draw on the 1834 Poor Law, current UK sanctions policy, or any theorist of reciprocity.)

Here's their answer, lightly condensed but I've kept their phrasing:

"The distinction goes back at least to the Elizabethan Poor Law of 1601, which separated the impotent poor, who were to be relieved, from the sturdy beggars, who were to be set to work or punished. The 1834 Poor Law Amendment Act turned this into an administrative device. Under the principle of less eligibility, conditions inside the workhouse were to be worse than those of the poorest independent labourer outside, so that only the genuinely destitute would accept them. The great virtue of this test was that it was self-targeting: it did not require officials to judge anyone's character, because the claimant's own willingness to bear the cost did the sorting.

Speenhamland is the counter-case. Wage subsidies scaled to the price of bread and family size pauperized the labouring poor, as Polanyi showed: employers cut wages knowing the parish would top them up, so the subsidy was captured by employers and the recipients stayed poor. That establishes that topping up wages is self-defeating wherever the recipient has no bargaining power, which is why the work allowance and taper under Universal Credit should worry us for exactly the same reason.

Modern conditionality looks different but is the same mechanism. UC claimants sign a claimant commitment and can be sanctioned for not meeting it. A sanction, like the workhouse, is a cost the claimant can avoid by complying, so it too sorts the willing from the unwilling without anyone having to make a moral judgement. The evidence bears this out: the evaluations found that sanctioned claimants leave benefit faster than comparable unsanctioned ones, so the mechanism does what its defenders say it does. And the fraud rationale is empirically empty anyway — official estimates put benefit fraud well below the tax gap, so the undeserving-poor rhetoric has nothing behind it.

The most interesting survival of the distinction is the Work Capability Assessment, but here the moral judgement has been replaced by a clinical one. A points scale measures how much work a person can do, and those above the threshold are found to have limited capability for work. That is a genuine improvement, because capacity is a descriptive matter rather than an evaluative one, and it means the deserving/undeserving split has finally been dissolved into a question of need.

Finally, reciprocity. Stuart White argues that a citizen cannot claim a share of the cooperative surplus without contributing to producing it. That licenses conditionality straightforwardly: someone who refuses available work is free-riding, and sanctioning them is just enforcing a duty they accepted when they claimed. Unlike the 1834 test, this gives conditionality a principled rather than a merely deterrent basis, so reciprocity theorists should turn out to be friends of sanctions, not their critics."
