I'm in a reading group this term that's basically arguing about truthfulness and its limits, and I've spent the last two weeks building an argument I'm way too attached to. Two people in the group told me flat out that the conclusion is absurd, which either means they haven't followed the steps or means one of the steps is broken, and I genuinely can't tell which anymore. Context for why I care: my roommate is in law school and she brought up the old Supreme Court perjury case — Bronston, I think — where a witness gave an answer that was literally true but was clearly engineered to make the examiner draw a false conclusion, and the Court said that's not perjury. Everyone I know reacts to that with "well, obviously the law is letting him off on a technicality." I wanted to show that the reaction is right and not just a vibe: that the literal-truth escape hatch has no moral content at all.

Here's what I've got.

(1) What makes lying wrong, when it is wrong, is that you take over your audience's mind for purposes of your own — you get them to believe something you selected, so you've used them as a tool rather than letting them work out how things stand on their own.
(2) In misleading, you say something you believe is true while intending your hearer to draw a conclusion you believe is false. The hearer still ends up holding a belief you selected, by design. That they supplied the last inferrence themselves is cosmetic, because you laid the track it ran on.
(3) So lying and misleading are wrong for the same reason and, other things equal, to the same degree — the distinction carries no moral weight whatsoever.
(4) Now widen it. Every assertion invites inferences the speaker neither intends nor controls: conversational defaults, implicature, the hearer's own background beliefs doing most of the work.
(5) If the thing that matters is responsibility for beliefs you've designed into your audience, then you're also on the hook for false beliefs your assertion foreseeably produces whether or not you meant them to. Anything less lets you launder a lie by making the hearer take the final step, which (3) just ruled out.
(6) But almost any assertion foreseeably produces some false belief in somebody — people mishear, overgeneralize, fill in details you never supplied.
(7) Therefore nearly all communication is already culpable under this account, so (1) can't be what makes lying wrong. Either lying isn't specially wrong, or nothing is, and we should stop treating truthfulness as a distinct duty at all.

The parts I want to keep are (1) and (3); (7) is the one I can't live with, and I suspect I've been defending it out of stubborness. So: tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs me — in particular whether I lose (3), which was the entire point. Be straight with me about whether you think the argument is actually valid and I just have to swallow a bitter premise, or whether there's a hidden slide in it, because if there is I want to know exactly where and why the slide was so easy to make. I'd also like the alternatives priced: what a defender of a real lying/misleading distinction has to give up (some norm internal to assertion, presumably, or a story about who does the epistemic work), and what I have to give up if I fold and concede that misleading is less bad than lying rather than just differently bad. And say how confident you actually are in your diagnosis — I'd rather get a rough answer with a stated confidence level and a clear flag on the one claim you're least sure of than something smooth that papers over the hard part.
