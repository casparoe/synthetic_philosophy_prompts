I hold the faculty seat on my university's Employee Health Plan Appeals Panel, which decides contested claims under the plan's medical-necessity rider. The rider covers treatment of "a disease, disorder, or pathological condition" and excludes "conditions constituting normal human variation," and it defines neither phrase; counsel drafted it, and no one with training in the philosophy of medicine has ever looked at it. Before the panel is an appeal from a research librarian with a marked delayed sleep phase. Every measure the plan's medical reviewer cites — hormonal, metabolic, neurocognitive — sits inside population-typical ranges, and his written rationale rests entirely on the absence of measurable physiological dysfunction. The appellant's evidence is functional and situational: under the library's fixed 8:00 a.m. opening schedule she has accumulated four documented performance deficiencies in two years, and her department's own productivity data show her output above the median for her rank once her hours are shifted. The reviewer concedes in writing that the requested light-and-melatonin protocol would reverse the deficiencies.

Two features make the case harder than a coverage decision usually is. First, the reviewer's normality claim turns on how the reference class is drawn: against all adults her age she is an outlier, against adults of her age and sex she is near-typical, and the panel last year paid for a comparable protocol for a night-shift technician without anyone raising the question at all, so consistency cuts in a direction no one has yet articulated. Second, the cheapest resolution is not a coverage decision but a change to her assigned hours, which is outside this panel's remit; referring the matter to the office that controls scheduling would delay any treatment by at least a full semester, during which the deficiencies continue to accrue against her.

Draft the opinion I will table at the meeting. It should state a definite disposition — cover, deny, cover subject to conditions, or refer — together with a time frame for implementation; it should propose replacement language for the "normal human variation" exclusion that a panel of non-specialists can actually apply to future cases, and say how that language disposes of the night-shift technician precedent; and it should give the strongest available argument for the disposition you reject, stated in terms its proponents would recognize rather than in a weakened form. End with a single line beginning "Disposition:" followed by your recommendation in five words or fewer.
