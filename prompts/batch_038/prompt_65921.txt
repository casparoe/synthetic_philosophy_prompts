I'm the TA for a 300-level comparative ethics seminar and the prof bailed off to a conference the week the final was supposed to be written, so it's landed on me. We did about four weeks on the Analects and the Mencius and one week on the contemporary role-ethics reading of them — the view that persons aren't autonomous selves who subsequently take on roles but are constituted by their roles, so that ren is something achieved in and through particular relations rather than a property an isolated individual could have. The class is split in an annoying way: half of them have read a fair amount of classical Chinese material and no western moral theory at all, the other half is the mirror image, and both halves have started throwing the phrase role ethics around like it's a settled label for a settled position. It's a closed-book final, 75 minutes, one essay question, 25 marks, and they're allowed a single handwritten crib sheet which in practice means half the class will walk in with a paragraph of definitions.

I want the question to turn on a difficulty I've been calling the forfeiture/residue pair, and I specifically dont want those words to appear in the question itself — I want the students to have to notice that there are two separate problems sitting in the same spot. The first: if a person just is her roles, what do we say about someone who holds a role and systematically violates it? Mencius, answering a king who asks whether a minister may slay his sovereign, in effect says he has heard of the execution of a mere fellow but not of the slaying of a ruler — which looks like it makes holding a role normative and forfeitable rather than merely descriptive, and the ruler-be-ruler / father-be-father material plus the stolen-sheep story the Duke of She tells pull in the same direction. The second problem is the one I care more about: if roles can be forfeited, is there anything left in the person who has forfeited every one of them, and if there is, hasn't the role ethicist quietly reintroduced the bare choosing self she was trying to get rid of? A good version of this question should make it impossible to score by explaining what role ethics is, and should force them to say whether the two problems share a solution or need separate ones. Please build in a concrete case to argue over rather than leaving it abstract — something like an adult child whose father was violent and then absent, who is now being asked to care for him in old age — and note that the texts' own remonstrance material is where I expect the interesting answers to go.

Deliverables: the question as the students will see it, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme I want the 25 marks broken into named components, each with a one-line note about the specific mistake that costs those marks — not "fails to address the question," but the actual confusion, e.g. treating role ethics as a species of virtue ethics, reading the remonstrance passages as an escape hatch that lets you exit a role rather than as a way of inhabiting it, treating li as etiquette, or dropping in Williams's point about the man choosing between his wife and a stranger as though it settled something rather than noticing it's an objection mounted from inside impartialist ethics. Then a short paragraph naming the exact move that separates an A answer from a B one — a named move, not a vibe. Don't pad any of it with citations, page numbers or a reading list; I can't check them and the students won't have them either, so anything the model answer leans on should be identifiable from the content of the passages themselves. Last thing: tell me honestly whether 75 minutes is realistic for the question as you've written it, and if not, what you'd cut.
