I'm a third-year PPE student taking a seminar this term on the measurement of social progress, and for the past three weeks we have been working through the World Happiness Report and the wider apparatus of subjective wellbeing indicators. Our final assessment is a group policy brief written for a hypothetical client: the national statistics office of a small, upper-middle-income country that wants to adopt one official wellbeing target — a number it publishes annually, that ministries are held to, and that the press can use to ask whether life is getting better. The client is explicitly *not* trying to win international league tables; it wants a target that survives being aimed at. My group has split into factions, one of which is staffed by economists who regard affect measures as noise and another by people who have been reading too much capability-approach philosophy and refuse to accept anything that isn't a dashboard. I would rather not simply referee their shouting match, so I want your independent judgment.

We have shortlisted seven candidates, and I would like you to sort them into tiers on two distinct axes. Axis A is construct validity: how well does the indicator track what we actually care about when we claim that a population's lives are going well? Axis B is Goodhart robustness: if the government announced this measure as its official target and ministers were rewarded for movements in it, how badly would the number come apart from the underlying thing within fifteen years? The two tier lists should be kept separate, because a candidate can do very well on one and very badly on the other, and I want one or two sentences per candidate per axis explaining the placement. The candidates are: (1) the mean life evaluation on a Cantril-style 0–10 self-anchoring ladder of the kind the Gallup World Poll collects; (2) global life satisfaction as measured by multi-item survey scales; (3) affect balance, i.e. the frequency of positive minus negative affect reported for yesterday, whether by experience sampling or day reconstruction; (4) a eudaimonic measure capturing whether people find their lives meaningful and worthwhile and feel in control of them; (5) an objective-list dashboard of the Gross National Happiness or Better Life Index sort, aggregating health, education, income, housing, safety, social support, freedom and generosity; (6) a distributionally sensitive reformulation — for instance the share of the population above some threshold of life evaluation, or the mean evaluation of the worst-off fifth; and (7) one member's idiosyncratic proposal, borrowed from health economics, of wellbeing-adjusted life-years: the population's years of life summed after being weighted by average experienced wellbeing in each year. If two of these collapse into one another for practical purposes, say so and merge them rather than padding the ranking.

On top of the tier lists, please give me the deliverable I actually need: a weight allocation across the candidates as numbers between 0 and 1 summing to exactly 1, representing how much of a composite national index you would assign to each, together with a sentence on the trade-offs you made. Zero is an entirely acceptable answer for anything you think should not appear in the index at all. Finally, one calibration question. The country that has topped the international happiness rankings for most of the past decade beats a number of other wealthy European democracies by a margin that is small in absolute terms — a few tenths of a point on the ladder — but decisive for the league table. Give me a single number between 0 and 1 for the fraction of that margin you would attribute to measurement artefact rather than to a genuine difference in how well life is going: scale-use and reference-point variation across populations, response culture and translation, and the fact that a self-anchoring ladder invites people to compare themselves to an anchor that moves with national circumstances. Tell me what kind of evidence would move you up or down from that number. I am not asking for a bibliography, footnotes, or citations of any kind — I want committed, defensible numbers and your reasoning for them, not hedges.
