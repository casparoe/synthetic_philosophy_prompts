So, slightly embarrassing situation. I write novels — six of them, the last one about a woman whose account of her brother's drowning keeps changing across three interleaved timelines — and through some institutional mix-up I've been put on the opening plenary of a three-day conference on evolution and narrative, where I'll be the only person in the room who has never run a regression. Twenty minutes to present an essay I wrote for a magazine. The essay runs about a fortnight after the conference, so if it gets shredded on stage I still have time to fix it, which is the one good thing about this.

The argument, roughly: the appetite for story is a spandrel. Nothing selected for narrative. Selection built a causal-modeling engine (what happens next if I do this — useful for planning, and for assigning blame) and a social-monitoring engine (who's allied with whom, who's lying, who's free-riding). Story is the shadow those two cast on each other when they run together in the same head. I'm borrowing the logic from Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper, but the part I think is actually mine is this: I grant that the *form* of the novel is genuinely designed — three-act shape, withheld information, resolution, the whole apparatus. The designer just isn't natural selection. It's four hundred years of novelists, readers, printers, prize committees and bankruptcy, filtering variants. Real design, second-hand design. I also claim it isn't an exaptation, because nothing was ever co-opted by selection *for* the storytelling job; the co-option happened inside cultural history, which is a different kind of event. Two of my observations: literary forms churn wildly century to century while the appetite doesn't budge at all, and people sob over deaths they know perfectly well are fabricated, which is not how a truth-tracking device is supposed to behave.

The panel I'm up against: two senior literary Darwinists, one committed to story as an adaptation for tracking coalitions, one to the fiction-as-flight-simulator line; a reading psychologist who runs eye-tracking and mind-reading studies on readers and is quietly furious about the replication mess in that literature; a field anthropologist with thirty years on forager storytelling, including what gets told at night versus in daylight, who thinks oral tradition is far more conserved across cultures than I'm claiming; a philosophy-of-biology grad student who does taxonomy policing; and one computational person who models stylistic change and will want to know how my cultural selection is distinguishable from drift.

What I need is the prep sheet, not a defense of me. The objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way each of them would phrase it — the anthropologist's and the grad student's will not sound alike — with a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds. I'm a terrible live speaker and I loose the thread if the sentences are written rather than spoken, so make them sayable. For each one tell me where my reply is soft, and which objection forces a concession I should make in the first three minutes to buy credibility cheap. Then: the single reply that has to land or the whole talk is dead; the two objections where the honest answer is that I'd have to revise the essay rather than parry; and whether I should embrace the old line about music being a kind of dessert engineered to tickle circuits built for other purposes, or run from it. Also give me a one-line version of each objection short enough to put in a character's mouth — that's how I check I've actually understood it and not just memorised it.

And finish with the question I'm praying nobody asks. I have a candidate — something like "does your account explain why you wrote six of these?" — but tell me if there's a worse one in the room. No reading list, please: I'll be doing all this on trains between book events with no signal, so just tell me what to say.
