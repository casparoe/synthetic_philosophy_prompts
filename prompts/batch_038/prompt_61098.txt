I have served for six years on the acquisitions committee of a county historical society — volunteer-run, founded in 1898, roughly 3,400 linear feet of holdings, most of which are the records of the rail companies, the lumber mills, the county board, and the families who owned them, along with a Works Progress Administration photograph survey from the 1930s. Our job is to decide which offered collections to accession; we meet the first Thursday of the month, and the minutes record our decisions and, sometimes, the reasons for them. A proposal is on next month's agenda that I do not think our record settles, and I want to reason about it the way a court reasons from precedent: what we should vote if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which of our past decisions is the closest analog and where the analogy breaks, and — if our past decisions genuinely leave this open — what principle we would be committing ourselves to with each possible vote.

The five most contested decisions of the last decade, with the reasoning as it appears in the minutes:

1. 2016, accepted without restriction. The day books, ledgers, and correspondence of a Black funeral home director, 1911–1968, donated by his granddaughter. The minutes note that for the county's Black residents in that period our holdings contained nothing but obituaries clipped from white-owned newspapers and entries in the county poor records, and that a funeral director's ledgers capture births, deaths, debts, church affiliations, and family networks recorded nowhere else.

2. 2018, accepted, sealed for fifty years except for aggregate research. About forty boxes of case files from the county Department of Public Welfare, 1948–1972, offered by the county after an office renovation. The files name recipients and list household income and medical conditions; they were written by caseworkers about people who did not choose to be written about, and several recipients' children are still living here. No one was consulted before the offer. The minutes record two hours of discussion about privacy and the conclusion that returning or destroying the files would be worse than holding them under seal.

3. 2019, declined. A photograph album and bundle of letters belonging to a woman who worked as a live-in domestic for a prominent local family from 1927 to 1961, offered by an out-of-state collector who had bought the lot at an estate sale. The album includes pictures of the woman's own children, a hospital admission, and notes in her employer's hand about her reliability. Her daughter, then eighty-one and living twelve miles away, wrote asking us not to take it. The minutes record only that the offer was declined at the request of the subject's family and that the collector was informed.

4. 2021, accepted, with names redacted on request. A retired state trooper's personal notebooks from eleven years on the county's vagrancy detail, 1955–1969: names, dates, places of arrest, and his own marginal remarks. He was the writer, not the person written about. His estate offered the notebooks; nobody objected; several of the people named are dead and several were living, and a local journalist published a well-received series drawing on them. The minutes observe that this was the only surviving record of how the detail actually operated.

5. 2023, accepted, with a five-year moratorium on any exhibit funded by the donor. The papers of the director of the city's redevelopment authority, 1958–1974, including the clearance files for the neighborhood demolished for the interstate, donated by his children through a foundation bearing his name. The foundation asked for its mark on the finding aid and proposed a jointly funded exhibit; there was an active public argument at the time about the highway and about who had been consulted in 1961. We took the papers, declined the exhibit money, and imposed the moratorium. Two members dissented: one argued that we had become the default destination for the papers of men who needed to look better than they had, the other that refusing would have left the clearance documented only by the newspapers.

Now the new offer. The city Department of Health, clearing a records center ahead of a move, has offered us the complete run of its sanitary inspection photographs and logs for the tenement districts, 1912–1939: about ninety boxes, some six thousand glass-plate and film negatives with contact sheets, plus the inspectors' log books. Inspectors photographed interiors door to door — kitchens, bedding, shared toilets, children in hallways — and annotated many of the images with the family name, the address, and remarks about cleanliness, congestion, and morality. Nearly everyone photographed is dead. There is no objection from anyone: a neighborhood history group, drawn largely from descendants of the immigrant families who lived in those districts, has written to say it wants the material preserved and would like copies for its own meeting house. The department has attached two requests — that it fund the digitization outright through a grant it has already secured, and that the accession be announced jointly with the department as part of its 150th-anniversary open-records campaign, with the director speaking at our annual meeting and the department's name on the collection's landing page. The department is also, this year, defending a lawsuit about its current inspection practices. We have eleven linear feet of free shelf space, no money for digitization, and no conservator. Two colleagues have already told me how they will vote. One says these are the only images of those households that will ever exist, and that we would be refusing them for a reason having nothing to do with what is in the boxes. The other says we would be letting the department turn a century of photographing other people's kitchens into a birthday present for itself, and that the right answer is to tell the department to send the boxes to the neighborhood group instead.

So: a recommended vote, with the reasoning from our own record that supports it; the closest analog among the five decisions and the points at which that analogy fails; the candidate principles our five decisions are jointly consistent with, stated precisely enough to govern offers we have not yet seen, since I suspect more than one principle fits everything above and I would like to see which ones are ruled out by 2018 and 2021 in particular; a prediction of how each of my two colleagues' principles would resolve two or three plausible future offers of your choosing; and a plain statement of what we would be committing ourselves to, and giving up, whichever way we vote. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine this, say so rather than manufacturing a decision rule to hide the gap — and then tell me what further hypothetical offer, one a society like ours could realistically receive, would best discriminate among the principles that do fit our record, so that we can decide this one knowing which of them we are adopting.
