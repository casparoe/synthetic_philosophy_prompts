Just got off a four hour argument with my roommate, who's a med student and therefore thinks he wins by default, and I want a sanity check that isn't the survey-course version of this debate. The thing I ended up defending is a pretty specific flavor of panpsychism, so please go after that flavor and not the generic one. Roughly: I'm happy to accept that there are intrinsic phenomenal properties all the way down, but I reject the constitutive gloss, the idea that my experience is literally built out of or composed of the experiences of the fundamental bits. What I say instead is that the micro-experiences ground my being a subject without being parts of it — grounding without composition. My macro subject is numerically distinct from all the micro subjects, and none of them are constituents of it. I've been using a skyline analogy, which is a bad analogy and I know it's bad: a city's skyline isn't made out of the interiors of the individual buildings, but which buildings there are and where they stand fully fixes whether there's a skyline and what it looks like. The payoff is that the old James-style complaint — pack a hundred feelings together as tightly as you please and you still just have a hundred separate feelings rather than a hundred-and-first one — never gets off the ground against me, because I never claimed any new feeling got assembled out of the old ones.

So what would count against this particular view? I want the defeaters ranked, fatal at the top, merely awkward at the bottom, and for each one tell me whether a serious defender should concede it as a real cost or is actually entitled to wave it off. Things I especially want hit: (1) does grounding-without-composition solve the combination problem or just rename it — I still owe some story about why THIS arrangement of micro subjects grounds a unified subject while a random pile of the exact same micro subjects grounds nothing at all, and if that story is just brute, am I now an emergentist wearing a panpsychist coat; (2) the collapse worry in general — at what point does my view stop being panpsychism and become straight property dualism, and would that be a defeat or secretly the destination; (3) the empirical stuff he kept throwing at me, anaesthesia, split-brain patients, the fact that something like 80% of the brain's neurons are down in the cerebellum and you can lose big chunks of it without obviously losing consciousness — can any of that genuinely bear on a view like mine, or does it only demonstrate that I've insulated myself; (4) whether the view predicts anything at all that a plain physicalist rival doesn't, or whether it's purely interpretive garnish on the same data.

And be blunt about the unfalsifiability charge, because that was his parting shot and I couldnt answer it at 1am. I sold him the view on parsimony and continuity with physics, nothing spooky added to the ontology, but if no experiment and no thought experiment could conceivably count against it, does that motivation survive, or does it actively backfire — i.e. is a view that risks nothing still entitled to claim it's the cheaper one? I'd rather hear a verdict than a balanced menu: where is the exposure worst, which single defeater would actually make me give it up, and how badly insulated is the view overall, even if the honest answer is "very insulated, and that's fine" or "very insulated, and that's fatal."
