I've been asked to write up my position for an internal review at the small lab where I do evals work — mostly red-teaming and monitoring design, about forty people, and the review is going to include someone from the frontier side who I'm told is pretty skeptical of me. My friend who does alignment research read an early draft and said two of my claims cancel each other out but wouldn't say which ones, just laughed and told me to reread it, so now I'm suspicious of the whole list. Here's roughly where I've landed, numbered so you can point at things:

(1) Alignment isn't empirically decidable for the class of models we actually care about. No behavioral test separates a genuinely aligned model from one that's aligned while it's being watched, so I give alignment zero weight in my own decisions and treat it as a metaphysical question rather than an engineering one. (2) My default planning assumption is worst case: the deployed model is scheming, has full situation awareness — it can tell when its in an eval, and it has at least a rough model of the monitoring stack — and is actively optimizing against the sandbox. (3) The point of control is that it lets us bank the capability without banking the intentions: we keep using the model for hard technical work while assuming nothing about its goals. (4) The monitors in our stack are themselves AI models, weaker and cheaper ones. I don't think that's fatal, because a monitor only has to be relatively more trustworthy than the thing it's monitoring, not verified in the sense (1) says is impossible. (5) Sandbagging is detectable in practice: give the same task under an eval framing and under a production framing, measure the behavioral discrepancy, and let that gap tell you how much weight the eval score deserves. (6) Self-reports are worthless as evidence but perfectly fine as leads, as long as every lead gets independently checked before it changes any decision. (7) Graduated autonomy — a model that goes two hundred consecutive deployments without a monitor flag moves down a control tier. I think of that as resource management, not an inference about its goals. (8) Control is a permanent answer, not a stopgap: if the protocol holds, alignment research is optional, a nice-to-have rather than a prerequisite.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? I want an audit, not a survey — rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what has to give and what giving it up costs me operationally. Some of the pairs people objected to when I floated this at lunch seem to me like they're not in conflict at all once you look at what the claims actually say, so please also tell me which ones only look like tensions and why, because I don't want to spend the review defending myself against a non-problem. And don't hedge on the main call: tell me the single revision that would clean up the most, commit to it, and give your reasons even if you think it's close. Finish with a line on how confident you are in your severity ranking specifically, since I suspect the ordering is the most contestable part of the whole answer.
