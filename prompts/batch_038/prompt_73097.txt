The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 paid every surviving person of Japanese ancestry who had been confined in American internment camps during the war a flat $20,000, along with a formal presidential apology and a public-education fund. It did not ask what anyone had lost. An earlier 1948 statute had already permitted claims for provable property damage and had left most internees far short of whole; the 1988 scheme abandoned that proof requirement and paid the same sum to a farmer who had lost a large productive operation and to a laborer who had lost a roomful of belongings. Eligibility ran to persons who had themselves been confined, not to their descendants; the class of claimants was defined by Japanese ancestry even though the wrong was described as racial prejudice; and nothing was recovered from the neighbors and competitors who had bought confiscated farms and businesses at distress prices — the whole cost fell on the federal treasury.

Tell me what theory of rectificatory justice that design presupposes. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with: do the flat sum and the dropped proof-of-loss requirement commit the program to a view on which the injury is dignitary and its repair is acknowledgment, rather than a measurable loss that can be compensated, or can administrative cost and the decay of evidence account for those features on an ordinary compensatory theory? Why would a statute define the victim class by ancestry while naming racial prejudice as the wrong? Which features are philosophically underdetermined, so that an expressivist account, a pragmatic corrective-justice account, and a forward-looking civic account would all license them? And what would have to change in the design, not merely in its rhetoric, under the view that the beneficiaries of the confiscations and not only the state owe the repair?

Close by saying which of these answers you hold least confidently, what argument or evidence would move you to a different verdict, and where your reconstruction is most likely to have gone wrong.
