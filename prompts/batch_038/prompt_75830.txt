I am preparing a chapter for a collected volume in the philosophy of social science, and my assigned topic is the methodological individualism versus holism debate. After months of reading both camps, my working impression is that the disagreement has not moved for decades, despite both sides being populated by careful people who agree about a great deal. What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a survey of the positions and not a verdict on which side is right.

Here is the setup as I understand it. Both sides now concede something like supervenience: no difference in social facts without some difference in facts about individuals. Individualists insist further that explanation must terminate in, or be reducible to, facts about individuals — their beliefs, dispositions, relations, and actions — and that treating social entities, structures, or systems as causes is at best shorthand for such facts and at worst reification. Holists and non-reductive social ontologists reply that macro-level properties are real, that they constrain and enable individual action, and that paraphrasing them into individual-level facts loses precisely what does the explanatory work: the pattern, the structure, the feedback loop. The exhibit that every discussion returns to is still Durkheim on suicide rates.

My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than adopt: once supervenience is conceded there is no metaphysical room left for the two sides to occupy, and the residual dispute is (i) partly definitional, turning on whether relational facts about individuals — who is married to whom, who reports to whom, who is tied to whom in a network — count as individual-level facts, since admitting them makes individualism true but toothless and excluding them makes it false but unbelievable; and (ii) partly a disagreement about norms of explanation rather than about the world, specifically about where an explanation is permitted to stop, which varies with the question asked and the audience addressed. On this reading the debate looks substantive only because both sides state methodological preferences in ontological vocabulary.

Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, partly right, or wrong, with reasons drawn from what each side actually argues rather than from generic characterizations. Please address at least the following:

- Is there a genuine metaphysical residue — downward causation, emergent social powers, the identity and persistence conditions of social entities — that survives the concession of supervenience, and if so, what would it take for a claim of that kind to be more than a redescription?
- Does the dispute in practice get relocated into causal modeling? Much quantitative social science treats macro variables — an inequality index, a measure of social trust, an institutional dummy — as causes of individual outcomes. Whether that is legitimate looks like a question about which interventions are admissible in a causal model: if only individual-level interventions are possible, macro variables cannot be causes, yet many interventions are implementable only at the macro level. Is this a decidable technical question that the philosophical debate has been circling, and would settling it resolve anything the two sides care about?
- How should the ecological fallacy be scored? Individualists have argued from the fact that aggregate correlations need not mirror individual-level relations that macro explanation is unreliable; the converse error, sometimes called the atomistic fallacy, is equally well documented. Does that pair of failures support individualism, holism, or neither?
- Has the mechanism program in analytical sociology dissolved the debate, relocated it to a different question, or simply stopped asking it?

Finally, state what would count as progress on this disagreement given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a decisive case study, a change of question, or a demonstration that one side has been answering a question the other never asked. If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, argue for that explicitly, and explain why the debate has nonetheless stayed alive.

No references or bibliography, please; I will supply those, and positions and arguments should be identified by description. Roughly 1,800 words. Be committed: a diagnosis concluding that both sides have a point and that the question is ambiguous will not serve my purpose, because ambiguity has to be located precisely — in which terms, on which side, and with what consequence for the rest of the dispute.
