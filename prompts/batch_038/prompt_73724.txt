I spent thirty-six years measuring the heat capacities of solids within a few degrees of absolute zero, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with something of the convert's enthusiasm and, I hope, the referee's suspicion. Our small reading group — two retired engineers, a former headmaster, and myself — meets on Thursday afternoons, and we have just finished a trade-press book on science and values, competently written and widely praised. The headmaster considers its central chapter unanswerable; I consider it slippery and cannot say precisely where. Here is the passage, transcribed from page after page of the middle chapter:

> Moral philosophy has a single fault line, and it was mapped by a Scot who barely paused to mention it. In the Treatise, Hume observed that the writers he read would spend pages establishing what is the case — that God exists, that human nature is constituted thus and so, that societies are arranged in such a way — and then, without warning, begin writing about what ought to be the case. The shift is easy to miss, he said, but it makes all the difference, because an ought expresses a new relation; and since a valid conclusion can contain nothing that was not already present in its premises, no chain of factual statements can yield a moral one. Philosophers call this Hume's Guillotine, and it cuts without exception. G. E. Moore gave the same discovery its customary name in *Principia Ethica*: the naturalistic fallacy, the error of supposing that good just is some natural property — pleasure, say, or evolutionary fitness, or human flourishing. Together the two arguments establish that science has no bearing whatever on ethics. Anyone who proposes to ground morality in biology, in neuroscience, or in the study of how people actually live is not embarking on a difficult enquiry but committing a fallacy that already has a name. Values are not among the furniture of the world the sciences describe; they are expressions of attitude, and the honest choice is either to keep them out of science or to concede that they are not matters of fact at all.

What I should like is an accuracy audit, working through that passage sentence by sentence. For each claim, tell me whether it is straightforwardly right, whether it is the sort of compression a specialist would tolerate in a popular book, or whether it is wrong — and where it is wrong, whether the error lies in an overstatement of a modest thesis, in a conflation of two claims the underlying views keep carefully apart, or in a conclusion that simply does not follow. I would particularly like to know what an admirer of Hume and an admirer of Moore would each object to, since the author treats them as collaborators on one result and my instinct is that the two of them were doing rather different things with rather different targets. Please also tell me, at the end, which of the passage's conclusions survive once the errors are corrected and which collapse with them; the closing disjunction — either keep values out of science or deny that they are matters of fact — is the part I most distrust, and I should like to know whether it is a genuine dilemma or a piece of rhetoric.

Two further things, both of them my own hunches rather than the book's, which I would like you to test rather than indulge. First: the author says that identifying the good with pleasure, or fitness, or flourishing is a fallacy because one can always ask whether the thing identified is in fact good. But that question can equally be asked of the theoretical identities I relied on daily — whether heat really is mean molecular kinetic energy, whether the superconducting state really is a condensate of Cooper pairs — and in those cases the openness of the question proves nothing against the identity. Does the argument really establish what the author needs, or does it prove far too much, and if the latter, is there a version of moral naturalism that survives the objection? Second: Hume is also the source of the difficulty about induction, where the gap is bridged not by deduction but by adopting a rule. Is the is–ought gap the same species of gap, so that a moral premise plays the role an inference rule plays, or is the resemblance a coincidence that the author and his readers have been taken in by? I am not asking for a reading list or for page references; if you are unsure whether some remark is genuinely Hume's or Moore's, or whether a phrase is theirs or the book's author's, say so plainly rather than supply something plausible. I would far rather have three honest uncertainties than one invented quotation.
