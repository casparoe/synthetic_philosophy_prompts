I'm one of nine members on a regional transit authority board (mid-size US city, ~450k people). In three weeks we vote on a staff proposal that I'd call a futarchy pilot, though the staff memo doesn't use that word anywhere.

The decision on the table: whether to add a late-night crosstown bus route. Staff peg the operating cost at $1.1M a year for three years, and their ridership forecast is basically "probably fine," based on a survey of a few hundred people.

The proposal is that instead of voting our judgment, we commit now, in writing, to a decision rule. We'd run an internal prediction market open to employees and residents, with a $2,000 prize pool, on one binary contract: it resolves YES if, conditional on the route actually running, average weekday boardings over the first twelve months exceed 400. A YES share pays $1 if it resolves YES. If the market price sits at or above $0.60 on the closing date, the route gets funded automaticly. Below that, it's dead. No further board discussion either way.

The people on the board pushing for it make a point I don't have a good answer to: our public hearings are captured by the same twenty people who always show up, whereas a market lets a night-shift nurse who actually rides that corridor put money on what she knows. And the binding-our-hands part is the whole point, because if we keep discretion we'll cave to pressure after the fact and nobody will ever trust a forecast we publish again.

I have three worries and I'm honestly not sure which survive scrutiny.

(1) The price now decides the action, so moving the price is worth more to a mover than the money at risk. There's a riders' association with maybe thirty members, at least one of whom has a few hundred dollars to spend. In a normal forecast market a manipulator gets punished by being wrong; here they get the policy they wanted as the payoff, and being wrong costs them gift cards.

(2) Something about the bears that I can't state cleanly. If I'm confident the route will fail, the only way I collect is if the price ends up above the threshold anyway and the route runs and flops. If I succeed at what I'm trying to do — push it below $0.60 — the contract never resolves and I get nothing. So does the price end up reflecting mostly the bullish information, because the pessimists have no way to be paid for being right? Or have I confused myself here; maybe a bear buys early and the refund... staff never said what happens to the pool if a contract never resolves, which is its own gap. Sort this one out and tell me whether it's a real structural tilt or a nothing-burger.

(3) A $2,000 pool is deciding $3.3M. The people whose guesses we're outsourcing bear none of the cost of being wrong, which seems to gut the skin-in-the-game pitch that makes this attractive in the first place.

Draft the memo I'll circulate to the other eight before the vote. It needs to land somewhere definite — fund it as written, kill it, or fund it with named changes to the design — and give reasons a member could actually argue back against, not a balanced tour of considerations. Engage the supporters' strongest point on its own terms, especially the commitment one; if it beats my objections, say so, because I'll change my vote. And if you think one of my three worries is bogus, or that I've described the setup in a way that matters and got it wrong, say that plainly rather than working politely inside my framing. I also want to know where you're genuinely confident and where you're extrapolating from general principles to a case you haven't seen.
