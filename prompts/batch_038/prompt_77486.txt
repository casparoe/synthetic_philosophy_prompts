I hold a view about the centipede game that I expect most game theorists would reject out of hand, and I would like it stress-tested rather than defended. Here is the view, as compactly as I can state it.

First, I think the so-called paradox is not a puzzle about rationality but a reductio of the vocabulary in which it is formulated. Common knowledge of rationality in a finite game of perfect information is not merely a false-but-coherent premise of the kind one assumes for the sake of a derivation. It is an incoherent premise: there is no assignment of beliefs to agents who are themselves carrying out the backward-induction reasoning that satisfies it, because that very reasoning is what determines what rationality requires at the nodes the premise declares unreachable. The familiar dismissal — the premises are false, so the conclusion is vacuous — therefore understates the damage. On my reading nothing follows from the premise, not even the conditional, because the antecedent has no models.

Second, what replaces it is a belief-revision account. At a node that was supposed to be unreachable, the rational player's response is to revise her beliefs about her opponent's rationality, about the payoffs, or about the description of the game she is actually playing; once that revision is made explicit, the instruction to take at every node no longer goes through. Continued passing can then be rational without anyone's having been irrational.

Third, and this is the part I most want examined, I claim the view is empirical and not merely therapeutic. The amount of passing near the start of a game should be a function of the players' prior confidence in one another's rationality, and the node at which a game ends should be predictable from that prior together with the length of the game. Anything that raises or lowers that prior — knowledge of the opponent's experience, whether the opponent is a person or a program, whether the same pairing will meet again — should shift the terminal node in the corresponding direction.

What would count against this? I am looking for a map of the position's exposure, not a rebuttal, and not a reading list; give me the arguments themselves rather than pointers to where they appear.

Specifically: rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which formal results would actually contradict the first claim — a precise epistemic model in which common knowledge of rationality holds in the centipede, a derivation of backward induction from premises weaker than it, or a demonstration that the relevant belief revision has no unique solution — and which of these merely redescribe my claim in a different notation? On the empirical side, which findings would be decisive rather than embarrassing: a terminal-node distribution that does not move when prior confidence is manipulated, an account of the same data by a single noise parameter that makes no reference to beliefs about the opponent at all, or an invariance across conditions my view says should differ? Note where the observed pattern — first-node take far from universal, take rates rising as the game proceeds — genuinely supports my third claim and where it is equally congenial to views I reject.

Then address the two worries I cannot resolve myself. Does the view collapse into a trembling-hand or stochastic-choice treatment, and if it is empirically indistinguishable from one, does the philosophical claim about common knowledge survive or become decoration? And is the second claim insulated from refutation by offering the revising player three separate attributions for any single anomaly — the opponent's irrationality, payoff uncertainty, misdescription — in which case say whether that insulation is a defect for a view of this kind or simply what Bayesian flexibility looks like elsewhere in the philosophy of science.

Finally, distinguish what a committed defender of this position would concede as counting against it from what they would dismiss as resting on a misreading, say what the view stands or falls with outside the centipede game — a general thesis about counterfactual reasoning in games, or about the semantics of common knowledge operators, or about whether epistemic solution concepts can bear empirical weight at all — and identify the single line of evidence I am most likely to be confronted with and least equipped to answer. I should add what moves me most: the possibility that a rigorous model satisfying common knowledge of rationality in this game exists, in which case my first claim is simply false and I am left with a quibble about interpretation.

For each item, tell me how confident you are that it is a genuine defeater rather than my own view returned to me in critical clothing, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of — indicating whether that uncertainty comes from the game theory, from the philosophy, or from not knowing what the experimental record actually shows.
