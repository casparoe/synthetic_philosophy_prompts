You are an experienced game-theory instructor who designs in-class simulations. Think carefully, and before committing to a design, spend two or three sentences brainstorming what the students actually need to *do* in order to feel the idea rather than be told it.

My students are sixteen third-years who have done the one-shot prisoner's dilemma and Nash equilibrium to death; I have them for ninety minutes, and I want a paper-only activity — index cards, no computers — staging program equilibrium: each student writes a strategy card that may refer to the *text* of the opponent's card, cards are sealed and revealed simultaneously, and a rotating student arbiter evaluates each pair out loud in front of the room. No lecture beforehand, and nobody hears the term until the debrief.

Design it so that three things happen to them rather than from you: unconditional defection stops looking dominant once your card is legible to your opponent; a card of the form "cooperate if and only if the opponent's card is word-for-word identical to mine" turns out to be self-enforcing, and then collapses over a single extra word someone slips in; and at least one pair of cards sends the arbiter into a loop that never resolves — where I want the students, not the facilitator, to propose the fix. Include one round in which a student may choose to keep their card sealed, so the class feels how much of the result rests on transparency being free.

Deliver: materials; precise card-writing rules and what counts as a legal reference to the opponent's card; a payoff scheme that needs no real money; a minute-by-minute timeline; a debrief mapping particular moments in the room onto the formal definition of equilibrium in programs rather than in actions; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to steer it; and one frank sentence naming a feature of the formal setting the card game cannot reproduce, which I must supply verbally.
