I am a fourth-year doctoral candidate in political theory, and this term I am instructor of record for a fourteen-student senior seminar on sortition, from the Athenian Council to the contemporary mini-public. The department requires me to file the final paper together with a model answer and a marking rubric; the final is a 48-hour take-home on which students write roughly 3,000 words for 30 marks. I would like you to write one essay question for that paper, a model answer of about 900 words, and the marking scheme to go with them.

The question has to sit on top of a body of institutional detail, because the puzzle is invisible unless the details are in front of the student, so please build the stem around the following. In Athens most magistracies were filled by lot: the Council of 500 was drawn annually, fifty per tribe, for a one-year term, and no citizen could sit on it more than twice in his lifetime; the nine archons were drawn from a shortlist of ten candidates per tribe, the shortlist itself chosen by lot, so the archonship was a lottery inside a lottery. Everyone so drawn still had to pass a dokimasia before taking up the office, in which his descent and citizenship, his age, his standing with regard to the family cults and tombs, his treatment of his parents, his military service and his taxes were examined, and in which any citizen present could raise objections; at the end of the term every magistrate faced a compulsory audit of his accounts, with penalties attaching to any irregularity found. The generals, by contrast, were elected by show of hands in the assembly and could be re-elected annually, which is how Pericles held the generalship for fifteen years running. Aristotle's comparative discussion of constitutions treats appointment by lot as the democratic way of distributing offices and appointment by vote as the aristocratic or oligarchic way; Xenophon has Socrates object that nobody would choose a steersman or a builder or a flute-player by lot; and the Laws proposes mixing the two methods for certain offices expressly as a middle course between the democratic and the oligarchic principle. The modern revival displays a related but displaced anxiety: the Irish Constitutional Convention of 2012–14 seated thirty-three sitting politicians alongside sixty-six randomly drawn citizens under an independent chair, whereas its successor, the Citizens' Assembly of 2016–18, seated ninety-nine randomly drawn citizens and a chair and no politicians whatsoever, and the two bodies fed directly into referendums, on marriage equality in 2015 and on the constitutional provision on abortion in 2018. In both the ancient and the modern case, moreover, the body that actually convenes is not the body that was drawn, because a substantial share of those selected decline.

What I do not want is a question answerable by reciting that sortition expresses political equality while election expresses aristocracy, or by digesting the seminar's reading — Manin on the anti-democratic origins of representative government, Landemore on cognitive diversity, Lafont on mini-publics as shortcuts — since the average answer in previous years has been exactly such a digest and it has been hard to justify separating the marks. The crux I want the question to force is that every sortition regime, ancient and modern, surrounds the random draw with filters and supplements, while the standard justification of the draw is that it treats citizens as equally fit to hold office. A strong answer must sort those filters into kinds — a filter on standing and eligibility as against a filter on expertise; ex ante selection as against ex post accountability; a filter on who composes the body as against a filter on what the body is asked, by whom, and with whose testimony — and then say what each kind does and does not show about the claim the draw is meant to express. It must also explain why a polity that took lot to be the democratic method nevertheless elected its generals and permitted them to serve indefinitely, without either pleading Athenian inconsistency or resting content with wartime emergency; and it should notice that the one Athenian office that became a career was the elected one, while the lot-filled offices were ringed about with short terms and non-repetition. Please give me the question as it would appear on the paper with its mark allocation; the model answer, written at a level a very good senior could plausibly reach in the time allowed; and a marking scheme totalling 30 marks, organised around five or six must-hit moves, each with the marks it carries and a one-line note on how students typically botch it. I want the A/B line drawn explicitly, by naming which moves a B answer will contain and which it will miss, and I want the mark-losing errors listed separately — among them, I expect, treating the dokimasia as a competence test rather than a test of standing, collapsing equality of access to office into equality of capacity for it, saying that sortition eliminates political ambition rather than relocating it to whoever sets the agenda and whoever is invited to testify, and assuming that a statistically representative draw yields a representative assembly once people have declined. Two further constraints: students are not required to supply quotations, Bekker or Stephanus numbers, or a bibliography, so the model answer should paraphrase the ancient sources rather than quote them and should not invent any passage; and my co-reader is a classicist who will notice carelessness about the institutions, so if you alter any of the descriptions above, tell me what you changed and why you think it is safe.
