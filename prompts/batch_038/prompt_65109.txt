I run acquisitions for a tiny new-media collection — about $180k a year, four purchases, and the board signs off on each. The problem is that a growing chunk of what we're asked to buy can't really be seen by anybody. Generative pieces that re-run differently every time, durational works where all we have is four minutes of documentation, net art whose host site is gone. So the whole first-hand-encounter story about aesthetic judgment falls apart for us, and what's left is other people's word: a registrar's condition note, a curator who watched the piece perform once in 2019, a review, the artist's own description of what it's supposed to do.

Write me a decision procedure my commitee can actually run — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops at don't-buy, and where it hands the decision back to a person because the question is about values rather than facts. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of aesthetics really says about testimony: the line that you can't come to know a work is any good on someone else's say-so, versus the line that a reliable witness's word is as good as looking when looking isn't an option. No bibliography, and no both-sides hedging — I want you to commit to whether first-hand acquaintance is doing genuine work here or is mostly a prejudice against secondhand knowledge, give your reason, and build the procedure on whichever side you take. Constraints: an hour a month of committee time, and nobody on it can read code.

Then run it on three cases: a generative work each of the three of us watched run once, for four minutes apiece; a 1997 durational piece that survives only as a recording plus the artist's notes, which two critics we trust describe in glowing but mutually inconsistent terms; and a photographic print we can hang in the viewing room all afternoon. Tell me where the procedure lands the same way on all three, where it splits them, which call you expect me to hate, and what the procedure structurally cannot settle no matter how well it's written.
