So I do research on neural prosthetics — my group builds implantable replacement circuits for small patches of cortical tissue, and I've gotten dragged into the philosophy side of things because our PI wants a framing section for a grant renewal. The usual thought experiment I keep running into is the gradual replacement one: swap out neurons one at a time for functionally identical chips, and either you stay conscious or your experience changes in a way you'd notice, and since you don't notice, you stay conscious.

But here's a wrinkle that nobody in that literature seems to consider, and it's literally what our protocol does. We don't remove the tissue before installing the prosthetic. For each patch, the prosthetic is wired in parallel and both the biological patch and its prosthetic run together, driving the same downstream targets, for a few weeks of calibration before we ablate the tissue. And since we go patch by patch over months, at any given moment only a small fraction of the subject's neurons are doubled up — there's never a moment where the whole system is redundant, but there's a long window where part of it is.

Can you explain what this does to that argument? Two things I'm stuck on. First: is a system with two simultaneously active, perfectly synced realizers of the same local function still isomorphoic to the original organization, or does the redundancy break the premise the argument leans on? Second, and I think this is the real one — the argument gets its force from "you'd notice if your experience changed." If what changes during the overlap window isn't the quality of anyone's experience but how many subjects there are, does that test just structurally fail to detect it? I have this feeling the doubling case and the split-brain case are supposed to be mirror images of each other and I can't say why.
