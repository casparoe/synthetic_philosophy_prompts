I am in the third year of a PhD in the history and philosophy of science, writing a chapter on the dispute over when the Anthropocene began and on the epistemic standing of the committees that adjudicate such questions. For a workshop I constructed the argument below. It was meant to establish something moderate: that the start date of the Anthropocene is settled by decision rather than by discovery. It appears instead to establish something I cannot accept, and I have not been able to locate the leak.

1. A chronostratigraphic boundary is discovered rather than decided only if the criteria used to identify it single out one candidate horizon. If two horizons satisfy the criteria equally well, the choice between them is made by the community rather than by the evidence.

2. The standard criteria for a golden spike — a signal that is global, sharply and precisely datable, correlative across independent archives, durable in the rock record, and expressive of a change in the trajectory of the Earth system — are satisfied by several candidate horizons for the onset of human influence at planetary scale: the mid-twentieth-century plutonium and radionuclide marker (the Crawford Lake proposal, with a boundary at 1950), the early-seventeenth-century minimum in ice-core carbon dioxide known as the Orbis spike (1610), the industrial-era rise in greenhouse gases from around 1800, and the Neolithic signature of deforestation, methane release, and megafaunal loss.

3. The reasons given for preferring the mid-twentieth-century candidate are not delivered by the criteria alone. They involve weighting judgements — how much precision of correlation to trade against magnitude of systemic change, how much of the human transformation of the Earth system ought to fall inside the new unit — and they are responsive to which account of humanity's rise (postwar acceleration, the Columbian exchange, industrial capitalism, the agricultural revolution) the epoch is meant to commemorate.

4. So the criteria do not single out a unique horizon: the start of the Anthropocene is underdetermined.

5. By (1), the start of the Anthropocene is a decision, not a discovery.

6. Nothing in the move from (2) to (5) relies on the Anthropocene's being recent, contested, or politically charged. Given any boundary and any set of criteria of this kind, a rival horizon satisfying them to a comparable degree can be produced, because such criteria admit of trade-offs, and because comparability, not equality, is all the reasoning needs.

7. Hence no boundary in the geologic time scale is discovered. The base of the Holocene, fixed to a Greenland ice core at roughly 11,700 years before 2000, and the bases of its three ages, one of which is defined by an aridification event around 4,200 years ago whose global synchrony has been questioned, are decisions ratified by vote exactly as an Anthropocene boundary would have been.

8. It follows that when the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted the Anthropocene proposal down in 2024, it settled a question about the Earth rather than about nomenclature: on (7), which epoch we inhabit just is a matter of which proposals the relevant committees have ratified. So the Earth did not enter a new epoch in the mid-twentieth century and remains, formally, in the Holocene.

Step (8) is where I have to stop. I am confident that the mass extinction horizon at the end of the Cretaceous marks a real transition in the history of the biosphere, and that ratification of a point on it is not what makes it real; nor am I willing to grant that a procedural vote can establish that the planet has or has not changed state. Yet (1) through (6) each strike me as true, and I cannot see which of them I may abandon without abandoning the moderate conclusion in (5) along with it. Two readers have pointed to different places — one at the opening conditional, one at the generalization — and I have no way to adjudicate between them.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, I would like (a) an account of why the rejected step looked unobjectionable, since I expect it to look that way again the next time I run the argument; (b) a judgement on whether your diagnosis is local to the Anthropocene case or general to the time scale — that is, whether rejecting the step preserves (5) or destroys it, and what it leaves of the question of who gets to decide when a period begins; and (c) a comparison of at least two candidate exits, with a ranking of which is cheapest and an explicit statement of what is paid at each. If you think the argument is valid, say so plainly, tell me which premise I should sacrifice, and say what a realist about the geologic time scale must then claim about the relation between committee ratification and the Earth's own history. For what it is worth, I would rather keep (2) and (4), since the competition among 1950, 1610, and 1800 is the empirical core of my chapter, and I would rather keep the realism asserted in the previous paragraph than anything else in the argument.

Please close by saying how confident you are in the diagnosis you have offered, and by singling out the point in it about which you are least sure.
