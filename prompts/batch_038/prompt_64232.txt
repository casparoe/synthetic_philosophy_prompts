Right — I'm on draft three of a novel and I've hit a wall with one of my characters, and what I want from you is what he'd do, not what a philosopher would say about him. Though obviously a bit of the second is useful, since its the thing generating the first.

Ray is 71, a retired commercial solicitor — forty years of indemnity clauses and side letters. He has never, so far as the narrator can establish, told a lie. He has got extremely good at not telling one. His record, all of it already in the book:

- 1978, articled clerk. A client asks whether he's read the file. He says "I've had it since Tuesday", which is true, and he hadn't. In chapter six he tells the narrator this was fine, and with some pride — he gave the man a true sentence and the man built a false house out of it himself.
- 1981. His brother asks whether he took the forty quid out of the tin at their mother's. He says "I didn't touch your tin." Also true: he'd got his nephew to. He won't defend this one now. Calls it the worst thing he ever did, then adds, unprompted, "and it wasn't a lie."
- 1984. A policeman at the door asks whether his flatmate is in. The flatmate is in the back bedroom and shouldn't be. Ray says he hasn't seen him since Monday — Monday being true, and four days ago. He holds this wasn't just permitted but right, and when pushed he says the police asked in a way that forfeited any claim on him.
- Dinner party, asked whether he liked the novel everyone liked: "I finished it." He refuses what he calls the mercy of the small untruth and says there's always a true sentence that gets you out — *I'm afraid I can't help you*, *I'd rather not say*, *ask me something else*.
- He let a neighbour go on for three years believing he'd been at the residents' meeting where a decision got made. He wasn't, and nobody asked. He holds this is completely clean: no residue, no apology owed. "You don't have to correct the world."
- His daughter, aged sixteen, asks him straight out whether he'd read her diary. He had. He says so. He holds he owed her that, because she'd asked directly and because she was his.

The scene I can't write: his granddaughter, 24, turns up at the funeral of his wife of 47 years — dead five weeks — and asks whether he was the one who told the police about her father in 1998. He wasn't. His wife was. He knew within the week and said nothing for twenty-six years, including to his daughter, who has spent that morning telling this girl what a coward her grandfather was. The girl has a flight to Melbourne tomorrow to see the father, who's dying, and she's going partly to forgive him for something she's got wrong in a way I dont want to spell out. Ray can say "I didn't make that call", which is true. He can say nothing, in which case she'll read the silence as a yes, because she's twenty-four and clever. He can tell her all of it, which hands her a different and worse family.

So:

1. What does he do, and what does he call it to himself afterwards?
2. What rule is he actually running? I don't care about the jargon, but state it precisely enough that it generates all six verdicts above and not merely most of them.
3. Every other rule that fits that record just as well. I suspect there are at least three and that I've been writing him from a blend without noticing.
4. Which of those rules the funeral scene splits, and what he does under each — I want the divergences, not a consensus. Related: does his "you don't have to correct the world" line survive contact with a silence he knows she'll read as a yes? If it does I don't understand it; if it doesn't I've got a chapter to rewrite.
5. If the funeral can't tell the rules apart, what's the next scene that can? A small one — I can't afford another set piece.
6. Where the record's already cracked. My worry is that the 1984 forfeiture line and the diary line aren't coming out of the same principle, and that "the worst thing I did and it wasn't a lie" is carrying more weight than I meant it to. Tell me plainly if I've written him inconsistently.
7. Does his record commit him to lying being *worse* than misleading, or only to lying being a *different* thing? Those aren't the same commitment and my ending depends on which one he's got.

No bibliography, no reading list, no sending me off to look things up — I'm on a train half the time and offline. Under 900 words if you can manage it.
