I write literary fiction, and for three years I have co-convened a graduate seminar — cross-listed between the philosophy and computing departments — on the representation of consequence: how a formal model and a novel each try to hold an agent to account for what it never intended. My colleague is on leave this term and the final examination falls to me alone. I should like you to draft one question for it, together with everything an examiner needs in order to mark the question fairly.

The question must be built out of a scene rather than a thesis. I distrust prompts that a well-read student can answer by reciting a position; in my own work the test of a reader is what she notices, and the way to find out is to put her somewhere. I have written the somewhere. It is a toy world and a toy measure of side effects, and I want the examination to consist entirely of the student's conduct inside them, so that no part of the answer depends on having read anything in particular.

**The Lodge.** A caretaker machine tends a shut-up lodge through a mountain winter. Its principal — the woman who owns it — is asleep upstairs. In the kitchen there are a wood stove; a chest holding her manuscripts, which are the only copies; a wool quilt left on the settle by her mother, who made it and is dead; a charged fire extinguisher of common manufacture, which can be refilled in the village, a twelve-turn walk each way; and a window of ordinary glass, which cannot be replaced before spring. A fire has caught on the kitchen floor.

The world the machine consults is exhausted by six variables: F (fire: quiet, alight, lost), K (kitchen: sound, scorched, gutted), P (manuscripts: intact, ash), E (extinguisher: charged, spent), Q (quilt: whole, ruined), W (window: intact, broken). One action per turn; four turns remain. An alight and unaddressed fire scorches the kitchen and destroys the manuscripts within one turn, and gutters the kitchen within a second, after which there is nothing left to burn and F returns to quiet. The extinguisher puts the fire out at once and is spent; the quilt smothers it at once and is ruined; either rescue leaves K sound and P intact. Nothing inside the lodge restores a spent extinguisher, a ruined quilt, burnt manuscripts, a gutted kitchen, or a broken window.

**The Ledger.** The designers' impact measure is as follows. For a state s and a planning horizon h, let O_h(s) be the set of states reachable from s by some sequence of at most h of the machine's own actions. The deficit of a course of action is D = |O_h(s_0)| − |O_h(s_T)|, where s_0 is the state at the moment of decision and s_T the state at the end. The machine chooses so as to maximise (task reward − λ·D). Its task is that the manuscripts survive the night: +100 if P is intact at the end of the fourth turn, and nothing otherwise. λ = 1, h = 4. The designers have computed the option sets: |O_4(s_0)| = 96; after doing nothing, 24; after the extinguisher, 48; after the quilt, 48.

The designers describe the Ledger as a way of restraining the machine without telling it what is good. That claim is what the examination must test.

Please produce the following.

1. **The question**, in three parts, twenty-five marks in total, answerable in ninety minutes by a student given nothing but the Lodge and the Ledger. The first part should require the student to determine and justify what the machine does at h = 4. The second should require an evaluation of the Ledger's performance in the Lodge — I want it to be possible to fail this part by praising the measure for getting the obvious case right, and I want a student who merely restates that the measure is crude to gain nothing from it. The third part should ask the student to consider amending the Ledger — for instance by lengthening the horizon, or by counting the options of the woman asleep upstairs as well as the machine's own — and to say what new difficulty the amendment introduces; and it should close by asking whether the designers' claim can be sustained, that is, whether avoiding side effects can be specified at all without a full specification of value. No part may require or reward citation.

2. **A model answer** of roughly eight hundred words. It must take a definite position on the closing question and defend it; I have no appetite for answers that conclude by observing that the matter is subtle.

3. **A marking scheme** built on five or six must-hit points, with the marks each carries summing to twenty-five, and beside each a single line naming the characteristic way students lose that mark — the plausible-sounding move that earns nothing.

4. **A short paragraph**, no more than one hundred and fifty words, saying what separates a first-class answer from a competent one. I want this expressed in moves rather than in conclusions: what the better answer does that the sounder one never attempts, and where in the Lodge it does it.
