I am helping to draft an internal standard for what should count as an adequate performance guarantee before we put a model into a consequential setting, and two of my colleagues have separately suggested that I am simply being inconsistent. I do not believe I am, but I cannot state the rule I am applying, so I would rather lay out my verdicts case by case and see what principle, if any, they add up to. These are simplified versions of arguments we have actually had. First: an automated braking stack whose only guarantee is an average misbehavior rate measured on logged fleet data, where novel object classes can appear at any time — inadequate. Second: a radiotherapy dose planner whose own guarantee is likewise merely average-case, but where an independent hardware interlock caps the deliverable dose regardless of what the planner requests — adequate. Third: a phishing filter that an adversary can degrade from a 0.1 percent error rate to 40 percent at will, with losses that are diffuse and recoverable — adequate. Fourth: that same filter with that same degradation, except that the adversary aims it so that one fraudulent message reaches one retiree who loses his savings — inadequate, and I cannot explain the difference between the third and fourth cases except by pointing at the victim. Fifth: a recommender making on the order of a hundred million small bounded decisions a day, with modest expected aggregate harm spread thinly and compensably across users — adequate. Sixth: a model with a genuine certified robustness guarantee over every input in some perturbation ball, where the ball is so small that the certificate is empty in practice, and where insisting on a larger ball would make the system abstain so often that it gets replaced by an uncertified one — inadequate, which surprises me, because here a real worst-case guarantee seems worse than a merely average-case one. Seventh: a code-drafting model whose output a competent human reviews before merge, except that the reviewer approves roughly 95 percent of it without reading carefully — inadequate, and unlike the second case, so the human is not functioning as an interlock and I do not know what would make one function as an interlock. Eighth: a system whose average-case guarantee is computed over a data distribution that the system itself is shaping — inadequate, notwithstanding that the numbers look excellent. Ninth: an objective made robust over a specified uncertainty set of value specifications, where the true specification sits outside that set — inadequate, because the worst-case quantifier only ranges as far as the set does.

My tentative principle is that a guarantee is adequate just in case the losses that cannot be reversed or compensated for the person who bears them are bounded by something that does not itself depend on the model being right. It handles the second case and the seventh, and I think it handles the fourth, but it seems to say the sixth case is fine when I am confident it is not, and it is silent on the eighth and the ninth, where the problem is not that the bound depends on the model but that the measure or the set over which the bound is stated depends on things the model influences or on choices we made before we ever saw data.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures these verdicts? Please state each candidate precisely, and for each clause identify the case above that motivates it, since I suspect the clauses will not be independent. I specifically want to know whether worst-case versus average-case is the axis my intuitions are actually tracking, or whether it is a proxy for something else that shows up in these cases — the concentration of the tail on a single uncompensatable victim, whether the bound is enforced inside or outside the learner, whether the guarantee is stable under the system's own interventions on its environment, whether the failures are common-mode or independent. Then give me the verdicts your principle delivers on two cases I have not listed: an emergency-call triage model with an average-case error guarantee and no external interlock, where the worst case is a missed stroke, but where the human-only status quo it would replace misses strictly more; and a pair of deployments with identical per-decision loss bounds and identical average-case guarantees, differing only in that one's failures are correlated across all instances following a single model update while the other's are independent across instances. Finally, tell me honestly which of my nine verdicts the best principle forces me to give up. If some more elaborate principle saves all nine, I want to know what it costs — how many free parameters, how much of it is fitted to my examples rather than motivated — and whether that cost is worth paying, or whether the simpler principle that overturns one of my verdicts is the better buy.
