I am a fourth-year doctoral student in mathematical logic, and in three weeks I am giving my department's general colloquium. The room will be mostly working algebraists (commutative algebra and representation theory) and analysts (functional analysis, plus one person in PDE), together with a category theorist who works in topos theory and a philosopher of mathematics from the neighbouring department whose research concerns explanation and understanding in proofs. Apart from me there will be no logician and no set theorist present. Nobody in the room treats the axiom of choice as an object of study; they treat it as something they were once told they were allowed to use without asking.

The thesis I am defending is that the ubiquity of Zorn's lemma in ordinary mathematics is an artefact of textbook economy rather than of logical need. The evidence I intend to present is an audit I have been running over a standard first-year algebra course and a standard first-year functional analysis course: for each place where Zorn's lemma is invoked, identify the weakest principle in the usual hierarchy that actually suffices, and where possible establish a matching lower bound by a permutation-model or symmetric-extension argument. My claim is that the large majority of these invocations sit at or below the Boolean prime ideal theorem, and that dependent choice covers essentially all of the analysis. The slogan for the talk is that full choice is a tax most mathematicians pay on transactions that never incurred it.

I intend to state the classical counterexamples to that slogan myself, and I need to be ready to defend the retreat behind them: Blass's theorem that if every vector space has a basis then the axiom of choice follows; Hodges's theorem that if every nonzero commutative ring with identity has a maximal ideal then the axiom of choice follows; and the fact that the full Tychonoff theorem is equivalent to choice whereas its restriction to products of compact Hausdorff spaces drops down to the Boolean prime ideal theorem, which is strictly weaker than choice. What I am prepared to concede on the podium is that the unrestricted forms of these existence statements really do consume all of choice. What I want to hold, and what the question period will attack, is the narrower claim: that for the structures mathematicians actually handle — countably generated algebras, Noetherian rings, separable Banach spaces, products indexed by countable or otherwise well-orderable sets — the corresponding existence results need far less, frequently nothing beyond dependent choice, and that in each case the entire logical cost is incurred precisely at the step from the concrete statement to the unrestricted one.

Please prepare my question period as a prep sheet rather than as a defence of the thesis. I would like:

1. The eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by the product of how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased in the vocabulary and register of the person raising it — a commutative algebraist who uses maximal-ideal existence several times a day and is indifferent to which principle licenses it will not put her objection the way the topos theorist puts his, and the philosopher of mathematics will not put it the way either of them does.

2. For each, a reply of roughly two minutes' speaking length, followed by an explicit note on where that reply is softest: the point at which I would be asserting something I cannot presently prove, or where the answer depends on a distinction the audience has no reason to accept.

3. A judgement on which of these objections are in fact harmless because they miss the thesis, and which two or three I should get ahead of by conceding inside the talk itself — together with the exact form the concession should take so that it reads as precision rather than as retreat.

4. The single reply that has to land for the talk to be judged a success, and why.

5. The question I am hoping nobody asks: the one that, from where my results currently stand, has no good answer, together with the least dishonest thing I could say if it is asked. Assume I will not have time to prove anything new before the colloquium.

Do not give me a reference list or a survey of the literature; where a known theorem does work inside a reply, name it in prose and move on. What I need is the dialectic, and a clear-eyed account of where my position is thinner than I would like to admit in front of my own department.
