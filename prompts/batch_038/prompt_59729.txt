Our hospital's organ-procurement ethics committee is revising its protocol for controlled donation after circulatory death, and two of its members have deadlocked over a single sentence. Write a dialogue between them.

Treat the following as stipulated facts of the case. Do not invent further empirical studies, and do not cite literature; the argument should run on the concepts and on these facts.

- The draft protocol declares death after five continuous minutes of absent circulation and respiration following asystole, in a patient whose life-sustaining treatment has been withdrawn in the operating room, and for whom a decision has been recorded that no attempt will be made to restore circulation.
- The committee operates under a dead donor rule: organs may be procured only from a donor already determined to be dead. Consent to donation is not consent to being killed, so a mistaken determination is not a technical error but homicide.
- New wrinkle: from next month, a normothermic regional perfusion rig will be parked at the bedside, staffed and primed, in every such case. It can restore circulation within sixty seconds of a single verbal order. There is a standing order not to use it — and that order can be rescinded by the attending at any moment, including partway through the observation period.
- The sentence the two members must jointly author is the one stating why the wait is five minutes rather than two or ten, and what, if anything, the waiting is evidence of.

The two members:

1. Dr. Helen Ashworth, a philosopher of medicine, holds that death is the irreversible loss of the integrated functioning of the organism as a whole; that irreversibility is a property of the body and not of anyone's intentions; and that the circulatory criterion is legitimate only insofar as it reliably tracks that loss.

2. Dr. Samuel Okoro, a transplant intensivist, holds that what the protocol in fact establishes is permanent rather than irreversible cessation; that permanence is fixed by the recorded decision not to restore; and that construing death as a modal claim about what technology could in principle undo renders the concept unusable and, in the limit, entails that no one is ever dead.

Requirements:

- Both are equally competent and argue their positions at full strength. Neither may be softened into agreement, made to misstate the other's view, or allowed to score points by caricature. Each should be shown understanding the other's position well enough to state it in terms its proponent would accept.
- By committee charter, the target of the determination is the death of the human organism: neither member may appeal to, or retreat into, a consciousness-based or personhood account of death, and neither may suggest the committee simply adopt a legal convention and stop asking. That escape route is closed, and the dialogue should acknowledge it as closed.
- The exchange must actually work through, in this order or another: (a) what the attending's rescinding of the standing order at minute three and restoring circulation would imply about whether anyone had died at minute one; (b) the objection that Dr. Ashworth's reading makes death hostage to available restorative capability, so that the bedside rig has changed nothing about the patient but everything about her status; (c) the objection that Dr. Okoro's reading makes death hostage to a revocable decision, so that his account can generate patients who are dead and then, on the same account, not dead; (d) what the interval is doing evidentially — spontaneous return of circulation after asystole, and the survival of brainstem and respiratory function under warm ischemia — and whether the two of them disagree about what the five minutes measures or only about how to describe it; (e) whether the deadlock is a disagreement about what death is or a disagreement about what a determination procedure may safely treat as proof, and whether telling those apart changes anything.
- Close the dialogue with the attempt to write the sentence: either one agreed text plus a short statement from each of what it cost them to sign it, or two rival texts, each followed by one sentence naming what its author would have to give up to accept the other's. Do not resolve the deadlock by splitting the difference unless the dialogue has genuinely earned that resolution.
- Length: 1,400 to 2,000 words, with realistic committee-meeting texture — interruptions, references to the draft language on the table, the practical pressure of a family waiting outside — but no padding.
