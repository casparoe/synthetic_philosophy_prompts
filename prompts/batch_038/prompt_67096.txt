Not a philosopher — I work in a city budget office, and for the past year I've been reading my way through the arguements around a proposal to launch a local currency here (we're weighing it partly as a way to keep spending circulating inside the city). What keeps jumping out at me isn't the policy, it's a pattern in how people talk, and I can't find a word for it. The same person, sometimes in the same comment thread, will treat money one way when it suits them and the opposite way when it doesn't. Example one: when the topic is our local currency, skeptics say money is nothing but a shared agreement, a token we decided to accept, no different in kind from air miles or arcade tokens, so there's no reason a town couldn't invent its own. Fine. Example two: when the topic shifts to inflation or bond issuance, the same voices say the currency has real value in it, that issuing more is a debasement, a kind of counterfeiting or theft — as though the value were a substance being watered down rather than a set of expectations we collectively manage. Flip it and you get the mirror image: crypto people insist a national currency is just paper backed by nothing but belief, while their coin is sound money because an algorithm caps the supply, which treats the algorithm as if it had the solidity of metal. And a third case from my own building: when a department wants to pay a small award in retail gift cards, someone objects that it isn't real money, and the objection clearly isn't about acceptability, since the cards are accepted nearly everywhere.

So: does this have a name? I know the pieces have names. There's a long-running dispute between people who think money is essentially a commodity and people who think it's essentially a creature of the state or of credit, and I've read that Aristotle says somewhere that money exists by convention rather than by nature and can be altered and made useless at will. There's also money illusion, doublethink, and plain equivocation. But none of those seem to be the thing I'm describing, which is specifically the opportunistic switching — reaching for the conventional view to deflate a money you dislike and the substantial view to harden a money you like (or the reverse), without ever flagging that you've switched, and often without noticing. Is there an established concept or a literature on that exact move, in philosophy of money, in the history of monetary thought, or in rhetoric more generally?

If there isn't one, I'd rather you say so plainly and then coin it: give it a name, define it tightly enough that I could tell whether a given speaker is doing it, and mark the edges. Especially this edge — is it ever legitimate to hold both views at once, e.g. treating money as a pure institution while also treating it as a store of value, and what separates that from the switch I'm seeing? Lead with the bottom line (the name, or "there isn't one"), then do the carving, and please keep the whole thing under 400 words — I've got a meeting at two.
