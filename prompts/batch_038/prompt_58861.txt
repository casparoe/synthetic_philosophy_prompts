Next month I am giving a forty-five-minute talk to a decision-theory reading group, and what I need from you is a question-and-answer prep sheet, not further argument for my side. The room is about a dozen people. Two work on dynamic choice: one is a committed resolute-choice advocate who holds that a rational agent's earlier plans bind her at later nodes, and one maintains that backward induction is the only coherent way to handle sequential decisions and that resoluteness smuggles in a preference for one's earlier self. There is a behavioral economist who studies present bias and commitment devices and is temperamentally suspicious of any static axiom set's claim to describe real agents. There is a formal epistemologist who attends these talks looking for Dutch-book and accuracy-style arguments. The rest are graduate students whose acquaintance with the material is the sure-thing argument as presented in a standard text. No one in the room is a moral philosopher, so please do not spend entries on whether rationality is normative at all unless you think that will genuinely surface.

The thesis I will state, in the form I will state it:

1. Money-pump arguments have a fixed shape: if betterness is cyclic, then there exists a sequence of individually preferred, costless trades that returns the agent to her original endowment minus a sum of money; therefore cyclicity must be ruled out.
2. The step from "each trade is individually preferred at its node" to "the sequence is completed" is not a fact about betterness. It requires a norm of sequential choice — call it stepwise maximization: at each node, take any trade you prefer, since declining costs nothing.
3. The familiar rejoinder, that a sophisticated agent who foresees the pump should refuse the first trade, is question-begging in a way that matters. Either its support is a diachronic norm — dynamic consistency, plan-binding, dominance over the whole sequence — whose own justification is the intuition the pump was introduced to vindicate, or its support is independent, in which case the pump argument is redundant: the diachronic norm does the work and the pump is an illustration.
4. Hence the result is not that cyclicity is impermissible. It is a dilemma about which norm governs sequential choice, together with a narrower point: an agent can be pumpable without being irrational. The resolute agent who binds herself in advance is exploitable by anyone who knows she has bound herself, and binding may still be the right thing to do.
5. The corollary I intend to close on: pumpability is a property of an agent together with a choice procedure, not a property of a betterness relation, and reading it as evidence about betterness mistakes one for the other.

What I would like back:

- The eight or so objections this particular room will raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument above — not by likelihood, and not by how comfortably I can answer it. For each: the objection phrased the way that person would phrase it, since the resolute-choice advocate will not put it the way the economist will; which of my five points it targets; a reply I can deliver in about two minutes while standing at a whiteboard; and a one-line note on where that reply is soft.
- A mark on each objection indicating whether it bites only against the strong version of point 3 — that the rejoinder begs the question, full stop — or also against the weaker disjunctive version, and a recommendation as to which version I should state given this room.
- A separation of the objections that depend on point 2, that stepwise maximization is doing real and independent work, from those that would still land if point 2 is false.
- What I should concede in the opening five minutes, so that the concessions are not extracted from me in the fortieth.
- The single reply that has to land for the talk to survive, and the question I am hoping no one asks.
- Any place where you think I have misdescribed the resolute-versus-sophisticated choice debate, since I will be presenting it to people who know it better than I do.

Please number the entries, and if a short table mapping the objections onto my five points helps you organize the material, put it at the top. No bibliography and no citations; I will supply references myself. Write in American English.

Finally, I do not want a balanced sheet that hedges on every entry. Tell me which objection on your list you think is actually correct rather than merely rhetorically dangerous, and say what would change your mind about it: which consideration, if you learned it were true, would make you reorder the list or concede that point 3 fails. Flag as well where you suspect your own reconstruction of my argument is least reliable, since a prep sheet built on a misreading of my position is worse for me than no prep sheet at all.
