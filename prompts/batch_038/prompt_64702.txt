I spent most of yesterday afternoon arguing with a friend who runs a trained sensory panel at a flavor house, and the argument arrived, as these things do, from somewhere else entirely: we had started on Dennett's case of Chase and Sanborn, the two coffee tasters on the Maxwell House payroll who, several years into the job, both announce that the coffee is no longer what it was, one insisting that the taste is unchanged and only his liking of it has altered, the other insisting that his liking is unchanged and the taste is not what it used to be. My line was the familiar Dennettian one — that from the inside there is no way for either man to tell which of these descriptions is true of him, because the only evidence he has is his own reports and dispositions, and those are equally consistent with a change in the experience and with a change in the standard he applies to it. Her reply was that this is an indeterminacy purchased cheaply, by describing the situation at an absurdly coarse grain. In her line of work, she said, nobody would accept "I cannot tell whether my palate has changed or whether my criteria have" as a conclusion, because the question is decomposable: detection and discrimination against archived physical reference standards, triangle tests, rank-order consistency across a fixed sample set, drift in what a descriptor is applied to, and the ability to train a novice to make the discrimination are each separately measurable, and once they are measured separately, the two diagnoses stop being empirically equivalent. We went around that loop for three hours and neither of us budged.

So I want to settle it by construction rather than by argument. Build me a Chase-and-Sanborn-style case in which finer measurement does not resolve the indeterminacy but sharpens it: a case where a methodologically scrupulous investigator, with full access to precisely the batteries my friend was appealing to, ends up holding more evidence and less of an answer than she started with. Constraints. The setting should be a realistic professional sensory job — a roaster's quality-control panel, a brewery, a tea blending house, a perfumer's organ — and the scenario itself should run to one paragraph. No zombies, no inverted spectra, no surgery, no pharmaceuticals, no disease, and no stipulated neural mechanism; the story must not tell us why anything has changed, and must not assert whether anything phenomenal has. The subject's first-order discrimination, tested against standards the company has kept under controlled storage for fifteen years, is intact and demonstrably unchanged: he passes the same odd-one-out triangles at the same rates he always did. The degradation shows up only in second-order competence — his rank orders across a fixed sample set no longer agree with themselves from week to week, his old vocabulary has drifted so that a term he still uses confidently picks out a different sample than it used to, and a novice he trains cannot reproduce his calls. Meanwhile his spontaneous reports are untroubled: he still says the blend is his favorite and still says it tastes exactly as it always has. Lay out the space of diagnoses the case admits — experience changed with the reports tracking it accurately, experience fixed with the standards changed, both, neither with the reports simply false — and say which of these the stated facts leave open and on what grounds. Then show, test by test, that each additional measurement the investigator performs fails to close any of them off; if one of them does close one off, say so and explain exactly what work that measurement is doing, because I suspect that is where our argument actually lives. Finish by naming the single feature of the case that is doing the work, saying what the case collapses into if you remove that feature — back into the original puzzle, or into something else — and giving my friend her strongest reply, namely that a first-order/second-order dissociation of this kind is itself a phenomenal finding rather than a merely functional one, together with your verdict on whether that reply succeeds.

For context on what would count as success: I am turning this into a problem-set item for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar in which half the room has read "Quining Qualia" and the other half is persuaded it is a sophism, and the point of the exercise is to make students see whether the Chase-and-Sanborn conclusion is a claim about the coarseness of our descriptions or a claim that there is no single thing answering to the taste experience at all. The case has to make that distinction visible without my asserting it, which means a case that earns its verdict by stipulating the phenomenology, by handing the subject an introspective report that already encodes the diagnosis, or by making the investigator sloppy does not count. If you conclude that the specification cannot be met as written, I would much rather have that said plainly, with an account of which constraint has to be relaxed and what the case becomes once it is, than a scenario that quietly assumes what I am trying to test.
