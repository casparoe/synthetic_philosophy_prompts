At a reading group last week we got onto the Darwin–Wallace mess of 1858, and our host waved the whole thing away with "the idea was already in the air — by then natural selection was inevitable, who actually wrote it down is just an accident." He then pushed it two steps further: that priority disputes are a waste of historians time, and that if Darwin had dropped dead on the Beagle we'd still have got the Origin in 1858 essentially unchanged. I don't buy it but I couldn't articulate why on the spot.

Can you unpack what "in the air" could actually be claiming? My instinct is that it's smuggling together several very different things — the background conditions being ripe (Malthus, Lyell, the Victorian collecting and correspondence networks), a bunch of people independently converging on the same region of problem space, and a flat-out counterfactual about a specific date and a specific content. Lay out the distinct theses it might stand for, say which are near-trivial, which are doing real empirical work, and which are just false — and then tell me which of them he'd need for each of his two extra claims. Also: is the multiples thing (Newton/Leibniz, Darwin/Wallace) even seperate evidence for any of these readings, given how many consequential discoveries *don't* come in multiples? And does the fact that Leibniz's notation won and Newton's didn't cut for or against the inevitability story?
