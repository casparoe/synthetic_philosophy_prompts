I'm a senior and I run my school's philosophy club, which is basically six of us in an empty classroom on thursdays. We've been stuck for three weeks on Kant's moral worth stuff — the shopkeeper who doesn't overcharge the kid, the guy who's lost all feeling for life but still helps people because it's his duty, the whole one-thought-too-many business with the friend visiting you in the hospital. My friend Dev has a position I can't get any traction on. He says moral worth isn't only about what actually moved you, it's about what you'd do in slightly different situations, like whether your getting-it-right is stable or a fluke. And I keep saying that can't be right, because you can't act for the sake of a counterfactual, nobody is motivated by what would happen in a world that isn't happening, so he's smuggling in something that isn't about motives at all. We want to settle it but every example either of us comes up with is contaminated: the fluke agent turns out to be obviously awful in general, or the stable one turns out to be secretly motivated by something else, and then it stops being a purely counterfactual diference.

So build me a case to spec. Two agents, same situation, same right action, and from the inside the same reason: each of them takes the act to be morally required and that's what settles it. No mistakes about what's required, no hidden nasty maxim that just happens to line up with the right answer, nothing either of them could uncover about themselves by introspecting harder or asking around. They differ only in this — for one, the recognition would have held up across a wide range of nearby situations; for the other it's local, so shuffle a few facts about their upbringing or their setting and they'd be confidently committed to something completely different, they just don't happen to be in that setting. Constraints: realistic, a school or a family or a small workplace, no trolleys and no organ transplants; the scenario itself has to fit in one paragraph; and the counterfactual difference has to be one that no evidence available to anyone in the story could reveal, even to a very sharp observer. Then give me the verdicts from three accounts: (1) a Kantian acting-from-duty account, (2) an account where moral worth depends on how strongly you respond to the features that actually make the act right, whether or not you ever think in terms of duty at all, and (3) the view where duty only has to function as a limiting condition that would have stopped you, not as the thing that moved you. At least one of these three has to rank my two agents in opposite orders or the case isn't doing its job.

After the verdicts, do the honest part. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work in splitting the accounts apart, what happens to the case if you pull that feature out (does it just collapse into one-agent-is-unreliable-in-general, which is exactly what I've been accusing Dev of?), and how the account that comes out worst would attack the case instead of accepting it. Then tell me where that leaves our argument: if the case works, does it show moral worth genuinely has a modal component, or does it only show that these accounts are using the same words for different things. No bibliography or reading list please, I can't look anything up and I don't want you guessing at sources. And don't just stipulate in the setup which agent has moral worth — the verdicts have to fall out of what each theory actually says, otherwise it's a rigged case. If you think the specs are jointly impossible, say so and show me which constraint has to go, that would be a useful answer too.
