**What the number is**

Every time a heritability figure appears in print — something like 0.8 for adult height, roughly 0.4 to 0.5 for IQ in childhood climbing towards 0.7 or 0.8 by adulthood, around 0.8 for schizophrenia — the commentary splits into two camps and both of them are wrong. One camp says the number proves the trait is fixed by biology. The other says the number is meaningless because it was estimated in one place at one time and won't travel. The second camp is closer, but it still hasn't said what the number is.

Formally, broad-sense heritability is the ratio of genetic variance to total phenotypic variance in a population, and narrow-sense heritability is the same ratio with only the additive genetic variance in the numerator. Narrow-sense can in principle come out higher than broad-sense, because non-additive effects sometimes get counted twice in the denominator. Either way, the quantity is a ratio of variances across a group of individuals. It is not a statement about any one person, and it is not a statement about the level of the trait, only about how the trait is spread out.

**The orchard**

Take two orchards. In each, the trees were grown from a random, genetically mixed seed lot. Orchard A sits on good loam, orchard B on thin chalk. Within each orchard the soil is uniform, so almost all of the tree-to-tree variation in height inside an orchard is genetic: within-orchard heritability is high, call it 0.9. And yet orchard B's trees average a metre shorter than orchard A's, and that entire gap is down to the chalk.

Now change the case. Plant both orchards with cuttings taken from one parent tree, so every tree in both orchards is a clone. Within-orchard heritability collapses to zero — there is no genetic variation left to explain anything — while the metre-long gap between the orchards is still there and still entirely soil.

Same species, same two environments, same difference between the orchards. The heritability estimate went from 0.9 to 0 and the between-orchard story didn't budge. Whatever else the number tracks, it does not track whether a gap between groups is biological.

**Two readings**

I want to suggest that most arguments about heritability are people using the same figure under two incompatible readings, and not noticing they've switched.

Call them the ledger reading and the sensitivity reading. On the ledger reading, a heritability of 0.8 means that in any given individual, eight tenths of the phenotype was contributed by genes and two tenths by environment, and that the two contributions can be added up to the whole. On the sensitivity reading, it means only that if you jiggle the environments this population actually happens to encounter, about eight tenths of the resulting jiggle in the trait will line up with genetic differences between its members.

The sensitivity reading is the only one the mathematics supports, and it has an awkward consequence: it says nothing whatever about an environment nobody in the sampled population has ever occupied. A heritability estimate is a claim about how a population responds to the range of environments it already lives in, which is exactly the range you are outside of when you introduce a genuinely new one. That is also why the estimate is not a ceiling on intervention. If height is 0.8 heritable, it does not follow that public health measures can shift the average by at most the remaining fifth; a mean shift is a different kind of quantity from a variance share, and the intervention is likely to sit outside the sampled range anyway.

Note too that a trait can be wholly genetic in origin and wholly treatable by environment. Phenylketonuria is caused by a single-gene defect, and the intellectual disability it produces is prevented almost completely by diet. Heritability near one, modifiability near one, no contradiction.

**So the honest summary is this.** Roughly 80% of your height is your genes and the other 20% is what you ate. What the figure can't tell you is why you are taller than your grandparents.
