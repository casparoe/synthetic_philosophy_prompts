A friend and I argued for two hours last night over whether the frame problem is a technical difficulty that has since been tamed or a standing indictment of the whole project of spelling out the world in explicit representations, and neither of us conceded anything. I want the question settled the way fiction settles things. Write a short story of roughly 1,500 words set in the house of an insomniac widow and her overnight care robot, whose designers dodged the problem rather than answering it: instead of inferring what stays the same, the machine treats sensing as just another act and re-verifies every fact it depends on by doing something to the world — the stove is off because it turned the knob, the front door is locked because it tried the handle, the widow is breathing because it woke her to ask. Over the course of a single night the checking begins to alter what is checked. The turn must come from that regress of verification alone: no malfunction, no rogue update, no robot discovering it has feelings. Keep the philosophy inside the machinery, the logs, and the widow's dialogue with it, never in narration that names the frame problem or explains our dispute, and end on a decision to stop verifying that a reader cannot tell whether to read as wisdom or negligence. Begin with the story itself, with no preface, framing, or commentary before or after.
