Setting the take-home for an upper-level ethics seminar — twelve students, mostly third-years who've done a unit on normative ethics and think they already know what supererogation is. 90 minutes, about 1000 words, 30 marks. I want a single essay question on supererogation that can't be answered by reciting the standard four-features definition everyone's seen (good but not obligatory, praiseworthy, optional, no blame for omission). Its closed book, so the question and the case need to be fully self-contained — they won't have my seminar notes or any texts.

Here's the case I've cooked up and want the question built around:

Nadia is a nurse with a full-time hospital job. Ten years ago, on her own initiative and without being asked, she started taking one extra unpaid Saturday shift a month at a small free clinic. By year three the roster was built around her. By year six the volunteer coordinator had started telling new recruits that Saturdays were already covered. In year eight the clinic turned down a grant that would have paid a weekend nurse, on the assumption her shift would keep happening. Nobody has ever asked her for a commitment and she's never made one. In year ten she wants the Saturday off for her daughter's graduation.

The question should make them decide whether the year-ten shift is still supererogatory, and if it isn't, say what changed — since the act didn't get any less good and Nadia never promised anything or took on a role. The strong answers should see that this is a general problem for the good-but-not-required definition rather than a quirk of the case: on that definition an act can loose its supererogatory status without changing in value at all, purely because of what other people started counting on. And I want them to push back as well, either by arguing the erosion is real and that supererogation was always going to be a temporary, self-undermining category (the more generous you are over time, the more you owe — which looks like it punishes exactly the behaviour we want more of), or by arguing that reliance is the wrong trigger and that whatever obligation there is comes from the latitude structure of an imperfect duty of beneficence rather than from anyone's expectations. An answer that spots the parallel outside ethics — that a reliably generous private helper can crowd out public provision, so her very reliability is what removes the pressure to fix the underlying problem — should get credit, but I don't want that required for full marks.

Please write: (1) the question as it'll appear on the paper, case spelled out; (2) a model answer of roughly 700 words; (3) a marking scheme out of 30 built around five or six must-hit moves, with marks against each and a line on how students typically mess each one up — for instance treating 'she'd be blameworthy for not going' and 'the act is now obligatory' as if they were the same claim, or assuming that once an act is obligatory it can't also be praiseworthy. And please settle one thing for me explicitly: whether a top-band answer can get there without ever mentioning moral residue or imperfect duty. My instinct is that it can, but I want the scheme to say so and to say what it would have to do instead.

No citations in the model answer and no reading list anywhere — students won't have sources in front of them, so naming positions matters more than naming people.
