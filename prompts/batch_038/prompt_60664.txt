I am a staff attorney at a small legal-aid office that represents the named subjects of petitions for involuntary psychiatric admission in our county. We receive roughly fifteen petitions a month; we have forty-eight hours to file either a short statement of objection or a notice that we do not object; the person is usually held for a further few days either way, and if we object there is a hearing at which the judge almost always defers to the clinician's testimony. Our office objected in nineteen percent of cases last year, and we have been told from both directions that this number is wrong. The two people who triage the petitions are paralegals with no clinical training, one of them a former secondary-school teacher, and they have perhaps twenty minutes per petition. I want to write them an internal procedure, and I want it built out of Thomas Szasz's argument in The Myth of Mental Illness rather than out of the ordinary capacity-and-risk vocabulary the petitions are already written in. That is not because my office accepts his conclusions: several of us think he overstated the case badly, and nobody here has ever been able to tell me what survives of the book once you grant that at least some psychiatric conditions have identifiable brain correlates and that at least some people are helped by medication. What I think does survive is a method — the insistence that disease is a term with a demanding home, that a great many claims in commitment paperwork are judgments about how a person ought to live wearing medical vocabulary, and that the step from "this person's conduct frightens or offends us" to "this person is ill" is precisely the step that has to be inspected rather than assumed. I want that inspection converted into something a non-clinician can actually run on a three-page form.

So please write the procedure itself: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops and files no objection, and the points at which it stops because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts and must be escalated to me, with a sentence saying which questions are value questions in disguise and therefore always escalate. Be concrete about what a paralegal can check from the paper alone without pretending to clinical judgment — whether a lesion, test, or measurement is actually cited rather than inferred from behavior; whether the claim being made is predictive (this person will do such-and-such) or evaluative (this person's way of living is not acceptable); whether the conduct described would be lawful and unremarkable if a differently situated person performed it; whether the subject is seeking help, refusing help, or was never asked; whether the intervention proposed is one the person could decline and walk away from; whether the reasoning is that the refusal is itself a symptom, so that the refusal cannot count against the diagnosis. I also want you to say explicitly which of Szasz's distinctions must survive the simplification for the procedure to be worth anything and which can be dropped: my instinct is that the difference between treatment by agreement and treatment by force, and the difference between a disorder of an organ and a problem in how to live, are load-bearing, while the flat denial that any mental condition is an illness is not. Tell me if that instinct is wrong, and why, and tell me where the procedure will misfire in practice — including where it will let through petitions that a clinician would rightly find appalling, and where its suspicion of the phrase "lacks insight" is really just a suspicion of expertise that my paralegals should not be indulging.

Then run the procedure on three petitions, showing the questions being asked and answered in each. The first names a twenty-two-year-old man brought in by police after he stood at the front of a church during a service and announced that the congregation was speaking to him; he has eaten almost nothing for two days, he is polite and answers questions, he has no prior admissions, he says he wants to go home, and the petition cites no test result and rests on the claim that he lacks insight into his condition and is a danger to himself. The second names a thirty-one-year-old woman with long-standing anorexia who refuses refeeding; the petition says her refusal is a manifestation of the disorder and so cannot count as a competent refusal, her weight is critically low, her family wants treatment, and she has written more than once over several years that she does not want to be fed against her will. The third names a fifty-four-year-old man who has taken a prescribed antipsychotic voluntarily for eleven years, says it lets him hold a job, and whose insurer now requires a diagnostic code to keep covering the drug; the hospital has offered to enter a fresh diagnosis for that purpose, he has never been detained, and he asks only that we not object. For each of the three, state what the procedure yields and whether that result is one I should accept; I expect the second to produce an answer I will hate, and I want you to say whether that is the procedure working or the procedure failing, without softening it either way. Finally, please give me all of this in continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter or an email — no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables. Referring to the first question, the second question, and so on within the paragraphs is fine, and I would rather read three tight pages of running text than anything laid out for skimming, since the paralegals will be reading it aloud to one another at a desk.
