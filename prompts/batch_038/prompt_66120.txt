The question set was this:

"Boorse's biostatistical theory is offered as a purely descriptive analysis of disease, with all the evaluative work discharged by a separate concept of illness. Explain the machinery by which this separation is achieved — in particular, the roles played by statistical normality, by reference classes, and by the two goals of survival and reproduction — and assess whether the separation holds. (Answer in no more than 600 words.)"

The candidate's script is reproduced below.

---

Boorse's aim is to give an account of disease belonging to theoretical medicine rather than to social practice: a description of what is wrong with an organism that a physiologist could endorse without appeal to what anybody values. He does so by defining health as normal functioning, disease as its absence, and normality statistically.

A function, for Boorse, is a typical causal contribution made by some part or process of the organism to one of two high-level goals, namely the survival of the individual and its reproduction. What the functions of the heart, the nephron or the thyroid are is therefore an empirical question answered by physiology; nothing about which effects a part has, or how efficiently it produces them, is settled by anybody's attitudes. Disease is then an internal state that depresses one or more such functions below the level typical of the organism's reference class — a class fixed by age and sex, and by race where there is evidence of a relevant functional difference. The statistical element does important work here: it is what relieves Boorse of the need to call every departure from an optimal functional profile pathological, since what counts is departure from the typical rather than from the best. This is also why statistically unusual but advantageous traits — an exceptional memory, an unusual aerobic capacity — are not diseases on his account, the subnormality having to run in the direction of impaired function.

The evaluative content of medical practice is discharged by a different concept altogether. Illness is the normative counterpart of disease: roughly, a condition that is disvalued, that incapacitates, or that constitutes a reason to seek treatment. Boorse can accordingly hold, without contradiction, that a person may be diseased without being ill, and ill without being diseased, the second of these reflecting the looseness of ordinary usage rather than any defect in the analysis. Whether someone is diseased is a matter of biological fact; whether they are ill, and whether they ought to be treated, are matters on which values legitimately bear.

The theory is not without problems, and two deserve mention. The first is that the choice of reference class looks to be interest-relative rather than purely natural. Which differences in typical function are relevant enough to carve the population into classes is decided by the questions an investigator asks, and the appeal to age, sex and race does not obviously settle that question independently of our concerns. If the objection is sound — and it is hard to see that it is not — then the statistical element at the heart of the theory is not value-free after all, and Boorse's naturalism collapses into the normativism it was designed to replace.

The second is that the analysis runs into difficulty with the elderly. If function is defined by contribution to survival and reproduction, then a woman past the menopause contributes nothing whatever to reproduction, and her reproductive organs are, by the definition, non-functional. Boorse is thus committed to holding that the loss of fertility in old age is a disease in every member of the relevant reference class, and the only way to avoid this is to narrow the two goals — a narrowing that is itself an evaluative act.

Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis is best understood as Boorse's theory with an additional clause. Like Boorse, Wakefield locates disorder in the failure of a part to perform the function it exists to perform; he then requires, in addition, that the condition cause harm to the person as judged by the standards of their culture. Since the dysfunction clause does the substantive work and the harm clause merely filters which dysfunctions we bother to name, Wakefield too is a naturalist about disease, and his dispute with Boorse concerns which biological facts matter rather than whether values enter the analysis.
