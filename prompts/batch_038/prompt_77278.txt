I am a mathematical economist, and my working vocabulary is axiomatic bargaining theory: a feasible set S in two-dimensional utility space, a disagreement point d, the Pareto frontier, invariance to positive affine rescalings, and solution concepts individuated by the axioms they satisfy. I know the Nash solution and its independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives axiom, and I know that Kalai and Smorodinsky drop that axiom in favour of a monotonicity requirement framed in terms of the best utility each player can secure conditional on the other receiving a given level, so that their solution is the point where the frontier is cut by the segment running from d to the ideal point of componentwise conditional maxima. I am fluent in all of this. My training in political philosophy is essentially nil.

A colleague in philosophy has spent several conversations insisting that a family of claims about the fair terms of social cooperation can be given genuine content in my apparatus, and that the axioms are doing normative work rather than bookkeeping. I want to test that claim rather than take it on faith. Please translate each of the following five claims, which I have written down roughly as he states them, as literally as you can into the language of a two-person bargaining problem.

1. The baseline against which a cooperative arrangement is judged is not simply the no-agreement outcome; which state of affairs plays the role of the baseline is itself a substantive normative question, and different morally defensible baselines yield different verdicts about the same arrangement.
2. Fairness is sensitive to what each party could reasonably hope for under favourable conditions, not only to what each forgoes by cooperating; an arrangement that becomes possible only because new options open up for one party owes that party something.
3. A division is legitimate only if it could be justified to each party individually, and not merely if it maximises or improves upon the sum; no one may be treated as a means to the aggregate.
4. What a party is entitled to depends partly on what she contributes and on what she brings to the table, so a solution that treats two parties alike when their contributions are unlike is not thereby fair.
5. Some of what people have claims to cannot be traded off against other goods at any rate of exchange, so no assignment of utility coordinates can represent the question of who gets it.

For each claim, give me the formal object or axiom that best expresses it, if one exists; state what has to be bent or abandoned to make it fit; and say plainly if it has no expression at all in an apparatus whose only inputs are S, d, and a choice of axioms. Then give the residue report proper: gather everything that failed to translate and, item by item, say whether the failure reveals a limitation of bargaining theory, something the framework cannot represent and arguably should, or evidence that the philosophical claim was less determinate than it sounded when he said it out loud.

Two further questions. First, he maintains that both d and the ideal point are normatively loaded primitives, and that most real philosophical disagreement sits in the choice of those two objects rather than in the axiom connecting them. Is he right, and if so what would arguing about d or about the ideal point even look like inside a theory that normally treats both as given? Second, running the translation in the opposite direction: is there a theorem or an axiom choice in bargaining theory that philosophers ought to find uncomfortable, and that I should be pressing on him, something my framework states cleanly that his handles badly or not at all?

Define your notation for S, d, and the ideal point so that I can check your formal statements. No history of either field, and no reading list: I want the translation and the residue, not references.
