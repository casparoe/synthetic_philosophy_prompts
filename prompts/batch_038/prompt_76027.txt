So I sit on our department's data-ethics review group (university, not industry) and last week we were arguing over a proposal to reuse publicly posted forum threads for a study. The plan was an opt-out notice: post a message in each thread, wait 30 days, and count anyone who doesnt object as fine with it. One of the senior members killed it with "silence is not consent," everyone nodded, and that was that. I left feeling like something was off but couldn't put my finger on it — because there obviously are cases where silence does seem to do the work. My advisor asks "any objections to moving the seminar to Thursdays?", nobody says anything, and we move it. And in contract law there's a whole set of carve-outs where inaction or conduct does bind you (prior dealing, taking the benefit of something you could have refused, that kind of thing). Meanwhile the older maxim runs the other way, that silence is taken for agreement.

Can you take that slogan apart for me? I want the distinct precise claims it could be standing for, ranging from the ones that are true by stipulation to the ones that are genuinely contentious or maybe just false. Please seperate it along at least three axes rather than as one list:

- what counts as "silence": no verbal signal at all? no objection after being directly asked? ambiguous nonverbal behavior — a shrug, staying seated, not pulling away? failing to opt out of a default somebody else set up? not knowing a decision was pending in the first place?
- what counts as "consent": a thing you do, a state of mind you're in, or a change in what others are now permitted to do?
- what "is not" is doing: is not identical to? is not evidence of? cannot be presumed to be? does not license treating someone as if they had?

Then give me a verdict on the readings that matter — platitude, substantive but defensible, or false — instead of just describing them.

And here's the part I actually care about: tell me which reading the senior member needed for his move to work, and whether that reading survives the obvious counterexamples. I have a suspicion the slogan gets its force from an unstated claim about background conditions — fear, surprise, power asymmetry, not understanding what was being proposed, whether the person even knew they had a live option to object — rather than from anything about silence as such. If that's right, it's a contingent premise, not a conceptual truth, and it means the slogan only applies to our forum case if certain facts about those users hold. So say what we'd have to check.

Lay it out as something I can bring to the next meeting and read off. No citations, I'll dig those up myself if anyone pushes back.
