So — weird request, but you're the person I'd ask. I'm a philosophy prof, epistemology, and for a few years now I've co-run a review group with the archaeology department. What we actually do is read draft interpretive text — museum labels, site-report summaries, press releases about a dig — before it goes out, and say whether the evidential weight it carries is one the finds can bear. It's about fifteen or twenty claims a semester, run by grad students from both sides, and the output gets read by the public and by local community groups who have been burned before by confident overreach. One bad label about, say, ritual killing in somebody's ancestral valley is a real cost, not a seminar exercise.

My students have had no formal epistemology and I can't make them sit through my course first. What I need is a procedure they can run on a single claim at a desk in about fourty minutes, with only an extract from the site report and photographs of the finds — no library, no specialist to ask. Two of them run it independently and compare. So it has to be a sequence of steps, in order, with a stated bar for passing each one, and an explicit output at the end: either the claim is supported roughly as written, or it has to be weakened to some specific weaker version — and in that case the procedure should tell them how to *name* the weaker version, not just say "hedge it" — or it isn't supported at all. Where two students disagree after running it, the procedure should say what happens next.

Three things I want built into the spine of it, not stapled on at the end as caveats:

1. What I've taken to calling the semantics slide — moving from a defensible claim about what people did to an undefended claim about what it meant to them. "Complete pots were deposited in these pits" and "these people believed the pots would serve the dead in the afterlife" get printed in the same tone of voice, and the second is doing enormous work the first can't pay for. I want a step that forces the writer of the label to say which of the two they're claiming, and a test for whether the second one has any independent support.

2. The Pompeii premise in the loose sense I mean it — treating a static deposit as a frozen frame of ongoing life, and ignoring that what survived is a heavily filtered sample. Half the claims on my desk are inflated because burnt things and stone things survive and everything else doesn't, which quietly makes the past look more violent and more monumental than it was.

3. Claims resting on absence. No fortifications, so no warfare; no rich graves, so no hierarchy. This needs its own handling, because absence inferences can be good and the conditions under which they're good are statable — you need to know what you'd expect to find if the thing were there, and that you'd have found it.

I also have a pet argument I want your procedure to be able to overrule, which is a test of it. I've argued that the standard move from uniformity to control — artifacts or houses are strikingly standardized across a region, therefore some authority was specifying them — is usually invalid, because local conventions, shared apprenticeship traditions, and copying-your-neighbour can generate homogeneity with nobody in charge. I call the alternative convergent tidiness. So: build the uniformity-to-control inference in as a step the procedure actually runs rather than as a bias to avoid, and let it sometimes come out in favour of the control reading. Then run the whole procedure on this one — a claim that near-identical house sizes and an absence of differential grave goods across a large settlement show an egalitarian political order. I've defended something like that in print, so I want to see whether your procedure lets it through or spits it back at me, and if it spits it back, I want to see exactly which step did the damage.

Two more worked cases, shorter: a set of pits across several villages in one valley, each holding an unbroken pot and an animal skull, offered as evidence of a shared belief system; and a storeroom with thousands of clay sealings bearing a handful of repeated stamp designs, offered as evidence of an administration controlling the redistribution of grain.

Then be honest with me about the seams. Which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes, and how would a student know they're in one? Where will this misfire systematically — what kind of claim is it structurally bad at, such that I should just send those to a person? And where does it stop being an evidential question and hand the decision to a human because it's about values, like whether to print a contested interpretation at all? I'd rather you tell me the procedure has a hole than paper over it, since I'll be teaching it.

Start with the procedure itself — step one, right away. No preface, no framing paragraph about how inference in archaeology is difficult, and don't restate what I've asked for.
