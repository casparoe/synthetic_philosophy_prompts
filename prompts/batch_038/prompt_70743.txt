I am in the second term of a philosophy of mind module, and our current unit concerns the extended mind thesis. Rather than arguing from the top down, I have been recording my own verdicts on a set of cases about whether some external resource is literally part of my mind, and I should like help working out what principle, if any, they add up to. The cases are drawn from my own life rather than from the literature, although I have read the notebook example in Clark and Chalmers (1998) and I accept their verdict about it: an external store that I carry with me, consult automatically and trust without checking seems to me to be doing the same job as biological memory, and I see no non-question-begging reason to treat the location of the store as decisive.

Here are my verdicts.

C1 — The paper notebook. I have carried the same notebook for six years. Every appointment, address, idea and obligation goes into it, in my own hand, at the moment I learn it. Without it I am helpless; with it I outperform most people I know at unaided recall. **In** — it is part of my mind.

C2 — The calendar. My phone calendar syncs to my watch and notifies me unprompted. I no longer consult it; it consults me. Entries are made by me, and I act on the notifications without verifying them. **In**, and more confidently than C1, because the coupling is tighter and more automatic.

C3 — My partner's memory. She remembers my dentist appointments, my mother's birthday and where I left my keys. I ask, she answers, and I never check her answer against any independent record. Functionally this looks identical to C1. **Out** — that is her mind, and I am merely causally coupled to it. I hold this verdict uneasily; I cannot say what makes it different from C1 other than the fact that the store happens to be a person.

C4 — The paid assistant. A virtual assistant I have never met maintains, for a monthly fee, a list of the very same items my partner maintains. I query it by message and act on the reply without checking. **Out**, and firmly this time; I feel no unease at all. Note the asymmetry with C3: I think both are out, but I seem to think C3 is out for a weightier reason than C4, and I cannot articulate the difference.

C5 — The language model. I consult one several times a day. It drafts my emails in something close to my own idiom, retrieves facts instantly, and I accept most of its output with only a cursory read. **Out**. It is a clever interlocutor, not a part of me; when it is unavailable I feel inconvenienced, not diminished. Yet the calendar in C2 also acts without being asked and is also accepted without checking, and there I said in.

C6 — The chess engine. I trust its evaluation far more than my own calculation, and I consult it after every serious game. **Out**, with no tension whatsoever. My sense is that its outputs arrive as recommendations I must decide to accept, whereas the entries in C1 and C2 were endorsed by me at the moment of writing.

C7 — The forged entry. Suppose that while I slept someone added to my notebook an appointment I never made, in handwriting indistinguishable from mine. On waking I read it, and it structures my day: I go to the appointment. **The notebook is still part of my mind, but that content is not my belief.** I realise this may be unstable — if the notebook's status derives from constant use and automatic trust, the forged entry appears to inherit that status for free.

C8 — The calculator. Beyond a certain size of arithmetic I reach for a calculator app, and I would be quite incompetent without it. **Out** — I would say my arithmetical capacity is supplemented, not extended, precisely because the app performs work I could not perform internally at all, whereas the diary performs work I could in principle do in my head.

C9 — The lifelogging pendant. A service records everything I see and answers voice queries about it. I query it perhaps twice a month, and I always check its answers before acting. **Out.**

C10 — My grandmother's recipe book. She died four years ago. I cook from her book weekly, follow her marginal instructions without hesitation, and they encode judgements she made about my own palate. **This is the case I keep going back and forth on.** The information is endorsed by me, constantly used and readily available, but no living agent can revise it, and the person who originally endorsed it is dead.

My own tentative principle, which I expect you to attack: an external resource is part of my mind when it (a) substitutes for a process I could otherwise perform internally, (b) carries contents I authored or consciously endorsed at the time of storage, and (c) is glued to me by constant availability, automatic retrieval and default trust. Please test it against C1–C10 and repair or replace it rather than merely noting where it strains.
