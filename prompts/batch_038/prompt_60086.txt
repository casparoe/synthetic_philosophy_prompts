I am a second-year doctoral student in a seminar on moral epistemology, and our instructor has organised the closing weeks around a deliberately unforgiving exercise: each of us draws a thesis at random and defends it for fifteen minutes without any appeal to the literature, since she has banned references on the grounds that a position you cannot argue from cases and distinctions is a position you do not yet own. I drew the thesis I find hardest, and I would like a full, self-contained statement of it that I can adapt into the talk. Complicating matters, my seminar partner drew the neighbouring position — a pessimism about moral testimony grounded in the value of moral understanding, roughly the line Alison Hills takes, on which what the hearer forfeits by deferring is not knowledge but a cluster of abilities: to grasp why an act is wrong, to draw the consequences of that fact in new cases, to explain the verdict to someone else, and to believe and act for the right reasons — so my paper cannot talk past that view; it has to meet it head-on and explain why it gets the diagnosis wrong.

The thesis I have to defend is this: the pessimist is right that something goes awry when we take moral verdicts on another's word, but has mislocated the defect. Nothing is wrong with the belief as such — the hearer may know what she is told, and may even come to understand it completely — and what goes wrong instead is that, in accepting a bare verdict on authority, she installs another person's judgment in a position within her own practical reasoning that only her judgment can occupy. The failure is one of authorship rather than of knowledge or understanding, and it therefore tracks a distinction I have been calling the difference between verdict-testimony and reasons-testimony: being told that an act is wrong and accepting that on the speaker's say-so, versus being told why it is wrong, following the reasoning, and adopting the conclusion on the strength of considerations one has oneself taken in. Three cases should do the work. First, the redundant authority: a faculty member who has thought at length about an industry-funded research partnership and concluded it is exploitative, but who at the decisive meeting simply votes as her more senior colleague votes, telling herself his judgment is better than hers; she knows, she understands, she could give the explanation unaided, and yet her deference still looks like a quiet abdication. Second, the explained verdict: someone told not only that a practice is wrong but why, who follows the reasoning and accepts the conclusion; nothing seems amiss here even though the belief is testimonial in origin. Third, the on-call resident: at three in the morning she has neither the time nor the background to work through a question about withdrawing treatment, accepts the attending's verdict, and means to think it through properly the following week; this seems far less troubling than the first case even though the relevant abilities are precisely what she lacks at the moment of action. The claim I need defended is that the understanding account sorts these cases badly — it must acquit the first, where the abilities are fully present, and indict the third, where they are absent — whereas an authorship-based account sorts all three, for instance by treating a verdict taken on trust pending one's own judgment as held in escrow rather than adopted outright and indefinitely.

Please argue for this position in roughly 1,300 words, in a formal register with clear signposting, and with no references, bibliography, or name-checking beyond what I have already supplied; the argument has to be carried by the cases and the distinctions. Having set out the diagnosis, take on the four objections I expect from the room. The genealogical objection: nearly everyone's moral outlook begins in testimony, so if adopting verdicts on authority is a defect in authorship, the view either convicts almost all of us or owes a developmental story about converting borrowed verdicts into one's own, which had better not collapse back into the understanding account it was meant to replace. The moral-progress objection: abolitionists, reformers, and people raised in corrupt moral communities have often had to defer to those who saw further than they did, so say whether deferring to a morally better-informed other can ever be the morally required thing to do, and whether the authorship view fetishises authenticity at the expense of getting things right. The status objection: if the loss is only one of authenticity, the pessimist should downgrade the worry to something aesthetic or existential and say so; if it is genuinely moral, explain in virtue of what — and say whether the view turns out to be a Kantian autonomy claim in disguise, and if so whether that commits it to a much broader pessimism about all normative deference, to art critics, spiritual directors, and the law alike. The stability objection: any competent testifier offers reasons implicitly and any hearer can ask for them, so either the verdict/reasons distinction collapses, or the authorship defect must survive the giving of reasons whenever the hearer accepts those reasons only because the speaker says they are the reasons — pick one and defend it. Close with a positive criterion for when verdict-deference is permissible, and then with a short paragraph identifying the single most damaging concession the position must make and why it can live with that concession.
