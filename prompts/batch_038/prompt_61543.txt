A friend and I spent two hours last night on whether repeated interaction is by itself enough to explain cooperation. His position is that Axelrod's tournaments settled the matter: tit-for-tat wins, so niceness, retaliation, and a long shadow of the future do the work. My objection, which I could not state cleanly at the time, is that tit-for-tat is not stable in the strict sense — an unconditional cooperator scores exactly the same against it, so neutral drift fills the population with pushovers, and once that happens defect-always sweeps the lot. On my account iteration is necessary but not sufficient, and the real work is done by something like assortment or population structure, which his story never mentions.

I now have forty minutes with a reading group of non-specialists — no mathematics, no biology — and I want to teach the disagreement itself rather than deliver a survey. Plan the session for me. What order must the pieces go in, and what depends on what: at which point can I even raise stability, and what has to be in place before drift means anything to them? Which single running example should carry both his claim and my objection, and what must that example contain and conspicuously lack in order to do so? Which misconceptions will they arrive with or form as I go — I expect "the players have to be thinking ahead," "nice means never defecting," and "evolutionarily stable just means hard to beat" — and at exactly which point in the sequence should each be headed off? Give me one closing question whose answer would tell me whether they have actually grasped my objection rather than merely found it plausible, and tell me what I should refuse to bring in at this level and announce that I am leaving out.
