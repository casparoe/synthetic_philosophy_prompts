My roommate and I spent most of a night on this and neither of us knows where to put the argument down. The subject is moral trade: the thought that if you and I weight moral reasons differently — I care more about animal suffering, you care more about emissions — we can each do better by exchanging concessions than by each acting singly on our own weights. I wanted to defend the practice against the usual objection that it just lets people buy their way out of wrongdoing, and my defense turned out to prove far too much.

(1) When agents who weight moral reasons differently interact, the right act for each of them is one that falls inside a cooperative arrangement leaving everyone better off, by their own lights, than acting singly on their own weights would.
(2) A trade in which each party accepts something their own weights count against, in exchange for the other's forbearance, is such an arrangement.
(3) So the act my weights count against — eating the factory-farmed steak — is permissible when it sits inside the trade.
(4) Nothing in that inference depended on the particular shape of the arrangement, on which acts were traded, or on who supplied the offsetting good. All that mattered was that the package beat the alternative. So for any wrong W and any good G worth more than W, the package {commit W, bring about G} beats the alternative in which W is not committed and G is not brought about.
(5) Therefore no wrong fails to be made permissible by pairing it with a sufficiently large offsetting good.
(6) But that is intolerable: I cannot assault a stranger and then fund enough bed nets to come out morally ahead.
(7) So (1) is false — and since (1) was the only thing separating moral trade from each of us simply doing as we please, the practice loses its justification altogether.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? If I had to choose, I would rather keep (1) and keep (6) than keep (5); what I cannot see is how to hold on to both. Tell me which step you reject and why that step looked innocent when I wrote it — I want the diagnosis, not just the verdict. Then price the alternatives: what does giving up that step cost, and does abandoning (4) in particular leave any principled line between a trade that is genuinely permissible and one that is just laundering, or does it collapse the practice from the other direction?

One further request about how you present this: as you go, please mark explicitly which parts of your diagnosis you take to be settled and which are live disagreements among people who actually work on moral trade and offsetting. I would rather be told that reasonable people split on the point than have a contested line dressed up as the consensus view.
