I'm helping specify the analysis protocol for a mission that will collect material from an ocean world and test it in situ, and our criteria for life keep failing in the same way: anything concrete enough to program into an instrument is Earth-specific, and anything general enough to be substrate-neutral is satisfied by a candle flame. Please write me a decision tree that takes a candidate system observed in such a sample and returns one of three outcomes — alive, not alive, or undecided for want of an available measurement. Every branch node must be a question that could be settled by stating an operation (what you would do to the sample and what you would record) rather than by identifying its chemistry; abstract criteria are admissible only if you say how you would detect them. Then run the tree on six cases: a self-sustaining reaction cycle that grows and fragments but never copies anything; a spore showing no measurable activity for centuries; a prion; a flame; a self-replicating program that has escaped into the spacecraft's own computer; and a mule.

The leaves should be positions on what life is rather than verdicts on the cases: the Darwinian-evolution account, the autopoietic account, the metabolism-first account, the thermodynamic account of life as a dissipative structure far from equilibrium, eliminativism about life as a natural kind, and pluralism. Each leaf should carry one sentence on which of the six cases it handles worst.

Two further requirements: flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate using only admissible operations, and flag any combination of answers that leads to no position at all, saying whether that cell is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply untried. Close by saying how confident you are in the tree's first split, and naming the single branch you would least want to defend.
