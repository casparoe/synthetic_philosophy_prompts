Here's the setup. Two people in a reading group — call them A and B — have been arguing for a year about whether panpsychism has ethical consequences. A is what I'll call the Expansionist: if experience goes all the way down, then the class of beings that matter morally explodes, and basically everything our ethical theorizing has been about is a rounding error. B is the Deflationist: they'll happily grant the metaphysics and insist that no moral consequences follow, because moral status doesn't track the mere presence of phenomenal properties. They keep talking past each other, and I want the document they should have produced instead: a joint statement both could sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration.

To stop either of them ducking into "well, panpsychism is false anyway," let's stipulate the metaphysics they share for the purposes of the exercise:

1. Constitutive panpsychism: every fundamental physical individual has some phenomenology, and the phenomenology of a human brain is constituted by — not merely correlated with — the phenomenology of its parts. No panprotopsychist escape hatch; the micro-properties are genuinely experiential.
2. Combination is partial and unexplained: some macro-objects have a unified subject over and above their micro-subjects, most don't. A rock has micro-subjects and no rock-subject; a mammal brain has both. Neither A nor B claims to have solved the combination problem.
3. The Valence Posit: at least some micro-phenomenology is hedonically toned — there is a fact of the matter, for a given micro-experience, about whether it is good, bad, or neutral for whoever is having it. Note that A and B disagree about whether this posit is even coherent, so treat it as contested rather than as common ground.
4. Numbers: on the order of 10^80 fundamental individuals in the observable universe, the overwhelming majority of them in stellar interiors, planets, diffuse gas, and whatever dark matter turns out to be.

Their own two named theses, which I'm giving you so you can write each side's position in its own vocabulary:

- A holds the Aggregate Prevalence Thesis: what matters morally is the total amount of disvalenced experience in the world, and it accrues at the level of the micro-subject whether or not those micro-subjects are parts of any larger unified subject. Corollary A likes to press: the stellar interiors of the universe are the morally dominant region of reality, and ethics has been ignoring it only because it's inconvenient.
- B holds the Unity Requirement: for there to be harm in the morally relevant sense there has to be a subject to whom the harm happens as a whole — something that can be frustrated, that has interests, that can register its own experience as aversive. Scattered disvalue with no subject to own it isn't a lesser amount of suffering; it isn't suffering at all. B is explicit that this is a substantive claim about welfare, not a claim about our ignorance.

Now write the joint statement. I want:

(a) A numbered section of agreed claims — maybe 8 to 12 — that both would actually sign, including things about the combination problem, about the total absence of direct evidence either way on micro-valence, about whether general anesthesia favors A or B, and about whether human and animal ethics gets overturned by any of this. Flag any item that only one side would sign without a qualifier, and flag any that you suspect are vacuous (if it's just "we both think consciousness matters somehow," cut it or sharpen it).

(b) A disagreements table with these columns: the issue; A's formulation of the disagreement in words A would sign; B's formulation in words B would sign; whether the residual difference is substantive or verbal; and which crux it feeds into. Getting (b) right is most of the work — neither column may be a strawman, and each side has to be able to read its own cell and say "yes, that's me."

(c) The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of them. For each, give the argument, test, or case both would accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance, plus what each would say if their prediction came out wrong. I suspect at least these are cruxes, but tell me if I'm wrong and add at least two I haven't thought of:
 - whether moral weight requires a subject unified enough to be harmed as a whole (the Unity Requirement vs. Aggregate Prevalence)
 - whether disvalue aggregates at all across subjects that are not parts of any larger subject
 - whether the Valence Posit is coherent, given that we've only ever encountered hedonic tone in creatures with appetites, avoidance behavior, and a way of registering their own states
 - whether B's move is a substantive claim about welfare or a claim about evidence dressed up as one
 - whether A's view, if it's right but can never guide action, is thereby defective or just describing an awful world we can't do anything about

(d) Three cases to run through the cruxes, each with both sides' committed predictions:
 - the condensate case: a lab cools a mole of helium-4 into a single Bose-Einstein condensate in which, let's stipulate, the constituent atoms lose their separate dynamical identities under one macroscopic quantum state. Does a new subject come into being? Is that a morally significant event? Would A and B disagree about the sign?
 - the anesthesia case: micro-physics runs on unchanged while unified consciousness apparently stops. Each side should say what a hypothetical instrument that could read micro-hedonic tone directly would show going under anesthesia, and what it would show coming back up. This one should discriminate between them if anything does.
 - the ordering case: force both to commit to a comparative judgment, not a shrug — is the plasma in the interior of a star morally better, worse, or incomparable to a large industrial animal farm? If either says "incomparable," make them say why, and check whether that's a principled position or an evasion.

(e) A short closing section that says plainly which parts of the year-long argument were merely verbal — I have a hunch a chunk of it is about the word "suffering" — and whether either view has been qualified into inertness, and if so whether that's a cost or just an honest description of the world.
