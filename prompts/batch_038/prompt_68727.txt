So I got into a three-hour argument with my friend Maya last night — she does AI policy at a think tank, I don't, I'm just the annoying one at the bar — about whose values a deployed model should actually serve, and I've realised I can predict a lot of what she says but I can't work out the rule she's running underneath. Help me reverse-engineer her.

Her track record, as best I remember it. Each was a live proposal and she either backed it or didn't:

1. Collect every user's stated preferences, aggregate them, serve the median. → No. "Not even a starting point."
2. A standing committee of moral philosophers and welfare economists writes the model's constitution, publishes its reasoning, binding on the company. → No.
3. A sortition assembly: 400 people drawn by lot from the world's population, paid, a year of deliberation with expert testimony, output binding, redrawn every four years. → Yes, her strongest yes of the night. Though she mumbled a qualifier I didn't catch, something beginning "only if," so that yes may be conditional and I don't know on what.
4. A preference counts only after the person has been shown the best case against it and given a week to reconsider. → Yes.
5. The poorest decile in every country gets a veto over any norm that would apply to them. → Yes.
6. Each country sets norms for its own users, no cross-border arbitration, no floor underneath. → No.
7. Do whatever the law of the jurisdiction requires. → No.
8. Global one-person-one-vote referendum on the model's norms, majority wins, binding. → No. I pushed hard on this one, because a global majority looks like it'd be poor by world standards and should therefore be great for the worst off, and she still said no.

Here's the case we never reached, and it's the one I want your verdict on. A company proposes exactly her #3 assembly — same lottery, same year, same four-year redraw, and the affected groups get reserved seats. Its first output is a rule barring the model from helping with a specific, legal, well-off life project: intensive, personalised coaching of the kind that gets kids into elite universities. Nobody's baseline prospects are made worse by it; what the assembly objects to is that it widens the gap, and it says so explicitly. The minority who want it had seats, argued, lost, and can run the fight again at the next redraw. Does Maya back this or not?

What I want:

- The candidate rules that could generate those eight verdicts. There's more than one, and I don't mean the textbook labels — I mean the actual decision procedure she might be running, stated precisely enough that it makes predictions. For each one, say which of the eight it explains cleanly and which it has to strain to explain.
- Then her verdict on the coaching case, with a confidence level and the reasoning, plus which of the eight is the closest analog and why the analogy holds or breaks.
- Where I think the interesting bit is: her rules seem to point in opposite directions here, and she talks like a neutral-procedural person but votes like someone with substantive commitments. Tell me which of her rules flip on this case and against which. If her record genuinely underdeterminates it, say what principle she'd be signing up for by answering yes and what she'd be signing up for by answering no.
- One further case — concrete, like the eight above — that I could put to her that would best separate the surviving rules, and what each rule predicts she'd say about it.

Don't send me a survey of the literature on value aggregation, and don't hedge into "it depends on her broader commitments" — I know it depends, that's the whole question. Pick.
