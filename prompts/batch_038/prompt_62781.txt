The argument over the hedonistic imperative — David Pearce's claim that we have a decisive moral reason to deploy biotechnology to phase out the biology of suffering in ourselves and in the rest of the sentient world — has been running for three decades, and I have come to think that neither side is any longer answering the other. The abolitionists hold that unpleasant experience is intrinsically disvaluable, that wellbeing can be arranged in gradients rather than merely protected against a negative floor, and that appeals to the edifying power of adversity confuse what has historically accompanied meaning with what is required for it. Their critics hold that a world without the possibility of pain would lack depth, character, moral seriousness and art, and that paradise engineering is wireheading under a more respectable name. Each camp repeats these moves at the other and nobody converts.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict on it. Please work through the following hypothesis of mine and say plainly where it fails: that the critics' appeal to the value of suffering is doing at least three distinguishable jobs at once, which the literature runs together — a contrastive job (that the goodness of pleasure is partly constituted by its difference from pain, so that abolishing the bad abolishes the good with it), a developmental job (that virtues, projects and achievements are partly constituted by the obstacles and costs that make them difficult), and a narrative job (that a life needs adversity in order to be the sort of thing that can be recounted, evaluated and regretted) — and that the abolitionists have genuine resources against the second and third but almost none against the first, which is why the exchange feels interminable: the objection that survives is the one nobody is arguing with.

On that basis, tell me what the residual disagreement actually is. Is it substantive axiological disagreement about whether aversive experience is intrinsically bad? Is it a disguised empirical dispute — about hedonic adaptation, about whether the systems that generate aversiveness are the same ones that generate care and motivation, about whether enhancement dampens agency? Is it a dispute about whose testimony counts, namely the reports of people who suffered and emerged better for it against the reports of people who never had the option? Or are the two sides answering different questions under a single heading, one about what we ought to bring about and one about what makes a life worth living? Argue from what each side actually says and does, and be explicit about which of the three jobs you think the objectors are leaning on when they claim to have refuted abolitionism.

Finally, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress here. Candidates I have in mind include a distinction that dissolves the contrastive objection, a substantive result about whether wellbeing can be graded without a negative pole, a reframing that shows the parties were never disputing the same proposition, or an argument that the impasse is structural and no convergence should be expected. Please do not survey the positions or recommend further reading; I know the literature, and I am after the diagnosis.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
