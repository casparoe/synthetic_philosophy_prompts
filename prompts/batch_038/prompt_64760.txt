Our reading group is on explanation in history and I built this argument, and now I cant present it without sounding like an idiot, but I also can't find the bad step.

Case on the table: a war that broke out after about a decade of rising tension. The list the group came up with has five items — the trigger assassination, the alliance system that turned a regional quarrel into a general war, the mobilization timetables that made it impossible to stop once the orders went out, twenty years of staff planning that assumed the war was coming sooner or later anyway, and the harvest failure plus fiscal squeeze that convinced the ruling group theyd lose more by not fighting. Every item has been offered by somebody as the real cause, and each comes with a story about how the war arrives anyway if you take one of the others out.

(1) Explaining why E happened is citing something that made a difference to E happening.
(2) F made a difference to E happening only if, without F, either E wouldn't have happened or it would have happened in some relevantly different way.
(3) In a case like ours, for every F on the list there's some other item F* on the list such that, F* still being there, the war comes about without F.
(4) That isn't a quirk of this case. The better studied an event is, the more causes get cited, and the more causes get cited, the more backup each one has. Dense multi-causality is the normal condition in history, not a curiosity.
(5) So nothing on the list made a difference to the war happening.
(6) So nothing on the list explains the war, and what historians are doing when they cite causes is telling a story that happens to be true.
(7) And it isn't just history: the firing squad where both shots are fatal, the second bullet that gets there a moment after the first, the two independent failures that each brought down the same plant — same reasoning, same result.
(8) So difference-making isn't what explanation turns on, and since nothing else looks able to play that role either, causal explanation as such collapses.

My supervisor says the argument is valid and one of (1)–(4) is simply false, and that I should pick the one I like least and live with it.
