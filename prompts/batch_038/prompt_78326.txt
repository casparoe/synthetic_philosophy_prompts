I'm three weeks into co-supervising a slightly bizarre project — an adaptive-systems lab at our place builds controllers for greenhouse climate regulation and for a couple of soft-robotics rigs, and they've decided they want to write things like "the system misrepresents the load" in their fault logs, and they've asked me to tell them when they're entitled to. So before I hand them anything I want the spec. Not a survey of positions, and definitely not a bibliography — my grad students are doing that lit review separately, and I don't trust them to know what to look for until we've fixed the target. What I want is the list of requirements a theory of mental content has to meet to be good enough for *this* job: a theory that would license content ascription across a range of systems where nobody is prepared to grant beliefs or desires, running from an insect's optomotor response, to a policy trained by reinforcement learning over a few hundred thousand episodes, to a lab-grown controller with no evolutionary ancestry and no engineer's intention behind it (the scaffold was designed, the thing that emerged in it wasnt). For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it. A bare requirement is useless to me — I want to be able to point at something concrete and say, if you can't handle this, you're out.

Here's the stuff I already know I'll get pushed on, so work these into the list rather than treating them as an appendix. (1) Error: a controller's internal state tracks ambient temperature pretty reliably, but it also tracks a heat gun pointed at its thermistor, and a miscalibrated thermistor. Any theory that can't say which of these it represents, and can't make misrepresentation a real possibility rather than a notational convenience, is dead on arrival — but I want to know whether that's an independent requirement or just the determinacy requirement wearing a hat. (2) Swampman, with a twist: the standard case is a duplicate of a person, where most people's intuition is that the duplicate's states are about nothing because there's no history behind them. But suppose what gets spontaneously assembled is a duplicate of one of the *engineered* controllers, down to the firmware. If your theory says the duplicate has no content, does it also have to say the original's content comes from the design team's intentions? And if so, has it stopped being a naturalistic theory of content and become a theory about what my colleagues are thinking? That's a live worry for me, because the lab's whole framing is "the function is whatever the designer intended," and I suspect that's a trap. (3) Distal vs. proximal: does the controller's state represent the room, or the voltage across the sensor? Some theories are forced to one answer by their machinery, others let our explanatory interests decide. Tell me whether the requirement to *pick one* is itself genuine or a prejudice. (4) Lineage and time-scale: does function-making require reproduction across generations, or can selection inside a single lifetime (or inside a training run) do the work? If it can, say what does the work that reproduction was supposed to do. (5) The over-generation worry, which I think is the one most likely to sink the teleological options: if content rides on having a proper function, then a thermostat has representations, and so does a liver, and so does a smoke detector — and at that point the theory hasn't explained anything, it's just spread the word "about" thinner. A good spec needs to say whether the theory owes us a line here, and if it does, what could draw it that isn't question-begging.

Then do the two things that actually make this a spec and not a list. First, sort the requirements into non-negotiable ones versus ones that are really the tastes of a particular camp — I already suspect that "must be reductive," "must be externalist," and "must ground a robust normativity that isn't just reliability-talk with a fancy word attached" don't all belong in the same column. Second, and this is the part I most want: tell me which subsets are jointly unsatisfiable. My own guess is that determinacy of content, full naturalism with no appeal to anybody's intentions or interests, and applicability to systems that have neither a selective lineage nor a designer, can't all be had at once — but I want you to say whether that trilemma is real, which horn you'd cut, and which single requirement, if you insist on it, knocks out the most candidates. Finish with a short paragraph, no more, on how a causal-covariance or indicator account, an etiological selected-effects account, and a design-or-artifact-friendly variant of teleosemantics each do against the list. Keep that part brief; the criteria are where the thinking is, and I'd rather have six well-motivated requirements than ten contenders ranked.
