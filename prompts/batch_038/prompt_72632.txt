I sit on the ethics advisory panel of a company that sells conversational replicas of the dead: a family uploads a relative's texts, voicemails, letters, and home video, and receives a chatbot that speaks in that person's register and answers questions about their life. Our marketing currently calls it time with them again, and the panel's standing worries are consent, the deception of the grieving, and who owns a person's voice. I keep going back to Douglas Hofstadter's dialogue in The Mind's I in which the preserved brain of the long-dead Einstein is hooked to a machine that can put questions to it and get answers, and which presses whether what answers is a mind still running or a residue being read — and, close beside that, whether a sufficiently complete written record of a mind could itself be conscious.

Please describe the most important implications of that thought experiment for our product. I want the analysis tied to the dialogue's own leverage rather than to a general checklist of AI ethics: say which of our standing worries it genuinely sharpens and which it leaves untouched, what it implies about the difference between a replica and a recording, whether the consciousness question has any operational bite at all for something transparently synthetic, and what, if anything, should change in what we tell customers the thing is. Please write in American English.
