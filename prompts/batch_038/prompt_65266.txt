I'm an associate editor at a mid-sized psychology journal, and I get handed anything that comes in claiming that beliefs, desires, moods, or expertise change what people literally see — "top-down perception," "cognitively penetrated perception," whatever the authors call it. Six to ten a month. Right now I triage them on vibes. Some are obviously fine, some are obviously a rating-scale experiment wearing a perception costume, and most sit in between, and I have maybe forty minutes per manuscript, no data, no chance of rerunning anything, and two reviewers who will disagree with each other and with me. Whatever I do has to survive being quoted back at the authors and to the editor-in-chief, who signs off on every desk reject and hates being ambushed. It also has to be handable to a new AE in their first week who has had one stats course and has never fitted a signal detection model.

So: write me the triage procedure. A numbered sequence of questions to ask a manuscript, in the order I should ask them, and for each one, what counts as a sufficient answer, what that answer does to the disposition — desk reject, or send out with a specific question attached for the reviewers, or "can't be settled at triage" — and which ones I genuinely cannot answer from a PDF alone and have to hand to a human. I've got as far as knowing there's a Pylyshyn-ish criterion, something about the effect having to be explained by the *content* of the mental state and not just by how intense it is, and that plenty of people think most of the published cases fail it. I don't know how to turn that into forty minutes of work. Tell me which distinctions have to survive the compression — I care a lot about seperating "what they saw changed" from "what they said about it changed," and I suspect most of these papers don't — and tell me which ones I'm allowed to drop at triage without quietly losing the point.

No reading list, I can't chase one and neither can my reviewers. I want the questions, the thresholds, and an honest marking of the places where the threshold is fake — where I'm really just exercising taste and the checklist is dressing it up as a rule.

Then run it on three:

1. Fasted participants judge a coin's diameter as larger than fed participants do. Magnitude estimation, no catch or control trials, n=48.
2. A threatening word flashed before a morphed face makes the face look angrier. They report d′ and c separately; d′ is flat across conditions and c moves with the prime. The discussion says this shows the face was seen as angrier.
3. The one that will annoy me. A group whose work I've cited approvingly for years: smokers judge a cigarette as longer than non-smokers do, within-subjects, the effect replicates across two samples — but the length estimate is given verbally after a delay and there's no comparison object of the same size with no smoking relevance.

On number three especially, tell me straight if the procedure comes out somewhere I won't like. I'd much rather it did that than bend to keep me comfortable. And close with what the procedure can't do: which kinds of real effect it will kill, what it does to authors working on genuinely novel claims, and whether a forty-minute screen is even the right instrument for a literature where the base rate of real findings is probably low.
