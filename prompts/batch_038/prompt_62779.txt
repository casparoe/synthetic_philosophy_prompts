I am a doctoral student in political philosophy preparing a twenty-five-minute paper for a small interdisciplinary workshop on vagueness and normative concepts; the audience will be roughly half philosophers of language and half literary scholars, so anything I say has to survive both groups. My paper reads George Orwell's *Animal Farm* through the sorites paradox, and I would like your help turning a working thesis into criticism that anticipates the objections. Please assume you have no ability to look anything up: I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or page references, and I would rather you describe a scene in your own words, hedged if you are unsure of the detail, than reproduce dialogue or chapter numbers you cannot check.

The thesis is this. The Seven Commandments painted on the barn wall are a political community's attempt to legislate precision — to fix, in advance and in public, the boundaries of predicates ("enemy," "comrade," "the habits of Man") that would otherwise be vague. Everything that follows is a campaign against that precision. Each revision adds a qualifier or swaps a word: the prohibition on sleeping in a bed acquires a clause about sheets, the prohibition on drink acquires a clause about excess, the prohibition on killing acquires a clause about cause, the sheep's original sharp binary chant is eventually inverted by the alteration of a single word, and the seven are finally reduced to one self-contradictory commandment to the effect that some animals are more equal than others. The animals accept every step because each step differs only marginally from the one before it, which is exactly the tolerance premise that generates the paradox: if one revision does not turn a comradeship into a tyranny, no revision can. I have been calling the resulting arrangement a *tolerance shelter* — a regime that paces its own transformation so that no single act ever licenses the community's vague evaluative verdicts, while the cumulative result is a betrayal that nobody could dispute if it were presented all at once. On my reading the closing tableau, in which the animals watch through the farmhouse window and can no longer distinguish the pigs from the human farmers they expelled, is the terminus of a sorites series rather than a mere emblem, and the renaming of the farm back to its old human name is simply the label catching up with a change that was completed long before.

Here is what I would like from you. First, tell me which moments in the book a listener should be pointed to, and describe them so that someone who read the novella at fourteen can place them; I suspect the animals' half-memory of the resolutions passed at the first Meeting, and the passage where the older animals try and fail to compare their present lives with the early days of the Rebellion, will matter more than the famous final image. Second, test my thesis against three competing accounts of vagueness and say which the book actually supports: an epistemicist line, on which there is a determinate revision at which the farm ceased to be the animals' and the animals are merely forever ignorant of where the boundary lay; a supervaluationist line, on which it is indeterminate when the betrayal occurred but determinate, on every admissible sharpening, that it did occur; and the dynamic or context-shifting reply, on which each revision moves the conversational standard along with the paint, so that there is never a fixed predicate and hence never a genuine series — a reply that would collapse my paradox into the more mundane point that whoever controls the record controls the meaning. If you think a credence-based or degree-theoretic treatment fits better — the animals' confidence in the farm's character ought to have decayed smoothly while the text shows it staying at certainty — say so and explain what that does to my argument. Third, and most importantly, tell me where the sorites lens distorts the novel, because I expect to be accused of exactly this: the reader is never vague at all and watches each step clearly, so the vagueness is located in the animals rather than in the language; several of the decisive changes are abrupt shocks rather than small steps; Benjamin remembers everything and says nothing, which is a problem of courage and not of semantics; and Squealer's real instruments are the threat of Jones's return and a stream of flattering statistics, not the tolerance principle. Finally, tell me whether "tolerance shelter" names something that already goes by another name in the literature — name the concept if there is one, but do not build me a reading list around it — and end with a paragraph on what the book contributes to the debate about vagueness that argument alone could not deliver, if you think it contributes anything at all. Plain prose, no jargon without a gloss, around twelve hundred words.
