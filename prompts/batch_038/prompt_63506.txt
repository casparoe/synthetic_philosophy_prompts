So I'm in year two of a philosophy PhD and my department has a small internal summer fellowship — eight weeks of funding, bascially enough that I don't have to TA and can actually read for once — and the proposal is due Friday. My half-formed idea came out of a seminar where we spent three weeks on split-brain patients and I left more confused than when I went in.

The one-subject camp says a commissurotomy patient is still one person because in ordinary life they act as an integrated whole: they get dressed, hold down a job, and the hemispheres cooperate constantly through the remaining subcortical routes and through sharing a body. The two-subject camp says that's just an artifact of only ever seeing these patients outside the lab — flash a word into one visual field and the other hemisphere demonstrably doesn't know it, the two hands sometimes actively undo each other's work, and the speaking hemisphere cheerfully invents a reason for a choice that was made on information it never had access to. What I think both sides are missing is the assumption that "how many subjects of experience are there in here" has one determinate, question-independent answer. I want to argue it doesnt — that the count is relative to which capacities and which questions you're asking, the way "how many objects are on the table" can be one or two depending on whether you're counting cups or cup-and-saucer units. And the kicker, which is the part I actually find exciting: if that's right, split-brain isn't exotic at all, because ordinary brains show the same relativity in miniature — inattentional blindness, change blindness, the fact that what's really in my "unified field" at any given moment is way sparser and gappier than it feels like from the inside.

Can you turn this into a proposal? What I need: (1) the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer and is clearly not just the old one-or-two question rebranded; (2) a paragraph on why the existing positions don't settle it — you can name people and views, but don't put in citations, I'll add those myself; (3) the method, roughly week by week over the eight weeks, including where I'd go in the actual clinical literature and specifically what I'd be looking for there; (4) the thing my supervisor will ask first, which is a case where my relativity account and each of the two standard accounts come out differently — I need at least one genuinely discriminating example rather than a restatement of my thesis, and I should warn you that the one I've been poking at, about whether a two-subject reading makes you count the patient's suffering twice for his own sake, doesn't feel strong enough to me; (5) what a deliverable looks like at the end (I'm assuming a paper-length thing pitched at a specific kind of audience, but tell me if that's unrealistic for eight weeks); and (6) an honest failure condition — under what circumstances would this turn out to be nothing but a fancy way of saying "it depends," and how would I notice that I'd drifted into that? Please also be blunt about scope, because I have a habit of proposing things that need a book.
