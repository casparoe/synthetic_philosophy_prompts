I run a monthly meetup that's sort of a rationalist-adjacent reading group, and this quarter we're doing a block on coordination failure — Alexander's Meditations on Moloch, some Ostrom, the usual race-to-the-bottom canon. The problem is that by week two half the group was using "Moloch" as a general-purpose word for "aspect of modern life I find depressing," and the other half was insisting that any situation where everyone ends up worse off is automatically a prisoner's dilemma. Both habits are useless for actually deciding what to do about anything. So what I want from you is a decision tree whose *input* is a plain-language description of a situation (the kind a journalist or one of my meetup people would give, with no payoff matrix attached) and whose *output* is a diagnosis: which species of trap it is, or which species of non-trap it is. I've got a whiteboard and about 40 minutes of a session to fill, so it needs to be something I can literally walk down out loud with the group.

Here's the shape I'm after. Seven to nine leaves, and every internal node phrased as a question someone could answer from the description alone — no "is the game supermodular," more like "could one participant stop doing the bad thing on their own, next quarter, without anyone else changing, and would they still be roughly as well off?" I want the first split to be about unilateral escape (who can move alone, and what it costs them to do it), and the second about who eats the loss (the players themselves, or bystanders who aren't at the table at all), because in my experience that second distinction is the one people most often fudge. Use standard vocabulary for leaves that have it — prisoner's dilemma, tragedy of the commons, assurance/stag hunt, positional arms race, Goodhart-style measurement trap, switching-cost lock-in, Ostrom-governable commons, pure externality with no race in it — and give each leaf four things: a name, a one-sentence structural definition, a "tell" (a single concrete observation that would confirm the diagnosis if you went and looked), and the characteristic policy blunder that follows from applying some other leaf's fix to it. Then be honest about the limits: flag any pair of leaves your questions can't really separate, and finish with a short section on empty cells — combinations of branch answers that no actual situation seems to occupy, each with your guess at whether that's because the combination is incoherent, self-unstable, or just nobody's bothered to look.

To keep you from writing something generic, run the finished tree on these four cases and show me the path each one takes. (1) Three frontier AI labs, each of which says an unaligned system built by anyone would be catastrophic, each of which thinks coming second is worse than coming first with less safety work, and none of which can verify what the others are actually spending on safety — I suspect this one comes out genuinely ambiguous between at least two of your leaves, and I'd like the tree to say which observation would break the tie. (2) A coastal fishery that gets described in every article as a textbook tragedy of the commons, except the boats are individually owned, the local skippers' association has been informally allocating grounds and setting seasonal limits for decades, and the stock is stable. (3) Enterprise software where every vendor keeps bundling in more telemetry and more AI features, customers uniformly say they hate it, churn stays low because migrating is miserable, and no vendor would gain customers by stripping features out. (4) A city where landlords keep converting units to short-term lets, rents climb, longtime residents get pushed out, and every landlord says they'd happily stop if the others stopped too. One hard rule for the whole thing: no leaf may be defined by how bad the outcome feels. "Everyone ends up worse off" is the *input* to the tree, not evidence for any particular output — and if one of these cases turns out to be a situation where no one is actually racing anybody, I want the tree to be able to say that out loud.
