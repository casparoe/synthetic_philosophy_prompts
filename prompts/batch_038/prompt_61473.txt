Six months ago I got handed the "AI rollout" at a mid-size freight brokerage — about 400 people, mostly dispatchers and customer service — and one of the things I wrote is our internal policy on what the model we deployed is allowed to do. The whole thing is built around a click. The model can draft anything it wants: emails to customers, rate quotes, incident write-ups, code. Nothing leaves the building without a named human clicking approve — outbound email, a quote that becomes a contract, a payment, a deploy to the system dispatchers actually work in. The human's name goes in the log as the accountable party; the model's name appears nowhere in the accountability chain. But there's a big carve-out: anything purely internal and undoable, it runs overnight with no seperate review at all — reformatting the ticket database, refactoring internal scripts, rewriting our knowledge-base articles. We keep what our risk guy calls a reversibility ledger, a spreadsheet listing every class of action with two columns: can it be undone, and how long does the undo take. Under about an hour and nothing external sees it, no click required. Weirdly we also let the model propose new rows for the ledger, which a human then approves, which... yeah, I know. On top of all that we give the model a quarterly scorecard — accuracy, escalation rate, "instances of unhelpful initiative" — structured basically like the performance reviews our human staff get.

A friend of mine who does philosophy of mind read the policy over dinner and said it has no idea what it thinks the thing is, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. Our counsel keeps calling it a chainsaw: extremely powerful, zero agency, liability lands on whoever has their hands on it. But the way we actually built the workflow reads a lot more like we hired an intern — standing tasks, a quarterly review, we say it "decided" to escalate, we worry about it going off-script, we tune its behavior over time to fit how this particular office works. And the click itself bugs me: our reviewers spend maybe forty seconds on a nine-hundred-line diff, and I've watched people approve customer emails they clearly half read. So I honestly can't tell whether the policy assumes the human's judgment is better than the model's, or only that somebody with a pulse has to own the outcome, and those feel like very different commitments.

What I want is for you to treat the policy as a philosophical artifact rather than a compliance document. What theory of agency does it presuppose — is agency located in the internal organization of the thing acting (having goals, a model of the situation, self-directedness) or in the footprint its behavior leaves on the world (reversibility, visibility to outsiders)? Because the ledger obviously picks the second one and I don't think anyone here ever argued for it. Which rival views do the carve-out and the click quietly rule out — for instance the idea that being a tool is a status that depends on whether the user can actually check the output, or that agency comes in degrees instead of being an on/off switch? Tell me where the policy is underdetermined, i.e. where several incompatible theories would license the exact same spreadsheet, versus where it really only makes sense under one of them. Can the chainsaw and the intern be held at the same time, or does one of them have to be fired? And if we adopted the strongest rival view you can construct, what specifically would change in the ledger and in the click — I'm presenting this to the board in about three weeks and I'd rather walk in knowing which parts of the thing are philosophically load-bearing than find out later that its all just theater.
