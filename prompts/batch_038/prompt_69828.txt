I am in the third year of a DPhil on decision theory, and the chapter I am meant to be writing is supposed to fix what a decision theory has to be able to do before any of us start adjudicating between the candidates. My supervisor read a draft last week and told me, fairly, that I had produced a survey of the usual talking points — dynamic consistency, the money-pump and Dutch-strategy arguments, the Newcomb-style cases — rather than a specification, and that a reader who already knew the literature would learn nothing from it. So I want to start again from the requirements themselves. The purpose I am fixing, since adequacy is relative to it, is this: a decision theory good enough to be handed to an artificial agent that can be copied, can be simulated, is likely to be modelled accurately by other agents, and whose policy is implemented by a trained system it cannot fully inspect. I want the criteria such a theory must meet, not the criteria that make a theory appealing to a human being deliberating about a career change.

Please write the desiderata as a numbered list. For each item I want the requirement stated in a sentence or two, then the case or consideration that generates it — a concrete scenario wherever that is possible — and then a mark saying whether it is non-negotiable, in the sense that a theory failing it is not merely unattractive but incoherent, or whether it is a preference of one camp wearing the costume of a requirement. I suspect a substantial number of the things demanded in this literature belong in the second category, and sorting them out is most of the work I need done. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates, and flag every pair or triple of requirements that you think cannot be jointly satisfied: if some subset is impossible, that impossibility is the result of my chapter and I would rather have it stated plainly than buried in a caveat. A table is fine for the negotiability marking, though I want the motivating cases in prose. At least half of the cases you give should be ones that do not already appear in every treatment of the topic; if all you can produce is a transparent-box predictor, a smoking lesion and a piece of blackmail, the list will tell me nothing I do not know.

Three requirements are unclear to me and I would like them treated explicitly rather than folded silently into the standard list. First, whether a theory must be able to recommend an act to an agent that does not know which decision rule it is itself implementing — suppose a predictor scores you not on what you choose but on the rule your behaviour reveals you to be following, while your own policy is opaque to you from the inside; is that a genuine further requirement, or a restatement of some transparency or self-knowledge condition? Second, implementation invariance: it seems clear that recommendations should not change if you hold the algorithm fixed and change the substrate, but should they also be invariant when you hold the causal structure fixed and change the code, and can any one theory deliver both invariances? Third, the distinction between being updateless in choice and declining to conditionalise in belief — by updatelessness I mean roughly the family of views on which a choice is settled by what the agent had to go on before it learned which situation it occupies, or more generally by a policy fixed without conditioning on locally available information. I want to know whether a theory that collapses choice-updatelessness with belief-updatelessness ends up commending agents who hold systematically false beliefs about their own circumstances, and whether keeping the two apart is a requirement in its own right.

Finally, and briefly — a short paragraph each, no more — say how the evidential approach going back to Jeffrey, Lewis's causal decision theory, and the updateless approaches fare against your list. Keep the scoring short: the criteria are where the thinking is, and my committee will score the candidates themselves. Please do not give me references, a bibliography or a reading list; I have more than I can work through already, and what I am after is the specification.
