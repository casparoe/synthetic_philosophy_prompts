Longtime lurker of the rationalist-adjacent internet, first time actually asking something like this, so bear with me — this is long and I probably mangled at least one step writing it up at 1am.

Situation: there's a novel I've re-read maybe six times over fifteen years. Last year it came out that the author had been doing something genuinely bad for decades — not edgy opinions, the kind of thing where you'd want to be on the other side of the street. I still don't know what that does to the book. I got into a long argument with a friend who's much better at this than I am, and she built the following chain, which lands on the conclusion I want — that the moral stuff really does bear on the book's value as a book, not just on whether I should keep recommending it. Which is exactly why I don't trust it. I've read enough of these blogs to know that "I'm glad this argument works" is a warning light, not a green one. And separately, the thing seems to prove way too much: if it works, it also demolishes a bunch of other stuff I don't want demolished.

Her numbering, as faithfully as I can reproduce it:

1. What a work is worth aesthetically is fixed entirely by features of the work.
2. The author's moral character is a fact about how the work came to exist, not a feature of the work.
3. So the author's moral character can't bear on the work's aesthetic value. (This is the seperate-the-art-from-the-artist step.)
4. Nothing in (1)–(2) turns on the property being a moral one. The author's intentions, the tradition the thing is playing off, what it was answering to, whether it was sincere — those are all facts about how it came to exist too.
5. So none of those can bear on aesthetic value either.
6. But then aesthetic value is fixed by intrinsic properties of the artifact alone — by whatever you'd pick up in a blind encounter with the object.
7. Which means two word-for-word identical poems, one scratched out by a dying man in a trench in 1917 and one written last week by a copywriter as a style exercise, are equally good. And a forgery nobody can tell from the original is just as good as the original.
8. That's false. Those pairs come apart, and a theory of aesthetic value that can't see the difference is broken.
9. So (1) is false: facts about how a work came to exist *can* bear on a work's aesthetic value.
10. Which means (3) was never available, and the moral facts about my novelist can bear on the book's value as a book — not just on whether I pay him or praise him in public.

I've stared at it for a week and can't find the exit. When I said it felt like it proved too much, her answer was that I'm splitting hairs: once you let historical properties into the base at all there's no principled place to stop short of morality, so I either accept (10) or I owe her a line. That's the part I actually want addressed — not the general lay of the land.

If I have to choose I'd rather keep (8) than (1), but I don't want that to drive the answer.
