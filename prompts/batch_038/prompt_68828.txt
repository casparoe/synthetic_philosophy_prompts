I work at a six-person startup doing ultra-low-power logic test chips, and for about two weeks we've been deadlocked on an argument I don't have the stat mech background to referee. I'm the only one here who's read anything about Maxwell's demon outside a wikipedia article, so I'm hoping you can settle it for me. The fight is over a specific scheme we sketched on the whiteboard, so let me describe it. It's a Szilard-style single-molecule engine: molecule in a box in contact with a heat bath at temperature T, a partition you can insert in the middle, and a demon that measures which half the molecule is in and then lets it push the partition to extract kT ln 2 of work. Everyone here agrees the demon's memory has to be dealt with before the next cycle, and the standard answer is Bennett's — the measurement itself can be done reversibly, but resetting the one-bit memory to a standard state costs at least kT ln 2, so the books balance and there's no free lunch. Our twist is that we dont reset the memory at all. Our memory bit is a particle in a double-well potential, and between cycles we just lower the barrier, let the particle diffuse through the whole volume, and raise the barrier again wherever it happens to have ended up. Our claim: that's randomization, not compression, so we never perform the many-to-one squeeze that Landauer's principle is about, no heat gets dumped into the bath, and the memory's entropy rises for free. The next cycle then conditions on the memory's actual physical state instead of an assumed standard state, so the kT ln 2 never gets paid and the engine nets work from a single heat bath at uniform temperature.

Our outside thermodynamics consultant says this is garbage: that writing a measured value into a memory whose state you don't already know is exactly as expensive as erasing one, that we've just relocated the cost from the reset step to the write step, and that the whole proposal is a failure to track mutual information between molecule and memory. Our CEO, who's the device physicist, says the consultant is begging the question — that the rule saying every logically irreversible step must dissipate at least kT ln 2 isn't a theorem of statistical mechanics but an inductive generalization from the handful of toy models anyone can solve exactly (ideal gas in a piston, degenerate two-level bit), that every attempted general proof either assumes the second law it's meant to secure or assumes the phase-space bookkeeping that's supposed to be derived from it, and that at the scale we're building at the second law is a statistical claim about averages anyway, so a device that nets work most of the time and fails occasionally isn't the perpetual motion machine he keeps getting accused of designing.

Write the dialogue between these two, standing in front of our whiteboard at 7pm, each trying to break the other's account of the actual device. Things I care about: neither of them is a strawman and neither wins just by sounding more confident; each has to engage the specific step of the cycle the other is pointing at rather than retreating to a slogan, and each should land at least one point the other can't wave away. I want the ledger made explicit as they argue — which step costs what in units of kT ln 2, where heat goes, where correlation between molecule and memory gets created and where it gets destroyed, and what happens to the step where the demon reads the state of its own randomized memory, which is the part I most suspect both of them are skating over. Don't let them converge and don't let either convert the other; have them end up agreeing on everything except one identifiable step, so I can see where the disagreement actually lives. Somewhere around 1000-1400 words of dialogue, and then four or five sentences outside it telling me which step of the ledger they diverge on, which of the two you think is right about that step, and what I'd have to build or calculate to find out.
