I've been putting together a session for our little reading group on rule systems that change themselves — the nomic stuff, self-repealing clauses, that whole family of puzzles — and to keep people from just reciting the usual constitutional-law chestnuts I wrote my own charter and made the group argue about a specific thing that happens under it. The group split into two camps and has been talking past each other for weeks, so I want to hand them the document they should have written instead. Here's the setup, which you should treat as the complete set of facts (don't go looking for more, and don't give me a reading list — I want the analysis, not the references).

The Ashgrove Charter says: Art. 1 — the members are the original signatories and anyone later admitted under Art. 7, and "every member may propose amendments to this Charter." Art. 4 — business happens at monthly meetings, quorum is half the membership, ordinary decisions by simple majority of those present. Art. 9(a) — "any provision of this Charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the full membership at two consecutive meetings, provided written notice of the exact proposed text was given to every member at least thirty days before the first of those meetings." Art. 9(b) — Art. 9 itself may be amended only by the procedure in 9(a). Art. 9(c) — "no amendment shall take effect that deprives any member of the right to propose amendments." Art. 12 — "this Charter is the complete and exclusive statement of the Society's governing rules; the Society acknowledges no source of obligation outside it." Then came what everyone in my group now just calls the Sequence. Notice was properly given, and at two consecutive meetings two-thirds of the full membership amended 9(a) to read: "any provision of this Charter may be amended by a simple majority of members present at one meeting." Three weeks later, under the new 9(a), a simple majority of those present repealed 9(c) outright. One week after that, again under the new 9(a), a simple majority of those present added Art. 13, vesting all powers of the Society — including the power to amend the Charter — in a standing committee of five, whose decisions are not subject to member vote. Members still exist and can still nominally "propose" things, but proposals now go to the committee, which is free to ignore them. The committee has since met twice. Nobody has quit.

Camp A, who I've taken to calling the chain-of-title people, say every step was enacted in strict compliance with the rule in force at the moment it was enacted, that 9(c) was repealed by a procedure 9(c) did not forbid, that Art. 12 means there is literally no external standard available to declare anything void, and that the Society is now governed by five people. Camp B, the ladder people, say the amendment power is a conferred power and cannot be turned on the condition of its own conferral; that steps two and three weren't amendments of the Charter but the substitution of a different one wearing its name; that the notice-and-two-meetings requirement existed precisely to make the Sequence impossible, so using it to dismantle itself is the paradigm case of self-consumption; and that Art. 13 is void and the members are still members.

So: draft the joint statement the two of them could actually both sign. I want a numbered section of agreed claims — things like what the text says, what happened in what order, which steps were procedurally regular on their face, what both sides are actually trying to explain — and I'd be surprised if there aren't at least six or seven of those that are non-vacuous. Then a disagreements table: for each live point of contention, give it in wording Camp A would sign as a statement of their own position, in wording Camp B would sign as a statement of theirs, and a third column saying whether what's left after that is a genuine disagreement or a fight about the word "valid." I suspect some of it is verbal — my guess is that "in force," "authorized," and "binding" are doing three different jobs and the camps keep swapping them — but tell me if I'm wrong about where. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody. My own candidates are whether a rule that confers a power can be used to alter the rule that confers it; whether there's a principled line between amending a charter and replacing it, or whether that line is just Camp B's conclusion in disguise; whether Art. 12 helps Camp A or secretly destroys them, since if the Charter is the only source of obligation then whatever it becomes is the only source of obligation; and whether the post-Sequence body is even the same Society. Throw out any of those that are duds and add the ones I missed. For each crux that survives, give a concrete case, thought experiment, or piece of reasoning both camps would agree bears on it — real-world cousins are fine here, e.g. permanently entrenched clauses in actual constitutions, or the way parliamentary bodies handle suspending their own rules, as long as you only lean on ones you're sure about — and write down each side's prediction about it in advance, before you say which one comes out better.

Last thing, and it's the part I actually care about: don't hand me a balanced document and shrug. The joint statement has to be genuinely co-signable, that's the whole exercise, but after it you drop the neutrality and tell me where you land — which camp's wording you'd sign on the single most important crux, whether the Sequence succeeded, and what would have to be true for you to change your mind about it. If you think the whole dispute is unresolvable or dissolved, say so plainly and say which of the two camps is closer to right anyway, with your reasons.
