I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and the least glamorous part of the job is a recurring editorial decision I currently make by reflex rather than by principle: whether a claim about the past counts as historical inquiry or as its counterfeit. The label is not decorative. Print it and the claimant's publisher, funder, or department chair calls my editor; decline to print it where it belongs and I have handed a large audience to a bad actor while telling myself I was being even-handed. I would like the principle before the next round of calls.

So: write out the desiderata. What must a criterion of historical demarcation actually deliver before I could rely on it? For each requirement, give the consideration or the case behind it — I want the reasoning, not a list of labels. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any adequate account and which are really the preferences of a particular camp (the falsificationist's, the sociologist's, the archivist's, the debunker's) dressed up as neutral conditions. If some subset cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list is itself an impossibility result — say so plainly and show exactly where it breaks. And tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate criteria, since that is the one I should be arguing about rather than the ones everyone agrees on.

To keep this out of the groove worn by the usual survey, here are the cases I need the list to get right.

1. The Atlantis lineage runs through three very different performers: Plato, whose dialogues were plainly doing something other than reporting; Ignatius Donnelly, who in 1882 converted them into a literal world history with a thesis list and comparative tables; and a contemporary television presenter who commissions surveys, interviews specialists on camera, presents himself as merely following the evidence, and treats every specialist who disagrees as captured by a consensus. A good criterion should be able to say that these three are not in the same category and should say why without appealing to who has the distribution deal.

2. A chronological revision advanced by a professional mathematician at a major research university and a member of his national academy, supported by statistical comparisons of chronicles and dynastic lists, which relocates essentially everything we call ancient Roman, Greek, and Egyptian history into the medieval period. The author has the credentials, the formal apparatus, and the citation practice of a scholar and none of the historical content. Tell me which item on my list does the work here — and note that if the answer is that the specialists reject it, my criterion has collapsed into an appeal to authority, which is precisely the charge the people I am arguing with make against me.

3. Velikovsky. Astronomers mounted a campaign to keep Worlds in Collision off the presses at all, working through threats to boycott its publisher's textbook division rather than through refutation, and they were broadly right about the physics. A criterion on which establishment rejection is decisive would have classified the book as pseudohistory on the strength of a suppression campaign. The same criterion should also explain why, decades after the physics was settled, the surrounding subculture continued to reproduce itself without ever engaging the disconfirming result — and it should be able to distinguish that from a research programme simply being slow to die.

4. The amateur who took a poem as a map. Schliemann went to Hisarlik because Homer said so, was treated as a nuisance by the professionals, excavated anyway, and announced that he had found Priam's city in a stratum now dated well over a millennium earlier than anything the epics could describe, with an excavation record that has been criticized ever since. My criterion cannot say that hunches drawn from literary sources are pseudohistorical, or it condemns him; and it cannot say that results vindicate method, or it makes demarcation hostage to luck and to the accidents of what survives.

5. Heyerdahl's raft. A diffusionist thesis about the peopling of the Pacific that specialists regarded as motivated fantasy, pursued by building a vessel and sailing it — an intervention that produced data nobody had bothered to gather — and which genomic work on present-day Polynesian populations has since left partially standing, though not in the form Heyerdahl claimed and not for the reasons he gave. Where does the criterion put someone who was wrong in the aggregate but whose method improved the field's evidential position?

6. Oral traditions that describe coastlines, islands, and landforms that no longer exist, apparently dating to the closing of the last glaciation. The evidential basis is testimony carried across hundreds of generations rather than artifacts, and the content arrives embedded in narrative, genealogy, and ceremony. Under any straightforward methodological test this is legend; under any test that credits independent corroboration against sea-level and geomorphological records, some of it is evidence. This case matters to me more than the others, because the same institution that would never knowingly run a pseudohistory piece has, for most of its existence, filed exactly this kind of testimony under folklore. The criterion has to explain the difference without conceding that the difference is who is doing the telling.

7. Function. Much ancient-astronaut and lost-civilization writing has a traceable lineage in colonial-era speculation about who could possibly have raised the monuments of Africa and the Americas, and its central inference — this structure is too sophisticated for its attested builders — is not obviously defective as an inference. Method-based criteria look blind to this. But a criterion that demarcates partly on the social function of a claim will sweep in nationalist textbook disputes, state-mandated curricula, and the Lost Cause industry, which are not pseudohistory in the same sense and which I do not want to be writing about in the same register.

Three constraints on the deliverable, which you should treat as part of the specification and are free to declare unsatisfiable if that is your verdict.

- It must be operable by someone who is not a specialist in the period under discussion and who has about a day. If applying the criterion requires knowing whether a statistical technique has been correctly applied to a corpus of chronicles, it is not a criterion, it is a description of the specialist consultation I already commission.
- It must be statable in print to a general reader in a way that does not reduce to trusting us.
- It must not be gameable by a practitioner who has read it. This one may be the crux, and if you think it is fatal, say so and say what I should be using in its place.

Close with a brief scoring pass — three sentences at most each — of a falsificationist account, a consensus-or-expertise account, an account in terms of immunizing strategies and degenerating programmes, and an account in terms of the social function of the claim, measured against the list you have built. I am not after a balanced tour: take a definite position on which requirements are genuine and which are one camp's preferences, on which one does the exclusionary work, and on what you would actually put in the style guide. If you conclude the project fails, argue for that conclusion outright instead of leaving me a menu.
