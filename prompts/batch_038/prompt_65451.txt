I am a senior in high school, and for the past year I have been running an independent study with our school's philosophy society on the problem Hofstadter named when he coined the word *sphexishness* in his Metamagical Themas columns: the story of the digger wasp that paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it there while she goes inside to check, and — if an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches during the inspection — comes out, drags it back to the entrance, goes in to check again, and so on, apparently forever. Both Hofstadter and Dennett have pressed the story into service, and in our meetings it has taken over a much larger question: whether the difference between a creature that runs a fixed routine and a creature that seems to act for reasons is a difference in degree or a difference in kind.

Our society has split into two camps that have been arguing past each other since October, and our faculty sponsor has suggested we attempt an adversarial collaboration. I would like you to draft the joint statement both camps could sign.

The Continuity camp holds that every agent, at every level of description, is running routines. What we call flexibility is the size and recombination of the repertoire plus its fit to the environment: the wasp looks rigid only because we perturb her outside her range, and we look free only because we perturb each other inside ours. They add that the higher-order business of noticing and revising one's own routines is itself a routine, and that the regress bottoms out in machinery. They are not dualists; they agree the brain is mechanism all the way down.

The Discontinuity camp agrees about mechanism all the way down but denies that the difference is merely quantitative. What matters to them is not repertoire size but architecture: whether the system carries a representation of the goal that survives the failure of the particular means currently serving it, and whether its own routines are available to it as objects it can inspect, describe, and override. For them the wasp's trouble is not that she lacks options but that when an option fails she has no way to register the failure as a failure of the routine.

Their argument keeps collapsing into two moves: Continuity says, show me the mechanism you are describing that is not itself a routine; Discontinuity says, then explain why the same mechanism, in a person, produces an apology.

To keep the document from dissolving into abstractions, I want it built around three notions I have devised, which neither camp has used. First, *recovery depth*: the number of nested, unanticipated interruptions an agent can absorb before it either abandons the goal or resumes a subroutine as though nothing had happened. Second, *return type*: once an interruption is cleared, does the agent go back to the goal and re-derive the means (goal-return), or back to the interrupted subroutine and re-execute it (program-return)? Third, the *interruption-distance profile*: for a given task, the probability of goal-return plotted against how far the perturbation is from anything the task normally calls for. My hypothesis, which neither camp has yet considered, is that the profile is continuous across species and across individual people, and that what actually divides the two camps is its shape — how steeply it falls, and whether the fall is graceful or catastrophic.

Here is the case I want the statement to explain. I memorized a seven-minute speech for a debate tournament. In the second round a judge interrupted me midway with a question I could answer perfectly well. I answered it, and then found that I could not resume where I had stopped: the only thing I could do was begin again from the first sentence. In that moment I was fully aware of being stuck and wholly unable to do anything about it.

Please produce the following.

1. A numbered section of claims both camps accept. These must be substantive enough that a serious opponent of both camps would object at least once, and none of them may be so thin that anything containing a thermostat could sign them.
2. A disagreements table with these columns: the disagreement as the Continuity camp would word it; the same disagreement as the Discontinuity camp would word it; a neutral formulation each side could read without wincing; whether the dispute is substantive or verbal; and, where it is verbal, which word is doing the damage.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp — and for each, the test or argument both camps accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance in the form "if we observe X, we will say Y."
4. At least one crux must be one I could actually settle this spring with twenty student volunteers, a stopwatch, and tasks I can build from index cards and a laptop. Describe that experiment concretely enough — materials, procedure, trial count, operational definitions of goal-return versus program-return, and which result favors which camp — that I could hand it to my sponsor unchanged.
5. A short section on the wasp: what the two camps agree the anecdote does and does not establish, given that the empirical standing of the standard retelling has been questioned, and what they agree would have to be shown before either side uses it as evidence again.
6. A list of sentences that look like agreements but that only one camp could actually sign — the traps we should stop saying to each other.
7. A closing paragraph from each camp stating what would count as evidence against its own position.

I am not asking you to decide who is right; the deliverable has to be a document both sides could put their names to. Please do not include a bibliography or citations, as I will add references myself. Aim for roughly 1,500 words, and if the only way to reach agreement on some point is for one camp to concede it, say so explicitly and say which camp is conceding.
