Three months ago, after eleven years of seizures that no combination of drugs has controlled, my epileptologist recommended a corpus callosotomy, and I am now on a surgical list for the autumn. I taught philosophy of mind for a decade, and Dennett's 1978 story Where Am I? — the man whose brain is removed and kept alive in a vat in Houston while his body is radio-driven to a site in Oklahoma, who then has to work out whether he is in Houston or in Tulsa, and later has to decide what to do about the duplicate brain that was made as a backup — appeared in nearly every undergraduate course I ran. I can no longer read it as a puzzle. Before the operation I want a disciplined and unsentimental analysis of the one real episode that seems to me its closest empirical cousin: the commissurotomy patients studied in Los Angeles from the early 1960s onward, the series that produced the widely read 1967 popular account of the split brain, the left hemisphere's confabulated explanation of why a shovel belongs with a chicken foot, the much-reported patient who stood up and walked off and explained that he had been going to the refrigerator for a Coke, and eventually a Nobel Prize in 1981.

Begin with the mapping, done explicitly rather than by gesture. Which element of Dennett's apparatus — the vat, the body, the radio link, the duplicate — has a correspondent in the commissurotomy patient, and which have none at all? I want a clear statement of what the fiction assumes that the patients falsify, and of what the patients exhibit that the fiction has no room for.

Then bring your own judgment to a distinction I have not been able to stop turning over. I suspect the story collapses two questions that come apart in the real cases: a locus question, about which physical system carries whatever makes me the same person over time, and an indexical question, about which of several simultaneously present candidates the first-person pronoun picks out. In the fiction the two track each other, so nothing is lost by treating them as one. In a commissurotomy patient the locus question may have a plural answer while the indexical question has a single one — or the reverse — because there is exactly one speech stream, one body, and one continuous autobiographical memory. Tell me whether this distinction does any real work or merely restates the puzzle, and, if it does work, which of the story's conclusions survive it.

Turn the analysis around afterward: what does the disconnection record show about the thought experiment as a method? Does it support the intuition the story is meant to elicit, undercut it, or reveal that the story was never really about the brain at all but about the narrator — the thing that goes on producing a first-person account whether or not it has any?

Two constraints. First, mark clearly where the evidence runs out and where the fiction is simply silent; I would rather be told that a view has nothing to say about the unity of agency, or about why these patients never report feeling doubled, than have it speak anyway. Second, take account of the methodological controversy of roughly the last decade over how much of the classic disconnection data survives more careful testing, and say what happens to the analysis if a substantial part of it does not.

One wrinkle to weigh rather than dismiss: my own operation is planned as a partial callosotomy sparing the splenium, so interhemispheric visual transfer is expected to remain largely intact. I will not become a split-brain patient in the sense the literature means. Say what that does to the mapping, and whether it should change what I take from the story.

I am not asking for reassurance, and not for a survey of the personal identity literature. No citations — I know where to look.
