I would like a case analysis that runs in both directions: Rousseau's *Letter to d'Alembert on the Theater* applied to the Festival of the Supreme Being, and the festival applied back to the letter.

The following facts may be taken as given, so you need not hedge about them. The Convention decreed the cult of the Supreme Being on 18 Floréal Year II (7 May 1794) on Robespierre's report. The inaugural festival was held on 20 Prairial Year II (8 June 1794), which fell on the Christian feast of Pentecost. It was designed by Jacques-Louis David, with François-Joseph Talma — then France's most celebrated tragedian — among its organizers. Robespierre, at that moment president of the Convention, presided and delivered the addresses: first in the Tuileries, where he put a torch to a figure of Atheism and let a statue of Wisdom emerge from the smoke, then after the procession had moved to the Champ de Mars, where an artificial mountain had been erected. The march was ordered by section and by social category — the aged, mothers carrying infants, boys, girls with flowers, soldiers — each assigned a place; a hymn written for the occasion was sung by massed choirs and taken up by the crowd. Every commune in France was ordered to hold its own version the same day. Seven weeks later Robespierre was dead. Only weeks before the festival, the Convention had transferred Rousseau's remains to the Panthéon, and Robespierre had been a devotee of Rousseau since his youth. Earlier in the Revolution the pattern had been different: at the Festival of Reason of November 1793, Notre-Dame was rededicated as a Temple of Reason, a stage mountain was built inside the cathedral, and the goddess was personified by a singer from the Opéra.

Now the lens. In the letter Rousseau denies that the stage can improve morals, holding that a play can only flatter and intensify dispositions the public already has; he attacks the doctrine of catharsis on the ground that theatrical pity is cheap and sterile, discharging in tears a compassion we then feel no obligation to act on; he reads Molière's *Misanthrope* as making the man of virtue ridiculous and the agreeable worldly man sympathetic; he concedes that theater is useful in a great corrupt city such as Paris, where it keeps people from worse mischief, while insisting it would be ruinous in a small republic such as Geneva, on grounds of idleness, expense, and enclosure; he treats the actor's trade as the public sale of one's own person and defends Geneva's exclusion of players while noting the inconsistency of a France that honors in Paris those its church excommunicates; and against all this he sets his festival — a stake crowned with flowers planted in a square, the people gathered around it, the spectators made into actors, a free people celebrating itself rather than watching a representation of itself.

To keep the mapping honest, sort the festival's features using this working test for whether an event is one of Rousseau's festivals rather than a piece of theater, and then tell me where the test itself breaks. (i) Authorship: is the event's script generated by the participants' own dispositions, or composed for them by someone else? (ii) Assignability: can any citizen present take up any role in it, or are roles distributed from above — by section, by age, by sex, by office? (iii) Excess: does the event leave its participants a surplus of feeling to spend elsewhere, as Rousseau charges against tragic pity, or is the feeling spent in the doing? My own guess is that 20 Prairial passes (iii) and fails (i) and (ii) comprehensively; I want to know whether that shows Rousseau's distinction is the wrong one, or that it is the right one and the festival was never an instance of it.

Then work through these problems, in this order. First, the Talma problem: Rousseau's objection to players concerns public self-exhibition for pay, yet the Republic's supreme anti-theatrical civic event was staged with the assistance of its greatest tragedian. Is Talma present as a citizen or as an actor, and does anything turn on whether he walks in the procession or only designs it? Note the tension this creates with criterion (i), since David and Talma are precisely the authors whose script the people then perform. Second, scale: Rousseau's festival presupposes a small, face-to-face city of homogeneous moeurs, and he was writing about a town of some twenty thousand; here a decree orders every commune in a nation of twenty-eight million to enact the same program on the same day. What does that do to the claim that the people become the spectacle — and does a festival whose attendance is mandated, in a city under revolutionary government at the height of the Terror, count as voluntary at all, given that absence is politically legible? Third, the foil: which of the two festivals does the letter condemn more severely — the earlier one, which is theater staged in a borrowed cathedral, or the later one, which is theater without a theater building — and is that verdict defensible? Fourth, the presiding magistrate: Robespierre speaks from an elevated platform while others march, and the representation of virtue is enacted by the man most identified with the guillotine. Say whether that irony does analytical work for Rousseau's account or is merely rhetoric, and be disciplined about the difference.

Finally, reverse the direction. Does the festival's inability to hold the regime together for seven weeks confirm Rousseau's central claim — that spectacles cannot remake moeurs, only express them — or does the episode disclose a third category his letter has no name for, in which mass spectacle functions as an instrument of state power rather than as an index of public morals? Does Rousseau's own chapter on civil religion in the *Social Contract* sit with the letter here or against it, given that the decree's articles read like the dogmas of that chapter put to music? And state plainly, wherever it applies, that the letter is silent: it never contemplates a festival composed by a government, enforced by decree, and presided over by its author.

Around fifteen hundred words, organized under the headings your argument actually needs. No references or reading lists — I want the analysis, including your best account of what the episode costs Rousseau's theory and what it saves.
