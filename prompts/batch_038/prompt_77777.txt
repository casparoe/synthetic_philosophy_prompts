So I got dragged into a three-hour argument at a diner last night and I still can't settle it in my own head, which is why I'm asking. I'm in a little econ-philosophy reading group and we've been slogging through Keynes's Treatise on Probability — my battered paperback copy is held together with tape at this point — and one of my friends, Dana, does quant stuff at a fund and thinks the whole "probability isn't always a number, and sometimes two probabilities can't even be ranked against each other" business is just intellectuals being squeamish about imprecise priors. Her line is that anything I can act on, I can put a number on, and everything else is a second-order uncertainty that could also get a number if I bothered. I don't think she's wrong about all of it, and that's the problem. What I want from you isn't a survey of the options — I want you to pick a side and argue for it.

Here's my case list, with my gut calls. A stranger's coin that came up heads 28 times running on the table in front of me: I'd bet hard on heads for the 29th, and being told afterwards that it came from a magic shop wouldn't move me an inch. A fresh coin straight out of a sealed mint box: I'd call it fifty-fifty, but the exact same magic-shop remark would make me throw the number out entirely. My verdict is that the second belief is shakier even though the two looked equally good as numbers — and I don't want to cash that out as "I'm less confident in my confidence," because I think what changed is how much was actually behind the belief. Barometer high, clouds black, do I take an umbrella: I say there's genuinely no fact about whether rain is more likely than not here, and I'm not being coy, but I also think it's completely fine to just pick one and get moving. Urn A known to be half red half black, urn B filled from a barrel of urns of unknown mixtures: pressed, I'd say half red for both, but I'd much rather bet on A, and I don't think that preference is a bias to be beaten out of me. Whether a startup survives five years: no probability, not even a fuzzy interval, and anyone claiming otherwise is dressing up a hunch — though I do sometimes invest anyway, and when I do I know perfectly well I'm just going along with a convention, and I don't feel guilty about it. A 40,000-person trial in a population nothing like my uncle versus a specialist's judgment from a dozen patients who are exactly like him: I trust the specialist, and I'd describe the trial as heavy but unaimed and her dozen cases as light but relevant — which makes me think amount of evidence and relevance of evidence are two separate knobs and I refuse to have them collapsed into one. Circumstantial case with dozens of small independent details versus one very confident eyewitness: convict on the circumstantial one, and if you tell me the two juries were "equally likely to be right" I'll say sure, but the first one is better justified.

The pair I can't reconcile is the last one against the startup. In the coin, umbrella, doctor and jury cases I'm treating relevant evidence as making a belief epistemically better in a way a single number can't capture. In the startup case I'm saying that piling on more relevant evidence — the sector, the founder, the term sheet, the comps — buys you literally nothing, because there was no probability to strengthen in the first place. So evidence both matters to whether you can rationally hold a degree of belief and doesn't matter to it at all. Related: I think the person who declines to bet on urn B is right, but I think someone who declines to bet on every long shot is a paranoid, and I can't draw that line without sounding arbitrary.

So: argue for a principle, or a small set of them, that says when a rational degree of belief exists at all, when it has to be a number, and when more evidence makes a belief better rather than merely different. Tie each clause to the case that forces it. Then take a side on the thing Dana and I actually fought about — does the Keynesian weight-of-argument and relevance distinction do real work that her imprecise-priors-plus-second-order-numbers story can't do, or is it a distinction without a difference — and defend it against the objection I think is strongest, which is that if weight never enters a decision or a judgment of justification, what on earth is it tracking. Tell me plainly which of my six verdicts your principle can't save and why I should give it up rather than complicate the principle. And run it on one I didn't bring up: a city council deciding whether to spend serious money now against a flood the models call 1-in-100-years but where half the engineers think the models dont apply here at all — does your account say there's a probability there, and does it say the council should act as if there were one anyway?
