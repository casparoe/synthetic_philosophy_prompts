My final paper was supposed to be about when a person is legally dead, and I thought I had a really clean argument, but the only comment on my draft was "your premise here conflates two distinct things — come see me," and then the semester ended before I could. I've been stewing on it for a week, and I've started to suspect he's the one who's confused and just didn't want to write it out. Maybe you can tell me which.

Here's the argument. I always assumed the whole "redefining death" controversy was that 1968 Harvard committee thing about patients on ventilators — brains destroyed, hearts kept pumping. That's brain death, and it's the controversial one. But DCD patients aren't brain dead at all. They're people whose families have decided to withdraw support, so death gets declared the old way, by circulation and breathing stopping. Fine. Except the protocol makes everyone wait a stand-by period after the heart stops — I keep seeing five minutes quoted, and I'd assumed that number was written into the law somewhere — and the stated reason for it is to make sure the heart doesn't start up again on its own. Then they declare death, and in the newer versions of the protocol, the regional perfusion ones, they open the chest, clamp things off, hook the body up to a pump that circulates warm oxygenated blood, and the donor's own heart starts beating again inside them. So: if the heart can be restarted, circulation had not irreversably ceased, and if circulation hadn't irreversibly ceased then the declaration of death was false as a matter of fact. Irreversible means can't be reversed. It's an English word, not a term of art that a transplant committee gets to quietly redefine. And then organs were taken from a living patient, and the "dead donor rule" is a slogan people repeat so they don't have to look at what's happening on the table. The only reason I can see that this isn't said out loud more often is that the beating heart doesn't reach the brain because of the clamps — but a beating heart is circulation, and if someone took the clamps off the brain would get blood too, which seems to me like exactly the point. I also get that the the brain-death and circulatory-death routes are supposed to be two ways of establishing the same single thing, but I can't see how a body that can be restarted counts as the same kind of dead as one that can't.
