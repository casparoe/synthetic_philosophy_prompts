Some background before I get to what I actually need. I'm a retired isotope geochronologist — thirty-six years in a lab, mostly argon-argon step heating with some U-Pb on the side, which is to say I spent my whole working life arguing with people about what a date actually is. Since I stopped working I've been auditing philosophy of science seminars at the university here and I've gotten unreasonably deep into the Anthropocene business. In April I'm giving a 35-minute invited talk at our environmental humanities center's annual workshop, with open discussion after. The room will be about fifty people and it is not my crowd at all: two Quaternary stratigraphers, one of whom works on varved lake records and followed the working group's Crawford Lake proposal closely, and one who is a hard formalist about the time scale and was privately delighted when the Subcommission voted the epoch proposal down in 2024; four or five environmental historians, mostly early modern, who think that pinning the start to 1952 lets the Columbian exchange off the hook and who lean toward something like the 1610 CO2 dip; two philosophers of science, one of whom works on models and analogy and one on realism about natural kinds; and a crowd of geography and STS grad students who will want to talk about expertise and who gets to decide. Crucially, almost everyone in the room already treats the Anthropocene as a settled cultural concept and the geologists' rejection as basically a technicality. I want to argue it wasn't a technicality, and that the vote exposed something structural.

Here's my thesis, roughly, so the objections have something to bite. I think two different notions of a boundary are being run together, and that stratigraphic practice makes the confusion unavoidable rather than accidental. Call them the index boundary — the level in a physical archive where a globally correlatable signal first appears, which is the only thing a golden spike can ever license — and the cause boundary, the point at which the human/Earth-system feedback actually became self-sustaining. The 1952 plutonium horizon is an index boundary standing in for a cause boundary nobody can point at. Now, my own discipline has known this forever and handles it honestly: an Ar-Ar age isn't the time a rock crystallised, it's the time a mineral cooled through its closure temperature — Dodson's result from 1973 — and the difference is a lag you calculate and publish rather than hide. Nobody in my old lab ever thought a plateau age was the age of the eruption; it's the age at which the system stopped forgetting. So my first proposal is that the Anthropocene gets the same treatment: publish the lag as part of the definition of the unit instead of pretending the spike is the event. Second, and this is definately the part I'll get hammered for — I think a boundary should be definable by an inflection rather than by a first appearance. I've been calling it a second-derivative boundary, on the model of how you actually decide whether an Ar-Ar release spectrum has a plateau: you don't take the first gas released, you look at the shape of the whole spectrum and ask whether a large contiguous stretch is flat. Translated: a unit begins where the rate of change of a specified set of Earth-system variables changes curvature and stays changed, not where some marker first shows up in one lake. Third, since a formal chronostratigraphic unit and a historical period are answers to different questions, we should run two periodizations openly, each with its own evidential standards, and require any proposal to state the gap between them — instead of fighting for a century over one number.
