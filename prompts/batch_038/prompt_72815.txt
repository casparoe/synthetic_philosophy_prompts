You are a philosopher of science who works on the scientific realism debate and who also has experience preparing moderators for interviews that are adversarial but civil. Please think carefully and work through the position before drafting anything.

I moderate a recorded interview series run by our department's graduate philosophy society. Next week's guest defends a view that is not mainstream, and this interview is the only chance our audience will have to hear it pressed rather than merely introduced. Her position, as I understand it from the book and from two articles:

- The pessimistic meta-induction has been aimed at the wrong object. The familiar lists in that literature are lists of *theories* — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, and the like — and the realist has a well-worn answer: whatever did the genuine explanatory work in those theories is preserved in its successors, so only the ontological surplus dies. She concedes that answer, at least provisionally, and does not want to relitigate it.
- Instead she runs the induction over *methods*: the procedures, standards of evidence, and inferential licenses by which a research tradition judges itself to be doing well. Her claim is that the record shows repeatedly that methods which were, by the internal standards of their own tradition, conspicuously successful — and were praised precisely for that success — were later abandoned not because they were applied badly but because the standards they embodied were themselves mistaken. Her coinage for the resulting thesis is methodological pessimism: our current inference licenses are, with high probability, ones that our successors will regard as unreliable in exactly the domains where they now look most reliable.
- She argues that this version escapes two standard realist replies, and this is where the view gets interesting. First, it escapes preservative and structural variants of realism, because those responses trade on continuity of *content*, and she is claiming nothing about content: her pessimism concerns the trustworthiness of the licenses themselves, which are not the sort of thing that gets carried forward inside a successor theory. Second, she turns the no-miracles argument around: that argument is an inference to the best explanation, and inference to the best explanation is itself one of the licenses on her list, so the realist's central argument inherits the doubt her induction raises rather than answering it.

The book has drawn three criticisms that I want to press in the interview:

(i) Self-undermining: her thesis is itself an inductive extrapolation from a historical sample, and she cannot simultaneously distrust extrapolation from samples of scientific success and depend on it for her own premise.
(ii) Case selection: no neutral criterion determines which abandoned procedures count as methods that succeeded by their own lights, as opposed to fringe programs or methods competently applied to a domain they were never licensed for. Her examples are chosen to fit.
(iii) Reach: even granting the premise, it does not get to realism, because a realist can accept that methods will be revised while holding that our current methods are approximately truth-conducive, and can point out that method change has itself been progressive on any measure of progress that does not beg the question.

Task: draft twelve to fourteen interview questions in the order I should actually ask them.

Before the questions, give me a short brainstorming section — no more than three hundred words — in which you identify where her position is genuinely strong and where it is genuinely exposed, and name the two or three deflating moves you expect her to make (for instance, retreating from "unreliable" to "less reliable than we assume," or conceding that her own argument is inductive while claiming that the asymmetry in burden of proof carries the weight, or treating methodological pessimism as a regulative caution rather than a factual prediction). Design at least some questions so that those moves cost her something.

Then the questions, subject to these requirements:

1. At least one question on each of (i), (ii), and (iii), and beneath each, a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Label these three questions explicitly so I can find them at speed.
2. One question on her claimed immunity to structural realism. If methods are individuated by the theories that license them, then method change may be nothing but content change in different vocabulary; make her say what a method is, and follow up with the case she will probably reach for — a procedure that survives a theory change intact and is nevertheless retrospectively judged to have been unreliable, or the converse — and ask her what her account predicts about that case in advance.
3. One question on the inference-to-the-best-explanation point. Ask her to specify what inheriting the doubt amounts to: does she claim the no-miracles inference is unreliable, or only that we presently lack grounds for trusting it? Follow up by asking whether her own historical generalization is any better placed, and by asking what she says if her answer is that hers needs no positive support because the burden sits with the realist.
4. One question that forces a falsifiable formulation: what historical discovery would convince her that methodological pessimism is false? Push past the reply I expect, which will be that no single case could disconfirm a probabilistic thesis, and ask her then what distinguishes her thesis from one that is merely unfalsifiable.
5. One question on the practical upshot. If our licenses are probably unreliable where they look most reliable, what should a working scientist do differently on Monday morning, and what should a funding body or a court relying on expert testimony do differently? She will likely answer that nothing changes locally; press her on whether the view is then idle, and on whether she has simply relocated the old underdetermination worry.
6. Include one question about the relationship between her thesis and the problem of unconceived alternatives, since audiences will assume they are the same argument: ask her what her view adds, given that the pessimist about unconceived alternatives already concludes that our theory space is impoverished in ways we cannot detect from inside it.
7. No biographical or generic questions, no softballs, and nothing that merely asks her to restate the view. Every question must be answerable aloud in under two minutes, and must contain enough of the objection that a listener who has not read the book can follow what is being asked.
8. Do not put words in anyone's mouth: no invented quotations from her, and no quoted passages from the realism literature. Describe other positions in the debate rather than citing them, and do not produce a reading list.

Format: the brainstorming section first, then the numbered questions with follow-ups indented beneath each, then a closing note of three sentences telling me which two questions you would cut if the recording runs over time and why.
