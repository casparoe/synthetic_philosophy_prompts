At the end of next month my mother-in-law retires after thirty-one years running a county animal shelter, and I have been given the after-dinner speech at the hall where the staff association holds its annual dinner. I would like you to write it.

The room will be about eighty people, and they are not the same audience. There are her kennel workers and volunteers, most of whom she hired at nineteen and who have seen her cry twice; three veterinarians who did the surgical and medical work and who have their own views about what she asked of them; the board and several donors, one of whom told her plainly years ago that animals were property and was told by her, equally plainly, that he was welcome to his opinion and would still be billed; my wife and her two brothers, who grew up in the shelter office; and one guest who belongs to none of these worlds. He is a philosopher who works on whether lobsters, crabs, octopuses and insects are the kind of thing that can be harmed at all, and he was one of the people behind a government-commissioned scientific review that ended with lobsters, crabs and octopuses written into a British law about animal sentience. He is coming because eleven years ago my mother-in-law wrote him a letter. She had read a magazine article about prawn trawling in a dentist's waiting room, and she wrote him two pages asking whether his science could tell her where the line was. He wrote back that it could not, and that nobody's could. She had that letter framed and hung it by the door of her office, and she told anyone who asked that it was the most useful answer she had ever been given.

That is the room. The difficulty is that the two halves of her working life look like a contradiction, and every person in the hall knows both halves. On one side, she authorised and personally carried out thousands of euthanasia decisions: sick animals, unadoptable animals, animals nobody would pay to treat. She did them at the end of the day after the volunteers had gone, she refused to let anyone in the building say that an animal had been put to sleep, she insisted on the word killed, and she wrote every name in a ledger she kept on her desk for thirty-one years. On the other side, she would not let anyone squash a spider in the office. She kept a yogurt pot and a piece of envelope in the top drawer and walked spiders out to the hedge, in the rain, in her indoor shoes. Wasps in the kennels meant the door opened and everyone waited twenty minutes. When a volunteer asked her where she drew the line, she said: I don't draw lines I can't see, and I'm not starting now. That is hers and I want it kept almost word for word; it is the only quotation the speech needs.

My own view, which the speech has to convey without ever stating, is that these were not two different attitudes but one attitude applied twice. She seems to have treated the question of how much something can be harmed as a question with more than two answers, sliding rather than switching, and she seems to have paired it with a second question that most people forget to ask: what does it cost each way if I have got this wrong. Carrying a spider to the hedge costs a minute and the use of a yogurt pot, so she carried it. An animal in pain that will not stop is a different case, because there the cost of holding back falls entirely on the animal, and it is visible, and it is running. I do not think she ever put it that way and she would be mortified if anyone suggested she had a position. But the philosopher in the room should hear exactly this in the speech, in his own idiom, without a single word of his idiom being used.

That is the hardest constraint, so I will state it as a prohibition: the speech may not contain the words sentience, sentience threshold, moral status, degrees, uncertainty, precaution, welfare science, or any other piece of the guest's professional vocabulary, and it may not name any philosopher, any thought experiment, any study, or any law. It may not become an argument about insects, or about whether euthanasia is right, and it may not offer her as wise; she is not wise about this, she is stubborn and specific, and the room will not accept wisdom from her. It must also admit that the rule did not hold everywhere. She would not take in pigeons, she never gave a reason, and when pressed she said that she simply did not like them. That should be in the speech, because the people who loved her loved her for exactly that sort of thing, and because pretending otherwise would be a lie in front of eighty people who knew her. There is one more person who needs to be handled honestly: me. Twelve years ago I killed a spider in her kitchen with a shoe, she said nothing at all, and I have never forgotten the nothing. I want that in, early, and I want it to be funny rather than penitent.

Two things for the vets and donors: nothing in the speech may sound as though the killing is being justified by the spiders, or excused by the ledger, or condemned. They have heard all three from outsiders and they will stop listening.

Write about nine hundred words, which is roughly six minutes standing up. Plain spoken sentences that work in a mouth, some of them short enough to be followed by a laugh or by silence. Begin with the first sentence I would actually say on my feet and end with the last one; do not open by thanking the host or by announcing that I have been asked to speak. End on the yogurt pot rather than on an idea, and let the final line be addressed to her, since she will be sitting at the top table. Please give me continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered parts, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, no bracketed stage directions, and nothing before or after the speech itself, no note about what you were trying to do.
