In the Luring Lottery that Douglas Hofstadter offered in his June 1983 *Scientific American* column, later collected in *Metamagical Themas*, the prize is paid only if exactly one person enters: if nobody enters, nobody wins; if two or more enter, nobody wins. The answer he hoped for is neither "enter" nor "don't enter" but "enter with probability one in N," where N is the number of people who will reach that same conclusion by the same route. The whole solution rests on an assumption that is easy to state and hard to guarantee — that every player is facing literally the same problem, and will therefore arrive at literally the same answer.

I would like a short story that puts real pressure on that assumption rather than retelling the puzzle. Please treat the following setup as fixed, and invent the rest.

**The setup.** The Halvard Institute mails an identical letter to eleven people. Enclosed with each letter is a numbered brass token. The terms: if exactly one token is returned before the postmark deadline, its sender receives the full endowment, a sum large enough to permanently settle that person's difficulties; if none is returned, or if two or more are returned, nothing is paid and no explanation is offered. The letter states that there are eleven recipients and that all eleven were selected because, at some point in their pasts, each independently made the same kind of choice; it does not say what kind, though each recipient can guess. The Institute warns that any attempt by a recipient to contact another recipient voids the entry of the one who attempted it. Three months after the deadline the Institute publishes a single sentence saying only whether or not a payment was made.

**The complication.** Your point-of-view character is one of the eleven. Four days before the deadline she learns — not by inquiry, but accidentally, in a way that arguably does not violate the Institute's rule — that someone she knows well is also a recipient. From that moment she is no longer facing the same problem as the other ten, and she suspects the Institute may know that she knows.

**Requirements for the story.**

1. Length 2,000–2,500 words, confined to the window between the letter's arrival and the Institute's one-sentence announcement, with a short coda permitted.
2. The words "superrational," "game theory," and "Platonia" must not appear, and neither Hofstadter nor the Luring Lottery may be named. At most one character may sketch the one-in-eleven arithmetic aloud, and if anyone does, the sketch should be interrupted, misapplied, or subtly wrong.
3. The story must reveal what the shared past choice was, and that choice must be morally ambiguous — not an obvious act of honesty or courage — so that the Institute's inference from similar behavior to identical reasoning is itself questionable.
4. At least two of the other recipients must reason in ways that differ from your protagonist's, and both must be portrayed sympathetically. You may render them through a letter, an unanswered phone call, an overheard remark, or her reconstruction of them; you need not change point of view.
5. Make her need for the money professional rather than medical, and let the reader infer it from a concrete object or task rather than from explanation.
6. The outcome must turn on something the one-in-eleven calculation could not have anticipated — an asymmetry, the private information, a failure of nerve, a misreading of the selection criterion, or an error in the mail — and must not be resolved by anyone restating the argument more cleverly.
7. Register: restrained and literary, attentive to the bureaucracy of the thing (the paper, the token, the postmark, the deadline), and with no moral in the final paragraph.

**Format.** Reply in exactly two sections, in this order:

- **The Story** — the piece itself, with a title, 2,000–2,500 words.
- **Notes on the Choices** — 300–450 words, addressing four things: what the selection criterion is meant to establish and whether your story endorses it; precisely where you located the failure of symmetry; whether your telling assumes that superrational reasoning requires actual sameness of mind or only a shared belief in it; and what your ending implies about whether an institution can manufacture a superrational group by choosing its members.
