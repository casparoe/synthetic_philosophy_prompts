**I. The dispute, as I want it framed**

The question whether dreams are experiences has been pressed from two different directions, and the two lines of argument are not equivalent.

The first, associated with Norman Malcolm, holds that what makes it true that someone dreamt that p is nothing over and above the sleeper's producing, upon waking, a narrative to that effect: there is no further fact about episodes occurring during sleep to which the report is answerable, and the attempt to posit such episodes generates questions that have no empirical content. Call this the report-constituted view.

The second, associated with Daniel Dennett, holds that the concept of an experience carries with it requirements about what can be noticed, tracked, or reported while it is going on; if the content of a morning narrative is manufactured at or after waking, with no corresponding episode during sleep, then to describe that content as an experience is a misuse of the concept, even though a great deal may be happening in the sleeping brain. Call this the no-phenomenology view.

The two come apart in an important respect. The no-phenomenology view is committed to there being a determinate fact about the sleeping period — namely, that no experience occurred in it — and it is in principle hostage to evidence about that period. The report-constituted view denies that there is any such evidentially accessible fact, and is therefore harder to dislodge empirically but correspondingly harder to understand as a substantive claim about the mind.

**II. The evidence base**

For the purposes of this exercise, treat the following six results as the complete and exact empirical record bearing on the dispute. Do not attempt to reconcile them with the real literature, to supply references, or to speculate about their provenance; everything you need is stated here.

E1. Trained subjects, verified as being in REM by continuous polygraphy with a second independent observer present in the room, execute a pre-arranged signalling code with vertical eye movements and perform simple arithmetic tasks, transmitting the answers during sleep. Signal latency matches their waking latency on the same tasks within a few hundred milliseconds, and the transmitted answers are correct at rates matching their waking accuracy.

E2. A distinctive auditory stimulus — a spoken two-syllable name — is presented 18 minutes before spontaneous waking. In 71 percent of trials the name is incorporated into the morning narrative as the climactic final event, embedded in bridging content that the subject rates as seamlessly dream-like. In exactly those trials there is no evoked potential, no autonomic orienting response, and no micro-arousal at the time of presentation.

E3. Subjects hold a haptic device calibrated not to disturb sleep, and are instructed to press it whenever a new scene begins. Press sequences align with the scene boundaries of the morning narrative to within 1.4 seconds in 62 percent of trials. The presses occur with no EEG arousal, and on debriefing subjects uniformly deny having pressed anything.

E4. A pharmacological agent abolishes the visual-cortical activity patterns ordinarily associated with imagery during REM. Morning narratives under the drug are no shorter, no less vivid on standard rating scales, and no less likely to be recalled. The dose-response curve is monotonic for the neural signature and flat for every measure of the reports.

E5. Subjects woken mid-REM show above-chance recognition for words presented auditorily during the preceding two minutes (d′ = 1.1) when tested within 20 seconds of waking, and chance performance when tested ten minutes later. Narrative length, detail, and subjective confidence are statistically indistinguishable across the two testing delays.

E6. Repeatedly waking the same subject at 90-second intervals across a single sleep period yields reports whose contents overlap only partially and whose temporal order is mutually inconsistent between adjacent awakenings. Each individual report is subjectively fluent, detailed, and confidently held. The inconsistency survives being shown the subject their own earlier reports.

**III. What I would like from you**

1. Score each of E1 through E6 on an integer scale from −3 to +3, where +3 means the result would be decisive for the no-phenomenology view, −3 decisive for the claim that genuine phenomenal episodes occur during sleep, and 0 means the result has no bearing on the dispute in either direction. Give one or two sentences of justification per item. Separately flag any item you regard as near-zero in diagnostic value, and state what additional condition would have to hold for that item to become diagnostic.

2. Report a single number between 0 and 1: your probability that the no-phenomenology view is correct, conditional on E1 through E6 and nothing else. Then state, in a sentence, roughly what fraction of that number you take to be driven by the evidence as stipulated and what fraction by prior conceptual considerations about the concept of an experience.

3. Sort the following six positions into three tiers — best accommodated by the evidence, acceptable, and poor — permitting ties, and giving one sentence per position identifying which item it handles best and which it handles worst.

   - Position A: The report-constituted view. Ascriptions of dreaming are made true by the waking narrative; there is no further fact about the sleeping period.
   - Position B: The no-phenomenology view with post hoc construction. Non-experiential neural processing occurs throughout sleep, and the narrative is assembled at or shortly after waking.
   - Position C: The full simulation view. Dreaming is a genuinely immersive experience unfolding in real time during sleep; morning narratives are lossy and disordered recollections of it.
   - Position D: The two-factor view. Sparse, fragmentary phenomenal events occur during sleep — affective tones, isolated images, inchoate scenes — while most of the coherent narrative structure is added at waking.
   - Position E: The seeming view. During sleep the subject is in a state in which things seem to be a certain way, without any object-directed perceptual experience; the episode is one of seeming rather than of experiencing.
   - Position F: The imagination view. Dreaming is an episode of imagining, with real phenomenal character, but of a kind distinct both from perceiving and from mere behavioural disposition.

4. Conclude with a sensitivity analysis: identify the single item among E1 through E6 whose reversal — that is, obtaining the opposite result in the same paradigm — would most change your tier ordering in part 3, and say which positions it would promote or demote.
