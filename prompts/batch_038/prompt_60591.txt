I am working through Book I, Chapter II, §5 of Mill's *A System of Logic* ("Of Connotative and Non-Connotative Names"), where he argues that proper names are non-connotative. The passage that has me stuck runs as follows:

"It may be said, indeed, that we must have had some reason for giving them those names, rather than any others; and this is true; but the name, once given, is independent of the reason. A man may have been named John, because that was the name of his father; a town may have been named Dartmouth, because it is situated at the mouth of the Dart. But it is no part of the signification of the word John, that the father of the person so called bore the same name; nor even of the word Dartmouth, to be situated at the mouth of the Dart. If sand should choke up the mouth of the river, or an earthquake change its course, and remove it to a distance from the town, the name of the town would not necessarily be changed. That fact, therefore, can form no part of the signification of the word; for otherwise, when the fact confessedly ceased to be true, no one would any longer think of applying the name. Proper names are attached to the objects themselves, and are not dependent on the continuance of any attribute of the object."

It is tempting to read this as a Victorian anticipation of the doctrine of rigid designation, but I should like to understand how far the analogy holds. Three questions:

1. Mill's argument concerns what happens when the actual situation *changes*: the river silts up or alters course, yet the town keeps its name. The rigidity thesis, as I understand it, concerns *counterfactual* circumstances — a rigid designator picks out the same object in every possible world in which that object exists, whatever has become of its properties. Does Mill's remark about the shifting river mouth commit him to anything so strong, or only to the weaker claim that no descriptive condition governs the application of a proper name? Could Mill consistently maintain that the name survives the river's change of course whilst denying that 'Dartmouth' picks out that very town in worlds where it never stood at the mouth of the Dart?

2. Mill concedes that there is typically a reason for conferring a name, yet insists that the name is, once conferred, independent of that reason. Does this amount to the familiar distinction between fixing the reference and giving the meaning, or does it leave the reason with no semantic work to do whatsoever — so that Mill is closer to a bare-tag picture than to any form of reference-fixing descriptivism? And if the reason does no semantic work, what is left of the notion of 'signification' that he is at pains to invoke in the passage?

3. Finally, on Mill's telling, 'Dartmouth is situated at the mouth of the Dart' is a real, non-verbal truth about a named object whose falsity would leave the name intact. Is there anything in the framework sketched here that would make room for a truth which is necessary yet known a posteriori, given that Mill seems to be locating necessity in the signification of words rather than in the world?
