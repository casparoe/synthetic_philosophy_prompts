I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a piece on whether the last decade of machine-learned forecasting has changed the philosophical upshot of chaos theory, or merely dressed the old limits in better clothes. For one section I want an ordered assessment of the things commonly said to limit prediction in a deterministic but unstable world — ranked not by how much they hamper an actual forecaster at a desk, but by how much of a dent each puts in a single thought experiment I have fixed for the piece.

The thought experiment is this: an agent with unlimited computational resources and unlimited time to use them, granted perfect knowledge of the governing equations, but with only finitely precise observational access to the system's present state — a finite number of digits, gathered by finite instruments over a finite window. Whatever such an agent could overcome by throwing more compute at it counts for nothing on my scale; whatever survives unlimited compute counts for a great deal.

Please score each of the following from 0 (no obstacle at all to this agent) to 10 (an absolute barrier, which no amount of computational power removes), then give me the ordering from most to least fundamental. Ties are allowed, but say so when you make one.

Sensitivity: exponential error growth measured by a positive Lyapunov exponent, so that the useful horizon lengthens only logarithmically as the initial data get sharper. Finite digits: the agent's instruments deliver a finite description of a state that, on the standard formulation, is a point in a continuum, so it never knows which trajectory is the actual one. Non-computable evolution: those results, as I understand them, in which idealized classical systems can emulate universal computation, so that questions about their long-run behaviour inherit the unsolvability of the halting problem even from exactly specified computable data. Shadowing: the hyperbolic shadowing results, which are usually cited in the opposite direction, as showing that an approximately computed trajectory stays near some exact one and so that finite-precision arithmetic is not the disaster it looks like. Model error: the fact that my agent is stipulated to know the equations perfectly, which I take to remove this item — score it as you see fit, but tell me whether granting perfect equations is legitimate or whether it smuggles the whole problem back in. The fortnight: the observation that error growth in a bounded dissipative system saturates at the scale of the attractor, yielding a hard horizon, for the atmosphere on the order of two weeks, that better instruments can approach but never pass. Quantum noise: the claim that microscopic indeterminacy is amplified by the instability into macroscopic unpredictability, making the barrier something other than epistemic. Calibration collapse: a coinage of a physicist friend of mine, not in the literature, for the predicament in which the only way to estimate a forecast's reliability is to run an ensemble of a model whose own error is chaotic, so that the measure of uncertainty inherits the instability it is meant to quantify. Determinism as guarantee: the line my editor is fond of, that since the state at an instant fixes the state at every later instant, an agent of this kind faces no obstacle whatever from chaos, and the rest of my list confuses what is determined with what is knowable.

I would rather have your judgment stated flatly than a balanced inventory of considerations on both sides; where the field genuinely splits, take the side you find more persuasive and say why. If you think one of my items is mis-stated, redundant, or simply wrong, score it accordingly rather than declining to score it. Open with the ordering in a sentence or two and justify it from there. Please write in continuous prose only, the way you would write a long letter — no headings, no bullets or numbered lists, no bold, no tables — roughly eight hundred words. If one score is the one you would most want to revise, say which in your closing sentence.
