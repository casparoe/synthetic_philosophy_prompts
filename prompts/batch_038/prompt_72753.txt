my brother-in-law is a software guy who read Gödel, Escher, Bach over the holidays and came out the other side of it with an argument I can't quite pin down. he's not doing the usual "computers can't be creative" thing, he's doing something more specific about the typogenetics section -- the bit where Hofstadter invents little fake enzymes that operate on strings of letters, gives you a made-up genetic code where pairs of letters stand for operations, and then slides the whole thing over onto real DNA and the actual genetic code.

his argument, as near as I can reconstruct it over dinner:

1. DNA is a code in the literal sense -- an alphabet, codons, a translation table. not "like" a code.
2. a cell reads that code and builds the machinery (proteins, ribosomes) that in turn reads and copies the code. so the system carries, inside itself, a description of its own reading apparatus. that's not a metaphor, that's just what happens in every cell.
3. Gödel's trick was exactly that shape: arithmetize the language so a system can represent statements about its own proofs, and then the diagonal sentence falls out automatically.
4. therefore an organism is a Gödelian system -- there are truths about it its own machinery can't derive -- and a system with underivable truths about itself can't be a machine. minds are what organisms grow, so minds aren't machines either. he said the ribosome is playing the role of the Gödel numbering function and looked extremely pleased with himself.

here's where I get stuck. steps 3 and 4 seem to be pointing in opposite directions. Gödel's theorem is a theorem about formal systems -- it bites only if the thing in question IS one, or at least if it has enough arithmetic in it and some way of expressing "this is a proof of that." if an organism satisfies the hypotheses, hasn't he just shown the organism is a formal system rather than showing it isn't one? when I said this he waved and said the real point is that the organism can see its own undecidable sentence from the outside, which sounded like the old Lucas/Penrose move, but he wouldn't say how a cell "sees" anything from outside itself.

so can you audit the analogy for me? I want the correspondences laid out explicitly as a table -- what in typogenetics and in real molecular biology plays the role of the symbols, the well-formed formulas, the rules of inference, the arithmetization step, the diagonalization -- and then a row-by-row verdict on which of those actually hold and which are sleight of hand. I especially want you to dig into that closed loop in step 2, because it feels like the one genuinely interesting thing in his argument: replication really does require the system to carry a description of the replicator. is that self-reference in the sense Gödel needs, or is it a seperate kind of loop entirely? something about causation versus derivability, maybe. also worth saying which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he wants and which ones are just trivia that a molecular biologist would enjoy but that leave his argument untouched.

then tell me plainly whether the anti-mechanist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire weight of it the whole time. and if there's a better analogy for what's really going on -- self-replication without self-reference, or self-description without a semantics, or whatever -- give me that too, and say what work it can and can't do.

around 900 words, American English please. no bibliography, I've never read the primary literature on any of this and I don't intend to -- I just want to be able to hold my ground at the next dinner without saying anything embarassing.
