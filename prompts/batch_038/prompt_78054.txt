So my reading group has been at it for three weeks straight on the adaptationism fights — the spandrels paper, the whole argument about whether you can test a hypthesis about the Pleistocene at all, the ethnography-of-hunter-gatherers-as-time-machine business — and every session collapses into someone saying "it's all just-so stories" and someone else saying "no it isn't," and nobody ever gets specific about what a bad adaptationist argument actually does versus what a good one does. I want to make a short piece for our department's photocopied undergrad zine that does that work properly, and I think the only way to do it without preaching is from the inside, deadpan, so the reader has to make the diagnosis themselves.

Here's what I want: a mock op-ed, roughly 650 words, in the voice it would have in a Sunday broadsheet's comment section — headline, one-line standfirst, then straight paragraphs. The author is an invented evolutionary psychologist (make him up, don't base him on anyone real and don't name any actual researchers) who has a signature theory, one mechanism he reaches for on every single occasion, something in the neighbourhood of "costly display of surplus provisioning capacity." He's writing to answer the charge that his work is just-so stories. He is not a clown: he's a serious, faintly wounded academic who finds the charge philistine, and the humour has to come entirely from the fact that each of his rebuttals is itself a textbook instance of what he's denying. Put him through at least three accusations: that his hypotheses can't come out false (he should demonstrate this himself, for instance by cheerfully explaining how a result that contradicted him would be read as a more sophisticated version of the same adaptation), that he only ever answers the function question and waves off mechanism, development and phylogeny, and that his "ancestral environment" and his "contemporary foragers" keep doing smuggling work for one another.

The part I care about most: don't straw-man him. One of the three defences has to be genuinely good, the kind of convergent-evidence case a real adaptationist is actually entitled to make — a cross-cultural regularity, something from the comparative method, a heritability estimate, and an optimality model that made a quantitative prediction before the data came in. A specialist reading it should be able to point at exactly which defence he's earned and tell that the writer knows the difference between that and the other two. Also slip in one brief quotation from his own earlier paper, offered approvingly as proof of his rigour, where the sentence he quotes contains, if you read it carefully, the admission that sinks him — and he must not notice. Keep it deadpan to the end: no winking, no authorial nudge, no moral, and let the last line be something he says with total sincerity. Don't let him use words like "unfalsifiable," "panadaptationism" or "spandrel" as labels in his own mouth; the satire has to be carried by the content, not by naming the sin.

And please give me the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter or an email — no subheads, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just the headline, the standfirst, and then paragraphs.
