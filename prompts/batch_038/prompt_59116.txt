You are a political philosopher who works on democratic theory and social choice. Please think carefully before answering, and treat the invented institution below as a real object of analysis rather than as an occasion for generalities about populism.

I am a novelist. My new book is set in a city that has abolished referendums and replaced them with something I made up and called the Citizens' Verdict: five hundred residents drawn by lot, three months of paid deliberation over adversarial expert testimony, and then one binding yes/no vote, whose outcome the city charter names the will of the people. I described the device to two friends — a political theorist and an investigative journalist — and within ten minutes they were arguing about that phrase rather than about my book.

Their exchange, as nearly as I can reconstruct it:

(1) The journalist said the phrase is a fraud, since there is no such thing as a collective will: there are only individual preferences, and any procedure that turns them into a single verdict must discard information and select a rule, so what emerges is the rule's output wearing the people's name. Impossibility results of the Arrow type, she said, show that no way of combining individual rankings satisfies all the conditions we would want from a fair procedure.

(2) The theorist said she had misdescribed the device: a sortition panel does not aggregate pre-existing private preferences at all, it forms a judgment under conditions of rough equality and shared information. The charter's phrase points at a deliberated verdict, not a tally — the old distinction between a general will and the mere sum of everyone's private wishes.

(3) The journalist said that distinction is precisely the populist maneuver: define the will of the people as whatever survives proper deliberation, then claim your favored procedure delivers it, and every actual majority that disagrees turns out not to be the people after all. The phrase becomes unfalsifiable, and unfalsifiable phrases are how minorities get told they do not exist.

(4) The theorist said unfalsifiability does not follow: the panel's verdict can be tested against a counterfactual — would citizens, given the same time and the same evidence, endorse it? Where the answer is yes, the phrase earns its keep.

(5) The journalist said that counterfactual can never be run, and a check nobody can perform is a decoration. She added that in my novel the mayor's office wrote the question the panel was asked, so even a scrupulous panel sits downstream of an elite decision.

(6) The theorist agreed that framing matters but said the objection proves too much: every institution is framed by somebody, and on that standard no political body has ever said anything, so there is nothing left for the phrase to be wrong about.

Please adjudicate. First, briefly lay out the two or three distinct claims the charter's phrase could be making, so that the moves can be scored against something. Then go move by move, rating each of the six from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying each rating in a sentence or two, including any move where the correct verdict is that it changes the subject. Then say who is more right overall about the phrase, and name the most important thing neither of them raised. Do not cite literature or suggest readings; where you invoke a result or a thinker, give the content in your own words.
