I sit on the membership committe of a community land trust in a rural parish. Forty households: the land is owned in common, each family has a 99-year lease on their plot, and the resale price is capped by a formula so nobody ever makes a windfall. Six plots come free over the next two years, mostly aging members. Our approved waiting list is 187 people long and the average wait is eleven years.

Right now the committee interviews applicants and votes, two thirds needed. In practice we've always favoured people the community already knows — locals, relatives of members, people who've been turning up to workdays at the farm for years. Nobody ever wrote that down, it just happened.

There's a formal proposal for our AGM in six weeks to reserve four of the six plots for applicants with a "tie to the parish": born here, schooled here, or five-plus years of paid or volunteer work on the commons. Their case has three legs — outside applicants can pick up and move at will while local young families are stuck in their parents' spare rooms; the whole region is being priced out and the trust was set up to keep *this* community alive, not to relieve housing need in the abstract; and a lottery would hand a home to someone who then never touches a shared obligation.

The other side says a preference keyed to birthplace and local ties is hereditary privilege in a nicer hat, that we take a property-tax abatement which is public money and that we control the only genuinely affordable housing in the parish, and — this is what got me — when somebody actually scored the "tie to the parish" test against the real list, it qualified 9 of the 187, and 6 of those 9 are adult children of current members, spread across four of the six founding households. On top of that, two of the plots coming free belong to households who put in the labour when the trust was broke and built the barns by hand, and their families are saying that's something their kids are owed. Our charter says the trust exists "for the benefit of the inhabitants of the parish and those who come after them," which both camps are currently throwing at each other.

And the analogy everyone keeps reaching for cuts both ways, which is why I can't settle it: one of the older members says if a country gets to decide who it lets in, a forty-household trust certainly does, and the younger ones shout back that if a country *doesn't* get to do that, neither do we. Neither side can say why the reasoning travels in their direction and not the other.

Also not innocent here — I moved in nine years ago largely because a member vouched for me, so I'm a product of the exact mechanism under dispute.
