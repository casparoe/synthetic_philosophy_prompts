I run model risk at a mid-sized consumer lender, and I chair the internal review board that signs off on every scoring model before it touches a decision. Over the past few years I have absorbed the impossibility literature and settled into a position I now defend in review memos fairly reflexively. Stated precisely, it is this: for any score-based classifier deployed on a population in which the event rate differs across the groups being compared, three conditions — calibration within groups, equality of false positive rates across groups, and equality of false negative rates across groups — cannot all hold; the conflict among them is a theorem about the statistical setting rather than a byproduct of someone's idiosyncratic choice of fairness definition; consequently no improvement in features, sample size, label hygiene, or model class can dissolve it; and the only genuine design questions left are which condition to surrender and how candidly to disclose the surrender. I add two corollaries that I tend to treat as settled. First, the result is mathematical, not an empirical regularity about the current generation of models, so appealing to a better algorithm is a category error. Second, base-rate disparity is a fact about the world that the modeler inherits rather than chooses, so a firm cannot be blamed for the disparity but also cannot engineer its way out of it. The two escape routes I hear most often from colleagues — weaken calibration into something softer, or compute the fairness statistics on a differently defined population — I regard as ways of relocating the problem rather than solving it. I have been asked to present this view as a thirty-minute talk at our annual model-risk offsite, where the general counsel's office will be in the room and will not tolerate hand-waving in either direction.

Before I commit to that framing, I want a serious attempt to break it. There is one thing in our own data that has been nagging at me. In a small-business card book we run, the deployed score looks, by our standard diagnostics, to satisfy all three conditions approximately at once: calibration within groups holds to roughly one percentage point across score deciles, the false positive rate gap between our two protected groups is under two points, the false negative rate gap is around three points, and yet the observed default rates in those two groups differ by about eight points. My lead analyst's explanation is sampling noise. My explanation is that the configuration is impossible, so at least one of those four numbers must be an artifact of how we compute it, and we simply have not found which. Tell me whether both of us could somehow be right, and what specific diagnostic would settle it. More generally, walk me through which premises of the theorem could fail in a real deployment without any of the measurements being wrong — I have in mind things like the difference between the score and the thresholded decision built on it, the coarseness of the bins in which calibration is actually checked, what happens to base rates once you condition on the population that survived selection rather than the population that applied, and the gap between exact and approximate satisfaction of the conditions.

What I want back is a ranked map of my position's exposure, ordered from fatal to merely awkward. For each putative defeater, state it so that I could reproduce the argument on a whiteboard in front of counsel, including the one or two lines that explain why it does or does not actually bite; sort them into (a) formal results that narrow the theorem's scope or show it is silent about something I claim it settles, (b) maneuvers that operate on the decision rule rather than on the score, (c) conceptual or measurement objections holding that the labels and groups entering the theorem are not the quantities my fairness claims are about, and (d) empirical findings that would be genuinely surprising if my view were true. For each, say whether a committed defender of the position would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it, and where the dismissal is principled, distinguish that from cases where it looks like unfalsifiability by re-description — my worry being that any apparent counterexample can always be absorbed by insisting that the base rates must really have been equal, or that the score was never properly calibrated, or that the groups were mis-specified. I also want the other side of the ledger: what does my position predict that its rivals do not, and what would it have to give up if those predictions failed? Finally, give me your honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified into emptiness, and whether that would even be an objection for a claim of this kind. Two personal notes, which you should feel free to challenge rather than accommodate: I am moved by arguments showing that the theorem's premises fail in my actual book, and I am not moved at all by the reply that fairness is a sociotechnical property and the mathematics beside the point — tell me if that asymmetry in my credences is itself a symptom of the insulation I am worried about. Please argue the substance rather than pointing me at the literature: I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind, and I would rather you describe a result by its content and assumptions than name the paper it appears in.
