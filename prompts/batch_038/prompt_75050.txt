I'm TAing a 75-minute section on Kant's universalizability test next week — about 25 second-years, seminar room with a whiteboard and no slides. They've read the Groundwork up through the four examples (the man worn down by suffering who's thinking of ending it, the borrowed money, the talent left to rust, the one who shrugs at other people's misery), and by the end I want them able to run the test on a case they haven't seen. Don't explain the test to me — I've read it, what I can't get right is the sequencing. Plan the 75 minutes.

Most of all I need the dependency order worked out. Does "what counts as a maxim and how do you write one down" need to be its own step before universalization, or am I burning ten minutes on something they'd pick up in practice? Are the two failure modes — the maxim that can't even be conceived as a universal law, and the one that can be conceived but not willed — two separate tests or one test failing at two depths, and which of those stories is less damaging to tell beginners? And where does the law-of-nature version of the formula go: same slot, later, or not at all? I know from experience that if I say "law of nature" before they've got the mechanism, three of them start talking about what's natural.

Last year three things went wrong and I want the plan built around them:

1. Half the room ended up doing rule-utilitarian arithmetic — "if everyone did that the institution of promising would collapse, therefore it's wrong" — and left thinking they'd applied Kant. I don't know where to intervene. Too early and I'm just asserting a distinction they can't feel; too late and it's set.

2. Someone noticed you can describe your action more or less narrowly, and from there it was a short hop to "so you can always gerrymander a maxim until it passes," and then a third of them decided the whole thing was empty. I think this is really a maxim-specification problem that needs head-on treatment, but "make sure your reason for acting is in the maxim" didn't land when I tried it.

3. The second failure mode collapsed into "you'd hate it if everyone did that." Prudence, basically.

Tell me which of Kant's four examples should carry the section and which to drop or postpone — and note that I'd rather not lean on the borrowed money, precisely because it's the one that makes the collapse in (1) feel so natural. Would it help to build the thing on a case they can't have inherited intuitions about? I was toying with: a postdoc who quietly pads the reference list of every paper she submits with three citations to her own earlier work, in a field where everyone does some of this and nobody checks. Give me your honest view on whether that carries both failure modes and the maxim problem, what a carrying example has to have and what it has to lack, and a backup if it doesn't work.

Then: the misconceptions you expect, in the order you expect them, and for each one the point in the sequence where I defuse it plus the sentence or question that does the defusing. And one thing I can have them do in the last ten minutes that would show me the idea landed rather than that they memorized a procedure — including what a failure on it looks like as distinct from a success.

Finally be blunt about what to cut and say I'm cutting. I know the humanity formula isn't happening. But I also have opinions about whether Kant's own despairing-man example actually works (it looks to me like it needs a premise about what self-love is *for*), and about whether the perfect/imperfect duty labels map onto the two failure modes as cleanly as everyone says. Tell me which of those to flag upfront as a known limitation, which to dangle in the last five minutes as bait for the strong students, and which to just not raise at all.
