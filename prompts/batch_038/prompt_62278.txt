I'm a materials chemist doing a part-time philosophy MA, and our reading group hit Mill's *System of Logic* (1843, Book III ch. 6, "Of the Composition of Causes") last week. Honestly everyone else seemed fine with it and I came away unable to say what the chapter is actually claiming. Here are the bits I keep going back over, with my skips marked:

> In this important class of cases of causation, one cause never, properly speaking, defeats or frustrates another; both have their full effect. […] This law of nature is called, in mechanical philosophy, the principle of the Composition of Forces: and in imitation of that well-chosen expression, I shall give the name of the Composition of Causes to the principle which is exemplified in all cases in which the joint effect of several causes is identical with the sum of their separate effects.

> The chemical combination of two substances produces, as is well known, a third substance with properties entirely different from those of either of the two substances separately, or of both of them taken together. Not a trace of the properties of hydrogen or of oxygen is observable in those of their compound, water. […] This explains why mechanics is a deductive or demonstrative science, and chemistry not. […] we are not, at least in the present state of our knowledge, able to foresee what result will follow from any new combination, until we have tried it by specific experiment.

> […] the phenomena of life, which result from the juxtaposition of those parts in a certain manner, bear no analogy to any of the effects which would be produced by the action of the component substances considered as mere physical agents. To whatever degree we might imagine our knowledge of the properties of the several ingredients of a living body to be extended and perfected, it is certain that no mere summing up of the separate actions of those elements will ever amount to the action of the living body itself. The tongue, for instance, is, like all other parts of the animal frame, composed of gelatine, fibrin, and other products of the chemistry of digestion, but from no knowledge of the properties of those substances could we ever predict that it could taste, unless gelatine or fibrin could themselves taste; for no elementary fact can be in the conclusion, which was not first in the premisses.

> […] the expansive force of the gases generated by the ignition of gunpowder tends to project a bullet towards the sky, while its gravity tends to make it fall to the ground.

Can you walk me through what he's doing? Five things in particular:

1. The Composition of Causes. He defines it as holding when the joint effect "is identical with the sum of their separate effects." Is that a law of nature he thinks he's established about mechanics, or is it a stipulative label for a class of cases? Relatedly: he says that in mechanics one cause "never, properly speaking, defeats or frustrates another," and then a bit later in the same chapter gives me a bullet that both tends to go skyward and tends to fall. What is "properly speaking" buying him? It looks like the individual tendencies survive but the resulting motion isn't a sum of the separate effects — which sounds like precisely what he said doesn't happen in mechanics.

2. The tongue sentence. Two separate problems. (a) What's the logical form of the "unless gelatine or fibrin could themselves taste" clause — is he saying the only route to predicting tasting is if tasting is already in the ingredients? (b) The last clause, "no elementary fact can be in the conclusion, which was not first in the premisses," reads like a triviality about deductive validity, but he's deploying it as support for a claim about how nature behaves. Is that a straight equivocation, or is there a non-trivial version of the move available to him? I've been collecting arguments of this shape and calling them ledger arguments — the target property never appears on the left-hand side, so it can't come out on the right — and I want to know whether Mill's is the strong version (reduction is metaphysically impossible here) or the weak one (we can't do the derivation).

3. There's an asymmetry nobody in the group picked up on. For chemistry he hedges: "at least in the present state of our knowledge." For living bodies there's no hedge whatsoever, just "to whatever degree we might imagine our knowledge […] extended and perfected, it is certain." Is that a deliberate claim that life differs in kind from chemistry, or is he being careless about where the limit sits? What turns on it either way?

4. His own ingredients may undercut the example. Gelatine and fibrin are introduced as "products of the chemistry of digestion" — so they aren't samples of the elements considered "as mere physical agents," they're already outputs of an organized body. Does that weaken the argument, or am I overreading a throwaway phrase?

5. This is the one I actually care about. I work on alloys. We routinely compute a melting point or an elastic constant for a composition nobody has ever made, from the elements, using electronic-structure calculations, and we're right often enough. On Mill's dichotomy that shouldn't be possible: either the joint effect is the sum of the separate effects (it plainly isn't — nobody thinks you get the alloy by adding up the pure metals), or the composition principle has broken down and you need a specific experiment (but we didn't do one). I think he's missing a third box: joint behaviour nothing like the sum of the parts, but still fixed by, and computable from, laws about the parts — note the calculation isn't a "mere summing up," so maybe he'd say I've conceded his point. Is my third box a real objection or does he have a reply, e.g. that the interatomic potentials the code runs on were themselves fitted to experiments on compounds? And if he does have that reply, does it also cover the tongue — i.e. does his life case survive the objection even if his chemistry case doesn't? (He has a term for the non-composing kind of case, something like heteropathic, which I think shows up later in the chapter; I can't check.)

Be blunt about which of my readings are just wrong. No references or reading lists — I can't look anything up right now anyway — I mainly want to know whether the chapter hangs together and what Mill ends up committed to.
