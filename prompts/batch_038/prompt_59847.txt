You are, for this exercise, a philosopher of mind and language whose specialty is concepts and categorization — natural kinds, family resemblance, the difference between what a thing is and what we take it for — and who happens to know a fair amount about typography, which is why I am coming to you rather than to a type designer. I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I have spent the last two months reporting a piece on machine-generated typefaces and on why handwriting recognition still trips over lowercase letters. Along the way I interviewed calligraphers, font engineers, and two people who build optical character recognition systems, and I came away holding a set of my own verdicts about when a mark is an 'a' that I cannot reduce to anything I would be willing to publish as an explanation. I would like you to work through them carefully: begin with a short diagnostic pass in which you note what sorts of principle my verdicts already rule out, before you propose anything, and only then tell me what principle, if any, actually captures the pattern.

Here they are. A single-story 'a' — the round bowl with a vertical stem on its right, the form you find in geometric sans-serifs and in most children's handwriting — and a double-story 'a', the one with the curved arc above and the little curling tail, are both beyond dispute 'a's, though they share essentially no strokes and a reader fluent in only one would still be a fluent reader. Move the bowl of a single-story 'a' to the left side of its stem, changing nothing else, and it stops being an 'a': it reads as a mirror image, a mark from some other alphabet. Yet squash a double-story 'a' to half its width, tilt it twelve degrees, and drag it through a dry brush until the strokes break up, and it is still an 'a', and a handsome one — the first change is small and fatal, the second enormous and harmless. Pinch the enclosed white inside the bowl down to a hairline slit, as very heavy weights do, and it remains an 'a'; fill that space in completely and it is a blob rather than a letter, though there is obviously a continuum between those states and I do not know what to say about the middle of it. A stencil 'a' with three clean gaps — bowl detached from the stem at top and bottom, stem interrupted midway — reads fine, and so does a handwritten 'a' whose bowl never quite closes; but leave the bowl open at the bottom as well and what remains looks like a fragment of an 'n'. One experimental display face I examined reduces every letter to a near-circle bearing a single distinguishing nick, and set into a sentence I can read that nicked circle as an 'a', slowly but surely; lift the identical glyph out and set it beside a conventional 'a' and it becomes a decoration, or an 'o'. My sister's handwriting contains a glyph that in isolation I would call a squashed 'e', and yet, knowing the rest of her alphabet and the word it sits in, I call it an 'a' without hesitation. But in one of the fonts I reviewed, the designer's own metadata labels as 'a' a glyph that everyone I showed it to read as 'e', in a face that already contains an unmistakable 'e' of its own, and there my verdict is that the label is simply wrong — so knowing what the maker intended rescues my sister's glyph and cannot rescue this one. Finally, in the wedgy, aggressive display face that was the original subject of my piece, the 'a' is at once and independently unmistakably an 'a' and unmistakably of that face; style and letter-identity seem to slide past one another on separate axes, and I want that fact preserved by your account rather than explained away.

What I want back is two or three candidate principles, each stated precisely enough that a reasonable person could apply it to a mark they had never seen before, with every clause traced back to the case that forced you to include it. Then run your leading candidate against cases I have not supplied: at minimum, the capital 'A' of a Roman inscription, an uppercase 'A' in a face whose lowercase 'a' is single-story, the letter as it is written in braille, and an 'a' rotated a hundred and eighty degrees. Tell me whether its verdicts there match what I suspect I believe, which is that the rotated mark is not an 'a' at all and that the inscriptional capital is not straightforwardly the same kind of thing as either lowercase form. Above all I need your ruling on the case I have been circling for a week: a letterform generated by one of the machine-learning tools in my piece, which has a bowl on the right, a closed counter, and a stem planted on the baseline, but whose stem curves inward at the top and enters the bowl from the inside, in a way no human face does. Shown on its own, roughly half the designers I interviewed called it an 'a' and half called it a failed 'd' or an abstract ornament. Say what your principle makes of it, and say whether that split is evidence that the concept is vague, that it is relational in some way I have misdescribed, or that one side is simply mistaken. Close by naming which of my verdicts each candidate principle cannot accommodate, and which single verdict I should be most willing to surrender — and if you think the way I have framed the question is itself confused, say so first rather than humouring it. I am not after references or a reading list, which I could not check on deadline and do not want; I am after the reasoning.
