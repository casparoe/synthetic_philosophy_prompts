Last night a friend and I argued ourselves into a stalemate over Popper's Poverty of Historicism, and what I want is an assessment of my reconstruction of the argument rather than a summary of it. My position was that his case against laws of history has to rest on the claim that history is a single, unrepeatable process: nothing observed in the past can license anything about a process that by definition will never run a second time. But if that is the load-bearing premise, then Popper has simply imported the old separation between the natural and the historical sciences, which sits very badly with his insistence that social inquiry is methodologically continuous with physics — and my friend, who said I had picked the wrong premise, could not tell me which one was right. So: what is actually doing the work, and does the conclusion really amount to the metaphysical claim that no laws govern historical development? What puzzles me most is that Popper seems happy enough with generalizations of the form that a regime which suppresses criticism will tend to lose its capacity to correct its own errors; if a conditional claim like that is law-like talk about social development, what is left of the denial, and what exactly is it a denial of?
