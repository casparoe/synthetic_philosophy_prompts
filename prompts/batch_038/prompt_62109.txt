Some background: I came to philosophy through the old rationalist blogs — Overcoming Bias, early LessWrong — and I've been reading that stuff for something like fifteen years. I'm now a fourth-year PhD student and this term I'm the TA for an upper-level epistemology seminar: twelve students, mostly seniors plus two first-years. They've had one week of epistemic logic, so they know what a normal modal logic is and that K is usually taken to be factive and to distribute over conjunctions, but none of them have met Fitch before. The prof has handed me the take-home that goes with the knowability unit — one single question, 48 hours, open book, no collaboration, 1,800 word limit — and I want it to actually be good rather than a comprehension check.

My problem is that every version of this question I've seen collapses into "state the derivation and then list the responses," which any competent student can fake from a SEP summary and which tells me nothing about whether they got what's weird about the result. The thing I find genuinely interesting — and that I spent way too long arguing about on a Discord server a few years back, when somebody insisted the "there are truths nobody will ever know" intuition is basically untouchable — is that people disagree about what the theorem even *shows*. One camp treats it as a straight refutation: the knowability principle is false, anti-realism loses, move on. Another camp thinks the interesting part isn't that a principle turned out false but that a view supposed to sit between "truth is what we actually know" and "truth is what an ideal knower knows" can't be stated as a logically distinct position at all — it just collapses into one of the endpoints. And then there's the separate worry about whether anything specifically *epistemic* is doing the work, or whether you'd get the same collapse out of any factive operator that distributes over conjunction, in which case concluding "verificationism is false" from it looks like a non sequitur.

So please write one take-home question that forces students to take a position on that, with two or three parts. I do want them to lay out the derivation once with the assumptions on K labeled explicitly, because I want them to be able to point at exactly which assumption is load-bearing — factivity, distribution over conjunction, the KK principle, the classical logic behind the reductio, the reading of the modal operator — but the question must not be answerable by *only* doing that. And it has to make them engage with the restriction everyone reaches for first (only apply the principle to truths nobody currently knows) and say whether that buys anything, given that the proposition the argument is run on is itself one nobody knows; plus at least one of temporalizing the operator, restricting to basic or decidable statements, or going intuitionistic about the modality, and in each case what the move costs. No marks for history — I don't care if they know about the 1945 referee report or Fitch's 1963 paper, and I don't want the question to reward name-dropping.

Deliverables: the question exactly as students will see it; a model answer of about 800–900 words that's fully self-contained — please don't pad it with citations or a bibliography, the students won't have the literature and I want to be able to check the reasoning myself; and a marking scheme out of 25 with five or six must-hit points, the marks each is worth, a one-line note per point on the most common way students screw it up, and a short list of answers that sound impressive but should be marked down (the classic being the student who announces the paradox is dissolved because knowledge isn't closed under conjunction without saying why anyone should believe that or what it would cost). Tell me explicitly where the A/B boundary sits. And if you think my framing is off — like, if the "refutation versus collapse" distinction isn't a live one and I've talked myself into a fake crux — say so and give me a better crux to build the question around instead. I'd rather have a good question than my question.
