I am a third-year PhD student, and our department's reading group on probability and conditionals has a visiting speaker next Thursday who defends Adams's Thesis — the claim that the probability of a conditional A→B equals the conditional probability of B on A — against Lewis's triviality results in both of his Philosophical Review papers on the topic. Her strategy, so far as I can reconstruct it from the abstract and one preprint, is not to abandon the thesis but to deny a background assumption Lewis needs: she restricts the class of probability functions over which the equation is supposed to hold, so that triviality never gets a grip, and she pairs the restriction with a semantics on which a conditional bet can be called off and come out void rather than true or false. I have twelve minutes of moderated Q&A, the room is half graduate students who know Bayes's theorem cold but have never read the conditionals literature, and she has a habit of answering with a page of notation on the board — so I need questions that can be put in two or three sentences and answered in words.

Please approach this as a philosopher of probability and philosophical logic who has worked through both triviality papers line by line and knows the subsequent work on compound conditionals, dynamic coherence for conditional credences, and conditional random quantities. Think the problem through before drafting anything: begin with a short paragraph identifying the three or four genuine pressure points on this particular combination of views — where the restriction strategy is exposed, and what it has to give up in order to stay coherent — and then convert each pressure point into questions. Give me eight to ten questions in total, each followed by one follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give rather than a generic prompt to elaborate.

I would like coverage of at least the following. First, what the restriction costs: whether a thesis that holds only for a curated subclass of probability functions is still a thesis about what conditionals mean or has become a technical device, and how an ordinary speaker is supposed to tell which functions are admissible. Second, the generality problem: Lewis's first theorem is not the only triviality result, and the more general ones proved since show that nearly any equation between the probability of a conditional and a function of the probabilities of its components collapses under quite weak assumptions — so which specific assumption of those stronger results does her restriction defeat, and does she have to concede anything about iterated or embedded conditionals to make it work? Third, nesting and embedding: what her account says about the probability of a conditional whose consequent is itself a conditional, in the style of McGee's counterexample to modus ponens, and whether her semantics can assign any content at all to a conditional under a negation or inside a disjunction. Fourth, learning: what it is to update on a conditional if updating on its antecedent by conditionalization is the wrong model, and whether her dynamic story leaves room for a Dutch book of the kind that motivated the thesis in the first place. Fifth, the third value: if a conditional bet can be called off, what it means to assign the conditional a probability at all, and whether a three-valued setting genuinely evades the theorems or merely fails to state the thesis in a form to which they apply.

Two further constraints. Do not ask her to name papers, cite sources, or supply dates, and do not include any question that could only be answered by someone who has the literature in front of them; everything must turn on the content of her view. And for each question, add a single sentence telling me whether the point I am pressing is one on which people who work on conditionals have substantially converged — for example, that Lewis's theorem is sound given its premises, and that a naive equation of conditional probability with the probability of a material conditional is dead — or one that is genuinely live and contested, and do not let a contested claim ride along inside the question as though it were settled. If you are unsure which way she will answer a given question, say so explicitly and provide two follow-ups, one for each branch.
