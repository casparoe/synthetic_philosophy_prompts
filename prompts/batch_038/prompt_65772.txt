Background first: I spent about thirty-five years in numerical weather prediction, mostly on the data assimilation side, and I've been retired four years now and have fallen headfirst into philosophy and economics, to my wife's considerable amusement. I run a fortnightly podcast out of the spare room — small audience, one guest, fifty minutes, no ads, no producer. Next week's guest is an economist who argues, quite aggressively, that the socialist calculation debate was settled in Lange's favour and that the Austrian side has been coasting on rhetoric ever since, Schumpeter's later verdict being in her view the honest one. Her position isn't crude nationalise-everything: she's a market socialist who wants real prices for consumer goods and labour, and her claim is that capital goods prices could be produced by a planning board running a Walrasian tâtonnement, that modern compute makes each iteration cheap, and that the rules Lange hands to managers — minimise average cost for a given output, produce where marginal cost equals the announced price — can be made incentive-compatible using mechanism design, which she says grew directly out of this argument via Hurwicz. She's articulate, she's funny, and she's very good at getting to the end of a question and answering a slightly easier one than the one asked.

Here's my difficulty, and the reason I'm asking you instead of winging it. I have a hobby horse from the old job and I genuinely can't tell whether it's a real objection or a physicist pattern-matching where there's nothing to match. In assimilation you often have to solve for a state while the thing you're solving for is moving: you iterate against coefficients that are being revised underneath you by incoming observations, and if the drift timescale is shorter than the convergence timescale you never converge, you just chase, and your error stops shrinking no matter how good the solver is. When I read Hayeks objection — that the board would face as many simultaneous equations as the market has participants and prices, and that by the time it has solved them the data have gone and changed — I read it as exactly that: not a claim about solvability in principle but a claim about convergence rate versus drift rate. I like that reading because it concedes the board could in principle hold all the information and still lose the race, which is much harder to wave away than the usual "but you can't possibly know everything". What I can't work out is whether she has a good answer, and I suspect she does: something along the lines that markets never reach equilibrium either, so the only fair criterion is comparative — which system tracks a moving target with less lag and less variance — and that stated that way, a compute-assisted board is at least in the running. So I want questions that test that reply rather than let her say it again more loudly.

What I'd like from you is eight to ten questions in the order I should actually ask them, and for each one, the follow-up keyed to the answer you think she's most likely to give — say briefly what you expect that answer to be so I can recognise it in real time. Between them they should cover: the drift-versus-convergence framing above, and whether it survives her comparative reply; tacit and local knowledge, pinned down to whether she thinks that's just a claim about transmission costs or a stronger claim that no vector of administered prices can carry the relevant content at all; the rule-following problem, i.e. who actually makes managers set marginal cost equal to the announced price, and whether her board has thereby stopped being an auctioneer and become a monitor, which is a different and much worse job; the entrepreneurial-discovery objection that prices are a by-product of alertness rather than the output of a solver — and I want a question that asks her whether that's an argument or just a redescription; the mechanism-design reframing, and what happens to it once you notice that actual markets fail the same informational and incentive tests she's applying (externalities, market power, adverse selection) — is she holding socialism to a standard capitalism never gets held to; the awkward empirical point that the economies which tried board-set capital goods prices ended up borrowing shadow prices from world markets, which looks suspiciously like importing the very signal she wants to replace; and one closing question that forces her to say what would count as evidence against her view. Please build the sequence so her comparative-institutions move gets progressively harder to deploy rather than being knocked down once and forgotten. No generic questions, nothing about what first drew her to the debate, no request that she recap it for listeners, and no asking her for readings.

Two things about how I want this written. Start with your bottom line in the very first sentence and then give the reasoning and the questions, with no preamble and no restating of what I've asked. British English, please.
