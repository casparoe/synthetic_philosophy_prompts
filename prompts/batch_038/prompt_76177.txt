I'm putting together a seminar session on Franklin's moral perfection project — the bit in the Autobiography where he lays out thirteen virtues with a short rule attached to each, works through them one at a time in a thirteen-week course, and keeps a little book with a page per virtue and a column per day, marking a black spot for every fault he catches himself in. He's confident the pages will come out cleaner and cleaner over repeated courses, and he also tells that story about the man who takes his axe to the smith to get it ground bright all over and, after a lot of labor and a lot of speckling, decides he likes a speckled ax best.

What I need from you is a case built to order. Construct a thought experiment in which a sincere, faithful adopter of that method finishes a full thirteen-week course worse in character than she started — while her ledger shows clean, steady improvment the whole way through. The point of the exercise is to isolate a structural defect in the method rather than a moral failing in the person, so a case where she fudges her marks or quietly abandons the project doesn't count, and neither does one where her life falls apart from outside.

Specifications:

- One scenario, one paragraph, contemporary and realistic: a school, a clinic, a family firm, a parish, whatever. No sci-fi, no AI, no exotic devices.
- No external catastrophe — no illness, layoff, bereavement, disaster, windfall. The deterioration has to come out of how the method organizes her attention.
- No weakness of will and no self-deception. She records honestly and accuratly everything the method asks her to record, and the method asks for exactly what Franklin's asks for: faults against the virtue currently under examination, marked at the day's evening reckoning.
- She ends up worse on every virtue she set out to improve, or at least on a clear majority including at least one she never once violated on paper. And the worsening has to be visible to an outside observer with no ledger — a colleague, a sibling, a student — while being structurally invisible to the ledger itself.
- The mechanism has to be one a careful reader could have predicted in advance from the design of the method, not a quirk of this particular woman's personality.

Then do the checking work, because the scenario alone is worthless to me:

1. Name the mechanism in one sentence and trace the causal chain from faithful execution of the method to the bad outcome. Step by step, not a vibe.
2. Say which specific design feature is doing the work — the strictly sequential one-at-a-time structure? the fault-only format? the fact that the examination is retrospective and self-administered? the way each virtue gets pinned down by a single precept? — and defend that choice against its nearest neighbors.
3. Remove that feature and hold everything else fixed. Does the case collapse into ordinary akrasia, survive in a weaker form, or turn into a different objection entirely?
4. Which of Franklin's own resources could have caught this — the Order precept, the Humility precept, the speckled-ax warning, the repeated-courses structure? For each, say whether it actually blocks the mechanism or only looks like it does. I'm expecting at least one of them to look like a fix and not be one, and I'd like you to say which.
5. Give the minimal modification to the method that blocks the mechanism without throwing away the ledger, and say plainly what the modification costs. No free lunch.

Finish with Franklin's best defense: a paragraph in which he, or a sympathetic reader, attacks the case — arguing the mechanism isn't generated by his method after all, or that my scenario smuggles in something he'd rightly disallow, or that the ledger was never meant to do the work I'm making it do. Then tell me honestly whether that defense lands.
