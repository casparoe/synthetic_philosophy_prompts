You are a political philosopher specializing in democratic theory, and in particular in the boundary problem: the question of who composes the demos and how any answer to that question could itself be legitimate. I am circulating a position statement at a workshop next month and want a rigorous audit of my own commitments before I do. Think carefully and work through the pairings explicitly rather than responding to the package as a whole; before judging a commitment, be clear about what work it is doing in the package.

Here is the package.

1. The correct principle of inclusion is all-subjected rather than all-affected: everyone subject to a polity's coercive authority has a claim to an equal say in it. I prefer it for exactly this reason — affectedness is unbounded, since nearly everything a large state does bears on someone somewhere, and it yields a constituency too indeterminate to be a demos at all.
2. Coercion, however, is not confined to territory. Sanctions regimes, conditions attached to lending, interdiction and preclearance arrangements operated inside foreign airports, and targeted strikes all impose coercively on people far outside the state's borders, and I want to say those people are subjects too, and so inside the relevant constituency.
3. The unit question cannot be settled democratically. Deciding by majority vote who gets to vote presupposes an answer to the very question it is being asked, so the boundaries of the demos must be fixed by a normative principle that is prior to, rather than produced by, democratic procedure.
4. Whatever fixes those boundaries must be justifiable to the people it excludes, not only to those it admits.
5. Citizens who cannot cast a ballot — young children, adults with profound cognitive disabilities — are full members of the demos. The incapacity is handled inside the franchise, by proxy, guardianship, or trustee representation; it is not a ground for placing them outside the constituency.
6. The current EU arrangement is legitimate. There is no European demos, but member-state electorates are internally inclusive, and EU-level authority is exercised through and answerable to those national demoi, so its coercion binds people who, taken severally, have a say in it.

Three deliverables. First, an audit: say which of these genuinely pull against one another and which merely look as though they do. For each genuine tension, give the mechanism — what it is about the two commitments that prevents both from holding — and state what would have to give and what that concession costs. Second, rank the genuine tensions by severity instead of listing them as equally damaging. Third, identify the single revision to the package that would clear up the most, and tell me what I lose by making it; I want the price of the fix, not just its availability.

I do not know where the weak joint is, and I am not signalling any attachment to particular items, so please do not assume that any one of them is the one I would rather keep.

Verdict: <number of the commitment to revise or drop> | <five words or fewer naming the revision>
