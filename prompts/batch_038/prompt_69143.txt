I've been keeping a list of when I think a belief counts as good and when it doesn't, and I've noticed I can't actually state the rule I'm using. Maybe you can find it for me. Here's the list, no particular order.

1. My uncle said a stock was going up because of a dream he had, put money on it, and it went up. Even though he was right, I don't think his belief was any good. Being true doesn't seem to be enough.

2. But then: two people both think it'll rain tomorrow. One is like 60% sure, one is 95% sure. It rains. I definitely think the 95% one did better. And if it hadn't rained, the 95% one did way worse, and that feels right too — confidently wrong is worse than sort of wrong, confidently right is better than sort of right. So there I'm grading beliefs purely on how they turn out, which is exactly what I refused to do in #1. I don't know how to hold both.

3. My cousin gets her politics from sketchy internet stuff and she called the last election right. Same as the uncle, I want to say her belief is bad. But alot of my own beliefs are things I soaked up from my parents and my town and never once checked, and plenty of them are true. I want to say mine are fine and hers aren't, and I honestly can't see the difference. If your rule puts us in the same boat, tell me.

4. It's true that 2+2=4 and it's true that my sister's second marriage is going to fail. Both are true beliefs of mine. The second one feels worth more — not because it matters to my life more exactly, more like there was less going for it beforehand. It was harder to land.

5. If someone has no opinion at all about something, never heard of it, I don't think they've done anything wrong, but I also don't think they've scored anything. Blank isn't good or bad.

6. Someone who suspends judgement on literally everything can never be wrong. I'm sure that's not the best you can do. But I can't say what my pile of mostly-true-beliefs-and-some-false-ones has that theirs is missing, besides "more true stuff" — and if avoiding error is the point, theirs wins outright. I refuse to accept that and I don't know why.

7. My neighbor believes his diet works and also believes it doesn't work. One of those has to be false, fine. But holding a contradiction feels worse to me than just holding two seperate ordinary false beliefs — worse than thinking it's Tuesday when it's Wednesday and also thinking your car's a Toyota when it's a Honda. Can't explain why.

8. A guy has four thousand true beliefs about baseball box scores and basically nothing else. I have maybe two hundred true beliefs spread over everything. I don't think he's better off than me. But I also think if you handed me one extra true belief for free I'd be better off. Those two are fighting each other.

9. My doctor was 90% sure a treatment would work and it didn't. I don't think she did anything wrong. So maybe in some of these I'm not grading the belief at all, I'm grading the person, and then #2 is about something completely different than #1 and #9. That might be the confusion underneath everything but I can't get it into focus.

My own guess at the rule, for what it's worth, is something like: a belief is good if it's true and you'd still have gotten it right if things had gone a bit differently. But I think that trips on #5 and #8 and I'm not sure it does anything for #7.

So — is there one principle behind all this, or a couple of competing ones that I'm switching between without noticing? State it as precisely as you can, precise enough that I could apply it to a new case myself without asking you. Then pick a case or two I didn't think of that would stress it and tell me what your principle says about them. And be straight about which of my verdicts it can't keep: if some have to go, say which ones and why, instead of bending the rule until everything fits. Plain language please. If these ideas have proper names, drop the names at the very end, but I don't want a list of stuff to go read.
