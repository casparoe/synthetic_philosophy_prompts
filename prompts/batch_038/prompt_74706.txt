I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and for two years I have been reporting on what I have come to think of, loosely and unhappily, as consent-by-default technology: automatic enrollment, algorithmic curation, remote shutoffs, caregiver overrides, tutoring systems that decide what a child is ready to be asked. Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor has become my private yardstick, which I would never admit in print — the aged cardinal in Seville who tells the returned Christ that he misjudged human nature when he refused the three temptations in the wilderness, that a freedom most people cannot carry is a cruelty dressed up as respect, and that the Church has since corrected his work by taking that burden onto a few strong shoulders and giving the multitude bread, settled answers, and quiet happiness in exchange. What unsettles me is that my own judgments about the systems I report on plainly do not track how much freedom they remove. Some removals I shrug at; others I call oppressive, and the difference is not the size of the choice taken away, nor how much better off people end up. I would like to know whether there is a principle behind my verdicts, or whether I am simply inconsistent in a way I have not yet been able to name.

Here they are, arrived at honestly and, I suspect, unevenly.

- Automatic enrollment of workers in a retirement plan, with an opt-out reachable in three clicks and no penalty: fine.
- A city that bans private cars from its center and replaces them with trams that run every four minutes: fine.
- An organ-donation opt-out in which the family is still consulted: fine. The same opt-out with the family's objection overruled: not fine — even though the number of transplants is roughly identical and the dead patient's own prior wishes are unchanged.
- A pillbox a daughter can lock until her elderly father has taken his dose: not fine, and the fact that he once asked her to look after him does not rescue it. But if he had asked her and retained a way to unlock it himself, at any hour, it would be fine.
- A feed curated because engagement rises when people cannot choose what they see: not fine. A feed curated because users say they want to be protected from outrage, and because the company's own research shows they sleep better and argue less: also not fine, and I cannot say why.
- A robotaxi that will not hand back the wheel in an emergency: not fine. A robotaxi that hands back the wheel, logs that almost no rider ever takes it, and keeps the option in place anyway: fine.
- Banning a recreational drug on the grounds that most who try it end up worse off: not fine. Mandating helmets for adult cyclists on the same grounds: fine. These two sit badly together and I have no idea how to fix them.
- The one I keep circling: an AI tutor that withholds a certain line of questioning from a nine-year-old because it predicts, correctly, that she would find it distressing and learn less from it. Fine, I think — and yet if the tutor volunteered an account of what it was withholding and why, I would want it to stop.

Is there a principle that gets all or most of these? I want it stated precisely enough that a colleague could apply it to a new system without telephoning me to ask what I meant, with every clause tied back to the case that forced it in, and with the candidate dimensions you considered and rejected — reversibility, who bears the burden of deciding, whether the option would actually have been exercised, whether the arrangement could be explained to the person it governs — dealt with explicitly rather than left implicit. Then give me its verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a utility that disconnects power remotely for nonpayment with no phone call and no human anywhere in the loop; an app that silently suppresses notifications after ten at night because its users' sleep measurably improves; and a prosecutor's standard plea offer, which is a real choice made under a threat so lopsided that nearly everyone accepts it. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot save. If the price of saving all of them is a principle so baroque that it merely restates my intuitions in longer words, say so plainly and give me the simpler one instead, naming the two or three verdicts it overturns and what I would have to argue to a reader who shares them and is not a philosopher. Finally: how much of this is really the Inquisitor's argument? Does my pattern suggest that his charge against Christ has force only under specifiable conditions, or are the conditions I seem to be groping toward ones he would have dismissed as beside the point — since his claim was precisely that people do not want to be asked?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle in one line, or the flat statement that no single principle fits my verdicts — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
