So I've spent the last couple of years defending a view I call restricted penetrability, and I want to know where it breaks — not a survey of the debate, I know the debate, I want the pressure points.

The view: perceptual experience is not penetrated by belief, desire, valuation or emotion, but it is penetrated by two other things that people sloppily lump in with cognition — the current attentional set, and long-run perceptual learning/expertise. So I take the motivated-perception and hill-slant literatures to be mostly junk (demand characteristics, uncontrolled eye movements, effects on what subjects say rather than what they see), and I take the expertise effects — radiologists, chess players, dog show judges, the Greeble training studies — to be perfectly real but not penetration at all, because a trained discrimination circuit isn't a belief doing the work. The thing I'm most attached to is the test I use to police the line. Call it the triple-match standard: an effect counts as penetration only if you can hold fixed the proximal stimulation, the attentional allocation (measured, not assumed), and the task and response demands, vary only the belief or the value, and still get a difference in what is seen — and the subject can report that difference while still having it, so it can't be a change in judgment or memory downstream.

What I want from you: a map of what would actually count against this package, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. For each candidate defeater say what the finding or argument would have to show, whether it's already on the table or only something that could in principle be run, and what a commited defender would say about it — I care a lot about which ones I'd be within my rights to dismiss and which ones I'd have to swallow.

A few things I specifically want you to hit:

1. The case that keeps me up at night. People learn two novel names for two shades, or you take the Russian two-blues result, and the discrimination advantage across the category boundary shows up inside a single session and gets wiped out by a concurrent verbal task. That's learning, so it should be on my side of the line — but it's minutes old and it's driven by language, so it looks like exactly the kind of thing I'm trying to exclude. Can the line hold here, or is this the crack the whole thing runs along?

2. Does my view risk anything? It explains why some top-down effects replicate and others don't, which is supposed to be an advantage over flat-out modularity, but tell me whether those explanations have been risky or whether they've all been cashed out after the fact. If the triple-match standard is so demanding that nothing could ever satisfy it, is that a defect — given that I'm advertising the view as answerable to experiment — or is that fine for a view of this kind?

3. The structural worry: if perception is inference under priors, and priors are built out of what the organism has learned about the world, then "attentional set" and "learning" may just be where cognition lives, and I've moved the boundary rather than drawn it. Say how serious that is. Same question for the fact that there are massive feedback projections into early visual areas — what would it take for that anatomy to become evidence against me rather than something I can shrug at?

4. What does my view predict that neither pure modularity nor thoroughgoing penetration predicts, and have any of those predictions been tested?

5. Finally, the philosophy of science stake. If Hanson's point gets redescribed as trained attention, am I actually denying the theory-ladenness of observation or just renaming it? I'd rather hear that my position is largely insulated and that this is not a problem, if that's the honest verdict, than a list of everything anyone has ever said against modularity.
