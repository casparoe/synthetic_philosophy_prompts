I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine — mostly AI and neuroscience — but I spent six years as a commercial contracts solicitor before that, and doctrine is still the language my brain defaults to. I'm working on a piece about software that commits: agents that accept terms, book slots, agree to arbitrate, tell a user "done, that's settled." To get the philosophy straight I need a translation, not a survey.

Take the debate over why promises bind — Hume's conventionalist story, Rawls's practice conception, the reliance-based line (Atiyah, Thomson), Scanlon's expectation-based account, Fried's view that contract law is basically the morality of promise enforced by courts, and the will- or autonomy-based line that treats a promise as handing someone temporary authority over a bit of your own future conduct — and restate each in the vocabulary I already have: offer and acceptance, consideration, reliance and promissory estoppel, expectation vs reliance damages, capacity, duress, void versus voidable, the objective theory of assent. Don't smuggle the philosophy in under legal labels and call it translation; if a term does double duty, flag it.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: which claims map cleanly, which only fit if you distort them, and which have no counterpart in doctrine at all. My hunch — tell me if it's wrong — is that the lawyers already answered the philosophers and answered them no: the courts refused to enforce the bare promise and invented consideration to stand in for it, so "does promising by itself create an obligation" got settled negatively by the people who had to make the thing work.

And does anything shift when the promisor is software? Where residue is doing real work, say whether its a hole in the legal vocabulary or a sign the philosophical question was never well posed.
