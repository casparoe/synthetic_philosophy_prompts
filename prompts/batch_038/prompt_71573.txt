I've lurked on rationalist comment threads for years and read way too many big-history takes, but last weekend two friends argued about this in my kitchen and I honestly could not tell who was winning. Neither of them is me — I mostly just kept the coffee coming — and I'd like a referee. Neither is a professional philosopher, both are smart, neither is a strawman.

Topic: whether Sapiens-style grand narratives explain anything or are dressed-up just-so stories.

(1) Rae opened with the claim that Harari's core move — Sapiens beat everything else because we cooperate flexibly in huge numbers by believing in shared fictions (gods, money, nations, limited liability companies) — is unfalsifiable for precisely the stretch of time it's meant to explain. Beliefs don't fossilize. So any evidence of large-scale cooperation gets retrodicted as "they must have had a shared myth," and any absence of it gets explained as "their myths didn't scale." Which makes the fiction thesis a dormitive virtue: the shared fiction just is the cooperation, redescribed in mental terms.

(2) Sam said fine, but that charge is a boomerang. The materialist default — grain surplus produced hierarchy — is no more observable for deep prehistory, and Graeber and Wengrow's counter-narrative, where people consciously try out seasonal authority and then walk away from it, requires you to read intentions into arrangements too. If unfalsifiability sinks Harari it sinks the whole genre, critics included, and Rae hasn't earned the right to pass judgment from inside the same boat.

(3) Rae said the asymmetry is in burden, not method. Harari writes as though he's explaining, and people downstream actually run predictions off the mechanism (imagined orders will keep holding us together). A polemic saying your story of inevitable linear progress is a myth isn't in the prediction business, so it doesn't inherit the same obligation. She added: the one time this story got operationalized into something testable — the moralizing-high-gods hypothesis — the big cross-cultural database work came out backwards, with gods who punish immoral behavior showing up after societies got huge rather than before, and then people spent years arguing about how the coders had defined a moralizing god in the first place. So the testable version failed, which is at least weak evidence about the untestable version.

(4) Sam: two problems. The gods result is soft given the coding fight, so it can't carry much weight in either direction. And gods are a tiny slice of what counts as a fiction — money, land title, corporate personhood, human rights need no deity — so a result about gods can't touch the general thesis. As for burden, "writes as though he's explaining" is a fact about marketing, not about epistemology.

(5) Rae: then say what's left. Strip out the gods and you land on "large-scale anonymous cooperation needs institutions, and institutions need shared beliefs about who or what counts as what." That's the social-ontology material on status functions and imagined communities with the serial numbers filed off and pushed back fifty thousand years. Acceptable as synthesis, but the distinctive claim — that this is the thing Sapiens has and chimps and Neanderthals don't — is where all the explanatory weight was sitting, and that's the part nobody can cash out.

(6) Sam conceded novelty in kind but said novelty was never the claim; the claim is about scope and about which factor does the causal work. And Sam said Rae's own move concedes the point: if the thesis really is a known mechanism with independent support from a separate literature, it isn't a just-so story, because a just-so story is defined by having no support beyond the phenomenon it was invented to fit.

Both walked away sure they'd won. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of (1)–(6), say whether it lands, partially lands, or fails, and why — then say who's more right overall. Two things I couldn't settle on my own: does Sam's boomerang in (2) actually neutralize the just-so charge, or does Rae's burden asymmetry in (3) survive it? And is Rae right in (5) that retreating to "it's not novel" guts the thesis, or is Sam right in (6) that independent support is exactly what separates a mechanism from a story? Also flag anything both of them are missing — I have a vague sense they're using "explains" in two different senses and talking past each other, but I couldn't of said which senses.
