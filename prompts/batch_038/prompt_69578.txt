I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I would ask that what follows be treated as a layperson's position that nonetheless deserves rigorous examination. I have arrived, largely from thinking about service-level agreements, cancellation windows, and coordination between systems that cannot inspect one another's internals, at a view about why promises bind. I want to know what would count against it.

The view, stated as precisely as I can manage:

1. A promise is not an act of will that binds the promisor directly and creates obligation out of nothing. It is a signal that licenses the promisee to plan as though some future action is settled.
2. The obligation to perform is generated entirely by what the promisee does with that license — specifically, by commitments the promisee makes on the strength of it that they can no longer cheaply reverse.
3. The strength of the obligation is therefore a function of the irreversibility of the reliance induced. Large, sunk, unrecoverable reliance produces a strong obligation; negligible or fully reversible reliance produces something close to none.
4. Where no such reliance exists, there is no obligation to perform. There remains a weaker obligation: to notify the promisee of non-performance early enough that they can still adjust. On this view, breaking a promise that nobody relied upon is not itself a wrong; failing to give timely notice is.
5. Promising differs from merely declaring an intention only in that it explicitly grants the other party permission to rely, and shifts onto the speaker the cost of the speaker's later change of mind.

I want a map of this position's exposure. Specifically:

- Which cases, arguments, or intuitions would count against it? I want the objections a careful opponent would actually press, not strawmen.
- Rank those defeaters from fatal — the view cannot survive them without ceasing to be this view — through serious but repairable, to merely awkward.
- For each, say what our judgments or the facts would have to look like for it to bite, and whether a committed proponent could absorb it (for instance by broadening irreversible reliance to cover forgone alternatives, reputational exposure, or the sheer difficulty of emotionally readjusting) or whether absorbing it costs the view something real.
- Tell me which of these a proponent would themselves concede as counting against the position rather than dismiss. I care about that distinction.
- What does this view predict that rival accounts of promissory obligation do not? Are there situations in which it visibly comes out wrong about who has been wronged, and by how much?
- Assess honestly whether the view can be insulated from all of this by stretching the notion of reliance far enough, and if it can, whether that insulation is a defect for a moral theory of this kind or an acceptable feature of something that is really a claim about the grounds of an obligation rather than an empirical hypothesis.

Two worries of my own, which you should overrule if you think they are the wrong ones. First, clause 4 seems to make every promise cancellable at will by giving sufficiently early notice, which would erase the distinction I claim in clause 5. Second, a deceitful promise — made with no intention of performance, where the promisee's reliance is subsequently prevented — looks on this account as though it stops binding at the moment the deception is discovered, which strikes me as plainly false.

I am not asking for citations, names, or a reading list; I have no way to follow them up and would rather have the reasoning. Please be willing to conclude that the position is in better shape, or worse, than I suspect.
