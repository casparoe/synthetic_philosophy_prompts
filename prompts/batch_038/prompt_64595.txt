ok so I'm auditing a grad seminar on the Annales school and I keep getting the assignment "explain Braudel's three time scales" which everyone has read a hundred summaries of. What I actually want is the thing nobody assigns: a real worked case where the framework gets stress-tested against an episode it was never built for. So — treat this as a commission from someone who reads both 20th-century French historiography and US environmental history and is tired of both of them talking past each other. You're the expert here; think carefully before you start, and spend a few hundred words brainstorming/arguing with yourself about the contested placements before you write up the analysis.

The episode is the Dust Bowl, and since I don't want you guessing at facts I'll just lay out what I take to be the case, and please correct me if I've garbled any of it. Native shortgrass prairie over the southern Plains — the Oklahoma and Texas panhandles, southwestern Kansas, southeastern Colorado, northeastern New Mexico — gets plowed out in earnest from the 1880s onward, encouraged by federal land policy (Homestead Act quarter-sections, which were sized for wet-farming regions and were arguably too small to be viable in a semi-arid one), by a widespread belief that cultivation itself would increase rainfall, and by WWI-era wheat prices. The 1920s price collapse doesn't stop the expansion; tractors and combines mean more marginal ground goes under. Drought sets in around 1931, and the loose exposed topsoil starts to move — storm days multiply year over year through the decade. On March 21, 1935, Hugh Hammond Bennett, head of the short-lived Soil Erosion Service, is testifying before a congressional subcommittee on a bill to create a permanent soil conservation agency; he'd been tipped off that a dust storm was moving east, and by the standard account he stretched his testimony until the sky outside the hearing room went dark. The Senate passed the bill the next day, and the Soil Conservation Act was signed on April 27, 1935, with Bennett running the new service. Three weeks earlier-er, later, sorry: on April 14, 1935 came the worst single storm, Black Sunday, and a Washington-based journalist apparently coined the term Dust Bowl in a dispatch right afterward. FDR's shelterbelt program then had the Forest Service, CCC and WPA planting roughly 220 million trees as farm windbreaks across six Plains states between 1935 and 1942. Rain largely returns around 1939, WWII wheat demand arrives, several hundred thousand people (somewhere between 200 and 300 thousand is the figure I remember for California alone) had already left for the West Coast, and then — the part I care about most — after the war center-pivot irrigation lets farmers start pumping the Ogallala/High Plains aquifer, which turns the same landscape into one of the most productive agricultural regions on earth while the water table falls steadily in the places where recharge is slowest.

Here's what I want from you, and please be rigorous about the mapping rather than gesturing at it. First: decompose the episode into Braudel's three time scales — geographic/structural time, social and economic conjonctures, and histoire événementielle — and put concrete named features in each bucket. Some placements will be genuinely arguable (is the drought a structure or an event? is the wheat price cycle a conjoncture or a slow structure with a fast name? is the aquifer a fourth tier Braudel doesn't have?), so argue those out instead of filing them tidily. Second, run it in the other direction. Braudel's whole rhetorical move is that events are surface disturbances, foam the tide carries along, and that the real history is nearly immobile — and yet here is a case where a single afternoon of parliamentary theater in Washington appears to have produced a permanent federal agency, an 18,000-mile tree-planting program, and a new technical regime for how millions of acres get farmed. Is that an event with genuine structural efficacy, i.e. causation flowing the wrong way up his hierarchy? Or would Braudel say the conservation state was already forming — soil science, the agronomic bureaus, the Depression-era administrative explosion — and the hearing was just the moment a slow process became photographable? Push on this until it hurts: what would have to happen, in principle, for an event to count as structurally efficacious on Braudel's account, and is there any answer that isn't unfalsifiable? Third, the individuation problem. Tell me when the Dust Bowl starts and stops, and what makes it one thing rather than several overlapping ones — the plow-up, the drought years, the 1935 legislation, the postwar pivot revolution, the aquifer drawdown that's still running. If the boundaries move depending on the resolution you set, is the longue durée a feature of the world or an artifact of the historian's chosen timescale, and does the fact that you can only see the structure retrospectively undercut its claim to explain anything? Fourth, a cross-cut I don't think anyone does: Braudel's later tripartite division of economic life — everyday material existence, the market economy, and capitalism as the anti-market of big firms and monopoly — doesn't line up with the three time scales at all. Does it cut across them here, and which tier is the Dust Bowl actually a story about: the market (Chicago wheat, the 1920s collapse), the anti-market (grain traders, railroads, implement makers, the banks that held the mortgages), or material life (the daily work of listing and furrowing, the household economy of a dryland farm)? Watch for the temptation to let the longue durée do moral work it hasn't earned — blaming "capitalism" or "the market" for what was in large part a very specific and datable set of federal land and credit policies. And finally, say where the framework goes silent rather than forcing it to speak: Braudel's habit of making geography the protagonist and the slowest character in the book, and whether promoting climate to an actor smuggles agency back into a method designed to drain agency out; plus the awkward fact that his own practice in the third part of the Mediterranean is pretty much conventional event-narrative about Philip II's wars, which reads like a confession. Does the Dust Bowl confirm, extend, or embarrass that tension?
