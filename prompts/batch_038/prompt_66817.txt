I run a small graduate seminar on the foundations of non-cooperative game theory, and the session I'm having the most trouble preparing is the one on backward induction in finite games — not the procedure, but the paradox. My students can reproduce Rosenthal's centipede and Selten's chain-store argument without difficulty, and they can also reproduce the familiar battery of replies: that common knowledge of rationality is doing all the work, that a small probability of an opponent who is not rational (or not believed to be rational) unravels the induction in the opposite direction, that the paradox is really a complaint about the payoff and information description rather than about rationality as such. What none of them can do is take that machinery to something that actually happened and say honestly where it fits and where it does not. I want a worked case to put in front of them, and the case I have chosen is the sequence of United States financial rescues between March and October 2008, with the weekend of Lehman Brothers' failure as the pivot.

Take the following as given, so that nothing turns on your recall of the record. In March 2008 the Federal Reserve helped arrange the sale of Bear Stearns to JPMorgan Chase, with public support behind the least attractive assets. On September 7 the two mortgage giants Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were placed into government conservatorship. Over the weekend of September 13–14 the Fed and the Treasury gathered the heads of the large banks at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York to engineer a private sale of Lehman, and the authorities declined to put public money behind any of the proposals on the table; Lehman filed for bankruptcy before dawn on September 15, still the largest bankruptcy filing in American history. That day and the next, the Reserve Primary Fund, one of the oldest money market funds and heavily exposed to financial-sector commercial paper, faced redemption demands amounting to more than half its assets within two days, declared its Lehman paper worthless, and announced a share price below one dollar; runs on other money market funds followed within days, and the Treasury stepped in to guarantee fund shares on a temporary basis. On September 16, the same day the fund broke the buck, the Fed extended an $85 billion secured loan to AIG and took effective control of the company. At the end of September the House of Representatives rejected the Treasury's emergency authority bill on a first vote, equities fell sharply, a revised $700 billion program passed within days, and by mid-October the government had shifted from buying troubled assets to injecting capital directly into the largest banks. Two further features matter for the analysis. The Treasury Secretary had told Lehman's chief executive as early as the spring that there would be no government rescue for his firm. And officials offered, then and afterwards, two different accounts of the September weekend: that they lacked legal authority to lend against inadequate collateral, and that they had decided the moment had come to stop rescuing. Still earlier episodes — the rescue of Continental Illinois in 1984 and the orchestrated wind-down of Long-Term Capital Management in 1998 — were in the background as precedents everyone in the room knew about.

Here is what I want. First, fix the game before you play it: who the players are (is the market a player, a set of players, or merely the environment? is the government one player or several with different payoffs and different legal powers? is Lehman a player or the stake being played for? where do the banks sit, given that they were both in the room and the counterparties?); what the decision nodes are; what the horizon is, and in particular whether the players could have known how long the sequence would run. Flag which of these are stipulations of mine rather than findings, because a game-theoretic reading of an episode like this smuggles in a great deal of structure before it explains anything. Second, run the induction: what does reasoning back from the terminal node actually predict for a sequence of rescue decisions — discipline imposed early or late? Bring the chain-store structure in explicitly, a government facing a succession of failing institutions where the behavior of the ones not yet failing rides on how it treats the one in front of it, and say whether that story predicts fighting the first entrant or the last. Then tell me whether there is any defensible centipede reading of the sequence, in which each rescue grows the pot that a later refusal forfeits, or whether the centipede is simply the wrong shape here; if it is the wrong shape, name the structural feature the case lacks instead of stretching the analogy. Third, account for the pattern that actually occurred: rescue, rescue, refusal, rescue again within thirty-six hours, then everything at once. The refusal landed in the middle of the sequence and was reversed almost immediately, which is close to the worst possible place for a backward-induction story. Diagnose which assumption failed — the payoffs at the terminal node, the information about counterparty exposure, the number and identity of the players, the horizon, the rationality assumption — and say whether the AIG reversal is a vindication of the reputational critique of backward induction or evidence that no strategic story was operative at all, given that a legal constraint and a strategic choice can look identical in observed play. Fourth, turn it around: what does the episode show about the paradox? Is it a case where backward induction failed, a case where it never got the chance to apply because the game was misdescribed from the outset, or a case in which the players were themselves reasoning back from a terminal node they had badly mis-estimated — and if the last, does that make the episode a confirmation of the theory's internal logic rather than a refutation of it? Finally, mark the places where the theory is simply silent: what the record shows that no model of this family can represent without arbitrary stipulation.

I do not want sources, a bibliography, or a list of further reading; the seminar already has all of that. I want the argument. Where you are unsure of a fact beyond what I have given you, flag the uncertainty rather than supplying a plausible detail, and where a piece of the machinery genuinely does not apply to the case, say that it does not apply instead of forcing it to speak. For my students, the value of the exercise lies precisely in the places where the mapping breaks.
