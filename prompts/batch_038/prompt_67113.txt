Question: State Kim's causal exclusion argument, identifying each of the premises it needs. Then evaluate the following response: multiple realizability shows that the exclusion principle cannot be applied to mental properties, since a multiply realizable property is identical to no physical property. Does the response work? If it doesn't, what should the nonreductive physicalist say instead? (take-home, 1500 words permitted)

Student's answer:

Kim's exclusion argument is an argument against nonreductive physicalism in particular, and it's worth saying up front that it isn't an argument against substance dualism. The dualist isn't committed to the completeness of physics; what she faces instead is the older interaction problem. The argument needs these premises.

(1) Mental causation: some mental events cause physical events. My decision to reach for the glass causes the reaching, and the reaching is a physical event.

(2) Causal closure of the physical: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. So in the case above there is some neural event N that is sufficient for the arm's going up.

(3) The exclusion principle: where an event already has a sufficient cause, no further distinct thing is also a cause of it, unless the effect is genuinely overdetermined.

(4) No systematic overdetermination: it isn't open to say that both D and N cause the arm to rise, because that would make every instance of mental causation a case of overdetermination, and overdetermination that happens as a matter of routine, in every single case, isn't anything we believe anywhere else.

From these it follows that either D is not distinct from N, in which case it is identical to it and we have given up nonreductivism, or D causes nothing. Reduction or epiphenomenalism. Kim accepts the second, which is why he's generally read as an epiphenomenalist about the mental.

Kim also needs supervenience, and this is the premise people understate. Mental properties supervene on physical ones, so D can't float free of N; there has to be a physical base for it. But supervenience is also what guarantees that D and N are distinct events — if they were the same event there would be nothing for the mental to supervene on — and it is precisely that distinctness which lets the exclusion principle get going. Supervenience does damage on its own account as well: if D is nothing over and above N, then whatever causal work D appears to do is already being done by N, and D's causal powers drain down into their base. That is Kim's argument against downward causation in general.

Now the multiple realizability response. Pain is realized by C-fibre firing in us, by a quite different neural arrangement in an octopus, and perhaps by silicon states in a machine; so pain is identical to none of these. It's claimed to follow that exclusion can't apply, because there is no single physical property competing with pain for the job of causing the wincing — the competitor changes with the species.

I think this response fails, and the failure is instructive. Non-identity is not a defence, it is the problem. What exclusion asks is whether the effect already has a sufficient cause, and in each case it does: the C-fibres are sufficient for the human wincing, the octopus state for the octopus wincing. Kim's principle applies token by token rather than across a species, so multiple realizability doesn't dissolve the exclusion problem, it multiplies it, one fresh instance for every realizer you add. Worse, it closes off the only reply that would actually have worked, which is identifying the mental property with the physical one.

What should the nonreductive physicalist say instead? The most promising line appeals to proportionality. On Yablo's version, a cause ought to be commensurate with its effect: sufficient for it, but not more specific than the effect requires. A pigeon trained to peck at red things pecks at a scarlet triangle, and the pecking is caused by the triangle's being red rather than by its being scarlet, since scarlet is more than the effect called for. Applied here, C-fibre firing is gratuitously specific relative to the wincing. The wincing would have happened had pain been realized the octopus way instead. So the neural state isn't necessary for the wincing, and a cause that could have been absent without the effect going missing isn't the sufficient cause that closure promised. Closure fails in cases like this one, and once it does, exclusion has nothing left to exclude with. The physical story leaves a gap exactly where the mental property sits.

There's a broader point available too, and I think it's the strongest thing the nonreductive physicalist has. Exclusion isn't a principle about the mental in particular. If it holds, it holds for geology and biology and meteorology as well: the motion of the billiard ball is fully accounted for by the molecules, the earthquake by the displacements of rock. Kim's principle would render every special science causally inert. We know perfectly well that it doesn't, so the principle is false.
