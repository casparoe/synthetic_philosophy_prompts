For fourteen years I taught epistemology to graduate students. Eighteen months ago I left the university to sit on the panel of a body that determines protection claims — that is, that decides whether a person who has arrived here saying they would be persecuted at home is to be believed. We hear roughly twenty-five cases a month and write reasons for each. My reasons for taking the post were not disinterested: my mother's own application was refused in the early nineteen-nineties on the ground that her account was internally inconsistent, and it took four years and a second panel to have substantially the same story accepted. I have thought about that first refusal for most of my adult life. Now that I sit on the other side of the table, I find I am no better at saying what I am doing than my colleagues are, and I suspect that whatever views on testimony I published were never tested against anything.

The template we are issued asks three questions: whether the claimant's account is credible, whether it is corroborated, and whether any inconsistencies are material. In practice the panel treats these as three questions about a single thing. I have become convinced they are at least three different things — whether this speaker is sincere, whether she is well placed to be accurate about this particular content, and whether the proposition is likely to be true given everything else in the file — and that collapsing them is where most of our bad decisions come from. I would like a decision procedure that makes the separation explicit, or that tells me the separation is a mistake and shows me why.

It has to be runnable by a colleague with no training in philosophy, in the two hours we have in the deliberation room before reasons are drafted. Ground it in what the epistemology of testimony actually says rather than in how tribunals phrase things: I want reductionist and anti-reductionist positions doing work at specific steps, not being name-checked in a preamble. Do not give me references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I will supply those myself. Assume I know the standard arguments and positions, and go past them.

At minimum, the procedure must settle the following, and must say what it is doing at each point:

- Which default it adopts. Does the panel begin from suspension and need reasons to accept, or from acceptance and need reasons to withhold? And is the "benefit of the doubt" standard we are told to apply a genuinely third thing, or a value judgment wearing epistemic clothing? If it is the latter, say so plainly and tell me where in the procedure that judgment enters.
- Partial fabrication. A claimant who, on a fixer's advice, invented a detail about the route or the date. Does a discovered falsehood in one part of an account give the panel any reason at all bearing on the rest, and if so, how much? This is the point at which our current template is most clearly wrong, and I want the correction stated as a rule I could apply.
- Implausibility. The panel finds a sequence of events unlikely because it does not match what members expect persecution to look like. Under what conditions can a hearer's background expectations function as a defeater rather than as prejudice, and how would a lay colleague tell the difference in the room?
- Claims that are in principle uncorroborable. If a rule requiring independent support can never be satisfied in a class of cases, what does the procedure do — exempt them, lower the demand, or refuse?
- Circularity. Our sense of which kinds of claim are usually reliable is built out of past files and past panels' findings, which were themselves testimonial through and through. Can that serve as the independent evidence a reductionist demands, or is it bootstrapping of a kind that disqualifies it? If it is disqualified, what is left for us to go on.

Mark explicitly every point where the procedure stops being epistemology and hands the decision to a person, because what remains is a question about how many false refusals we are prepared to risk rather than a question about evidence. I would rather have two such points named precisely than a general caveat at the end.

Then run it on three cases of the kind we actually see, and make one of them a case whose outcome I will not welcome — a case in which the procedure says a panel is entitled to refuse, or says that a refusal on the evidence then available was not obviously wrong. If it comes out for the claimant every time, it is not a procedure and I will know you are flattering me.

Please organize the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **The default** — about 300 words. Which starting point you are building in, what the alternative would have cost, and what turns on the choice.
2. **The procedure** — about 900 words. Numbered steps in the order they are to be run. For each: the question to be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether that step can terminate in acceptance, terminate in refusal, or must hand off to a person.
3. **Three cases** — about 800 words. The steps actually executed, including where they bind and where they go quiet.
4. **What this procedure cannot do** — about 300 words. The failures it will produce with some regularity, and which of them I should accept as the price of having a procedure at all.
5. **Objections from both camps** — about 250 words. What a global reductionist would say is wrong with it, what an anti-reductionist would say is wrong with it, and which of the two objections I ought to concede.
