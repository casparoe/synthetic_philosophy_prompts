I'm drafting a chapter on the ethics of blame, and I've noticed that every account I read seems to have built its own success criteria into the examples it picks. Before I commit to one, I want the criteria themselves, stated independently of any candidate theory. So: write the specification that an account of standing to blame — and in particular of whether and how hypocrisy defeats standing — has to satisfy to count as adequate, given the purpose I have in mind. That purpose is practical rather than taxonomic: the theory should be usable by an ordinary agent deciding in the moment whether she is entitled to say what she thinks, and by an institution deciding whether its condemnation of one of its own members is legitimate. I want the phenomena such a theory must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must refuse to collapse, and the results or settled practices elsewhere that it must not contradict — each with the consideration behind it, and each tagged as non-negotiable or as a preference of one particular camp. No bibliography, no citations, no named papers; describe positions rather than attribute them.

Some raw material, so you aren't guessing what I count as data. There is the tu quoque intuition: condemnation from someone who does exactly what she condemns feels defective in a way that a stranger's identical words do not, and yet the person on the receiving end acquires no excuse — what he did remains wrong, and he knows it. There is the fact that the rejoinder asking who the speaker is to say such a thing sounds entirely legitimate in ordinary life while being a textbook ad hominem in argument, and a theory owes us an account of why both things can be true. There is also our ready acceptance of blame from reformed offenders, from the penitent, and from people who name the very fault they are guilty of as they name it, which puts a hard ceiling on how much explanatory weight any account can place on behavioral inconsistency. Then the cases I want the list tested against. The open hypocrite: a smoker who prefaces every warning to his daughter with an acknowledgment that he smokes, conceals nothing, and is if anything more credible for it. The closet complier: someone who has never once committed the fault she loudly condemns, but knows she would in a heartbeat under slightly different circumstances, and privately approves of it when others do it. The repentant elder: someone who did the wrong for two decades, stopped, made restitution, and now condemns a much younger person for it. The reciprocal standoff: two colleagues, both quietly skimming from the same fund, each privately convinced the other is corrupt, each blaming the other. The effective hypocrite: a counselor in active relapse whose warnings land far better with her patients than a spotless colleague's ever do. The collective: a state that legislated and enforced a practice now condemns a junior official for a small-scale private version of it. The self-flagellant: someone who judges himself for a fault far more harshly than he judges anyone else for the same fault. The silent judge: someone who voices no condemnation to anyone, ever, but whose private evaluations are wildly double-standard.

The specification should also fix the distinctions an account may not blur. Standing as a precondition on performing the act of blaming, versus standing as a factor in the moral quality of blame once performed. Hypocrisy as a standing-defeater, versus the defeaters that compete with it and are often run together with it — meddling where one has no business, complicity in the very wrong, absence of moral understanding, blame extracted under pressure, blame from someone with no relationship to the target at all — together with the question whether a theory that cannot separate these has thereby failed. Mere inconsistency versus hypocrisy proper; occurrent double standards versus dispositional ones; blaming others versus blaming oneself; the standing of an individual versus the standing of an institution, which acts through members none of whom need be individually compromised. And the account should sit coherently with adjacent territory: the clean-hands doctrine in equity, which denies relief to a party tainted by his own conduct in the same transaction, along with a judgment about whether that is a genuine analogy or a legal artifact with no moral counterpart; whatever the psychological evidence actually supports about how readily double standards are detected and about whether a compromised condemner makes the condemned conduct more or less likely; and the fact that blaming is one of the main mechanisms by which communities sustain their norms, so that a theory which silences most blame most of the time is being asked to pay an enormous price and should be made to say so explicitly.

Three things matter most, so give them the most room. First, identify any subset of these requirements that you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and say precisely which one has to be given up. My own suspicion is that the binding constraint runs between a demand that hypocrisy's defect be located in the blamer's own agency rather than in anyone's response to it, a demand that the defect be explained without appeal to consequences, and a demand that the account not imply permanent silencing for anyone who has ever committed the fault — but argue this rather than simply accepting it. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate accounts, i.e. the one that, if you had to bet, separates the theories worth taking seriously from the rest. Third, briefly — a paragraph, not a survey — how the main families of answer fare against the list: those that locate the defect in the tension between what the hypocrite's condemnation presupposes and how she lives, those that locate it in the damage to the practice of blame, those that locate it in a forfeited authority to make second-personal demands, and those that locate it in what the hypocrisy reveals about her character.
