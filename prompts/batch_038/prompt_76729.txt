I sat in on a reading group last night where two people got properly stuck on Anscombe's case against Truman — the argument behind her protest at Oxford giving him an honorary degree. Paraphrasing from memory, numbered so you can grade each one:

1. Priya: the elegant thing is her argument doesn't need any body counts. Truman's own stated reason — the bomb would shorten the war and save lives — already concedes that killing the city's civilians was the *means* to the good outcome. Add her principle that choosing to kill the innocent as a means is always murder, and it's settled.
2. Dev: but that principle is precisely what's in dispute. If you think what matters is how many die overall, nothing she's said engages you. She argues in a circle — the conclusion is tucked into a premise the consequentialist already rejects.
3. Priya: not circular, she stress-tests the rival view instead. If killing the harmless is licensed whenever it does more good, then killing enemy soldiers in their sleep is licensed too, and you end up with people saying the man who signed the order didn't kill anybody. Those consequences show the rival principle is corrupt, not that hers is unargued.
4. Dev: the sleeping-soldiers point backfires on her. If she accepts that killing combatants is permissible, she can't be working with "harmless right now" as the test — she needs a status notion. And once status is doing the work, a city with a garrison and war plants isn't cleanly civilian, so her factual premise about the target is shakier than she lets on.
5. Priya: hers is a test about intention, not about who happened to die. The bomb went onto an undifferentiated urban area becuase the horror was supposed to travel; the terror wasn't an unfortunate byproduct, it was the mechanism. Double effect can't bless that, since the effect doing the causal work is the forbidden one.
6. Dev: then she owes us the counterfactual, and that's where she's softest — she herself says the Allies' insistence on unconditional surrender is what made the bomb look necessary. And even granting her the act, what Oxford faced was whether to confer an honor, which is a conventional gesture toward a wartime head of state, not a verdict on one decision. She's sliding between honoring someone and endorsing what he did.

Nobody budged. Can you go through move by move and rate each one — clearly right, partly right, clearly wrong — with a sentence of justification per rating? Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who comes out more right overall.
