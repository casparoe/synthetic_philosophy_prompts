I write a fortnightly column on the science of reasoning for a general-interest magazine, and for an upcoming issue devoted to uncertainty I am commissioning an imagined symposium — a two-hander dialogue — to run alongside my reported piece. I would like you to write it.

The experimental setting is my own, so please take it as given rather than improving on it. A laboratory has invited me to observe a variant of the urn task that Ellsberg made famous. In every version there is a Known Urn — ninety balls, thirty red, thirty black, thirty green — and an Unknown Urn of ninety balls whose composition the subject is not told. In each version the subject faces the same choice: one bet pays a hundred dollars if a red ball is drawn from the Known Urn, the other pays a hundred dollars if a red ball is drawn from the Unknown Urn. Separately, the subject is offered the corresponding pair of bets on black. What differs across versions is only the story the subject is told about how the Unknown Urn came to be filled:

- Version A: the composition was set by a mechanical spinner, which the subject watched select uniformly among all compositions expressible in multiples of ten balls; the outcome was printed on a card and sealed in an opaque envelope left on the table.
- Version B: the composition was chosen by an earlier, anonymous participant, who was told only to pick any split they liked and was given no incentive bearing on the later subject's choice; that choice is likewise sealed in an envelope on the table.
- Version C: the composition was chosen by the experimenters, who state that it is not all one colour and that they had no reason to favour any particular split; again, sealed in an envelope.

In all three versions the subject may also pay, before committing, to open the envelope. Opening it reveals the composition. It does not alter the urns, the bet already on offer, or the payoffs.

The dialogue is between two decision theorists of equal ability who disagree about what these three versions show. The first holds that the difference between a known one-in-three and an unknown split is a real difference in the subject's epistemic situation, not merely a difference in how the same situation has been described, and that in some cases a single precise probability is simply not there to be had. The second holds that every appearance of a distinct attitude toward ambiguity dissolves under inspection: it is either a failure to complete an elementary inference, or ordinary probabilistic reasoning about a chooser whose motives have been only partially described, or a confusion between the chances in the world and the wording on the page.

Please observe the following:

1. Roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words, in alternating turns of substantial length, under plain labels — either A and B or two invented names — each preceded by a single line describing the speaker.
2. All three versions must be argued over. The two speakers should disagree about whether a theory can coherently license one pattern of choices in A and a different pattern in B and C. Make them confront the question of whether Version A genuinely collapses into a known one-in-three chance of red, and what each theory owes us if subjects nonetheless treat A the way they treat C.
3. Early in the exchange, one of them should work out what the two bets on the Unknown Urn — the red bet together with the matching black bet — are jointly worth, and what that does to the usual story about hedging. Get this arithmetic right; it constrains everything that follows.
4. One exchange must turn on the envelope: what each theory predicts a rational subject would pay to open it, and whether a positive price for information that cannot change the decision is a symptom of incoherence or evidence for something else. Each speaker should say what their view implies about the price, not merely gesture at the issue.
5. One character should press the dynamic-consistency worry — that ambiguity-averse decision rules can be exploited when choices are made in sequence — and the other should reply in a way that keeps the sequential case distinct from the static one. Let the reply be strong; do not let it concede more than it must.
6. The defender of ambiguity must be explicit about which decision rule their character applies once beliefs are imprecise, and must meet the objection that this choice is arbitrary among the alternatives available to someone in that epistemic position.
7. The sceptic must answer the charge that their precise distribution over compositions in Version C is manufactured by a symmetry principle that the evidence does not license, and must say what they think does license it.
8. Neither side may be a strawman, and neither may win outright. Each should land at least one point the other cannot fully parry. Do not resolve the dialogue by agreeing that both are right about different things; end instead with each speaker stating, in one or two sentences, the single observation that would most change their mind.
9. Write in a register a curious general reader can follow, but do not sand off the technical content. It is wanted that the characters speak plainly of compound lotteries, sets of priors, dominance, and the sure-thing principle.
10. No citations, no reference list, no suggested further reading, no publication years or page numbers. If you name a real argument or a real figure, do so only where the name carries weight.

Deliver the finished dialogue on its own, without a preface from me and without an afterword explaining what it was meant to show.
