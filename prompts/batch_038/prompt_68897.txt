I spent thirty-one years in a geochemistry laboratory, most of them running isotope-dilution mass spectrometry, and I have now been retired for four. Retirement has not left me with more golf and less to think about, as I had assumed it would: I chair a seven-member research-integrity panel at a small university a short drive from my house, and I also sit on the board of a regional land trust. Both are bodies that reach conclusions by counting hands and then write down reasons for them, and it is the second half of that sequence that has come to worry me. In the laboratory we were trained to record raw counts before combining them, and never to settle on an averaging scheme after seeing the data; my panel does something closer to the reverse, in that we have never specified in advance how seven members' individual findings are to be turned into the institution's finding, and last term we discovered that the two most obvious ways of doing this give opposite answers on the same ballot. A former colleague who teaches political theory tells me the situation has a name — the discursive dilemma, or doctrinal paradox — and that an impossibility result stands behind it, to the effect that one cannot simultaneously have: an answer for every admissible pattern of individual views, an internally consistent set of institutional answers, equal weight for each member, and a rule that does not quietly install a hierarchy among the propositions being voted on. I should like to know whether that is a fair paraphrase, and if so which of the four a panel of seven that meets quarterly ought to give up and at what price. Please answer as a professional philosopher who works on social choice and collective agency, think the problem through carefully, and begin with a short section of analysis intended for my eyes only — not for the board — in which you set out the candidate rules and their trade-offs before committing to a recommendation.

The case that started this is set out below, with names replaced by letters. Our governing policy provides that a finding of research misconduct requires all three of the following: P1, that the image in Figure 3 was altered; P2, that the alteration was deliberate rather than careless; P3, that the alteration is material to the paper's central claim. I vote along with the others.

| Member | P1: altered | P2: deliberate | P3: material | Would affirm the finding outright |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| B | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| C | yes | yes | no | no |
| D | yes | yes | no | no |
| E | yes | no | yes | no |
| F | no | yes | yes | no |
| G | no | no | no | no |

Five of us affirm P1, five affirm P2, four affirm P3 — and only two would affirm the finding itself, five voting against it. Counting premise by premise we find misconduct; counting the finding directly we do not. Two complications make our version nastier than the textbook one. First, member F's row looks incoherent on its face — no alteration, yet deliberate and material — but F maintains that *if* the alteration occurred it was deliberate, and our ballot form puts each proposition separately; tell me whether my procedure should admit such conditional votes on a premise a member believes false, and what their presence does to the standing of the reasons we publish. Second, the policy contains an alternative route: a finding may also rest on reckless disregard for the integrity of the data rather than on deliberateness, and three of the five who reject P2 would accept that alternative. There is therefore more than one road to the same conclusion, and I suspect a road that a majority endorses at every step may still be a road that no majority endorses as a whole.

What I need is not an essay but a procedure my panel secretary can run without having read anything on the subject, and that I could hand without embarrassment to the retired schoolteacher and the county surveyor who make up the rest of the board. Write it as an ordered sequence of steps with explicit decision points, saying at each step what counts as a sufficient answer, where the sequence terminates in a finding, where it terminates in no finding, and where it must hand the question back to the members because what remains is a matter of judgment or of institutional policy rather than of counting. Specify how abstentions, recusals, and ties enter the premise tallies; and specify whether members are to vote sincerely on each premise or may vote a premise against their own belief in order to secure the conclusion they favour — if the latter is a live risk, say how the procedure detects or discourages it, and if it cannot, say so plainly rather than papering over it. Fix what must be settled before the meeting rather than during it, since choosing the aggregation rule after seeing the votes strikes me as the same species of offence as choosing the baseline after seeing the chromatogram. Then run your procedure on three cases: the ballot above; a variant in which the panel rehearing the matter next year has one different member and the pattern flips; and at least one case in which the rule you recommend forces my panel to publish a written rationale that a majority of its own members do not accept. I expect that last outcome may be unavoidable, and I would far rather see it confronted than designed away. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do — which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the clothes of arithmetic — and, if you do recommend a standing rule, tell me how I should explain its cost to the members who will lose because of it.

Please do not send me a bibliography or a reading list; I have no way of checking references and no wish to pretend otherwise. If you invoke a theorem, state it in your own words together with the exact conditions it requires, and if you are unsure of an attribution, say so rather than naming a paper. Keep jargon out of the procedure itself, though you may attach a half-page technical note for me at the end. When you are finished, put your recommendation in machine-readable form on the very last line, in exactly this shape: \answer{premise-based}, \answer{conclusion-based}, \answer{sequential-priority}, or \answer{hybrid: four words or fewer} — naming the standing rule you would have my panel adopt by default, in advance, before it knows what any ballot will look like.
