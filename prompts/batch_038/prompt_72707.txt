I am auditing a graduate seminar on the history of the public sphere at a small institute in northern Europe; my own training is in logic and philosophy of language, which I suspect is why I keep reading this material too formally. This week we are working through Kierkegaard's book-length review from 1846, the one usually printed in English under the title *Two Ages*, and I have been assigned the second half, where he stops discussing the novel and turns on the present age itself. My presentation is on Friday and I am stuck on one paragraph that the rest of the argument seems to depend on. Two practical constraints on your reply: I have no library access this week and I am not after secondary literature, so please do not recommend readings or reconstruct what some commentator has said about the passage — I want your own reconstruction of the argument. And because I am working from a loaned copy, I have not transcribed the translator's wording. What follows is my own close rendering, in which I preserved his punctuation, his dashes and his habit of starting a sentence only to abandon it, but substituted my own vocabulary throughout. Read it as the shape of the reasoning rather than as a translation to be corrected, and please do not spend time warning me where I may have flattened the Danish.

The present age is an age of understanding and of reflection, essentially passionless, flaring up momentarily in something like passion and then settling back into a shrewd indolence. In such an age the individual does not belong to a generation, or to a people, or to a community — for these are things one must be present for, and able to act within — but instead to an abstraction, the public, which is a something that is not. For what is the public? It is not a people, since a people is a body of individuals standing to one another in concrete relations; it is not a generation, not a congregation, not an association, not a society; it is a phantom, and this phantom is what the press has made possible. Consider: never before have so many who have no dealings with one another, who will never meet, who do not live in the same town, been able to agree about something at the same moment; each sits alone in his room, reads the same sheet, forms an opinion; and then, because these opinions are counted rather than expressed, a totality arises — and this totality is the public. No one who belongs to it is responsible for it, and yet everyone speaks in its name. Leveling is the work of this abstraction; but leveling is not, as one might suppose, the destruction of the outstanding individual by an envious crowd — envy is only the negative, the traveling companion — leveling is rather the process by which the categories themselves, all the distinctions by which an age once organized its life, are dissolved into a numerical equality in which no one is anything in particular and yet everyone is included. And here is the strange part: leveling, taken religiously, is a divine discipline, for only when the individual has been stripped of every support — his estate, his town, his party, his generation — can he stand as the single individual; the same movement that produces the public can therefore, in a different relation, produce the individual.

Five things I cannot resolve on my own. First, the ontology: when he calls the public a something that is not, is that a metaphysical claim or a diagnostic one, and what test is he applying when he runs through the list of things the public is not — co-presence of bodies, shared action, or shared responsibility? I want to know which of those does the actual excluding work. Second, the direction of explanation: leveling is described as the work of the abstraction, yet the abstraction is itself produced by the press, so I cannot tell whether the public is the agent of leveling or its residue, and the sentence structure seems to allow both. Third, the status of the press: is it essential, in the sense that the public is conceptually impossible without an institution that delivers the same content simultaneously to dispersed readers, or merely the instrument that happened to arrive first, such that telegraphy, mass balloting, or opinion polling would do the same job? Fourth, and hardest for me: how can one and the same movement produce both the public and the single individual? Is the difference located in the movement or only in the individual's relation to it, and is he making a quasi-Hegelian point about a negative moment that must be passed through — in which case the religious consolation would seem to cancel the social diagnosis — or something structurally different? Please also tell me how this paragraph relates to his much later insistence that the crowd is untruth, since my seminar partner thinks the public and the crowd are the same object and I think he is careful to keep them apart.

Finally, the reason I care, and the part where I most need pushback rather than encouragement. My own project asks whether this framework survives contact with an algorithmically personalized feed, and the complication is exact: in his picture the unity of the public depends on everyone reading the same sheet in the same hour and agreeing numerically, whereas in mine no two users are shown the same thing at all — the aggregation is behavioral and individualized, the shared object has vanished — and yet the sense of an opinion climate, of being counted, of speaking in the name of a totality for which no one is responsible, is if anything stronger. So I need you to tell me which feature in the passage is load-bearing: the distance between the members, the abstraction of their unity, the counting rather than expressing, or the passionlessness. Depending on the answer, my case is either a textbook instance of the public with the same sheet replaced by a shared statistical tendency, or it is something his vocabulary has no room for — a totality with no shared content, which I do not think 1846 gives him the resources to describe. If it is the latter, I would like your proposal for what the missing category would have to be, and what work it would have to do that the public no longer does.
