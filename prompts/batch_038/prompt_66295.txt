So — I've been asked to give the opening statement at a law school workshop on migration and the welfare state. Twenty minutes, then a room full of people who mostly disagree with me. Before I write it I want to pin down what I actually hold and find out whether it hangs together. I'm not after a survey of the debate, and definitely not a reading list. I want an audit of the package below.

Here's the package:

(1) The right to migrate is grounded in freedom of association — but the association that matters is the would-be migrant's and the residents who want to admit them, not the state's. States are gatekeepers, not parties to the association.

(2) So I reject the Wellman-style move: legitimate states are associations, individuals have freedom of association, therefore states have a right to exclude. I think that inference is just bad. Groups don't inherit their members' associational prerogatives, any more than a university inherits its faculty's right to refuse to talk to the press.

(3) At the same time I do think there are real special obligations to compatriots. Not instrumental, not cosmopolitanism with extra steps. They arise from sharing a cooperative scheme adn they are weighty.

(4) But I reject the contribution/reciprocity argument for exclusion. People who pay taxes don't thereby acquire a quasi-property stake in the institutions they fund. The welfare state isn't a club and citizens aren't shareholders.

(5) Nevertheless I think a state may impose a residency requirement — call it five years — before a newcomer is eligible for means-tested transfers. I usually gloss this with a distinction I like, between the border gate and the membership gate: states should be tightly constrained about who they let in, and much freer about what they attach to becoming a full member.

(6) I also hold something like Carens's firewall: immigration status must not gate access to schools, emergency care, labour standards enforcement, or the courts.

(7) Exclusion for the sake of cultural preservation is illegitimate. A state may not keep people out to protect a way of life, and I dont think the social-trust versions of that argument are anything better dressed up.

(8) Integration requirements — language and civics testing — are permissible for permanent residents. Not for citizens.

(9) Claims strengthen with time and with ties: the longer someone has been here, the less the state may do to them. And the kids of irregular migrants who grew up here get regularized automatically — no application, no discretion.

What I want from you:

- Which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? Rank the tensions by severity instead of giving me a flat list, and for each one say which commitment has to give and what the cheapest revision is that saves the most of the rest.
- I expect some of these pairs only look bad, and I'd much rather know which ones than have all nine flagged. So say explicitly where a tension dissolves under inspection, and why it dissolves — what distinction does the work.
- Is the border gate / membership gate distinction in (5) doing any real work, or is it a label I've put on the very thing I said I reject in (4)? If it can be made to do work, tell me what it would have to amount to, and whether (6) survives it.
- Given the whole package, what's the single question the most hostile person in that room should open with? I want to know the shape of the attack before I walk in.

I genuinely don't know where the weak link is, so don't treat anything on the list as obviously the problem or as obviously fine.
