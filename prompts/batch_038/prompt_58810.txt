The dispute over whether national character names anything real has been running for three-quarters of a century, and I have come to think neither side has ever engaged the other's actual claim. The lineage is familiar: the wartime culture-at-a-distance studies, Ruth Benedict's portrait of Japan produced for the Office of War Information without her having set foot there, and the mid-century attempt to define national character as the modal personality of a sociocultural system; then the collapse of that program under charges that it reified, that it mistook the ideology of an educated elite for a whole people's psychology, that it froze a moment of acute social dislocation into a permanent norm, and that its evidence — literature, film, press, émigré interviews — could be read to confirm anything. On the other side, contemporary defenders point to large cross-national administrations of translated Big Five instruments, to aggregate trait profiles that replicate across independent samples, and to stereotype-accuracy research arguing that many widely held group beliefs sit closer to criterion measures than the pejorative label implies. They also insist that the standard dismissal — within-nation variance dwarfs between-nation variance — is a non sequitur, since the same holds of height and sex, and nobody concludes that sex differences in height are unreal.

Neither move lands. My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than accepted, is that national character is being asked to carry three claims with quite different evidential fortunes, rarely separated: a distributional claim, that the central tendency of some psychological property differs across populations by enough relative to within-population spread to license fallible inference about individuals; a configural claim, that the traits cohere into a distinctive pattern expressive of a shared form of life, over and above a list of mean differences; and a constitutive claim, that individuals have the property in virtue of group membership, so that the group enters irreducibly into the explanation. My suspicion is that skeptics refuted the constitutive claim and largely the configural one, then treated this as refuting the distributional claim; defenders establish the distributional claim, or something near it, then treat that as vindicating the configural one. Each keeps winning a fight the other never lost. A second suspicion: what is genuinely contested may not be psychology but the epistemology of level-crossing inference — from aggregate distributions to expectations about particular people — conducted under asymmetric moral cost, which would explain why measurement invariance, reference-group effects in self-report, and the choice of reference class carry more argumentative weight than anyone concedes.

Diagnose the disagreement. Is it substantive; partly verbal, over character, culture, and stereotype; a collision of evidential standards; two questions sharing one name; or bedrock disagreement about what one may permissibly infer and utter? Engage what each side asserts, not the history of the quarrel. Then state what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction, a measurement result, a formal theorem about aggregation, a reframing, or a demonstration that the question was always ill-posed — or argue that none should be expected, and why. Offer no generalizations about any particular nation or people, and no bibliography.
