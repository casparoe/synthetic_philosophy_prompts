I have spent the last year covering disputes over models — river-basin flood forecasting, fishery stock assessments, the arguments about how much of a pandemic curve is policy and how much is artifact — and in nearly every one of them a defender says some version of the same thing: of course the assumptions are false, that is what assumptions are for, and the more of a stretch they are, the more the model must be doing right. The claim is usually credited to Friedman's 1953 methodology essay, and the disparaging label attached to it comes from one of his antagonists. My editors want a column that states the defense better than my sources do, so I sat down and reconstructed it as an argument. The reconstruction is what troubles me, because it proves considerably more than anyone I interviewed would accept.

(1) A model is an instrument for generating predictions about a target system, and the only thing one is entitled to ask of an instrument is that it perform: that its predictions come out right over the range of cases it is put to.
(2) An assumption can bear on a model only through the predictions it helps generate, so its truth or falsity matters only insofar as it changes those predictions.
(3) Hence if two assumptions generate the same predictions over every case a model is put to, a model containing one is exactly as good as a model containing the other, and no fact about which of them is true can be a reason to prefer either.
(4) The range of cases any model is put to is finite: some finite body of data already in hand, plus whatever further cases are later brought to it.
(5) Given any assumption and any finite set of predictions, one can write down an assumption as contrary to fact as one pleases that reproduces those predictions exactly.
(6) Therefore every model, however carefully its assumptions were drawn from the system it depicts, has a rival that is at least as good and as unrealistic as we like; realism of assumptions is not a virtue a model can possess.
(7) And the strong form follows: since unrealisticness costs nothing while simplicity and breadth are worth having, the more unrealistic a model's assumptions, the better the model.

Here is the case that convinced me of (3). Two models of how traffic distributes itself among a city's river crossings. The first assumes every driver solves for the journey that minimizes their own travel time given everyone else's choices, so flows settle at equilibrium — a claim about human cognition that no traffic psychologist would sign. The second assumes drivers imitate whichever neighbours arrived earliest and adjust slowly. Both reproduce eight years of measured link flows within instrument error. Over everything the models have been asked, they are on a par, and the first is by far the more unrealistic. When I point out that the city is now proposing a toll on the central span and that the two models then diverge sharply, I have a reply ready: the toll scenario was not among the cases either model had been put to, so (3) was satisfied and remains satisfied; the toll is merely a further case, and we use whichever model survives it, which is exactly what (1) instructs.

I can defend each step in a room and I cannot print (6). (7) is the sentence I would have to publish, and I know it is false, because I have watched the same defence waved at a stock assessment that collapsed two seasons later. I would rather keep (1) and (2) than any other premise: they seem to me an accurate description of how my sources actually work.

Please give me: (a) the step at which the reasoning first fails, or, if you judge the argument valid with a false premise, the premise you would surrender; (b) why that step looked acceptable, and specifically what it trades on that the preceding steps do not supply; (c) the cost of rejecting each of (1), (2), and (3), since I want to give up as little as possible; (d) whether your diagnosis damns only (7) or also the modest claim my sources and I share — that a model is not made worse by an assumption merely because that assumption is false; and (e) whether my reply about the toll works.

End with a single line of the form \answer{N}, where N is the number of the first step that fails, or of the premise you would surrender if you hold the argument to be valid.
