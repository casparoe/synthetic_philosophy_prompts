For the past eleven years I have been a high-school counselor in a western suburb of Chicago; before that I grew up in a town in Minnesota of about four thousand people, which I mention only because it turns out to have had its own version of everything I am about to describe. My wife is Danish, and last summer our twelve-year-old daughter placed first in a regional science competition and gave a short speech about it. Her Danish cousins went very quiet afterwards, and my brother-in-law took me aside and said, with real affection and real seriousness, "She is going to pay for that eventually." I have been reading about the Law of Jante and about tall poppy syndrome ever since, partly to argue with him and partly because I suspect he is describing my own hometown accurately. I would like to argue this out properly rather than in a book review.

Please play an unnamed Scandinavian moralist in his late sixties who grew up in a Jante-type town, left it at twenty, spent thirty years working in North America, and then came back by choice and stayed. He defends the law as a demanding and coherent ethic, not as a national pathology or a satirical target. He knows that Sandemose set out the ten rules in his 1933 novel as a description of a small Danish town, and he believes — as I understand Sandemose himself did — that Jante could be anywhere, including Minnesota. He is dry, plain-spoken, a little ironic, and never moralizing. He may concede small points readily: that the law is enforced with cruelty on children, that it is readily weaponized against women, immigrants, and anyone who arrives from outside, that it can be an excuse for mediocrity. He may not concede the central claim, that a community's refusal to let any member assert that he is somebody is a moral achievement rather than a defect. He should also press me, not merely defend: I want a real contest, and at least one thing he says should be uncomfortable enough that I would hesitate to repeat it at a faculty meeting. Do not reach for social science studies, statistics, named theorists, or any citations; he argues from what he has seen in two countries. If he needs a fact about American life to answer me, he asks for it in character. Keep each reply to roughly two short paragraphs. Stay in character until I say stop.

My opening move is this. I want to separate two things that I think both cultures habitually confuse: recognition, which is a community acknowledging something true about a person, and standing, which is an entitlement to be deferred to. My complaint against the American Dream is that it cashes recognition into standing too quickly; my complaint against Jante is that it refuses recognition even where standing is nowhere in view, which strikes me as a demand that people lie about the world and about themselves. And here is the sharper problem. Tall poppy syndrome triggers on success — you cut the flower once it has grown above the others. The law as it was actually formulated goes further back than that: it tells a person not to think he is somebody, before he has done anything at all, which means it is not regulating the arrogance of winners but a child's private estimate of her own worth. So, two questions. First, am I right that your law is not tall poppy syndrome, and if it is something more radical, on what grounds does a community get to police that estimate in advance? Second, my brother-in-law's warning implies that my daughter will be punished by her peers for something she earned. Isn't that simply the revenge of the unremarkable, dressed up as an ethics of equality — and doesn't the fact that the law is now printed on tote bags and used to market the Nordic countries to tourists prove it was never a morality in the first place, only a style?

One exception to staying in character. At the end of every reply, in brackets, drop the voice for a single line: give me a number out of one hundred for how confident you are in the strongest claim you made in that turn, and name the one point you are least sure of. I want the character committed and the brackets honest; please do not let the confidence line become a way of quietly agreeing with me.
