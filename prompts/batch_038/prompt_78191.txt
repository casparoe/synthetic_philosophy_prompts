For the past seven years I have coordinated a volunteer tutoring program in our county that works with adults who left school early. Most of our funding comes from a single county grant, and this year the grant office has begun requiring an annual scorecard. My own training is in warehouse logistics rather than anything scholarly, but I have been reading on the problem of measurement for about two years now, and I have arrived at a conclusion that I intend to put in writing to the funder. Before I do, I would like it examined by someone who knows the material better than I do.

I call my conclusion the measurability tax. It begins from Goodhart's law — the result established in the 1970s by a British economist studying monetary targets, that a measure which once carried useful information stops carrying it as soon as it is adopted as a target. My reading of the more recent formal work is that there is a proof to the effect that no proxy can be optimal: whichever stand-in measure you nominate, the world can be arranged so that pushing on it moves you away from what you actually wanted. If that is right, the tax ought to be proportional to precision. The more exactly a thing can be counted, the harder it can be pushed, and therefore the more certainly pushing it diverges from the aim. A quantity that cannot be counted at all cannot be optimized, and so cannot be corrupted.

Our own experience appears to confirm this. Two years ago we added a twelve-month retention rate to our reporting. It climbed from 61 percent to 84 percent, and the county was pleased. I have since learned that the improvement came largely from tutors quietly declining to enroll people with unstable housing — precisely the group the program exists to serve. Reading level, as measured by a commercial test, has been flat throughout.

It seems to follow that the honest scorecard for a program like ours contains no numbers. What I propose to submit instead is a set of written case accounts: a page or two about an individual learner and an individual tutor, selected by the tutors themselves, describing what changed over the year. My reasoning is that a narrative is the one reporting format exempt from the tax, since there is nothing in it to maximize, and exempt as well from the sort of cheating the law is usually illustrated with, since a story cannot be gamed the way a completion rate can.

Two questions. Is the inference from the proof to the measurability tax sound, and if it fails, where precisely does it fail? And given your answer, what should I tell the county — I am after a course of action I could carry out, not a summary of the difficulties.
