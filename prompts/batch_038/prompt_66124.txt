ok so i'm working through Clarke's Boyle lectures for 1704 — *A Demonstration of the Being and Attributes of God* — with a couple of friends, and I've hit a wall on Proposition III. Here's the stretch I can't parse (verbatim except that I've cut his marginal page cross-references and a Latin footnote from Newton):

> Now, to be self-existent is not to be produced by itself; for that is an express contradiction. But it is, (which is the only idea we can frame of self-existence; and without which, the word seems to have no signification at all;) it is, I say, to exist by an absolute necessity originally in the nature of the thing itself: And this necessity must be antecedent; not, indeed, in time, to the existence of the being itself, because that is eternal; but it must be antecedent in the natural order of our ideas, to our supposition of its being; that is, this necessity must not barely be consequent upon our supposition of the existence of such a being; (for then it would not be a necessity absolutely such in itself, nor be the ground or foundation of the existence of any thing, being on the contrary only a consequent of it;) but it must antecedently force itself upon us, whether we will or no, even when we are endeavouring to suppose that no such being exists. For example: when we are endeavouring to suppose, that there is no being in the universe that exists necessarily, we always find in our minds, we always find in our minds, I say, some ideas, as of infinity and eternity; which to remove, that is, to suppose that there is no being, no substance in the universe, to which these attributes or modes of existence are necessarily inherent, is a contradiction in the very terms. For modes and attributes exist only by the existence of the substance to which they belong. Now, he that can suppose eternity and immensity (and consequently the substance by whose existence these modes or attributes exist,) removed out of the universe, may, if he please, as easily remove the relation of equality between twice two and four.
>
> To suppose any part of space removed, is to suppose it removed from and out of itself: and to suppose the whole to be taken away, is supposing it to be taken away from itself, that is, to be taken away while it still remains; which is a contradiction in terms. There is no obscurity in this argument but what arises to those who think immense space to be absolutely nothing: which notion is itself likewise an express contradiction; for nothing is that which has no properties or modes whatsoever; that is to say, it is that of which nothing can truly be affirmed, and of which every thing can truly be denied; which is not the case of immensity or space.

Three things I'm stuck on, in increasing order of how much they annoy me.

1. The antecedent/consequent necessity business. I think I get the negative half: he's ruling out the trivial conditional — suppose the thing exists and of course its existing follows, but that grounds nothing. Fine. The problem is the positive gloss. What he says an antecedent necessity *is*, is something that "must antecedently force itself upon us, whether we will or no, even when we are endeavouring to suppose that no such being exists." That's a sentence about what happens in my head. Is it just period loose talk for a modal point — there's a real, non-conditional necessity in the nature of the thing, and the mind-talk is only a way of exhibiting it — or is the psychological premise actually load bearing? If its load bearing, the inference seems to run from "I can't think away immensity" to "it's impossible that there be no necessarily existing substance," and I want to know whether Clarke takes that step to be licensed by something he says, or just assumes it.

2. How the space material gets him a being at all. Up to "For modes and attributes exist only by the existence of the substance to which they belong" he's talking about immensity and eternity; one clause later he's got a substance. So what is that premise exactly — a general claim about inherence, that no mode or attribute can be instantiated without a subject that has it? Has he argued for it anywhere in the passage, or is it picked up from the Newton footnote I deleted? And is "immensity" here just space, or space-plus-something? He seems to alternate between "immensity" and "space" and I can't tell whether that alternation is doing any work or is just his idiom.

3. The one that actually keeps me up: the title page advertises this as an answer to Hobbes, Spinoza and their followers, but as far as I can tell every claim in the quoted stretch is one Spinoza would sign — a necessarily existing, eternal, infinite, unique substance. So does Proposition III have any anti-Spinozist content, or is it a lemma both sides accept, with the real fight deferred to the later propositions on intelligence and free agency? If it's the latter that changes how I'd read all the "self-existence" talk, and I suspect I'm missing something in this passage that already blocks Spinoza.

For each of the three: give me the reading you think is correct, then the strongest alternative reading, and say what tips the balance between them. I don't want a reading list or a tour of the scholarship — I have the text in front of me, I want help with what it's doing. And be straight about where your reconstruction is shakiest: which of the three questions is the one you'd be least surprised to learn you got wrong, and what argument or consideration — a distinction I've glossed over, a different way of taking "antecedent in the order of our ideas," something about how Clarke is using 'nothing' — would move you to the other verdict?
