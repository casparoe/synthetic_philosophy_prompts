I'm building one session for a grad seminar that's split down the middle between philosophy students and film students, and I want to hang it on Memento. My working thesis, which I haven't said out loud yet because I suspect it's too cute, is that Leonard Shelby is basically an updateless agent. I'd like you to pressure-test that and then, if it survives, write the actual reading I could hand the class.

Quick gloss so you don't have to assume anybody's read the rationalist stuff: an updateless decision theory says don't pick your action by conditionalizing on whatever you happen to have observed — pick your whole policy once, from a standpoint before you know which observations you'll get. The usual motivations are Newcomb cases where the predictor knows your disposition, Counterfactual Mugging, and the absent-minded driver problem from Piccione and Rubinstein, where the guy can't remember which of two exits he's at and does better by fixing his plan when he gets on the highway than by reasoning afresh at each exit. Leonard is that driver with the amnesia turned all the way up: he has to write the policy down in advance — notes, polaroids, tattoos — because the version of him that acts won't be the version that decided, and won't remember the deciding.

What I want:

1. Is the comparison doing real work, or is it just a pun on imperfect recall? Where does the film actually dramatize the updateless structure — the gap between the planning standpoint and the action standpoint, the fact that a policy that was right when you chose it looks crazy from inside the moment? Point me at specific scenes, described rather than quoted; I'll rewatch to check and I don't want invented dialogue.

2. The ending is where I think it gets genuinely interesting and also where I might be wrong. After Teddy lays it all out — Sammy Jankis, the year-old killing, the whole loop — Leonard burns the photographs and then arranges things, the license plate, the annotation on Teddy's polaroid, so that his next, ignorant self will hunt Teddy down. That isn't just binding yourself, it's manufacturing the evidence your bound self will act on. Is that still updatelessness, or is it a seperate thing: a Ulysses who doesn't merely tie himself to the mast but pays the crew to lie to him about where the ship is? Does that distinction have any decision-theoretic content, or is it purely a moral one? And does the film endorse what he does, indict it, or refuse to take a side?

3. The reverse chronology. Does the form itself hand the viewer something like an updateless standpoint — we keep arriving at scenes already knowing what the character did and not why — or am I reaching for a symmetry that isn't there?

4. Where it breaks. My worry: Leonard's problem is that he can't retain observations at all, which sounds like a flat-out cognitive defect rather than the situation that motivates updatelessness. In Newcomb-type cases the trouble is that your evidence is entangled with your own decision procedure; in Counterfactual Mugging it's that you get scored on the branch you're not in. Neither thing is happening in Memento, is it? If not, is the honest conclusion that the film is about something else entirely — self-deception, narrative identity, agency without memory — and I should drop the frame?

Then close with what Memento contributes to the updatelessness debate that the formal write-ups can't: something a model of an agent choosing its policy behind a veil of ignorance leaves out, that a film about a man doing exactly that, badly and on purpose, makes visible.

Aim for maybe 1500 words. No citations, no bibliography, and don't bother re-explaining UDT past my gloss. I care about whether the comparison survives contact, not about being told it's interesting.
