For four years I have worked on a programmable superconducting-qubit simulator whose main use, as far as my part of the collaboration goes, is Loschmidt echoes. The protocol is simple to state: prepare ten qubits in a product state; evolve them under a deliberately non-integrable Hamiltonian so that initially local information spreads across the whole array; then apply the time-reversed evolution for the same duration and measure how much of the initial state comes back. With flawless control the echo would be complete regardless of how long we scrambled. With realistic control error it is not, and the shortfall grows with the scrambling time. Along the way we track the entanglement entropy of a half-array, which climbs steeply during the forward evolution and then, during the reversal, turns over and comes back down — sometimes most of the way, sometimes not much at all.

Last spring our institution's press office wrote a release about one of these runs. I did not write it and I did not approve it, but my name was on the paper, and its headline said, in effect, that we had reversed the arrow of time. Physicists were irritated in the ordinary way. What unsettled me more were two letters from philosophers of physics, in different traditions, saying substantially opposite things: one that the release was confused because our experiment is a small local entropy decrease purchased at enormous global cost, and therefore not a reversal of anything; the other that the release was confused for the opposite reason, because there is no single global quantity that could be reversed in the first place, and our own papers write as though there were. I have been arguing with both of them by email ever since, and in the autumn I am moderating a session at an interdisciplinary workshop on exactly this question. My sympathies have moved at least twice. Before I have to say anything in public, I want to see both positions argued at full strength, by people considerably better at them than I am, in front of my own data.

So: write a dialogue between two such people, standing in the control room at the end of a run, looking at the same two panels on the same screen — the echo-fidelity curve and the half-array entanglement entropy, frozen at the moment the entropy turns over.

The first is a mathematical physicist who holds that the arrow of time is an objective, mind-independent feature of this universe's history. On her account, the asymmetry is grounded in a special, very low-entropy condition at the beginning of the universe, together with a statistical postulate over the microstates compatible with a given macrostate; given that pair, entropy increase follows with overwhelming probability for systems of any interesting size. She holds that coarse-graining is not up to us in any sense that threatens the objectivity of the result: the macrovariables that matter are the ones the dynamics makes stable, robustly measurable, and controllable, and the Second Law comes out the same for every such choice anyone has ever managed to make. She regards the Loschmidt and recurrence objections as answered, and she regards our experiment as no reversal of anything — a small, engineered, temporary entropy decrease inside a device whose cryostat, control electronics, amplifiers, and measurement records are dumping entropy elsewhere, in quantities that dwarf it.

The second is a philosopher of physics who holds that there is no well-defined quantity called the entropy of the universe, and that treating one as though there were is the source of most of the confusion. On his account, entropy is always indexed to a description — to a choice of which degrees of freedom count as the system and which as irrelevant — and the Second Law is a theorem about what an embedded subsystem with limited, macroscopic access to its surroundings can predict, control, and record, not a law about the world viewed from nowhere. He will press the point that matters most in our room: a closed quantum system evolving unitarily has a constant von Neumann entropy, so whatever is rising on our second panel is the entanglement entropy of a subsystem, which depends on how the Hilbert space is factored into a half-array and a rest, and that factorization is a piece of our description rather than a piece of the apparatus. On his view the arrow is not reversed by our experiment either, but for a different reason: our experiment shows that arrows attach to descriptions, and that a better-chosen description of the very same run has no arrow in it at all.

Both must be equally good. Neither may be permitted a weak premise that only a strawman would hold, and neither may win by the other's carelessness. I want real back-and-forth: each must respond to the distinction the other has just drawn, not to a paraphrase of it prepared in advance. Specifically, the dialogue should include at least two places where a character, having been hit by an objection they did not expect, reformulates their own view so as to survive it and says what they are giving up in the reformulation; and at least one place where they realize that a stretch of the argument was verbal — that they were using one of the words "entropy," "closed," "reversed," or "objective" in two different senses — and repair it explicitly rather than letting the reader spot it.

It should pass through, without turning into a checklist: what, if anything, the two panels on the screen have to do with the arrow of time; whether the cosmological low-entropy condition is a law, a contingent boundary condition, or an explanatory surcharge; whether the perspectival account can avoid making the Second Law either trivially true or a claim about our ignorance alone; what explains the asymmetry of records and memory, and whether either account can explain it without already presupposing the asymmetry; the fluctuation worry, in which a universe at equilibrium makes it overwhelmingly likely that we are a brief local dip rather than the descendants of a special beginning; the accounting of what our reversal cost, including the erasure of the information the controller had to acquire; and finally, whether there is any conceivable observation, in this room or in principle, on which the two accounts diverge — with each of them committing to an answer rather than retreating into agreement about all the data.

Do not adjudicate. No narrator, no closing summary, no third character who sees both sides clearly, and no last line in which one of them is persuaded. Let it end with the disagreement sharper and better located than it was at the start, and let the reader be able to say which of the two they would have to become to keep working in this lab. Keep it between roughly 1,800 and 2,600 words. Characters may use standard terminology and the customary historical names for the well-known objections, and may write out a short expression where it genuinely helps; they must not cite or attribute claims to particular books, papers, or living authors, and there should be no reference list of any kind.
