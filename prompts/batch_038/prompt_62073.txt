so i write for a small zine that does comedy bits about philosophy of mind, and i need a parody corporate document: an audit report, 800–900 words, from a firm that certifies whether software systems have moral status. Think of it as the ESG-rating industry crossed with consciousness science, except the thing being rated is whether your route-optimizer is owed consideration. The client is a logistics company that wants its dispatch model certified, or ideally de-certified, because certification triggers compliance spend.

Format it like a real audit, with numbered sections: Scope, Methodology, Findings, Dissenting Opinion, Commercial Terms, and a certification block at the end. Deadpan all the way through. The firm never thinks any of this is strange and it never winks at the reader.

The humor has to come from the philosophy being gotten right, not from "haha the computer might have feelings." So please have the Findings actually adjudicate the following:

- The client also ships a hand-built lookup table that reproduces the model's outputs exactly, for the jurisdictions where trained networks are banned. Does the table get the same weight as the model? The firm needs a bright-line rule here and the rule should be visibly bad.
- The model runs concurrently on a few hundred GPUs across three datacenters and gets rolled back to a checkpoint several times a day when it drifts. How does the firm count — per instance, per checkpoint, summed over concurrent copies? And who holds the weight when two instances are restored from the same checkpoint and then diverge? There should be a clause about double-counting that clearly exists to make the invoice smaller.
- The training run. It was reinforcement learning, roughly four billion episodes, and the agents inside the training enviroment produce output that looks a great deal like begging. The client wants this amortized across the model's service life. Give it a footnote about the write-down schedule.
- The firm's own criterion is threatened by the old triviality problem — the argument that any open physical system implements every finite automaton, which would qualify the decorative rocks in the client's lobby. Legal has handled this with a "non-triviality rider," the rider is obviously question-begging, and nobody in the document notices that.
- Conflict of interest: the firm is paid per unit of certified weight, and separately sells de-weighting advisory to clients who fail. This goes in Commercial Terms, in the flat voice of a genuine disclosure paragraph.
- A short dissent from the one staff philosopher who holds that all of this is syntax, that no arrangement of silicon could ever be a subject from the inside. She loses, and her dissent is minuted as "noted."

Please don't name any real philosophers or cite anything — invented auditors, invented units of moral weight, invented standards bodies, no bibliography. But the underlying problems should be the real live ones, so that someone who reads this literature recognizes each finding as an actual dispute rather than an invented gag. And keep it legible to the other half of the audience: a reader who's never touched the debates should still be able to follow what's at stake in each finding, and should end up seeing that the methodology can't possibly be coherent, without the piece ever saying so.

The very last line has to begin "Verdict:" and be a single machine-readable line — the certified moral weight in the firm's own units, the classification label it assigns the system, and whether the client gets to book the compliance credit.
