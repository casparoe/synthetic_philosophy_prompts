I'm working on a seminar paper for a course on political economy and imperial governance, comparing how late-eighteenth-century British writers on political economy handled the American question, and my chapter is on Adam Smith. I've been going slowly through the colonial discussion in the Wealth of Nations alongside his later treatment of public revenue and the national debt, and I've hit a difficulty I can't resolve on my own. My supervisor has told me twice now that I'm still describing the problem rather than solving it, so I'd like to lay it out as clearly as I can and get a proper conceptual reconstruction of the argument.

Here is the tension as I see it. Smith's whole project is an argument against the direction of industry by government: the mercantile system fails, on his account, because legislators and administrators cannot channel a nation's capital as well as the people whose fortunes depend on it, and the colonial system is the paradigm case of commerce organized for political ends. So I had expected his indictment of empire to be essentially fiscal bookkeeping — Britain spends heavily on defending and administering the American and West Indian possessions, gets very little revenue back, and would be materially richer if it washed its hands of the whole enterprise and simply traded with the former colonies on ordinary terms. What I did not expect, and what my adviser keeps pressing me on, is the constitutional proposal that closes that discussion: that Britain should offer the colonies a voluntary union with seats in the Westminster parliament scaled to what they contribute to imperial revenue, and should be prepared to part company amicably and gradually if the offer is refused. Enlarging the representative machinery of the state across the Atlantic looks like the exact opposite of getting government out of the economy. It looks like building a bigger and more intrusive political union precisely where Smith has just argued that political union has been ruinous — which is why a classmate of mine insists that the union plan is an afterthought, a concession to the American crisis bolted onto a free-trade argument that doesn't need it.

Could you help me work out how the proposal is supposed to follow from the diagnosis, or whether my classmate is right that it doesn't? Specifically: is the damage done by the monopoly of the colony trade in Smith's account really a line item on the imperial balance sheet, or is it something about the structure of British capital, the general rate of profit, and the pace of accumulation, and if so what is the mechanism he is relying on? Is the representation scheme generated by that mechanism, or is it answering a different question — and if a different one, why does a political economist writing about trade think the remedy has to be constitutional? How does his endorsement of navigation restrictions on the ground that the security of the country matters more than its wealth sit with reading him as an opponent of state intervention, and what turns out to be his actual test for when intervention is legitimate? And is his case against empire an argument about the aggregate wealth of the nation at all, or is it fundamentally about which groups inside Britain, and inside the colonies, gain and lose? I don't need quotations, paragraph numbers, or a list of the secondary literature — I have the text and will do that checking myself; what I want is the argument reconstructed in your own words. And if it turns out that I've located the tension in the wrong place, say so plainly and tell me where the real fault line runs.
