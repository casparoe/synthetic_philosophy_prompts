Please take the role of a veteran teacher of mathematical logic who also designs hands-on sessions for teenagers: someone who can make an abstract structural idea land in a room full of people who have never met it before. Think the design problem through carefully, and show me the reasoning behind your choices rather than only the final product.

I am a junior, and for two years I have run a lunchtime club at my high school that we call the Contradiction Society. We meet once a week for forty-five minutes in an ordinary classroom, and attendance is usually about fourteen students in grades ten through twelve. Exactly one of them has heard of Gödel's incompleteness theorem from a video and brings it up every single meeting; none of them has read Hofstadter. Our school collects phones at the start of the day, so whatever you design has to run on a printer, a whiteboard, index cards, pens, and perhaps a pair of dice.

The idea I want them to run into is the one Hofstadter organizes around Escher's Drawing Hands, the lithograph of two hands each in the act of drawing the other into existence, together with the claim that beneath every such tangled hierarchy there must lie an inviolate level: a level that nothing inside the tangle can reach, alter, or describe. I want my members to feel both halves of that. First, that the moment the rules of a system are permitted to make claims about the rules, the levels stop being neatly stacked and start looping back into one another. Second, and more importantly, the dilemma that appears when they try to locate the level that untangles the loop: either it turns out to be reachable and describable from inside after all, in which case it was never really outside, or it is genuinely outside, in which case it cannot be written down, cited in an argument, or used by anyone playing the game, which was the entire reason they wanted it.

A few constraints. Nobody should hear the words self-reference, strange loop, Gödel, or inviolate level until the debrief; the tangle has to be produced by the rules of the activity and not by anything I narrate. No member may be given a role whose job is to explain the point to the others.

Here is what I would like you to produce. Begin by brainstorming at least three genuinely distinct mechanics that could generate this experience — I have vaguely considered a card-numbering scheme and a committee that drafts the rules of its own game, but I am not attached to either. For each, say in a sentence or two what the participants actually do, what wall they hit, and why it might fail with fourteen teenagers in forty-five minutes; then choose one and defend the choice. Next, write that activity up so completely that a classmate who has never heard of any of this could run it from your description alone: the exact text of every card, handout, or sheet I would need to print, the roles and how to assign them, the rules stated precisely, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the moment the loop closes described from the participants' point of view rather than mine. Then give me the debrief, mapping particular moments in the room onto particular parts of the idea, and telling me where Drawing Hands belongs in that map and what the inviolate level turned out to be inside the game. After that: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the student who writes a self-undermining sentence on a card within two minutes and declares victory, the group that plays so cautiously that it never tangles at all, the one who announces that this is just word games — and what I should do in each case. Then state the strongest objection a bright student in that room will raise, and whether he is in fact right. Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by explanation, and tell me what to say so that nobody leaves believing something false: I specifically do not want them convinced that arithmetic is inconsistent, that nothing can be proved, or that every sentence about itself is paradoxical.

If forty-five minutes is genuinely not enough, give me the shorter cut and say exactly what it loses.
