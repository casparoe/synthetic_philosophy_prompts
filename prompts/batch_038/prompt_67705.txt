In RL there's a standard trick for making learning go faster: you add an extra reward term that isn't part of what you actually care about. A bonus for getting closer to the goal, for poking around, whatever. The respectable version of this restricts the bonus to a "potential" form — a fixed function of the state you land in, discounted, minus the same function of the state you left — and there's a theorem saying bonuses of that shape can't change which policies are optimal. So practioners treat the shaped reward as pure scaffolding: it changes how fast the agent learns, not what it ends up learning to do, and you can crank the coefficient up or rip the term out afterwards without touching the target behavior.

Meanwhile the same field constantly talks about reward as the thing the agent wants — agents that seek reward out, that would tamper with their own reward signal if we let them, that we're nervous about wireheading.

What theory of reward does the shaping practice presuppose? My hunch is that it only makes sense if reward is a purely informational, behavior-steering number rather than something with a felt valence. Because if reward were the agent's pleasure or pain, a potential-based bonus would change the agent's experience enormously while leaving its optimal behavior exactly the same, and the theorem's upshot — nothing relevant changed — would be false in precisely the respect an ethicist would care about. So: reconstruct the implicit view, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and tell me whether shaping-reward-as-inert-scaffolding and reward-as-the-thing-the-agent-is-after can both be true at once, or whether the field is quietly equivocating. And if reward really were valence for the agent, what would have to change about how people do shaping — would the invariance theorem stop being the right standard of correctness?
