I run a small university digital repository — scanned manuscripts, some born-digital files, a couple of old websites we captured years ago. Several times a week I have to make the same call: after some event (format migration, re-scan at higher resolution, a checksum mismatch, a disk failure where we restore from backup, a donor emailing a corrected file) is what we now hold still the object we accessioned, or a new object that replaced it? Right now it's whoever on shift's gut feel, and it leaks into the records: we have citations that resolve to something other than what a 2004 reader actually saw, and one incident where we told a donor wed lost the original when all we'd done was migrate it.

I've absorbed more Parfit and four-dimensionalism than is healthy for a person, and this is that debate wearing a lanyard. If the object is wholly present at each moment, then a migration is either the same object with new properties or a replacement, and I need a rule for which. If it's a spacetime worm made of temporal parts, then "the object" is just a bundle I'm choosing, and the real question is what makes a bundle maximal — continuity of bits, of function, of the causal chain from the donor's hands? Annoyingly our identifier layer forces a binary answer even if the metaphysics is graded: one persistent ID per object, and an ID can't point at two things.

So: write me a procedure my staff can run on an event in about ten minutes. None of them have any philosophy, and any verdict of "new object" comes to me to sign off. It should say which picture we're committing to — or how to hold both without the records contradicting each other — and give a determinate same-object / new-version / new-object answer, mark which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which we can drop, flag the steps that are values calls dressed up as procedure (what is a citation even a promise about?), and say where it'll misfire. Then run it on three cases: (1) we migrated 300 TIFFs to JPEG2000 in 2019 and someone's 2004 paper cites the TIFF; (2) our only copy fails a checksum and we restore from a backup made three years earlier; (3) a donor sends a corrected version of a file we hold and says the one we had was never finished.

And be honest with me: if the two views make zero difference to what ends up in the database, say so, and tell me where the difference actually shows up, if anywhere.
