I direct the social-norms portfolio at a mid-sized international NGO. Roughly twice a year our country teams send us six to ten proposals aimed at practices that cause serious harm but are sustained locally by social standing rather than by law or by explicit moral endorsement: an initiation rite that includes the cutting of girls, adolescent marriage arranged by families, and public accusation and violence directed at women suspected of sexual contact outside marriage. Budgets range from about fifty thousand to two million dollars, the practices affect thousands of people, and a badly designed intervention can make things worse by restoring standing to the very practice we are attacking. Our board signs off on each cycle from a written rationale, and our field staff have no training in moral philosophy.

The literature I keep returning to suggests our default approach is mistaken. We fund awareness work and moral persuasion on the assumption that a practice ends once people are convinced it is unjust. The writing on honor-driven moral change points elsewhere: Kwame Anthony Appiah's account of dueling, footbinding, and the British abolition of the slave trade emphasizes how honor is allocated, whether an honorable alternative is actually available, and what it takes for a practice to become something people cannot take seriously; Bernard Williams's suggestion is that practices of this kind are typically not so much refuted as discredited, ending when the social standing that made them something a serious person would do drains away. I want a decision procedure built on that rather than on the persuasion model, and I want it usable by people who have never read either author.

Please write the procedure itself: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; what observable evidence a field team can realistically gather to support each answer; where the procedure terminates in a no-fund or not-yet verdict; and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is evaluative rather than factual — for instance, whether a proposed substitute rite is genuinely a different practice or the same practice with the harm removed, and whose judgment about honor counts. Say explicitly which distinctions from the theory have to survive the simplification and which can be dropped, what a team should do when the evidence at a step is simply unavailable, and where you expect the procedure to misfire.

Then run it on three proposals now in front of us. First, funding an alternative coming-of-age ceremony that preserves the seclusion, the feast, and the public recognition while removing the cutting. Second, a district-level program pairing cash transfers and household debt relief with a collective public commitment from elders to stop arranging marriages for girls under sixteen. Third, a national media campaign — already announced with a named donor, and our largest single line item for the year — that publishes the names of practitioners and lobbies for a new criminal penalty. Give the verdict for each together with the reasoning the board would read, and do not soften the third if the procedure says stop.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which judgments it merely relocates rather than settles, what it quietly assumes about our standing to intervene, and how I would know after the fact whether a change we funded was caused by us.
