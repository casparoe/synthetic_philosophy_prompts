So I've been going back and forth between two views that both get called internalist, and I've become convinced they aren't cousins at all — they're rivals wearing the same badge, and I want the comparison done properly rather than a "here's a taxonomy of internalisms" answer.

One is Williams's internal reasons account: A has a reason to φ only if A could arrive at wanting to φ by deliberating soundly from elements already in his subjective motivational set. And Williams is generous about what sound deliberation includes — not just means-end reasoning but correcting false beliefs, thinking through consequences, imagining things vividly, and the possibility that deliberation itself generates new motivations. Step outside that and you're making a claim he thinks is obscure: it can't explain action, and it can't do the work we want blame to do.

The other is Michael Smith's dispositional analysis: A has most reason to φ iff A would desire that he φ if he were fully rational — roughly, fully informed and with a coherent, unified desire set.

Here's what I want you to work through.

1. Does Smith's account actually meet Williams's condition? My fully rational self might want things that no route from my actual motivational set gets me to — most sharply, it might want me to acquire a desire I currently want to be rid of. If that's possible, is Smith an external reasons theorist with extra steps, or does Williams's generosity about deliberation (imagination, belief-correction, deliberation creating new motivations) swallow the case after all? Say which, and where exactly the line falls.

2. Both want to preserve a link between reasons and motivation, but they're different links — one is about the actual agent being reachable, one is about a counterfactual idealized agent. So tell me what each can say about the person who sincerely assents that he has most reason to do something and feels nothing whatsoever. For each view: is that a failure of rationality, a sign he doesn't have the reason, a sign he doesn't really assent, or none of these?

3. The case I keep turning over. Dev is offered a pill that will permanently rewire him — afterwards he'll be happy, absorbed, and brilliant, and completely indifferent to his kids and his oldest friend. Suppose post-pill Dev is fully rational in Smith's sense and wholeheartedly endorses his own state, and suppose pre-pill Dev, fully informed and coherent, would also endorse taking it. Does Smith's account give actual Dev most reason to take the pill? Does Williams's? My gut says Williams says no and Smith is forced to say yes or do something evasive, but I might have mis-stated Smith.

4. This is the bit I made up and most want tested. Suppose you iterate the idealization instead of doing it once: idealize the actual person, then idealize the result, and keep going until the desire set stops moving — a fixed point — and then say A has reason to φ iff the fixed point wants A to φ. Is that view any different from Smith's, and is it internalist in Williams's sense? My worry is a dilemma: either the iteration converges in one step, in which case it's just Smith with a fancier description, or it doesn't converge, in which case nobody has reasons; and neither horn is escaped by insisting the fixed point is what the actual agent was reaching for all along. Is that a real dilemma, or am I missing an obvious third option?

Also: tell me where you think this whole dispute is verbal — where the two camps mean different things by "internal" and the disagreement just evaporates once you notice.

No bibliography please, I'm not chasing anything down and neither are you. But do say how confident you are in each of the four answers separately, and single out the one point you're least sure of. I suspect it's somewhere on the Smith side, since I'm reconstructing him from memory and you may well be doing the same.
