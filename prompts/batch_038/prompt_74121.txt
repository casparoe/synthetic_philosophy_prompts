I cover science and ideas for a general-interest mag and my next column is about how thought experiments manage to produce knowledge at all — basically the Norton vs Brown standoff. Norton's line, roughly: every thought experiment is a picturesque argument, and you can always reconstruct it out of ordinary empirical premises, so nothing new arrives from nowhere. Brown's line: some of them (he calls them platonic ones) get you an a priori insight into a law of nature that a faithful reconstruction doesn't actually deliver — the reconstruction either smuggles in an extra premise or throws away the thing you genuinely saw.

I want a poem to run alongside the piece, and I want the form to do the arguing rather than just decorate it. A sestina: six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoy, six end-words of your choosing that are the kind of words a reconstruction would need (premise, case, see, therefore, that register — but yours), and they should each be able to do double duty inside Stevin's setup. The thought experiment to work from is Stevin's wreath of spheres: the closed chain of equal balls draped over a triangular prism, which obviously can't just start turning forever, so the equilibrium on the two slopes falls out of that.

Here's the trick I'm after. Run the real sestina permutation cleanly through the first three stanzas, because thats Norton — the same six words coming back in a different order, nothing entering that wasn't already sitting in the premises. Then in stanza four break the rotation on purpose: a seventh end-word appears that the first eighteen lines couldnt have generated, and the remaining stanzas plus the envoy have to live with it.

Make the lines something a person could read out loud without wincing, not permutation-fodder, and no footnotes, no bibliography, no name-checking beyond what I've given you. Afterwards, 100-150 words: which side of the debate the poem's structure ends up favoring, whether that seventh word is fair to Norton or a strawman of him (be blunt — if it's a strawman, say so), and anywhere you bent the physics or the history of the case.

And if you think my framing is off — a sestina can't carry that much weight, or the break at stanza four inevitably cheats, or I've mis-stated what one of these two guys is committed to — say that plainly instead of writing something that quietly papers over it.
