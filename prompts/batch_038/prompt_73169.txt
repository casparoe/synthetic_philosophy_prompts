I build and tune the flight controllers for a company that makes small survey drones. I have no formal training in physics or philosophy — what I know I've picked up from instrument manuals, a couple of popular books, and thinking hard about my own work — but there is something in what I do every day that I cannot resolve, and I would like it treated seriously rather than explained away.

Here is the situation. Our aircraft know when they are turning without looking at anything. The gyroscope on the board is a tiny vibrating structure; when the board rotates, a force appears on that structure which has nothing to do with any outside object, and from the instrument's own point of view it is not rotating relative to anything at all. The accelerometer next to it reads zero in free fall and reads a definite nonzero number when it is being held up or pushed, again without consulting anything external. An inertial navigation system integrates those two signals and tracks its own orientation in cloud, at night, with no GPS, no stars, no landmarks, nothing in view. And the same goes for a Foucault pendulum, which I saw once in a museum: its plane of swing slowly turns relative to the floor beneath it, at a rate fixed entirely by latitude.

Meanwhile the physics I've read insists that there is no such thing as detectable absolute motion. Constant velocity in a straight line is purely relative; nothing, even in principle, could reveal it, and arguably there is no fact of the matter about it. That is supposed to be the central lesson of relativity. So my instruments appear to be measuring rotation with respect to nothing, which is exactly what the theory says cannot be done — and yet everyone agrees the instruments are correct, because the drone flies.

Three things I've read make this worse rather than better. First, the old thought experiment with water in a bucket hung on a rope: once the water is at rest relative to the bucket, the surface is still climbing the walls, which is usually taken to show that what matters is rotation relative to something other than the bucket. Second, that some physicists have proposed that this "something other" is the mass of the distant universe, and that there is a real measured effect in which a spinning planet very slightly drags the local inertial frame around with it — apparently confirmed by satellite experiments. Third, that Einstein's field equations have perfectly good solutions for a completely empty universe which still possesses a definite geometry and a definite notion of which paths are unaccelerated, which seems to imply that something is there even when nothing is in it.

What I want is an analysis of the practice, not a survey of the controversy:

1. What does the practice of building and trusting instruments that report rotation and acceleration without any external reference — while denying that any instrument could report velocity in the same way — actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit account of space or spacetime as precisely as you can. Does it assume that space is a thing with a state of its own, that there is a fact about non-acceleration which is not a fact about relations among material bodies, or some third thing I don't have a name for?

2. Separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it comfortably. My suspicion is that a great deal gets conflated here: perhaps all we need is that there is a physically privileged class of non-accelerating states, plus a rule for detecting membership in it, and nothing whatever about what space is made of. Tell me exactly where that line falls, and which claims an engineer is licensed to make and which are metaphysical surplus loaded onto the instruments.

3. Is the practice internally consistent, given the asymmetry that keeps tripping me up — rotation absolute, velocity relative, both by the same devices? Reconcile the two halves, or show that they cannot both stand, and if they cannot, say which half the engineering actually depends on.

4. Take the rival account, on which rotation is always rotation relative to the distribution of matter in the universe. What would have to be true for my gyroscope to be tracking that instead? Would my drone fly differently, or would any part of the design, calibration, or testing change? If the two accounts make no contact with anything anyone could measure, say so plainly rather than diplomatically — I would rather be told that this is a difference with no difference than be given a balanced summary.

5. Finally, tell me what would change in the practice itself — how we calibrate, how we justify the instruments to a customer, how we teach a technician why the box knows it is turning — if the presupposed account were replaced by the rival one. And if you think the practice really presupposes a third thing, geometry as a physical field that happens to be there rather than either a substance or a system of relations, say what that would commit us to and why it is not simply the first view under a new label.

Please do not give me the history of the debate or a reading list. I want the implicit theory reconstructed and then judged: what it commits us to, what it does not, where the practice is genuinely underdetermined so that several rival theories would license it equally, and where my own description of the practice has quietly assumed one of them.
