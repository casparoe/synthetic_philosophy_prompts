I am three years into a novel, and one of its strands is set in South Africa in the second half of the 1990s, in and around the amnesty hearings of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. I have done the archival work; I do not need it done for me, and I would rather you not rehearse the history back to me. What I need is the philosophy, and I need it argued rather than surveyed, because two of my characters — a commissioner who signs the amnesty findings and a widow who opposes one application — keep speaking past each other on the page, and I suspect the reason is that I have not yet worked out what they are actually disagreeing about.

So: analyze the amnesty provision as a test case for the debate between retributivism and consequentialism about punishment. The salient facts, as I have them, and you may take them as given without checking them: amnesty was not general but had to be applied for individually, act by act; it was available only where the applicant established that the act had a political objective; it was conditional on full disclosure of the relevant facts; it was granted to a minority of applicants, and victims and their families had standing to appear and oppose; those refused, or who never applied, remained in principle liable to prosecution, though the practical prospect of prosecution was thin; and the provision survived a challenge in the new Constitutional Court. If the exact numbers or dates matter to a point you want to make, flag that they matter rather than inventing them.

Four things I want from the analysis.

First, separate two questions the debate tends to fuse: whether the amnesty scheme was permissible, and whether any given amnesty was affirmatively right. Relatedly, distinguish the justification of the institution from the justification of decisions taken inside it, and tell me whether the two theories can differ at these two levels — whether a theory might condemn the scheme and commend the ruling, or the reverse. My commissioner signs individual findings; my widow contests the institution. I want to know if they are even on the same battlefield.

Second, work with a three-way distinction I have been turning over and cannot resolve. Was amnesty the remission of a punishment that was owed; or a declaration that no punishment had ever been owed, because the authority imposing it lacked the standing to impose it on acts committed in the course of a conflict it was party to; or something third — a suspension of the state's standing to punish while the standing itself persists? Retributivism and consequentialism seem to attach differently to each description, and I suspect one of the three is where the whole difficulty lives. Say which, and say why.

Third, the price. Amnesty was not free; it was purchased with disclosure. That makes it look like an exchange, and I want to know whether a theory on which punishment is owed, and therefore not available to be traded, can participate in a bargain at all without ceasing to be that theory — or whether the concession to second-best conditions (prosecution was never realistically going to happen, so something was better than nothing) quietly converts retributivism into consequentialism by the back door. Then run the same pressure the other way: the benefit of disclosure accrued largely to third parties and to the historical record, not to the woman in the hall. Can the consequentialist calculus give her resentment a weight that is not simply one preference among others, to be aggregated and possibly outweighed?

Fourth — and this is the part I care most about, because it is where my drafts fail — tell me where each theory falls silent. The public naming, the fact that a disclosure could be judged insufficient on grounds that had nothing to do with the moral gravity of the act, the fact that two applicants for comparable acts could receive opposite rulings depending on the quality of their accounts: which of these features does each theory have no vocabulary for, as opposed to merely an unwelcome verdict about? Be precise about the difference between silence and disagreement; I cannot write a scene in which a theory speaks where it does not speak.

Conclude by saying which way the episode cuts. Does it confirm one theory, expose an anomaly the theory can absorb, or reveal that the retributivist–consequentialist debate was built for a case it does not fit — a settled state punishing its own subjects under uncontested authority — and that a negotiated transition is simply outside its domain? If you think one position comes out better here, say so plainly and say why; I am tired of analyses that end in a draw, and my characters would not survive one.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no biographical throat-clearing about either theory's history. Roughly fifteen hundred words. And if you can do it in a final paragraph without sentimentalizing it: tell me which of the two positions allows my commissioner to be wrong without being a monster, since that is the sentence I have spent a year failing to write.
