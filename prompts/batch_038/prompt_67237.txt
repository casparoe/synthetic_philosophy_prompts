You are a philosopher of mind who works on the philosophy of pain and who also writes comic prose for a general audience. Think carefully before drafting: open your response with a short bullet-point brainstorm identifying the genuine pressure points in the debate that your satire will exploit, then write the piece itself.

Write a direct-to-consumer advertisement, roughly 650 words, for an invented elective procedure sold by a private clinic: a permanent, targeted intervention that abolishes the unpleasantness of pain while leaving its sensory-discriminative function intact, so that clients continue to feel, localize, and rate pain but cease to mind it. The copy should read like real boutique medical marketing — a headline, a confident layperson's mechanism-of-action paragraph, two testimonials, a short FAQ, and a boxed block of consent-form fine print at the end.

Your target is the view that pain's badness is exhausted by its unpleasantness, together with the promotional register that would sell removing that unpleasantness as an unambiguous good. Do not name real philosophers, patients, papers, or clinics, and do not quote the literature. The humor has to come from getting both the philosophy and the clinical picture right, so that a specialist laughs rather than winces. In particular, the advertisement should inadvertently expose — while never conceding — at least four of the following: that the syndrome it imitates is messier than its before-and-after framing suggests, since some patients still flinch at or learn to avoid repeated painful stimulation; that pain's aversiveness is doing protective work, so the clinic must quietly sell supplementary monitoring to compensate for what it has removed; that the deficit may not be specific to pain at all but a general failure to respond to demands and commands, which its own intake questionnaire keeps hinting at; that a client who can mind nothing may be worse off in a way the clinic's stated theory cannot register; and that informed consent is here addressed to someone whose reasons the procedure is designed to dissolve.

Stay deadpan throughout: the clinic never winks, never concedes an objection, and remains internally consistent with the theory it announces in the first paragraph. A reader who has never heard of the syndrome should nonetheless be able to see what is being ironized.
