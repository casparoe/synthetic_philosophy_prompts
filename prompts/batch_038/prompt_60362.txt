I am three years into a novel built around an invented catastrophe: the fire that destroyed the Corliss textile mill in a New Hampshire mill town on the night of October 14, 1911, killing thirty-one workers. The book's conceit is that each chapter follows one causal thread into the fire — the locked stair door, the lint-saturated air, the foreman's decision to run the looms an extra shift, the summer drought that lowered the river feeding the mill's tank — and that between the chapters sit three documents offering rival accounts of the same night: a coroner's inquest finding, an insurance adjuster's report, and a union organizer's pamphlet. Each of the three has to be a genuine explanation rather than a parody, and each has to fail at something the others get right. Before I can write them that way, I need to know what the standard is.

So: the desiderata for an adequate theory of historical explanation — specifically, explanation of a singular, non-repeatable event such as my fire. Give me a numbered list, and for each requirement the case or consideration behind it, so I can see why the theory would be inadequate without it. Two things I especially need the list to settle. First, what makes one explanation of the fire *better* rather than merely better written; where does explanatory force live, if not in the prose? Second, how a theory is supposed to constrain the account so that it cannot be assembled after the fact out of whatever antecedents happen to be on hand — my event is over-determined enough that I could write four mutually incompatible versions, all of them internally coherent and all of them citing real conditions.

Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable for anyone working in the area and which are the signature commitments of a single camp wearing the costume of a criterion. I suspect that "must subsume the event under a law," on one side, and some requirement concerning narrative shape, on the other, both belong in the second pile; make the case either way, since I don't want to import my own bias into the specification. Next, identify any subset of the requirements you judge jointly unsatisfiable, and name the single requirement that eliminates the most otherwise attractive theories. Finally, if the answer differs for the coroner, the adjuster, and the organizer — if their purposes license different adequate accounts of the same night — say so plainly, and say what that does to the assumption that one of them is simply right and the others wrong.

One further item to assess rather than assume: a test I have been applying in revision, which I will call the shuffle test. Take a passage that purports to explain the fire and reorder its sentences into bare chronological sequence, stripping the connective tissue entirely. If it no longer explains, I have been mistaking narrative momentum for explanatory force. Is that a genuine desideratum — that an explanation's content be separable from its order of presentation — or is it a prejudice of mine that simply assumes what the partisans of narrative explanation deny? Argue it out rather than settling it by stipulation.

Close by scoring the principal contenders against your list, but briefly: a paragraph each is ample, and no citations, no bibliography, no titles. I want the specification, not the scholarship.
