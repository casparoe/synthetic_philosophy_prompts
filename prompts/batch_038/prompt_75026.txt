I am a fourth-year doctoral student in an experimental mesoscopics group, and this term I have been reassigned to a project I did not choose. Our group runs a feedback-controlled colloidal information engine: a micron-scale bead held in an optical trap whose stiffness and center we modulate, with a controller that samples the bead's position, writes a bit into a physical memory realized as a double-well potential, and then shifts the trap so that the bead's subsequent relaxation lifts a weight, so to speak, in the calibrated potential. We are co-writing an interdisciplinary review on what such devices are supposed to establish about the second law, and the opening section — what would count as resolving Maxwell's demon at all — has fallen to me. That is not a question I was trained to ask. One of my coauthors is confident the matter was closed decades ago by the erasure-based accounting; a philosopher of physics on my committee disagrees, and has already warned me that most of what my coauthors want to write either begs the question against the demon, or simply restates the second law in information-theoretic vocabulary and calls it an explanation.

So I would like the specification before I draft a single paragraph: the desiderata any proposed resolution of the demon's challenge has to satisfy. For each requirement, give me the statement of it, the concrete case or consideration that motivates it — ideally a scenario in which a would-be resolution that omits the requirement visibly goes wrong — and a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or whether it is really the standing preference of one camp (for instance, the insistence on strictly cyclic operation, the insistence that the accounting be derivable from time-reversal-invariant microdynamics without any stipulated coarse-graining, the insistence that the demon be treated as an autonomous physical system rather than an external controller, or the deflationary view that the second law was always statistical and therefore needs no rescuing). Roughly a dozen requirements is about right; more is fine if they are genuinely distinct, but please collapse anything that is doing the same work twice, and explicitly flag any candidate requirement that turns out to be a restatement of the second law itself, since such a requirement has no power to discriminate between resolutions.

The list has to cover three variants that keep coming up around our apparatus, two of which I made up myself and none of which I can find handled cleanly in anything I have read. First, an append-only demon: a device that measures and records but never overwrites anything, so that on any single run it never erases a bit, and which is operated exactly once rather than cyclically — I want to know what a resolution owes us here, and whether the demand that such a demon eventually fail is a physical requirement or an appeal to a practical limit on memory. Second, a demon whose memory bit is reset not by controlled work but by a spontaneous thermal fluctuation of the bath, so that the dissipation is, on the lucky runs, paid back by the reservoir itself: what must a resolution say about the status of the bound on individual trajectories as opposed to averages, and about whether an account that only holds on average has answered the challenge? Third, a demon that performs no measurement at all and merely harvests a correlation that was established earlier at no thermodynamic cost — I want the requirement that governs when pre-existing correlation counts as usable fuel and when it does not, because this seems to be where accounting schemes either become trivially permissive or arbitrarily restrictive. After the list, tell me which subsets of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — if the tension between strict cyclicity, an unassisted finite-memory demon, and no appeal to special initial conditions or to an unexplained low-entropy past is real, say so plainly, because I suspect that is the most interesting thing I could put in this section — and identify the single requirement that excludes the most candidates. Then finish with a brief scorecard, no more than a table, of the main families of resolution against the list: the one-molecule-engine accounting in its original form, the view that measurement itself carries an unavoidable thermodynamic price, the erasure-based accounting, the modern framework that folds acquired mutual information into a modified work bound, the persistent dissent holding that the erasure bound has never been properly established and that engine-based derivations smuggle in the irreversibility they claim to derive, and the deflationary view that there was never a paradox. Refer to these positions descriptively rather than by citation — I will add references myself, and I do not want invented ones — and where a camp's failure against a requirement is contested rather than agreed, say that it is contested.
