ok so context, sorry in advance for the length. i'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background whatsoever, i took one elective and dropped it.

I've spent six years at a small studio that runs a social VR world called Drift — less a game, more a place. About 9,000 people log in most nights. They built themselves a whole district called the bell tower quarter out of a physics exploit we never patched, there have been weddings in there, there's a weekly court night where people settle disputes by arguing at each other until someone concedes. Its shutting down. Servers go dark in nine days. There's a sunset party in a rented hall in Oakland and, because we're sentimental, we're simulcasting it into the world itself — so a few hundred people will be hearing my speech while standing, as avatars, inside the thing that's about to stop existing. Please use that, it's the whole reason I said yes.

I've got about five minutes. The room is mixed: roughly 40 of us who built it, most of the people I'd call friends, our CEO, and maybe 300 players attending as avatars, some of them there since year one.

Here's the thing I can't get past, and I want the speech to be about it without ever naming it as a topic. A couple years ago my sister lent me a book by a consciousness philosopher arguing that virtual stuff is real — not physically real, not made of atoms, but genuinely real in the sense that what happens there really happens and really changes the person it happens to. I only got a third of the way in, but the idea got lodged, and honestly six years of moderation logs convinced me more than the book did.

Problem one: our CEO's farewell blog post called Drift "just a game we were lucky to get to make." He's a decent guy, not malicious at all, but half the avatar audience read that post and a good chunk of those are hurt. I don't want to embarrass him in front of everybody and I don't want to walk his sentence back either. I need a speech in which "just a game" is simply never available as a description of the thing, but where nobody has to lose face.

Problem two, and this is the harder one. In year four there was a coordinated griefing campaign — a group found a way to permanently delete other players' built objects, and they razed a lighthouse one player had spent three years assembling piece by piece in memory of her mother. At the time a lot of people, including me in a Slack channel I'm not proud of, said some version of "well, you can log off, that's the difference, nobody's actually hurt." We restored the lighthouse from backups for her, but we could only restore it with a visible seam where the rebuild met the original. She kept the seam. She said she liked it.

My own view now, which I want the speech to carry: the exit is real and it's a genuine difference from physical life — you can set a world down, you can't set your body down. But for someone who has built a life inside, logging off doesn't prevent the loss, it relocates it. It costs you the world to escape harm in the world. That's not nothing, and its also not a broken arm, and I don't want the speech pretending it is. One of the year-one players died in March — actually died — and we left his avatar standing in the bell tower quarter, and his friends log in to sit next to him. Any speech that treats those two facts as the same kind of fact insults everyone in both audiences at once.

So: can you write it? Constraints, roughly:

- Five minutes, about 700 words, meant to be said out loud. Plain language. No philosophy vocabulary whatsoever — no ontology, nothing that sounds like a conference panel.
- Don't name the philosopher, the book, or the argument. The idea has to do structural work, not decoration — I want to be able to point at the specific sentences that would come out false if you flipped it.
- One joke maximum, and it has to land for the engineers and the players simultaneously.
- Build to a toast. There will be actual glasses in the hall and actual people inside the world, so give the avatars something to do with what they've got.
- Before the speech itself, give me four or five lines telling me, in your own words, what the distinction is that you're building on and where in the speech it's load bearing. So I can check you didn't just write me a nice sad thing.
