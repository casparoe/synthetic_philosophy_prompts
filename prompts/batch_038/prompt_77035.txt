**The exam question (Question 3, 25 marks, suggested length 900 words)**

In the opening of his *Life of Alexander*, Plutarch writes: "For it is not Histories that I am writing, but Lives; and in the most illustrious deeds there is not always a manifestation of virtue or vice, nay, a slight thing like a phrase or a jest often makes a greater revelation of character than battles where thousands fall, or the greatest armaments, or sieges of cities." (Perrin trans.) Explain what Plutarch thinks biography can do that history cannot, and what he thinks a reader is supposed to get from a life. Then assess his position: does the aim of edification make his project a species of hagiography, and can a life written to improve its readers still be a truthful one?

**The answer submitted**

Plutarch's claim at the opening of the *Alexander* is that biography and history are different kinds of writing with different objects. The historian is concerned with great public actions — battles, armaments, sieges — while the biographer is concerned with character, and character shows itself in small and often undignified things: a phrase, a joke, a gesture. He compares himself to a portrait painter, who takes the likeness from the face and especially from the expression of the eyes and makes very little account of the rest of the body. The consequence for method is that the biographer must be selective and is entitled to leave out material a historian could not leave out; a life is not required to be complete in the way an account of a campaign is.

The point Plutarch is most insistent on is that this selectivity makes biography a more accurate form of writing than history. Because the biographer confines himself to a single life, he can examine its details closely and confirm them, whereas the historian of a war depends on conflicting reports of events that happened far away. Plutarch's preference for the slight thing over the great thing is therefore an epistemic preference: the small detail is better evidence because it is better attested. That is also why he thinks the most illustrious deeds are not always a manifestation of virtue or vice — publicity distorts them.

His purpose in writing lives is moral rather than merely informative. He wants readers to have before them characters to admire and to imitate, and he remarks elsewhere that writing the Lives turned out to be a kind of improvement for himself. The paired structure of the *Parallel Lives* serves this purpose: by setting a Greek beside a Roman of comparable character and achievement, Plutarch lets the reader see which of two similar men handled similar situations better, and so learn what to do in a comparable situation himself. Because the reader is meant to imitate, Plutarch treats his subjects as exemplars in the strict sense — the moral value of a life is proportional to the admirability of the actions recorded in it, and a biography is more useful the more admirable its subject's conduct.

Here is where the danger of hagiography arises. Plutarch is candid that he does not want to dwell on his subjects' failings: since it is difficult or impossible to represent a man's life as stainless, he says the biographer should treat transgressions as shortcomings in some particular excellence rather than as the vile products of positive baseness, and should not delineate them too zealously. But a rule of that kind, applied consistently, means that the Lives contain no genuine criticism of their subjects at all. Plutarch has decided in advance that these men are admirable and then selects the evidence to fit, so a reader who treats the *Life of Alexander* as evidence about Alexander's character is being manipulated rather than informed. Edification has displaced truth, and the result is hagiography in all but name — the same impulse that later produced lives of the saints written to support a cult, in which inconvenient facts are simply absent.

It is worth adding that Plutarch's confidence in his method was vindicated by its reception. The Lives were a standard school text for centuries, and Shakespeare built his Roman plays on North's translation. Whatever the theoretical objections, a civilisation that improved itself on these books for a thousand years is strong evidence that edifying biography does work as moral education, and that Plutarch was right about what biography can do.
