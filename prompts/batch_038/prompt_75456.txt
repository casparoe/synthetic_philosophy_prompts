I teach a decision-theory seminar to philosophy grad students — 14 of them, 90 minutes, all fine with conditional probability but none have met Good's theorem on the value of information before. I want an in-class game where they trip over the theorem themselves, and then trip over exactly the places it stops holding. My half-built version: two states, three actions, and a free signal about the state that each player chooses to take or decline before acting, with the numbers arranged so that looking is weakly better for everybody in round one. Round two: I hand a third of the room a card that forces them, after looking, to act by a fixed rule rather than by whatever the signal suggests — and those players should end up genuinely wishing they'd stayed ignorant. Round three I want the payoff itself to depend on whether you looked (something like a gift that's only worth as much if you didn't know it was coming), which I think breaks a different assumption again. Please build the whole thing: state/action payoff tables with actual numbers that are mutually consistent — I botch this every time — the round-by-round timeline, materials, the exact instructions I read aloud so nobody learns the theorem's name beforehand, and a debrief that maps each round onto the specific hypothesis it stresses. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it with real people, including whether the round-two twist is telegraphed too early, and be blunt about which feature of the theorem the game just cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand. Also: how confident are you in the numbers and in the claim that each round isolates a seperate assumption? Flag the single point you're least sure of.
