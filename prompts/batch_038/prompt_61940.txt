Some background first: I spent 38 years in analytical chemistry, mostly trace metals in water samples, and I've been retired four years now. Retirement did what I feared it would and I fell into philosophy head first — started with the Meno on a whim, and the ending undid me a bit. Socrates spends the whole dialogue chasing a definition and then turns around and admits that a true opinion guides you to Larissa just as well as knowledge does, and that knowledge is only better because its more stable, beliefs having to be tied down like those runaway statues he goes on about.

Our town library runs a philosophy circle, about 20 of us, mostly ex-engineers, a couple of former schoolteachers, one actuary who is terrifying. This year they've let me write the end-of-term paper. They sit it properly, two hours, closed book, no notes, and they like it that way.

I want one essay question on that value issue — not the analysis issue, I have no interest in a Gettier recital — and I want it built out of something I actually know rather than out of thought experiments involving barns. Here's the raw material I'd give them:

Case A. A bench pH meter that hasn't been calibrated in two years reads 7.02 on a sample. The sample really is 7.02. A student writes it in the notebook and believes it.
Case B. An identical meter, calibrated that morning against two buffers traceable to national standards, reads 7.02 on the same sample. Same belief, same truth, same action taken on it.

Every scientist I've ever worked with says B is worth more and would take it as an insult to be told otherwise. But here's the argument people throw at me: once you're holding the true reading, the calibration has already been paid for. The reliability of the instrument is worth exactly what its product is worth, and adding the two together is double counting. Apparently the standard illustration is a good cup of coffee — it isn't made any better by having come from a reliable machine, since you wanted the coffee and you have the coffee.

The escape I've been offered is that B wins not on this reading but on all the readings the calibrated meter will give you next week and next year. So now add this: the instrument is a single-use sensor, and it is destroyed a second after it gives the reading. There is no next week. Nothing else will ever be measured with it.

And a third wrinkle: Case C is exactly like B, except the person reading it is a visitor who has no idea whether that meter was calibrated this morning or in 1998.

What I'd like from you:

Write the question as it would actually appear on the paper — 40 minutes, 25 marks — with whatever background I need to state up front, on the assumption they've read the Meno and my two handouts and nothing else. Nobody should lose marks for not having read the specialist literature, so if the question needs a term of art defined, define it in the question.

Then a model answer, roughly 700 words, that actually resolves the one-shot case rather than waving at it.

Then a marking scheme totalling 25, with marks per point, and it must seperate an A from a B by naming the specific moves — not "shows nuance," tell me which move is worth 4 marks and which paragraph an A has that a B doesn't. I already suspect two of the errors I want penalised: sliding from "the calibrated belief is more useful" to "the calibrated belief is more valuable" without noticing you've changed the subject, and disposing of the destroyed-sensor case by saying reliability is a disposition and dispositions have value even unmanifested, without explaining why an unmanifested disposition adds anything to the value of this true belief. Give me those two properly and add whatever else you think they'll trip on, including anything to do with Case C.

Two smaller things. Should I name the coffee argument's usual label in the question, or will naming it just let them parrot a slogan back at me and pick up marks for vocabulary? And if you think this whole framing is my laboratory habits in a philosophy costume — that the value difference I care about is really about practice and institutions and not about knowledge at all — say so straight rather than indulging me, and tell me whether the question survives that objection or should be rewritten around it.
