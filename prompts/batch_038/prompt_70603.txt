I'm in a reading group this year where some of us are in an ethics seminar and some of us are doing Buddhist studies, and I've ended up with a stack of gut verdicts that I can't get to line up. I don't need a survey of the field — I've read enough of the back-and-forth over whether Buddhist ethics is really a virtue ethics or really a consequentialism to know that both answers get floated and both get attacked. What I want is whether there's an actual principle under my own intuitions or whether some of them have to be thrown out. Here's the list and where I landed: the skillful-means case where the ship captain (a bodhisattva in a former life) kills the bandit who's about to slaughter five hundred people aboard — right, even praiseworthy. The monastic case where a group of monks, having spent a retreat contemplating the foulness of the body, got so disgusted that they killed themselves or paid someone to do it, and it was ruled an offense that ends your monastic life — also correct. The version of that where the monks just talk up death to a sick monk until he dies, and they're held as guilty as if they'd held the blade — fine, except I notice I'm half-judging them by what would have happened to the man otherwise, which is scoreboard thinking, and half by their minds, which isn't. A guy who shoots at a rival, misses, and harms nobody: exactly as blameworthy as if he'd hit. Two people who kill out of identical rage with identical histories, one killing a market porter and one killing a monk who's been in retreat for forty years: the second is much worse, and this one bugs me, because if the mind is what's recorded, why should the victim's status change the weight? A man who funds a hospital purely for prestige, and the hospital really does save a lot of people: I want to say the act isn't good, or is only accidentally good, despite the suffering removed. A monk who breaks a training rule in secret, harms nobody, is never found out, feels no remorse: still bad. A bodhisattva who puts off her own liberation to stay and help others: good, even though it costs her.

Here's what I suspect is going on, using a coinage from a friend that I can't stop reaching for. Call it the ledger versus the scoreboard. The scoreboard is the standard consequentialist tally — how much suffering and how much clarity is in the world after you act, whoever's in it. The ledger is a record that keeps itself: what the act did to your own continuum and to the trajectories of the particular beings you touched, entered whether or not anyone evaluates you and whether or not the worldly result lands. Looking back, the ones I called fine seem to run on the ledger — the shooter who misses, the secret rule-breaker, the prestige hospital — while the captain and the sick monk pull me onto the scoreboard. The victim-status case runs on neither cleanly. But the real mess is the captain-euthanasia pair: on the surface it's the same structure, a killing performed with a calm, compassionate mind that spares a lot of people a lot of misery, and I say one is meritorious and the other is about the worst thing you can do. I've tried patching it by saying the captain's compassion extended to the bandit and not just the five hundred — killing him prevented the eons of demerit he was on his way to — whereas the monks talking up death are just relieving physical misery that was ending on its own anyway, and maybe robbing a being of the opportunity the remaining life would have given him. But I honestly don't know if that's a principle or a story I invented after the fact. It feels a bit like a rule I'd only accept if it also explained the porter-versus-retreat-monk case, which I'm not sure it does.

So — is there a principle, single or two-part, that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it so it actually predicts something rather than just restating the cases with fancier vocabulary, and say what it commits you to about the ledger/scoreboard question: does one of the two do all the explaining, or do I genuinely have to carry two ledgers and accept they can conflict? Then run it on three cases I keep flip-flopping on: a surgeon who harvests organs from a healthy traveler to save five patients, doing it without any hatred and purely out of concern for the five; a teacher who lies to a student about her talent to keep her at it, and the lie pays off; and a practitioner who spends forty years generating goodwill toward every being in the universe with zero observable effect on any of them. Give me the verdict the principle delivers on those even if it's not the one I want. And be straight about which of my original verdicts it can't accommodate — if saving all eight means adding clauses until nothing is falsifiable, tell me which verdict goes. Last thing: whatever you land on, does the result count as a consequentialism with an unusual theory of value, or has it stopped being consequentialism somewhere along the way, and where? No bibliography needed, I just want the reasoning, and you can use unskillfull and skillful in the technical sense if it's handy.
