I'm teaching a week on rationality over time in a grad seminar — money pumps, diachronic choice, the whole argument that cyclical preferences leave you exploitable and therefore count as irrational — and I want to build the session around Memento. Everyone in the room has seen it or at least knows the setup: Leonard can't lay down new memories, so he runs on tattoos, annotated Polaroids and notes-to-self while hunting his wife's killer.

My problem is that I think the film is a close-to-perfect dramatisation of precommitment and its failures, but I also think the obvious way of saying that is wrong, and I don't want to hand my students a clean allegory that falls apart the second somebody presses on it. So give me an actual reading, not a plot summary with philosophy words sprinkled on it.

Things I want worked through:

1. Leonard's system. Are the tattoos and photo captions genuinely precommitments, or something else — self-binding or other-binding, and does that distinction even survive his condition? The thing that keeps bugging me is that he re-reads his own notes as fresh evidence rather than as decisions he already made. Does that break the analogy to the Ulysses-at-the-mast style of binding, or does it just show a binding device that's badly engineered?

2. Who's running the pump, and on what. This is the bit I care about most. Leonard's preferences at any given moment don't look cyclical in the textbook way — nobody is walking him through a sequence of trades that exploits an intransitive ranking. What Teddy and Natalie exploit is seperate: his rules for forming beliefs and for treating his own handwriting as authoritative. So is the honest claim that he's pumpable without being intransitive at all? Pick out the scene or stretch where the pump actually runs, and say what gets extracted at each step and why he keeps paying it.

3. Then settle the dispute I want the seminar to have. One side says being pumpable shows you're irrational, full stop. The other says Leonard is pumpable and yet not irrational in any sense the argument was ever aimed at, so the money-pump argument overreaches and needs patching. What actually distinguishes Leonard from the ordinary pump victim? Give me something operational I could apply to the film, not a vibe about psychological unity or personal identity. And say which side you think wins and under what restrictions.

4. The reverse chronology. The colour sequences run backwards, so we see where Leonard ends up long before we see the choices that got him there. Does that structure do philosophical work a straight telling couldn't — making the shape of the pump visible before the steps that produce it — or is it a gimmick that just happens to rhyme with amnesia? Be honest if it's mostly the second one.

5. The ending. Once Teddy has told him everything, including how the Sammy Jankis story is really his own, Leonard sets up his next target anyway, knowing full well he won't remember choosing it. That looks like deliberate self-pumping: manufacturing evidence now in order to control a future self who can't see the manufacturing. Is there any sense in which that's rational? Does it make him more of an agent or less, and what does it do to the idea that binding your future self is a solution rather then just a different problem?

Two constraints. Don't send me a reading list, and don't pretend to quote the film — I've got the disc, I don't need line readings, paraphrase is fine. And wherever your interpretation has to bend the movie or bend the argument to keep the analogy alive, say so plainly: I'd much rather know exactly which parts of the reading are load-bearing on something the film doesn't actually give me.
