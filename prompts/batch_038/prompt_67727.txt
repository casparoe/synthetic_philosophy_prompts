I spent about 35 years working on plasma confinement and control — tokamaks mostly — and retired three years ago. Since then I've been reading philosophy for fun, and I got properly stuck on Kant's Perpetual Peace. I can follow most of the sentences but I can't tell whether I actually understand the thing, because the words he uses (right, duty, nature's guarantee, hospitality) don't line up with anything already in my head. So I want to try an experiment on you.

Translate the architecture of the essay into the vocabulary I actually think in — equilibria, stability margins, feedback loops, loop gain, damping, actuators and sensors, disturbance rejection, open-loop versus closed-loop, saturation, attractors and their basins — as faithfully as you can manage without cheating. Go part by part: the six preliminary articles, the three definitive articles, the supplement on the guarantee of nature, and the appendix where he seperates the moral politician from the political moralist. For each, tell me what maps cleanly onto a stability notion, what you had to bend out of shape to make it fit, and what has no expression in my language at all. And please don't smuggle my assumptions in and then call it translation — if Kant is claiming something control theory simply cannot say, say so plainly.

Its the residue that matters to me, and there are two places where I suspect it piles up:

- He rejects a world state and wants instead a federation of free states with no power to coerce its own members. In my language that's a controller with no actuator, which shouldn't work at all. Is that the fair analogy, or is he closer to a passive, Lyapunov-style design where you reshape the energy landscape instead of applying force?
- The guarantee of nature reads to me like a claim that the system reaches the attractor whether or not the agents want it to, which would make the entire moral argument otiose. Does the translation expose that tension, or dissolve it?

Then the verdict: is the leftover material a genuine limitation of my vocabulary — stability theory has no word for ought and that's just that — or does it suggest the whole project is less substantive than it first appeared? I'd rather hear the second answer if it's the true one. I'm not after sources or further reading, just your honest read.
