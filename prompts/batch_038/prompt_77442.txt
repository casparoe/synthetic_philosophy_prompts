For eleven years I have facilitated a monthly evening discussion group in a public library: twelve to sixteen adults, working people and retirees, none of them trained in philosophy, myself included. We have no syllabus. I put a question on the table, we argue for ninety minutes, and we reliably leave with the sense that we argued well and arrived nowhere.

Over the years I have formed a view about that feeling, and I want the group to reach it on their own rather than be handed it. My view is this. When people ask whether philosophy makes progress, they nearly always picture progress as convergence: a field slowly settling on answers. But the thing that actually prevents my group from converging is that we never debate the same question twice. Each time a position is defeated or refined, the question we are answering has quietly changed shape. The words survive; the content does not. So there is nothing for the next answer to converge toward, and the absence of agreement may be a sign that the questions are still being located rather than a sign of failure.

Design an activity that makes this felt, in one ninety-minute session, by people with no training and no preparation, and that makes them feel it before anyone names it.

The core mechanic I want you to build: the group debates a chain of connected questions across several rounds, and the wording of the question for each round is rewritten by the previous round's own recorder, in that recorder's own words, from the previous round's written record alone. The drift must be produced by the rules of the game, not staged by me.

Please supply the following.

1. The question chain. Four to six rounds. Each question must be debatable in roughly ten minutes by untrained people with no reading, must be a plausible outgrowth of what the previous round will probably conclude, and must not be a stock case: no trolleys, no brain in a vat, no ship rebuilt plank by plank, no experience machine. If the chain can sensibly fork, give me a rule for choosing the branch on the night.

2. Roles and materials. I want a recorder, and I want some device that guarantees a serious objection gets voiced in every round rather than the group drifting toward politeness. Specify exactly what goes on the running sheet and what the recorder is forbidden to write; my instinct is that the sheet should carry questions, positions, distinctions, and abandoned positions but no justifications, so that each round inherits conclusions without reasons. Tell me whether that is right or whether it breaks the mechanic.

3. The verdict phase. At the end, before any discussion, each participant must classify the whole record, individually and in writing, as showing progress or not, and must propose the measure they are using. I expect them to disagree both about the verdict and about what counts as a measure. Give me the procedure that reliably produces that disagreement rather than a shrug.

4. A debrief that maps particular moments of the evening onto the actual positions in this debate, naming the views only at that point.

5. A frank account of what will go wrong the first time I run it, and how I should handle it.

6. An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. I suspect the two losses are the timescale of real philosophy and the fact that real philosophers inherit arguments, not summaries, whereas my group inherits only a sheet of paper. Tell me whether that is the right diagnosis or whether something larger is missing.

Also give me a minute-by-minute timeline and a reduced version for a night when only eight people come. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and nothing in the activity should depend on anyone having read anything.
