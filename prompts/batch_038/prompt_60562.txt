I write about science and ideas for a weekly, and I keep coming back to somthing odd. In his 1950 Mind paper Turing took up extrasensory perception as one of the objections to thinking machines, and he said in effect that the statistical evidence for telepathy at any rate looked overwhelming to him — he found it genuinely unsettling, partly because it seemed to be a thing minds could do that machines couldn't. I want to stage that tension. Write me a dialogue between Turing and a contemporary experimental psychologist who has spent the last fifteen years on replication and pre-registration, and who has lived through the fallout from that 2011 precognition paper that got into a top social psychology journal, plus the long-running quarrel over the ganzfeld meta-analyses. Both need to be good at their job — no strawmen: the psychologist shouldn't just sneer, and Turing shouldn't come across as a gullible spiritualist. The disagreement I want dug out is whether Turing, the last person you'd expect to be taken in by numbers, was in fact taken in by numbers, or whether its a different and more defensible point about what counts as evidence when there's no mechanism in view and the effect is small. Let them actually answer each other's moves rather than trading set pieces, and land somewhere honest instead of tying a bow on it. If you bring in what Turing wrote, paraphrase him — I can't check quotations. British English, please.
