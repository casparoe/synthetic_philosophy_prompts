I am a software engineer with no philosophy training beyond what I have picked up on my own, and I would like help untangling a sentence a colleague of mine reaches for constantly. Our team runs a timed technical assessment for engineering candidates: a shared-screen exercise in which the candidate designs and implements a small service while thinking aloud. The question of whether candidates should be permitted to use an AI coding assistant during it came up at a calibration meeting, and when I argued that permitting it would make the results uninterpretable, since we would no longer know whose competence we were measuring, a senior colleague waved the objection aside with "the mind doesn't stop at the skull". On his view, a candidate using the assistant is no different from one using a notebook, a calculator, or a colleague in the room, because the tool is simply part of the thinking. He deploys the very same line, in a quite different register, about our employer's telemetry. Every prompt he sends to the assistant is logged and retained by the company, and he objects to this not as a matter of policy but as a matter of kind: reading his prompt history, he says, is not like reading his files or his commit messages, because the phone is part of the mind, so what the logs contain is something closer to his thoughts than to his property.

What I am after is not a summary of the extended mind thesis, which I have read a little about: the 1998 paper by Clark and Chalmers, the man with the notebook, the standard objections about coupling and about everything turning out to count as cognition. What I want is a disambiguation of the slogan itself, because I suspect it is doing at least three different jobs in his mouth and that at most one of them is true. Please give me a numbered menu of the distinct claims that "the mind doesn't stop at the skull" could be expressing, arranged from the near-trivial to the most radical: the banal causal point that thinking depends on traffic arriving through the eyes and on external aids; the weaker claim that external artefacts can be genuine parts of a cognitive process; the claim that particular mental states, beliefs for instance, can be literally located outside the head; and whatever stronger claims about the boundaries of the self or about the privileged status of the mental might be hiding behind it. For each reading, tell me what would have to be true for it to hold, whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, something genuinely contested, or simply false, and then, which is the part I actually need, whether that reading is strong enough to support each of his two conclusions taken separately. My suspicion is that one of them demands far more than the other, and that he slides between them precisely because the sentence is vague enough to cover both.

One further thing to weigh, which may be incidental or may go to the heart of the matter: his analogy is to a notebook, but the assistant behaves nothing like one. It volunteers suggestions nobody asked for, it is confidently wrong often enough that I have learned to check its output rather than accept it, it can be revoked or silently swapped for a different model version, and it is a shared service that other people also use and that was trained on their material. A notebook's contents are what I put in it and I do not argue with them; the assistant's contents are neither. Say whether those differences are engineering trivia that any sensible version of the claim can absorb, or whether they defeat the analogy and with it the readings he needs. Please write in British English, and do not trouble me with a bibliography or a reading list; names of positions and thought experiments are welcome where they clarify, but what I want to carry back into the next calibration meeting is the list of candidate claims and a verdict on each.
