I'm auditing a philosophy-of-mind reading group attached to the pain service at my teaching hospital, mostly as an excuse to read around the subject before I start my clinical rotations. Two weeks ago the group took up pain asymbolia — the condition, typically following insular damage or certain ablative procedures, in which patients report that they do feel pain, and can localize it and grade its intensity, but say they are not bothered by it. (The group was careful to distinguish it from congenital insensitivity, where the nociceptive signal never arrives; the philosophical interest of asymbolia is supposed to lie in the fact that the signal does arrive.) Afterwards two members, Ilse, who works in palliative care, and Daniel, a philosophy graduate student, got into a prolonged argument in the corridor that carried over into a coffee shop and then, so they tell me, into a forty-message email thread. I was present for the corridor part and have read the thread, and I could not decide who was getting the better of it at any point. Their exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) Daniel opened by saying asymbolia refutes the claim that pain is intrinsically bad. The patients retain the sensory-discriminative dimension in full and lack only the aversive one, so the felt quality of pain and its badness must be separable. On his account there are two things people conflate under "pain's badness": its *hedonic load*, which is genuinely bad and which asymbolics lack, and its *alarm function*, which is not bad at all but useful. Pain is bad only derivatively, because it normally carries a hedonic load; therefore a treatment that abolished aversiveness while preserving nociception would be a straightforward improvement, and that is roughly the ambition behind cingulotomy and high-dose opioid management. (2) Ilse replied that the last clause inverts the clinical picture and reveals what his distinction leaves out. Asymbolia is not an improvement; it is a disability. Because the aversive system is what gives nociception its *practical authority* — its capacity to make a demand on the patient rather than merely inform him — the asymbolic fails to guard a joint, fails to shift his weight, sits on a scalding surface, and presents with ulcers, fractures and neuropathic arthropathy. If pain were bad only through its hedonic load, there would be no sense in which restoring aversiveness to such a patient benefits him, and yet clinicians plainly treat the restoration of protective pain responses as a benefit. (3) Daniel said this concedes the point while trying to escape it: she has simply relocated badness from a felt evil to a functional deficit, and a functional deficit is bad in the same way that a lost blink reflex or a lost sense of smell is bad — instrumentally, and without any implication that the sensation in question was evil. Nobody calls the blink reflex intrinsically bad. So asymbolia's cost is entirely one of lost warning, which is compatible with, indeed predicted by, his view. (4) Ilse answered that he owes an explanation of the testimony. When a patient says it hurts but that he doesn't mind, Daniel reads the second clause as a verdict on the first, but there is a live alternative on which his introspective access to the aversive dimension is itself degraded, so the utterance licenses no inference that a fully unpleasant pain is present and simply fails to grip. And the case reports are not as clean as his reading needs: some described patients do flinch, withdraw, and avoid re-exposure in at least some contexts, which suggests attenuation and context-sensitivity rather than clean absence — in which case asymbolia is a gradient phenomenon and says nothing about separability. (5) Daniel said that move is self-undermining. If suffering is what makes pain bad, she cannot also hold that patients are poor judges of whether they suffer, since she would then have no way of determining when the badness is present; her account would be immune to evidence. Moreover the flinching data helps him: reflex withdrawal is not aversiveness, so withdrawal surviving while the minding does not is further reason to drive a wedge between nociceptive response and badness. (6) Ilse then proposed a test case. Suppose a drug existed that, in an asymbolic patient whose condition makes frequent tissue damage unavoidable, restored the aversive quality of nociception while leaving sensory acuity, cognition and mood untouched. On Daniel's view administering it adds intrinsic badness and buys only an instrumental signal, so it makes the patient's life worse; on hers it may be a net benefit. She said that if his view reliably delivers the wrong verdict about such cases, the view should go rather than the intuition. Daniel responded that her case smuggles the dispute into the stipulation by describing the instrumental gain as substantial; she responded that his description of the gain as "purely instrumental" performs the identical trick in the opposite direction. At that point both declared the other to be arguing in a circle and stopped.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the six numbered moves, please rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and give a short justification of the rating, saying in particular where the burden of proof actually sits in move (4) — whether the skepticism about introspective reports there is a legitimate cost to Daniel's view or an ad hoc escape hatch, and whether his reply in move (5) successfully charges it with unfalsifiability. Then tell me who is right about the underlying question, whether asymbolia shows that the badness of pain is separable from pain's felt quality, and who handles Ilse's drug case better. Finally, please say what both of them are missing, and state whether their disagreement is substantive or partly verbal — a dispute about the work that "intrinsically bad" is being asked to do — and if it is partly verbal, propose the disambiguation that would either dissolve the argument or relocate it to a place where it can actually be settled.
