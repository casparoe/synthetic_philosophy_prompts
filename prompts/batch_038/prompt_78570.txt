Second-year political theory PhD student here, ostensibly writing my quals paper on democratic boundaries, actually just going in circles in a way I've started to suspect is itself the topic. I know the standard setup fine: you can't settle who's in the demos by a democratic decision, because the decision already presupposes a demos, so the question has to be answered from outside or not at all. I can state that, I've read the chapter, no problem. What I can't find anywhere is a name for the pattern I hit every single time I try to actually *use* one of the proposed fixes.

Three instances from this month alone. (1) A paper argues the franchise should extend to everyone whose interests are affected by a decision — so not just residents but commuters, downstream populations, foreigners hit by trade policy. Fine, I'm sympathetic. But then who adopts the all-affected rule? The legislature. So the demos that installs the criterion is the demos the criterion was designed to correct, and the people the rule was meant to let in have no say in whether the rule exists. (2) Someone in my workgroup proposed the opposite move: the boundary question isn't democratic at all, it's fixed by moral facts about who is subject to which coercive order, and democracy only operates inside a frame morality draws first. That answers my objection cleanly, but it also seems to imply the shape of the demos is never anybody's decision — it's just true, like a fact about chemistry, something we discover rather than choose, which is a weird thing for a democratic theory to end up saying. (3) So I tried the historical escape: borders get set by treaties, conquests, referendums, whatever actually happened, and legitimacy accretes over time. But that just relocates the problem, because the treaty was signed by some demos, and the plebiscite on secession was voted in by precisely the people asking whether they should be allowed to vote.

Here's the thing I think is distinctive, and why I don't think I'm just restating the familiar puzzle. Every candidate answer to "who's in?" gets pushed, under questioning, onto one of two sides: either the demos decides its own boundaries and you're going in a circle, or something outside democracy decides them and the boundary stops being a political question at all. And whichever side a theory grabs onto, the next critic pushes it back toward the other one, and it slides. It's not a one-shot impossibility so much as an oscillation between two horns that proposals keep getting bounced between — and the bouncing seems to me to be the interesting part, because it raises a question the one-shot version doesn't: whether any answer can stay planted on one horn without that itself being a cost, and if so, what kind of cost. My supervisor thinks I'm padding. I want to check that before I build a chapter on it.

So: is there an established name for this? I keep seeing words like bootstrapping, regress, and vicious circle used nearby, and there's presumably some older story about a rule not being able to validate itself, but none of them seem to be doing the work I need. What would actually help me is (a) the nearest existing concepts, and for each one, exactly how what I've described differs — is mine just the standard problem with extra steps, or a species of a general regress that also shows up in law and in epistemology? (b) whether anyone has focused on the *movement* between the two horns rather than the horns themselves; and (c) if it genuinely is unnamed, a name and a definition sharp enough that I could test whether a given proposal falls inside it — including boundary cases you pick, like whether a constitutional convention that ratifies its own electorate counts, whether a randomly drawn demos escapes the whole structure, or whether a federation deciding admission of new members is a case of it at one level up. Don't just hand me a label; I need the edges of the category, and where it stops applying.
