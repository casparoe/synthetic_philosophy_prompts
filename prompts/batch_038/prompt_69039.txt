A chapter in a popular history of the modern world frames its whole subject in its opening pages like this:

'For more than a thousand years China was the most technically advanced civilization on earth — printing, gunpowder, the compass, cast iron — while Europe was a poor and fragmented backwater. Then, within a couple of generations around 1600, Europe produced a mathematical and experimental science, and shortly after that an industrial economy; China produced neither. Historians call this the Needham question, and it is really the Great Divergence under another name, only about knowledge rather than wealth. The puzzle is well posed, and the reason it is well posed is worth being clear about: both halves of it are matters of fact rather than interpretation. Europe did develop modern science and China did not, and a genuine absence stands in as much need of a cause as a genuine presence does. The standard answers are institutional and intellectual — political fragmentation forced European thinkers to compete for patrons, secure property rights let merchants fund investigation, and a theology of a lawgiving God made nature look like a system of rules waiting to be decoded — whereas China's unified bureaucracy channelled its cleverest men into examination preparation, and its elite preferred moral self-cultivation to mechanism. So a single list of causes discharges both halves of the question: whatever explains Europe's success explains China's failure.'

I'm inclined to think this is roughly fair — Europe obviously ended up with Newtonian mechanics and China obviously didn't — but I half-remember from a philosophy of science course that 'why P rather than Q' questions are indexed to a contrast class, so that swapping the foil can change what counts as an answer or dissolve the question entirely, and I can't tell whether that has real bite here or is a technicality. Go through the passage claim by claim: what would a historian of the divergence sign off on, what would they want qualified, and what would they reject; and separately, does the well-posedness argument actually work, and does my contrast-class point apply? If the framing needs correcting, is there still a question left for the rest of the book to answer?
