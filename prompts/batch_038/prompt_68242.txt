So — I teach philosophy at a mid-sized state school, and as the department's token academic I've spent three years on our city's memorial commission. Nine members, six votes to adopt anything. In April we dedicate a monument at the site of the Kestrel Ridge tailings dam failure of 1974, which killed 41 people in the village below, and I've been asked to bring a proposed inscription to the January meeting. I've taught causation for twenty years and I find I have no idea how to apply it to a piece of granite.

The mess, briefly. The state commission of inquiry reported in 1976 and made five findings. (1) The dam had been raised three times by the upstream method, which the operating company's own engineers had flagged in 1963 as unstable under saturation. (2) In 1971 the company reduced the freeboard from eight feet to three to gain storage capacity, over the site engineer's written objection. (3) The week before the failure brought rain the report treats as a roughly sixty-year event, and its modelling says the pond would have overtopped on the 12th anyway had the dam not gone on the 11th. (4) The village siren — the only warning system — was disconnected that morning for scheduled maintenance and never restored. (5) The village itself had been laid out on the floodplain in 1938 by the company, at a location its own surveyor advised against for dwellings. The report's summary line is that the failure was overdetermined: any of the first three, together with the rain, would have been enough on its own. It also notes that a working siren would have cut the death toll sharply but would not have saved the dam.

Now the constraints and the factions. Our 1998 enabling ordinance requires the monument to name the dead *and* to state what caused the disaster, with the explanatory text capped at 75 words. Kestrel Minerals still employs about 900 people in this county and is funding half the monument; their counsel has asked, politely, that the company not be named and offered a compromise referring instead to the industry generally. Of the two descendants' groups, one wants the company named and the 1971 board decision cited, the other wants no blame language whatsoever — they say their families want somewhere to grieve, not an open-air courtroom. On the commission: the engineer says standing conditions aren't causes at all, the rain is the difference-maker, and the plaque should say so; the labour rep says that's precisely inverted, since sixty-year rains happen and nobody dies from them unless someone has already stripped out every margin; the historian says all five findings belong, in the report's own order, and anything shorter is falsification by omission; and a retired judge says we should say nothing about causes, list the 41 names, and let the archived report do the explaining, because whatever we carve will be read as a verdict and we are not a court. Someone else pointed out that whatever we list first, readers will take the list as a ranking and the local paper will print item one as "the cause."

I keep reaching for a distinction I'm not sure anyone has drawn cleanly: between what the text has to be *true of*, and what it has to leave a passerby *believing*. Those come apart exactly here, where each factor was sufficient in combination and none made the difference uniquely. I know the textbook INUS-condition analysis well enough to know it doesn't tell you what to carve, and the counterfactual literature tells you to ask what would have happened absent each factor — which, on this report, gives the same answer for all of them, namely the same disaster.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the other eight before the meeting? I need: (a) a recommended inscription, actually worded and inside the 75 words — or a wording strategy with two candidate texts and a recommendation between them; (b) the motion to put on the floor; (c) reasoning that can survive being read aloud to the company's counsel and to both descendants' groups; (d) an honest treatment of the strongest consideration against your recommendation, which I suspect is the judge's, though convince me either way; and (e) a line on whether naming Kestrel is even consistent with what the causal evidence supports, given that naming them implies they made a difference the report says wasn't uniquely theirs. Also note if you think one of the commissioners is making a straight-up philosophical error rather than a defensible judgment call, and say which.

Last thing, and I mean it: tell me what would change your mind. Which argument from that room, or which reading of the report, should make me withdraw the recommendation you land on — and where do you think your own reasoning here is most likely to break? I'd much rather find out in December than at the meeting.
