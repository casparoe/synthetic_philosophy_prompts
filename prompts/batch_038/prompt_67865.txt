I am a fourth-year PhD student in statistics, and for the past year I have served as the graduate analyst on a joint project between my department and our state's environmental agency. The question before us is whether a decommissioned industrial site must be remediated because of a contaminant for which we have eleven rodent bioassays, two in vitro studies, no human epidemiology, and a mechanism that the toxicologists in our own working group disagree about. Under the authorizing statute, the agency must act if the modeled probability that exposure will produce excess cancer cases above a stated level exceeds five percent, so somebody has to write down a number.

In the last month three difficulties have surfaced, and I would like them assessed separately rather than in the aggregate.

1. My supervisor will not accept anything but a point estimate accompanied by a conventional interval. When I told him that what the evidence actually licenses is a set of candidate probability assignments — some of which put the risk near zero, some of which put it well above the statutory threshold — and that reporting the set itself, rather than one of its members plus an error bar, is the honest thing to do, he replied that this is indecision dressed up as rigor, and that a client who is not told which member of my set to use has not been told anything at all. I believe he is wrong, but I cannot say where.

2. This is the one that worries me. A further animal study is now in press, and when I worked out what its result would do to my set, I found that the set is exactly as wide after conditioning on either possible outcome as it was before. The new evidence appears to buy me nothing, by construction. My supervisor says this demonstrates that I chose my set badly. My own suspicion, which I cannot yet defend, is that the difficulty is structural: that any set wide enough to represent how little we actually know will behave this way with respect to some piece of pending evidence, and that the fault therefore lies somewhere other than in my choice of set.

3. Finally, the agency's decision rule is conservative, and a committee member observed that because a conservative rule always acts on the pessimistic end of a range, a range is functionally indistinguishable from the single number sitting at its boundary. If he is right, my objection to the point estimate collapses and I should withdraw it.

I am not looking for general guidance about communicating uncertainty to non-specialists. I want to know whether the representation I favor is defensible on its own terms, what each of these three objections actually establishes and what it fails to establish, and what I should put in front of the committee in the end. I would also ask that you distinguish, throughout, what is settled among the people who work on these questions from what remains genuinely disputed between them. Where the field is split — and I suspect it is split on at least the second of my three points — please say so plainly, and identify which position you find better supported and why, rather than writing as though one side were the consensus.
