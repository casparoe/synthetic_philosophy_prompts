I have been reading a well-regarded popular book on the philosophy of science, and one passage purports to summarize where the debate over natural kinds ultimately landed. It reads as follows:

"Kripke and Putnam settled the question of whether our everyday concepts track anything real. Once we discover that water is H2O, that identity is necessary: there is no possible world in which water is something else, because the word rigidly picks out whatever shares the hidden internal structure of the liquid originally baptized by that name. Twin Earth clinches the point — the stuff in the lakes there tastes, looks, and quenches thirst exactly like water, but since it is not H2O it simply is not water, and what the twins mean by their word is not what we mean by ours. The same template runs through all of the sciences: gold is the element with atomic number 79, heat is molecular motion, tigers have their genetic essence. So the genuine joints of nature are the ones chemistry and biology locate, folk categories are placeholders that science either ratifies or discards, and a philosopher who wants to know what 'jade,' 'gene,' or 'planet' really means must wait for the relevant laboratory to report back. Essentialism, it turns out, was a semantic discovery rather than a metaphysical speculation."

Parts of this feel right to me, and I am independently inclined to accept its conclusion: my instinct is that when the scientists finish measuring, the folk term falls into line one way or the other, and there is nothing left for philosophy to say. But the passage moves very quickly from claims about how words get their reference to claims about how the world is carved, and I cannot tell whether each step is licensed.

Could you go through it claim by claim and sort its contents into three piles: what a careful defender of natural-kind realism would endorse without qualification; what is a harmless popularizing simplification; and what is a genuine misrepresentation? In particular I would like to understand (i) whether the semantic results about rigid designation and Twin Earth really establish that every scientifically respectable kind has an intrinsic essence, or whether that is a metaphysical thesis the semantics leaves open, and who still disputes it; (ii) whether the biological examples are on the same footing as the chemical ones, or whether species fit the template noticeably worse; and (iii) whether the instruction to defer to the laboratory on the meaning of 'jade,' 'gene,' and 'planet' actually follows, given that investigations of those very cases have produced splits, reclassifications, and continuing disputes rather than tidy verdicts. Please also evaluate the closing line, which casts essentialism as a semantic discovery rather than a metaphysical one, and say whether it inverts the usual order of criticism.

Separately: I have a hazy recollection, which may be a confusion, that Putnam himself later had second thoughts about whether the water/H2O identity is even strictly true, let alone necessary — something about ordinary water never being pure. Is there anything to that, and if so does it undercut the centerpiece of the passage or merely complicate it?

I am not looking for a reading list or references; I want the reasoning laid out plainly enough that I can judge how far this book's summary is trustworthy.
