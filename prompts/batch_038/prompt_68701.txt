Been going back and forth with the alignment forum on and off for like fifteen years now, and the iterated amplification-and-distillation argument is the one I keep returning to, mostly because nobody ever settles it — the optimists and the skeptics just restate their priors at each other in longer and longer posts.

So here's what I want: pretend the two camps have finally agreed to sit down for a real adversarial collaboration, and draft the joint statement they'd both sign. Not a survey of the debate, not a verdict on who's right — the document itself.

Set it up like this. Camp A, call them the decompositionists, hold that if you build the system by recursion — weak models assisting human overseers, doing a hard task by breaking it into subtasks, then distilling the amplified system's behaviour into one fast model, then iterating with that distilled model as the new base — then alignment survives each round, because every individual step is small enough to be checked, and because the whole engine of the construction is the asymmetry between judging an answer and producing one. Camp B, the compounders, hold that this is close to a category error: reliability doesn't propagate down a recursion the way the story assumes; the overseers' blind spots get inherited and then amplified; the distillation step is where the guarantees quietly evaporate, since you're training a fast model on outputs nobody can re-derive or re-check; and the "checking is easier than doing" asymmetry runs backwards for exactly the failures that matter, because a subtly misaligned output is often harder to detect than it was to generate.

Now the part I actually want you to invent, so the two sides have something specific to fight over rather than trading slogans:

- A four-level recursion doing something concrete, say producing a 200-page assessment of whether a research programme is worth funding. Level 4's output is checked by level-3 humans-plus-assistants. Ask the awkward question: does anyone at level 3 read it, and if not, what is the oversight actually consisting of?
- A quantity the compounders call oversight debt: the standing gap between what the level-n system can produce and what the level-n overseer can discriminate, and whether that gap widens, stays flat, or gets absorbed as you add levels.
- Camp A's ε-budget: each round costs at most ε of alignment, so with small enough ε and enough rounds you converge. Camp B's reply, which I think is their best card and should be stated at full strength: the errors aren't independent across rounds. They're correlated, because the dataset each distillation is trained on comes from the previous level, and because the overseer at level n is itself partly a product of level n-1's distillation. So it isn't ε per round with random sign, it's a bias that recurs with the same sign every time.
- Whatever other crux-generating machinery you think belongs in there.

Deliverables: a numbered section of claims both camps can actually sign — including about the formal shape of the recursion, about which failure modes are real in principle even if they disagree about likelihood, and about what each side concedes the other side has never been shown wrong about. Then a disagreements table, one row per dispute, each stated in wording that the relevant camp's own people would accept as fair — no strawmanning, and no watering either side down into something vacuous. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling it would genuinely move at least one camp, and for each one the argument, thought experiment, or — if you think there could be one — real experiment that both would accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction written down beforehand.

And the thing I care most about: commit. Don't hand me a scrupulously balanced document and then a shrug. Where you think a supposed disagreement is verbal — I suspect at least one of them is two different definitions of "alignment" in a trenchcoat — say which, and give the disambiguation that dissolves it. Where you think a camp's public position is weaker than its own members would admit, put that in a short unsigned-by-anyone section after the document, don't smuggle it into the neutral wording. On the predictions, give the ones you'd sign yourself, not the ones each camp would find most comfortable. And if you actually think the collaboration is pointless because one camp is right and the dispute isn't live, say so and argue it — but still show me what the joint statement would have looked like, because I want to see what you're claiming can't be written.
