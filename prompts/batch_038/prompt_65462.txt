I'm a doctoral student in philosophy, and for the past three sessions I've been auditing a reading group on testimony run by a former research editor who spent twenty-odd years vetting contested material for a reference publisher and is now assembling a documentary sourcebook of disputed reports. Each meeting she circulates a dossier — she mixes real cases with invented ones and never says which is which — and puts a single house-style question to the room: should the sourcebook print the account as established, print it only as-reported (the witnesses' credibility affirmed, the content not endorsed), or leave it out? We vote, she then gives her own verdict with one sentence of gloss, and she has never articulated the rule she is applying, though the group opened the term by reading Hume's essay on miracles and she was dismissive of what she called the undergraduate reading of it. I want to predict her verdict on the ninth dossier, which arrives next week and which I have already seen.

Her verdicts so far:

1. An 1847 account of a stone falling through a farmhouse roof at midday, heard by the farmer and two labourers and recovered still warm, the three accounts taken separately thirty years apart and never varying, the specimen's composition later matching known meteoritic material. Print as established. Her gloss: the stone is on the shelf and the stone does not argue.
2. A 1762 account of a man certified dead by a physician, laid out two days, then reviving and living eleven more years, sworn by twelve townspeople of unimpeachable standing. Leave it out. Her gloss: the physician's own casebook shows he certified death by the standard of his day, which did not include a pulse.
3. A 1934 account of a sudden cure before several hundred witnesses, examined by a standing medical bureau, but with the original diagnosis never independently confirmed, no record of comparable cases that went uncured, and a documented regional incidence of the same kind of spontaneous remission. Leave it out. Her gloss: the bureau examined only the cases sent to it.
4. A 1987 account of full sensation restored within ninety minutes to a surgically severed and repaired nerve, attested by eleven unconnected witnesses whose relevant accuracy rate had been audited, with the examining surgeon a paid skeptic who published his own conversion, and no physical specimen ever recovered. Print as-reported. Her gloss: I cannot say how they were all wrong, and I cannot say that they were right.
5. Two rival shrines in one valley claiming an identical cure at the same spring forty years apart, each supported by its own impeccable local testimony. Print neither. Her gloss: neither side's witnesses gain by the other side's losing.
6. A 2011 anomalous trace from one station in an amateur network, whose operator later admitted to drink and uncalibrated equipment, and which no other station in the network recorded. Leave it out.
7. The final hours of a ship lost with all hands in 1798, surviving in a single deposition taken eleven years afterwards by an insurer's agent with an interest in the outcome. Leave it out.
8. A 1978 account by a lighthouse keeper of twenty years' faultless service who reported that his lamp went out for four minutes and was relit by a match he had not struck. Print as-reported. Her gloss: improbable, not impossible, and one man's word for it.

The ninth dossier concerns a volunteer observing network of some 140 stations, whose collective error rate over eleven years of operation had been formally audited and published, together with the false-alarm rate of comparable reports. On one night in the network's sixth year, all 141 observers on duty separately recorded the same thing, none aware that the others were observing it; every log was written up, sealed and posted before dawn and opened only years later; no observer had any connection to any other, to the auditor, or to any party with an interest; and what they recorded is the sort of thing that would, if it happened, run against a law nobody in the room disputes. During the session, three accounts of how they were all mistaken were proposed — an auroral afterimage, contagion through suggestion, and a coordinated hoax — and I understand she disposed of each, chiefly on the strength of the sealed logs and the observers' mutual independence. The file contains no positive account of what did happen, no physical trace of any kind, and nothing that speaks to whether the witnesses were confabulating.

Predict her verdict on the ninth dossier. I want, first, the candidate rules that account for all eight of her earlier verdicts — stated sharply enough that I could check them against a ninth case I invent myself, and including any rule you think does better than the obvious probabilistic one about testimony being credited only where its falsehood would be more extraordinary than the event it reports. Second, say what each candidate rule yields on dossier nine, since I suspect they diverge and that at least two of them would have delivered a different verdict on dossier four, which would be worth knowing before I argue the point in the room. Third, give me your best single guess at her verdict, with the reason you prefer that rule over the others — her one-sentence glosses seem to me to carry more information than the group has been giving them credit for, especially the two that sound deliberately symmetrical. Fourth, identify which of the eight is the closest analog to dossier nine and where the analogy strains. And fifth, describe one further dossier, concrete enough that I could circulate it at the following session, that would discriminate between the leading candidate rules — not a case they already handle differently, but one on which they must come apart. Finally, if you think her eight verdicts genuinely underdetermine the ninth, say which principle she would be committing herself to with each of the three available options, so that I can put the choice to her as a choice about principles rather than about this case.
