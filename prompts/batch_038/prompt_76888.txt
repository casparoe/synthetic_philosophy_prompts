Retired three years ago after 35 as a physicist in defence research — mostly sensors and target discrimination — and I've fallen hard for the philosophy of war. The ethics faculty at our regional staff college have let me give a 40 minute talk next month, and a lot of them know me from my old job.

My line: the moral equality of combatants isn't a deep moral truth, and the revisionists are right about that, but its also not a blunder. It's more like a no-fault insurance scheme between states. Because nobody can reliably adjudicate whose cause is just while the fighting is going on, states collectively agree to treat all combatants alike — the way two drivers agree in advance not to litigate fault. The convention earns its keep from what it buys (reciprocity, POW treatment, fewer atrocities), not from any right the unjust soldier has.

Audience, maybe 25 people: mostly career officers, several with recent combat tours, who were taught jus in bello as rules to follow and will not enjoy being told their training rests on a convenient fiction. Two academic military ethicists — one broadly Walzerian, one sympathetic to the revisionists. And a legal advisor who cares about combatant privilege and what any of this does to the Geneva conventions. They all know I'm an amateur.

Write me a prep sheet. The objections I'll actually get from these people, in roughly the order they'll come and ranked by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way each group would phrase them — the lawyer won't put it like the lieutenant does. For each, a two minute reply plus a note on where my reply is soft. Flag the one I'm hoping nobody raises; I suspect its either that this just collapses into rule-consequentialism with extra steps, or what my view tells a lieutenant who has good evidence his own government lied to him about the casus belli. And tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so I'm not spending the whole Q&A defending it.
