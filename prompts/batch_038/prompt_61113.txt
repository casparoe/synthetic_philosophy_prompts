I am preparing a session for a reading group on minds and machines, and one member keeps pressing a favourite illustration into service as an argument. The illustration is Hofstadter's MU puzzle: begin with the string MI and apply the four rules of the MIU system — append a U to any string that ends in I; double whatever follows the initial M; replace any occurrence of III with U; delete any occurrence of UU — and try to reach MU. It cannot be reached, and the way to see this is to stop generating strings and notice an invariant instead: the number of I's starts at 1 and can only be doubled or lowered by three, so it is never a multiple of three, whereas MU has none at all.

My colleague's use of it runs as follows. Grinding out strings from the rules is what a machine does; stepping back to the invariant is what he calls jumping out of the system; and since no procedure confined to those rules could ever see that MU is out of reach, human mathematical insight cannot be such a procedure — a cousin of the older arguments from Gödel's theorem against mechanism. He then extends the moral to large language models: they too operate within a fixed repertoire of string transformations, so they can search but cannot step outside, which he offers as the explanation for why they thrash on problems whose solution requires a change of viewpoint.

I doubt the illustration bears that load, but I want the audit rather than my hunch. Please give me the explicit mapping — what in the puzzle plays the machine, what plays the rules, what plays the Gödel sentence, what plays the insight that steps outside — and then separate the disanalogies that merely limit the illustration's charm from those that actually defeat the inference. Three suspected pressure points, offered as hypotheses rather than conclusions. First, the invariant argument is an ordinary piece of number theory, reproducible in any system strong enough to do arithmetic, so what looks like leaving the system may be only a move to a stronger one, and then to a stronger one again; is jumping out of the system anything over and above that ascent? Second, unlike a genuine Gödel sentence, the unreachability of MU is not expressible anywhere inside MIU, which has no negation and no vocabulary for its own strings; and derivability in MIU in fact admits of a complete decision procedure — a string is derivable from MI exactly when it begins with M, contains no other M, and has a number of I's that is not a multiple of three — so the puzzle may illustrate a system whose limits are fully surveyable rather than one that is essentially incomplete. Third, in the case of a language model there is no enumerated axiom set, no derivation relation to preserve, and no reason to regard the system as knowably sound, which are precisely the premises such an argument needs; nor is there a determinate single question of which its outputs are the attempted proofs.

Tell me which of these bite and which are beside the point, what the analogy quietly imports from the puzzle that the target lacks — the transparency and finitude of the rule list, the certainty of their application, the existence of one well-posed question with a definite answer — and whether my colleague's conclusion survives. If it can be rescued by relocating it, say to what: perhaps the difference between generating candidates and discovering invariants, which is a claim about heuristics rather than about formal limits. And if some other illustration would do the work he wants from this one, describe what it would have to have in it.
