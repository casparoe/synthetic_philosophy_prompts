**Question 3 (30 points; suggested length 700–900 words).** Epicurus argues that death is nothing to us. (a) State his argument as precisely as you can. Identify which premises are doing the real work, and say what would have to be false for the argument to fail. (b) Lucretius presses a further "symmetry" or "mirror" argument in *De Rerum Natura* III. Explain how it is supposed to add force to the Epicurean case, and say whether it is an independent argument or one that depends on Epicurus's own premises. (c) Nagel's "Death" develops what is now called the deprivation account. And here is the student's answer, transcribed:

> (a) Epicurus's argument goes like this. Every good and every bad thing consists in sensation — pleasure is good, pain is bad, and there is nothing else that is good or bad for a person. Death is the permanent end of all sensation. So death is neither good nor bad for the one who dies: where we are, death hasn't come, and where death has come, we aren't, so there is never anyone present for it to be bad for. That's why he thinks the wise man neither clings to life nor is afraid of not living. The point isn't mainly metaphysical, it's therapeutic: the argument is meant to take away the fear of death, and he thinks that fear is what actually ruins our lives. The premises doing the real work are the first two, hedonism about the good and the claim that death ends sensation. Deny the second — say, by holding that the soul survives — and the whole thing collapses. Deny the first and there could be goods and bads that aren't sensations at all, in which case death could be bad without ever being felt. Notice this covers dying as well: the process of dying terminates in death, and everything on the far side of it isn't ours, so there's nobody left to be harmed by the dying either.
>
> (b) Lucretius — though really Epicurus makes the point first in the Letter to Menoeceus and Lucretius only expands on it — says the stretch of time before we were born is a mirror of the stretch after we die. Nature holds it up to us as a picture: you feel no grief about the countless centuries before your birth, even though there was a you-shaped gap in the world and you missed all of it, so you have no business grieving the centuries after. This adds force because it doesn't lean on the sensation premise the same way. Even someone who thinks death is bad because it deprives us of goods still owes an explanation of why they regret only one of the two symmetrical deprivations. So it's an independent argument that traps the deprivation theorist on his own ground, and its target is regret rather than fear.
>
> (c) The deprivation account says death is bad because of what it takes away: it robs us of the goods we would otherwise have had. Nagel's real argument isn't a head-to-head comparison of possible lives, it's an attack on the assumption that a harm has to be experienced by the person it befalls. His cases: a man betrayed by friends who laugh at him behind his back and never find out, and an intelligent person reduced by a stroke to a cheerful, childlike condition whom everyone pities even though he isn't unhappy. In both, we say the person is worse off without his experiencing anything bad, and that refutes Epicurus's hedonism, since the betrayed man's balance of pleasure is unchanged and yet he's worse off. Hedonism is Epicurus's first premise, so once it goes the argument goes with it. What survives is that the two of them agree about the facts: neither thinks there's a moment at which the dead person is suffering. Their quarrel is over whether to label the deprivation a harm, which makes it largely verbal. Nagel does owe us something, though — he has to explain why the symmetry fails, and if he can't, Lucretius wins by default.
