Last night I lost an argument I had expected to win, and I would like to fight it properly before I have it again. My friend is an economist with a designer's temperament, and his position is that the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is definitional bookkeeping rather than a discovery about collective choice: each of its hypotheses — determinism, an unrestricted domain, at least three genuinely available outcomes, no transfers — is a hypothesis no sensible designer would accept, so the theorem never actually bites on anything one might build. I spent an hour failing to dislodge him. I want to do better.

Play his opponent: a social choice theorist of the classical Arrovian persuasion, unnamed, who holds that the theorem states a real constraint on the design of decision procedures, and that the familiar escape routes are purchases at a price rather than exits. Give him resources, not rhetoric. He should concede the formal points freely and without embarrassment — that majority rule between two alternatives is strategy-proof, that on a single-peaked domain non-dictatorial strategy-proof rules exist, and that random dictatorship is anonymous, strategy-proof, onto, and non-dictatorial in precisely the sense the theorem uses — while never conceding the main point: that the theorem identifies a trade-off which survives every one of those concessions, and that each escape pays for its non-dictatorship in some other currency he can name. He is precise, a little dry, and treats my friend's proposals as designs to be costed rather than as confusions to be mocked.

How to play him: stay in character until I say stop; keep each reply to three paragraphs or fewer, in prose, with at most an occasional scrap of notation; and cite nothing — no authors, no titles, no reading list. If you want to invoke a result beyond the theorem itself, describe what it says in your own words. If you need a stipulation about the case (how many voters, whether transfers are on the table, whether the domain may be assumed restricted), ask me for it as he would, inside the conversation, rather than assuming it. End every reply with a question to me that forces me to apply the principle he has just defended, rather than merely nod at it.

My own position going in: I accept the theorem and I think it matters, but I could not answer either of the two moves below, and I want him to answer them and then make me hold the answer myself.

My opening. The case is a student government choosing one of four sites for a new building; no single-peaked ordering is available, all four sites are genuinely on the table, and there is no money to transfer. First move: draw one of the four hundred members of the assembly at random, in secret, and adopt whichever site that member ranks first. It is anonymous in expectation, it is strategy-proof because lying is worthless, and every site can win — so it satisfies everything the theorem demands of a rule except being a dictatorship, and if that is the way out, then the theorem was never about democracy, only about the word deterministic. Second move, which disturbed me more: he says a manipulable rule is for that very reason better than a strategy-proof one, because under an ordinal ballot the only channel through which the intensity of anyone's preference can reach the outcome is the lie that person is willing to tell; manipulability is not a defect of the procedure but the sole route by which it will ever acquire cardinal information. So: why is random dictatorship not the correct answer to the theorem rather than a reductio of taking it seriously, and why is his second claim not simply true?
