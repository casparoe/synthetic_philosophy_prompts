A friend and I spent most of a Saturday arguing about the frame problem and got nowhere, and I have come to suspect the argument failed because we were using the phrase to name two different difficulties. What I want is a careful, self-contained conceptual explanation that lets me locate where the exchange went wrong — not a survey of proposed solutions and not an account of who said what.

The point of departure was the familiar one: for any action you can state what it changes, but you also seem obliged to state that it changes nothing else — that lifting a book off a table does not repaint the table, does not move the Pacific, does not repeal a law of physics — and the inventory of non-effects has no end. My friend holds that this is an artifact of an impoverished formalism rather than a real problem. His picture is that if you have (a) a dependence structure over the relevant state variables and (b) a default rule to the effect that whatever is not determined to change persists, then nothing need be enumerated: you propagate forward along the dependence structure, and the default handles everything the structure fails to touch. He describes this as a matter of letting the model carry the frame, and he thinks the philosophical elaboration of the topic converted a bookkeeping annoyance into a supposed crisis about intelligence as such.

My counter was that (b) inherits its credibility entirely from the assumption that the dependence structure is complete and correct, and that no system can build, revise, or even consult such a structure without making prior judgments about which portions of it bear on the case at hand — so the difficulty returns one level up, in the maintenance and interrogation of the model rather than in the drafting of axioms. He replied that what I had described is simply the problem of induction, or of priors, which every learning system faces and which has nothing especially to do with frames; and that if I want to insist on a separate difficulty, I owe him a case in which a system equipped with an accurate causal model and a persistence default still confronts something over and above ordinary empirical uncertainty.

At some point we converged on a test while disagreeing about what it demonstrates. Call it the index test. Suppose that for each action you can produce the list of state propositions whose truth values must be re-examined afterward, and that this list can be generated mechanically from the model instead of being written out by hand. My friend says the index test is passed trivially: trace which variables depend on those the action touches and you have your list, which shows there was never a genuine problem. I objected that the list cannot be keyed to the action alone but must be keyed to the action together with the agent's current goals and background beliefs, since what deserves re-examination after opening a refrigerator differs for someone looking for leftovers, someone checking whether the light comes on, and someone hiding from an intruder. He accepted the amendment without hesitation: key the index to action–goal pairs, because goal-dependence is precisely what makes the index finite, and it is the point of the exercise rather than an embarrassment. I said the index must then be rebuilt whenever goals or beliefs shift, and that rebuilding it is itself a matter of settling what is relevant to what — the very thing we set out to explain. He said I was describing an ordinary maintenance cost and dressing it up as a regress.

With that in front of you, please explain the following.

1. Draw as sharp a line as you can among three difficulties I think we kept conflating: the impossibility of enumerating all the conditions under which an action has its intended effect (the qualification problem), the impossibility of enumerating all of an action's indirect consequences (the ramification problem), and the impossibility of delimiting in advance what bears on a situation at all. For each, say whether a dependence structure plus a persistence default genuinely solves it, partially solves it by relocating it, or fails to address it — and explain what turns on the difference among those three verdicts, rather than simply asserting it.

2. Take up the index test directly. Is goal-indexing the relevance list a benign refinement, as my friend maintains, or does it reintroduce the problem it was meant to settle? In particular, explain what makes a regress vicious as opposed to merely long: what would have to be true for an open-ended chain of relevance determinations to be harmless, and if such a chain can be harmless, does anything survive of the claim that the frame problem marks a limit in principle rather than a cost that scales?

3. There is a distinction we slid across repeatedly that I would like made explicit: the difference between a problem that has no solution and a problem whose solution cannot be stated in advance of the situations it covers. My friend reasons that if a bounded domain admits a working persistence default, there is no principled problem, only a scaling question. I think there is a real gap between a system that has been handed a frame and a system that can construct, extend, or repair frames, and that the second capacity is what the inflated talk was reaching for. Is that gap coherent? Can it be characterized precisely, and what has to be given up in order to do so?

4. Finally, the stock demonstration of the problem — listing what moving a book fails to change — is often dismissed as a rhetorical device, and I would like to know why. Is the underlying complaint about the length of the list, which would make it a combinatorial point, or about its character, which would make it the claim that no finite specification can be closed under the exceptions? These are quite different charges, and I want to understand which of them, if either, survives the reply my friend was making.

Throughout, treat the positions as I have stated them rather than reconstructing anyone's published argument, and where you think my friend or I simply got it wrong, say so outright.
