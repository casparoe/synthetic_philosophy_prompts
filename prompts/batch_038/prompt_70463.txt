I spent most of yesterday arguing with a friend about the Arras sequence in *Les Misérables*, and neither of us moved, so I want a considered third opinion. Please answer as a philosopher of religion who also reads nineteenth-century French fiction closely — someone equally at home with David Lewis's essay on whether we believe in penal substitution and with Hugo's novel. Think the problem through before you write, and if it helps, work out privately first which episodes do the real argumentative work and where the analogy breaks; I want only the finished reading.

The idea I have in mind. Lewis pushes back on the ordinary assumption that punishment is strictly identity-locked, so that a substitute's suffering could never count as the offender's punishment. As I understand him, he makes two main moves. First, our own practice is already substitutionist wherever fines are concerned: a fine paid by a friend, an employer, or an insurer discharges the sentence even though the offender himself is deprived of nothing, which shows the tie between guilt and the particular body that suffers is looser than retributivist rhetoric implies. Second, an evil inflicted on an offender is never confined to his own skin — his mother, his wife, his children suffer with him, and that suffering is not a regrettable side effect but part of what makes the punishment the punishment it is; a designated substitute who bears part of it therefore marks a difference of degree rather than of kind. He then suggests that something like the atonement stops looking incoherent once there is a genuine causal and representative link between the substitute and those whose place he takes. If I have misstated any of this, correct me in the course of your answer rather than quietly working around the error.

Where my friend and I got stuck. My friend says the Arras trial is Hugo refuting penal substitution decades in advance: an innocent vagrant, Champmathieu, is about to be condemned for another man's crimes, and the only morally available act is Valjean's — stand up and stop the transfer. I said my friend has located the wrongness in the wrong place. On my reading, what makes the scene unbearable is not that the penalty has moved to another body but the conditions under which it moved: Champmathieu neither knows nor consents, the court cannot even establish who he is, its identification resting on three former convicts, and the state is entirely content to let the machinery run. If transfer as such were the obscenity, then Fantine's sale of her hair and her teeth, and Valjean's later absorption of Cosette's and Marius's suffering, would have to be obscene too — and the novel plainly does not present them that way.
