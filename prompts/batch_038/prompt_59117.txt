I am chairing the closing Q&A of a two-day graduate workshop on agency and its neural correlates. Our guest is a philosopher of neuroscience whose published work defends a specific package of claims about the readiness potential. I will summarize that package so your questions can engage it directly rather than a caricature of it:

(a) The readiness potential is not the trace of an unconscious decision beginning long before the subject reports having decided. It is what you get when you average movement-aligned epochs of EEG in a task where spontaneous movement is produced by a threshold applied to a noisy accumulator fed by spontaneous cortical fluctuation — a picture compounded by the fact that only epochs ending in a movement ever enter the average. On her view, the standard inference from the ordering of RP onset and reported decision time to the conclusion that the brain decided first is a fallacy about averaged waveforms.

(b) None of this rescues libertarian free will. An accumulator crossing threshold is still an event with causes — noise, plus whatever biases the drift rate — so she holds that the neuroscientific evidence was never where the free will problem lives. Her positive view is a compatibilism about guidance control: what matters is whether the action issues from a mechanism responsive to the agent's reasons, not whether some neural event went uncaused.

(c) The remaining job for consciousness is intentional inhibition, a veto exercised in the final couple of hundred milliseconds before movement onset, which she treats as an empirically tractable phenomenon rather than a philosopher's consolation prize.

(d) She treats arbitrary decisions (the move-whenever-you-feel-like-it paradigm) and deliberate ones (reasoned, consequential choices) as different in kind, and she leans on ERP comparisons in which a clear readiness potential appears for arbitrary choices but is largely absent for deliberate ones as evidence that the classic paradigm does not generalize.

Please write nine questions for that Q&A. For each, give three parts: the question as I would actually speak it aloud; a two- or three-sentence prediction of the answer she is most likely to give, drawn from the commitments above; and a follow-up that stays effective against that predicted answer rather than against a weaker version of it. Label the parts clearly and number the questions.

Further constraints:

1. Assume an audience of philosophy graduate students with basic methods literacy. No primer questions, nothing about how she came to the subject, and nothing answerable by defining the readiness potential, free will, or compatibilism.

2. At least three of your follow-ups must survive a restatement of (a): they should keep the pressure even if she repeats that the waveform is an artifact of averaging and of backward selection.

3. Include one question on the apparent tension between the generality of her mechanism — the claim that spontaneous movement recruits the same accumulator-plus-threshold machinery used in evidence-based decisions — and the reported absence of a readiness potential in deliberate decisions. Ask her to say which available explanation she endorses: strong evidence-driven drift that compresses or masks the ramp, epoch and baseline choices that make the deliberate case unmeasurable, or a genuinely different mechanism; and ask what measurement would discriminate among them.

4. Include one question on the veto. You may note the interrupted-movement variants of the paradigm, in which a cue requires the subject to move promptly at an unpredictable moment, and which show that subthreshold ramps frequently fail to issue in action. Then ask: if one and the same accumulator dynamic explains both the movements that occur and the movements that do not, in what sense is vetoing a faculty rather than a threshold that happened not to be crossed?

5. Include one question on choice prediction. Single-trial decoding, including lateralized signals in two-alternative versions of the task, beats chance at predicting which hand will move well before the reported decision. Ask what has to be true of the drift rate for that to be compatible with a pure-noise account, and what is left of the word noise if the drift rate is modulated by the agent's standing preferences.

6. Include one question on her differential trust in introspective reports: she discards the reported time of decision as unreliable, yet the veto window is specified relative to reports of the same sort. Ask which reports she accepts and on what principled basis the discrimination is made.

7. Include one question on the division of labor between her account and the older philosophical objection that an urge to move is not a decision to move. Ask whether her model renders that objection redundant, and if not, what the two accounts predict differently.

8. Include one question asking what work claim (b) does inside her program: if her compatibilism is true independently of the readiness potential controversy, what does the reinterpretation add to the argument; and if it adds nothing, why does so much of the program depend on it.

9. Close with a question asking her to name concretely one result that would falsify the noise account and one that would force her to abandon the compatibilist thesis, together with a follow-up, in case she resists, on whether the two theses are then merely co-located in a single research program.

Do not attribute to her any claim not implied by (a) through (d), and do not design questions she could only answer by surrendering the whole position: each should be one she could plausibly answer well, and interesting because of how she answers it.
