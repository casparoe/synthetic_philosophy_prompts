The course pack for this unit contains the abstract of Libet and colleagues' 1983 paper in Brain, several pages of Libet's 1985 Behavioral and Brain Sciences discussion article, and Schurger, Sitt, and Dehaene's 2012 PNAS paper on the accumulator reinterpretation of the readiness potential; the students were told they would be graded on their use of these rather than on anything they tracked down themselves.

QUESTION (worth fifteen percent of the seminar grade; three-hundred-fifty-word limit). Describe what Libet and his colleagues measured in their self-paced movement experiments, identifying each of the three temporal landmarks and saying how each was obtained. Then state what Libet himself took the results to establish about the role of conscious will in the initiation of action, including any qualification he offered. Finally, evaluate one objection to the inference from his data to that conclusion, and say what accepting the objection would commit its target to giving up, if anything.

STUDENT ANSWER (submitted at 341 words). Libet's participants were asked to flex a wrist or finger whenever they felt like it, while watching a dot sweeping quickly around a clock face so that they could afterwards report where the dot had stood at the moment they first felt the urge to move. Scalp electrodes recorded the readiness potential, a slow negative shift over motor areas, and an EMG trace fixed the moment of movement onset, M. The finding was that the readiness potential began roughly 550 ms before M, while participants located their conscious urge, W, at only about 200 ms before M. So the brain had begun preparing the movement some 350 ms before the person became aware of wanting to make it, and on the natural reading of that interval the neural process has already settled which movement will occur, and when, before consciousness arrives on the scene. Consciousness therefore cannot be what initiates the action, which is what Libet took his results to show: free will, in the sense of a conscious cause of voluntary acts, is ruled out. Libet did add that consciousness retains a power of veto, so that during the roughly 200 ms between W and M a subject can still halt an act already under way — free won't rather than free will. That qualification was a concession he tacked on afterwards, and he acknowledged that nothing in any of the experimental series bore on it. The most serious objection to the whole enterprise is that the W reports are worthless, since participants are asked to date an event they cannot observe and may simply confabulate a position on the clock. A second objection is Schurger and colleagues' accumulator model, on which the readiness potential reflects random fluctuations in neural activity drifting toward a threshold, so that aligning the average to movement onset makes preparation look far earlier than it is. If that is right, Libet's measurements were invalid and no conclusion about the timing of intention follows.
