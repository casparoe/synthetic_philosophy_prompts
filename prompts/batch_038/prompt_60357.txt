I'm putting together the final exam for a third-year philosophy of science module — two hours, three questions, all compulsory, each worth a third of the paper — and I've decided to set the Lakatos question this year. Problem: my colleague has set the Einstein-versus-Lorentz version of it for four years running, and the students have now read so many summaries of that episode that they just recite "hard core, protective belt, progressive problemshift" at me and coast to a low 2:1. Half of them have never actually had to decide whether a given sequence of theory changes was progressive or degenerating; they've only ever been asked to label one they'd already been told the answer to. So I want to invent a fake research programme with a fake history and make them do the assessment on material they can't have seen before. It's closed book, closed notes, so please don't tell them to consult anything and don't pad the model answer with references — I'm not marking bibliographies they're reconstructing from memory, and a citation-heavy answer would just reward the wrong skill.

Here's the rough history I've sketched, its a bit thin so please flesh it out into a proper vignette of about 350 words, keeping the dates and making the findings concrete. 1962: Vandermolen proposes that every case of Vellman's dystonia, a rare progressive muscle disorder, is caused by chronic under-perfusion of a particular capillary bed in the spinal cord; no imaging technique available at the time can see the mechanism at all. She builds the hypothesis around an already-known oddity, namely that cases cluster in high-altitude towns in two separate mountain ranges. 1968: the programme predicts a distinctive branching pattern in retinal vessels that should show up years before onset, and a prospective study of 400 first-degree relatives confirms it — nobody had used that pattern in constructing the core, nobody had even looked for it. 1973: it predicts that a vasodilator should halt progression if given early; a large multicentre trial fails to show any effect. The belt is modified: the compound supposedly doesn't reach the relevant capillaries, and a close relative of it is predicted to work instead. 1978: the second compound also fails. Belt modified again: treatment has to begin before first symptoms, but the only pre-symptomatic marker is the retinal pattern, which turns up in 60% of eventual patients and 8% of healthy controls, so the auxiliary is testable in principle but no trial could be run at any realistic cost. 1983: the programme predicts a genetic marker; it's found, but at almost the same frequency in healthy controls, so the belt grows a two-hit model which also explains, after the fact, why the altitude clustering is patchy. 1989: a rival, Dacre's single-enzyme metabolic programme, explains both the altitude clustering and the retinal pattern with one mechanism, and in 1994 a drug aimed at that enzyme slows progression in a trial. 1996: Vandermolen's group publishes the claim that the enzyme defect is downstream of perfusion failure, so the rival's success actually confirms the core.

What I want out of you is the exam question itself in three parts, the model answer, and a marking scheme. For the question: (a) ask them, as of 1983 — so before Dacre exists and before anyone can appeal to the rival's later success — whether the Vandermolen programme is progressive or degenerating on Lakatos's own criteria, and which of those criteria actually do any work on this material; (b) include a student interlocutor who says that the two failed drug trials flatly refuted the core and that Lakatos's refusal to count them as refutations is just shielding the theory from evidence, and ask them to assess that; (c) ask them to advise a funding body in 1983 and to say whether Lakatos's methodology can actually deliver a verdict at that date, or only in hindsight, and if it can't, what would have to be added to the criteria to make it able to. Then a model answer of roughly 700 words that takes a position rather than surveying both sides — I want to see how a good student handles the fact that the retinal success is genuinely novel in the sense that matters while the altitude clustering is not, and that the 1978 auxiliary sits right on the line between a legitimate move in the protective belt and an ad hoc rescue. Marking scheme out of 25: allocate marks across the three parts, give me five must-hit moves with the marks each carries, and for each part name the single mistake that most reliably separates a first from a 2:1 — plus a short list of things that should actively cost marks, like calling the programme falsified, treating degenerating as a synonym for false, judging ad hocness of one auxiliary in isolation rather than of the whole sequence, or writing that Lakatos is just Popper with a time lag built in. Finally, two small things: tell me roughly how long you'd expect a strong candidate to spend on it, and flag anything in my sketch that a Lakatos specialist would object to as a mischaracterisation of how the criteria are supposed to be applied — I'd rather fix that now than have a colleague point it out at the exam board.
