Bit of an unusual prep request. In about three weeks I'm giving a 40 minute paper at a UK mental capacity conference, and then a 20 minute Q&A which is the part I'm actually scared of. The room is roughly: two thirds clinicians — geriatric psychiatrists and best-interests assessors who do capacity work day in day out — a couple of solicitors who live inside the Mental Capacity Act and think entirely in terms of whether an advance decision is valid and applicable, and maybe four or five philosophers, one of whom works on personal identity and will absolutely go for the metaphysics.

Here's the view I'm defending. Standard framing says an advance directive has authority because the earlier self is the same person as the later demented one, or because it expresses the patient's authentic values. I reject both. My claim is that a directive is a piece of standing authorisation: at the time of writing, a person with the relevant capacities delegates a decision to a proxy under conditions they specify, and what gives it force later is not identity or authenticity but the fact that nobody with the standing to withdraw the authorisation has withdrawn it. That gives me three commitments I want objections aimed at:

1. The standing condition. Withdrawing the authorisation requires second-order capacities — the ability to hold and revise a view of one's own good over time, not just a present want. So an in-the-moment refusal by someone in advanced dementia doesn't cancel the directive, but a refusal during a genuinely lucid interval does, even if the person then relapses.
2. The revocation asymmetry. The same act (saying no to the treatment) has different normative weight depending on standing, which sounds formalist to clinicians and I know it.
3. The contentment override. Because authority comes from standing and not from well-being, I concede that if the later person is settled and untroubled, that fact can outweigh the directive in practice — the directive tells you who decides, not automatically what the decision is.

The case I'll build the talk around: a woman who wrote a directive refusing long-term tube feeding after a diagnosis of early onset dementia, and who eight years later is cheerful, sociable, resistant at each feeding, and by every measure content.

So: give me a prep sheet, not a defence of the view. The seven or eight objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, and phrased the way each subgroup would phrase it — the psychiatrist's version and the solicitor's version of the same complaint are not the same sentence. For each one, a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, an honest note on where that reply is softest, and whether I should concede it up front in the talk to defuse it. Then tell me the single thing that has to land for the position to survive the room, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks (I have a guess but I want to know if you think it's the right one). No references or bibliography please — I can't check them and I don't want invented ones. Write the whole thing in British English.
