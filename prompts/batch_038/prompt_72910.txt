For several years I have belonged to a working group on cultural evolution in which one disagreement recurs at every meeting and never advances. The common ground is unusually wide: everyone in the room accepts that humans transmit behaviour and information by social learning, that variants differ in how successfully they spread, and that the upshot is cumulative cultural adaptation of a kind no other species manages. What nobody can agree about is whether any of this involves replicators.

There are three camps. The first holds that a meme is a genuine replicator: the Darwinian algorithm is substrate-neutral, so nothing about nucleic acid is essential to selection, and a tune, a knot, a technique, or a doctrine that is passed from person to person with variation and differential retention is doing precisely what a gene does. The second, descended from the dual-inheritance tradition, is Darwinian about culture but denies that the meme is a workable unit; it models cultural change with population-level transmission equations — prestige bias, conformist bias, content bias — and maintains that the fidelity of cultural transmission is manufactured by cognitive machinery (imitation, teaching, language, and eventually writing and notation) rather than by any self-copying structure, so heredity without replicators is not merely possible but what in fact happens. The third, coming from the epidemiology of representations, rejects the shared premise of the first two: cultural items are not copied but reconstructed, and what persists across a population is not a lineage of tokens but an attractor, a stable tendency of minds and environments to transform inputs toward certain forms. On this view the other two camps misdescribe the explanandum, and the durability of a tradition is a fact about psychology and ecology rather than about copying.

My hypothesis, offered as one I expect to be corrected, is that the impasse is not about culture at all. It is about what an account of design-like order must contain: the first camp is answering an ontological question (which entities exist and are transmitted), the second a question of explanatory strategy (which formalism lets you actually model transmission), the third a mechanistic question (what produces the recurrence). The term doing the damage, on this reading, is 'replication', whose fidelity conditions no one has fixed — especially since the replicator has long been treated as a role concept, in the manner of Hull's replicator/interactor framework, defined by what it does rather than by what it is made of. A role concept can always be claimed as filled or shown to be vacant by the same formalism, which is what seems to be happening here.

A concrete episode is what led me to this conjecture. A colleague and I built a transmission-chain model of a variant melody: individuals hear a rendition, produce one of their own, and we score both whole-rendition fidelity and per-feature variance across the chain. Presented to the group, the first camp said it vindicated discrete replication, since fidelity was measurable and lineages were recoverable; the third camp said it vindicated attractors, since the variance clustered in the directions the psychology rendered attractive rather than being distributed according to descent. Both read the identical output. We could not say what we would have had to alter in the model for one of them to be wrong.

So I am asking for a diagnosis, not an adjudication. Is this dispute verbal — and if so over which term, and between which senses of it — or substantive? Are the camps answering different questions under a single heading, disagreeing about which cases count as data, disagreeing about what a satisfying explanation owes us, or sitting on top of a hidden empirical question that nobody has operationalised? Work from what each side actually argues rather than giving me a taxonomy of positions, which I can produce myself. Then tell me, on your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result (for example, whether cumulative adaptation can be generated with and without discrete heredity, and what observable would separate the two regimes), an experiment whose possible outcomes would land asymmetrically on the camps, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected, and explain why. Finally, say what would have to be true for my own conjecture to be the wrong one, and whether anything in our melody model could in principle decide between the first and the third camps. I want the reasoning, not the references: please give no bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus.
