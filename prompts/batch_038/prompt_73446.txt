I've been slowly going a bit mad about secession ever since my department asked me to run a couple of seminar sessions on self-determination in the spring, and I've realised I dont actually have a position — just a pile of reactions. So here are the reactions, honestly, untidied. Scotland 2014: London and Edinburgh agreed the vote, everyone campaigned freely, it went No — but I think a Yes would have been perfectly legitimate, even though nobody in Scotland was oppressed in any interesting sense. So apparently agreement plus a ballot is enough for me. Catalonia 2017: Madrid refused, the constitutional court killed the law, they ran it anyway with police taking ballot boxes off people, 43% turnout and nine out of ten voting Yes — I think that one was not legitimate, but "they broke the rules" doesn't feel like my real reason, because I'd say the same about an identically messy vote that the state had authorised. Quebec 1995: No by well under a point on a 93% turnout, and I feel a Yes there would have been legitimate, and simultaneously that half-plus-one of one province shouldn't be allowed to break up a country — which is in tension with what I just said about Scotland and with everything I believe about majorities elsewhere. Kosovo: legitimate, on remedial grounds, after the late 90s. Crimea: not legitimate despite a referendum with a huge reported majority, and when I ask why I say it's because the foreign troops were already on the ground — but Bangladesh became real in 1971 partly because a much bigger army marched in, and I think that one was legitimate, so foreign force can't be the disqualifier. Biafra: I want to say legitimate, the pogroms first and then the blockade and the starvation, but almost nobody I've read says so and I genuinely cannot articulate what separates it from Bangladesh except that Bangladesh won. Bougainville: they voted 98% for independence in a referendum both sides had agreed to years earlier, and years on they still don't have a state — am I committed to saying they're already entitled and Port Moresby is just failing to hand it over, or does entitlement require somebody to grant it? Somaliland makes that worse: functioning, comparatively well-run, self-governing for over thirty years, recognised by nobody, and I think recognition is overdue, which suggests part of my criterion is whether the thing works.

Then the ones that make me suspect I'm not a democrat about this at all. If a rich region votes to leave purely so it can stop sending money to poorer compatriots, I think that's not a legitimate ground — even on a fully agreed, high-turnout vote. But if that same region also has a suppressed language and decades of being ruled from a capital that ignores it, my gut flips, and I can't tell whether the cultural grievance is doing real work or just laundering the money motive. Rural counties in northern California keep passing advisory votes about leaving the state and joining Idaho and I find those faintly ridiculous rather than wicked, which is probably a size-and-seriousness bias I haven't earned. Recursive cases: if Kosovo is legitimate, what about the Serb-majority parts of Kosovo that want to join Serbia, which I think are not legitimate — same argument one level down, made by a local majority. Same for the anglophone neighbourhoods of Montreal inside an independent Quebec. I also think the Kurds got a raw deal in 2017 when their overwhelming Yes was ignored and then punished, but I'd be much less confident about the same group if it were dispersed across four countries with no contiguous territory at all, which makes me worry that what I actually care about is already-existing internal borders, wearing a costume as a principle.

My draft principle, such as it is: a group may secede if either it suffers serious and persistent injustice within the state, or the state's own constitutional order has authorised a vote on the question and the group wins it clearly. Please try to break that — I expect it fails on the rich-region case (authorised vote, clear win, still wrong), on Somaliland and Biafra (neither clause applies), and maybe on Bougainville. What I want back is the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best fits everything above, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I've never seen, with each clause tied to the specific verdict that forces it. Then tell me what it says about cases I haven't listed: a region whose demographics were deliberately engineered for decades by the central government, so that the local majority is itself a product of the injustice; a region that wants to join a neighbouring state rather than become a new one; a group with a strong claim and no state capacity whatsoever; and whether a supermajority or turnout threshold is a legitimate guard or just the status quo defending itself with extra steps. And the part I dread — which of my verdicts has to be given up, and how many. If the pile is unsaveable, say so and tell me which one to drop first and what the second-best principle looks like once I've dropped it. Also, be blunt: am I confusing two seperate questions the whole way through, namely whether a group is morally entitled to secede and whether other states should recognise it? I suspect several of my verdicts are secretly about the second one, and that's part of why they contradict each other.
