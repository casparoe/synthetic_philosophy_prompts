So I'm on the social committee for our philosophy department and every spring we put on this thing where somebody writes a fake advice column that gets read aloud at the end-of-year dinner, and this year I drew the short straw. My pitch is a column called Dear Impartial Spectator, where the columnist literally *is* Adam Smith's impartial spectator — the imagined bystander whose verdicts are supposed to constitute our moral judgments — fielding letters from grad students, adjuncts, and one very persistent alum who works in branding now. I want roughly 900 words total, and I need it to work out loud in a room of people who are mildly drunk, which means the sentences have to be short enough to say and funny enough to land without footnotes.

The bit I'm going for is not "18th century philosopher confused by modern life," please don't do that, it's the oldest joke in the genre and the room will hate it. I want the comedy to come from the machinery being applied with total sincerity to petty contemporary situations, so that someone who only knows the invisible hand gets something and someone who's actually taught the Theory of Moral Sentiments laughs harder. The pieces of Smith I want load-bearing: sympathy isn't catching somebody else's feeling, it's the spectator changing places with the agent in imagination and asking what he himself would feel in that exact situation, which is why the person who's hurting has to bring the intensity of their emotion *down* to the pitch a bystander can go along with, and why it's so much easier to enter into other people's joy than their grief. There's also the split between propriety, whether the motive fits the occasion, and merit, whether the gratitude or resentment of the people affected looks appropriate from where I'm standing — those come apart constantly and at least one letter should turn entirely on the gap. And I want one letter where the columnist has to own the shaky part of his own construction: he was assembled out of the verdicts of actual spectators, so he inherited their distortions, and in particular he keeps catching himself over-admiring the rich and the impressive and discounting the poor, which Smith treats as a corruption of our moral sentiments and somehow also as glue that holds a commercial society together. Maybe work in his story about the ambitious young man from a poor family who spends his whole life chasing after the trappings of wealth and only at the end realizes the trinkets were never going to deliver the tranquility he was promised — I think that's the emotional center of the whole book and it'd be great as the one answer that isn't funny, so the piece has somewhere to land.

The target of the satire is the modern empathy industry — corporate well-being programs, empathy training decks, that whole register where "we hear you" substitutes for actually judging anything — because Smith's spectator is a judging apparatus wearing the costume of a feeling one, and I want that contrast to be unmistakable by the end without anybody saying it. So: commit. Pick one account of what the spectator actually is (a useful fiction that disciplines us, a genuine tracker of what's right, something in between, whatever you think is truest) and let all five answers be consistently that, don't waffle between them halfway through, and tell me in one line which you chose and why. Format it as exactly five numbered exchanges, each one a letter of two to four sentences from a distinct and slightly embarrassing asker followed by an answer of about 100-150 words, no introduction, no sign-off, no closing paragraph. Don't put any verbatim Smith in quotation marks — partly because period sentences die on their feet when read aloud, partly because I refuse to be the guy who misquotes Smith at his own department's dinner — so paraphrase his ideas in the columnist's own clipped modern-ish voice, and you can keep a little 18th century stiffness in his syntax for flavor. Then after the fifth item, add exactly two sentences under a heading like "liberties taken" flagging which contested readings of Smith you leaned on and where you exaggerated for the joke, because I will get questions from the Smith person in our faculty and I'd rather walk in knowing where the soft spots are.
