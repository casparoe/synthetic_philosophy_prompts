I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine and I've been sitting on a piece about costly signalling for three weeks, mostly because I've realised that any explainer I write just makes the bad version stickier. You know the version: signals have to hurt, otherwise nobody believes them — peacock tails, Veblen goods, the guy buying the watch he can't afford. I was arguing about this with friends over the weekend and one of them, who does behavioural ecology, kept telling me I'd got it wrong in a way I only half followed. Something like: it isn't the absolute cost that keeps a signal honest, it's that the *marginal* cost of the next increment is steeper for the low-quality signaller than for the high-quality one; and separately, some of the classic models assume you can add costs and benefits in one currency when you actually can't, because a survival probability and a mating payoff don't add, they multiply; and separately again there are these "index" signals that are honest because they're physically unfakeable rather than because they're expensive, which is a completely different mechanism that everyone lumps in with handicaps. Rather than write another explainer I want to commission a piece of short fiction that carries those distinctions in its plot, so a reader who's never heard of Zahavi or Grafen ends up holding the right intuitions anyway.

Here's the seed I've got — please feel free to rearrange it, I care about the beats more than the furniture. About 1,500–1,800 words, third person, set over one breeding season on an invented island with an invented bird (no real species, no real researchers, nothing I'd have to fact-check). The male display should be genuinely expensive at the start: performed from an exposed perch at dusk, visible to females and to a raptor, and burning reserves the males can't easily spare. Then the beats I want:

1. Baseline — the displays are costly and the ordering they produce tracks male condition pretty faithfully, shown through observation rather than asserted.
2. A conservation program removes the cost for everyone equally (say, the raptor gets eradicated or excluded). The characters who believe the slogan expect the whole signalling system to collapse into bluffing within a season or two. It doesn't, and the reader has to be able to see *why* from what actually happens on the island, not from anyone explaining it.
3. A second, more targeted intervention — supplementary feeding aimed at the poorest-condition males, done for entirely sympathetic reasons — does blow the system up. This is the real point of the story and it should land as a quiet disaster, not a gotcha.
4. Running underneath all of it, one component of the display that can't be faked at any price for structural reasons, which stays reliable throughout and works as the control that makes the difference between the two mechanisms visible.

For cast I'm picturing a grad student who believes the popular version and whose simulation model is broken in exactly the additive-costs way described above (she should be smart and sympathetic, not a strawman — she's doing what the textbook told her), a long-term local field technician with twenty years of notebooks and no theory at all, and optionally a visiting economist who keeps drawing a parallel to job-market signalling and is both right and wrong about it in interesting ways.

Two hard constraints. Nobody in the story is allowed to deliver a lecture: no monologues, no scene where someone spells out the moral at a bar, and please don't let any character say the phrase "handicap principle" out loud. And no citations anywhere — I don't want a reference list or a bibliography or named papers, the names I dropped above are just for your orientation and should stay out of the fiction. After the story, add roughly 250 words under the heading "What's load-bearing": map each of the four beats to the theoretical claim it's carrying, say which beat you found hardest to make work dramatically, and flag one place where you compressed or fudged the biology for story purposes so I know not to repeat it as fact in my column.
