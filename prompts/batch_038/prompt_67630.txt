In my decision theory seminars I encounter a persistent objection to the orthodox treatment of the Allais paradox, and I should like an essay that engages it directly rather than rehearsing the familiar catalogue of responses to Allais. The objectors concede that their preferences over the four prospects violate the independence axiom, but they deny that anything problematic follows: a money pump, they say, presupposes cyclical preferences among complete alternatives, whereas their ordering remains both transitive and complete, and the pump can gain no purchase unless trades are offered one at a time to a short-sighted chooser who fails to anticipate the sequence, whereas a chooser confronted with the alternatives together will simply take the combination she prefers.

Write a paper adjudicating this dispute. It should set out precisely what a pumping argument against a violator of independence requires; consider whether transitivity and completeness are by themselves sufficient to block such an argument; and assess the philosophical work done by sequential presentation, by the agent's foresight of the choices to come, and by her capacity to decline a later trade at no cost. Conclude with a defended verdict on whether the objection survives, and say what turns on the answer for the claim that independence is normatively binding rather than merely a convenient axiomatisation. Please write in British English.
