So I'm the only undergrad on my college's student-managed investment fund — a real slice of the endowment, about $2M, that a committee of students votes on each term, with a faculty advisor who mostly stays quiet. Our governing doc says the fund should "maximize long-run growth without jeopardizing the purchasing power available to future students," and for two years we've just voted on positions by vibes and whoever talked loudest.

Now a member has proposed we size positions with a Kelly-style rule from next term. Concretely: for each thesis someone brings, that person submits their own probability that the name beats our benchmark over the holding period, plus their estimate of the payoff if right and the loss if wrong; we plug those into the Kelly fraction; we take half of that as the position size; and no single name can exceed 8% of the book. Their pitch is that Kelly maximizes the portfolio's long-run growth rate, that this is a mathematical fact rather than a stance about risk, and that therefore the "how aggressive should we be" arguments we keep having aren't actually matters of taste at all. Two others push back. One says this is just log utility in a trench coat — that choosing log is exactly as much a value judgment as choosing anything else, and that the "not a preference" claim is doing all the work. The other says Kelly assumes you can replay the bet many times with known-ish odds, whereas our theses are basically one-shot, correlated with each other, and fed by probablity estimates from 20-year-olds who read earnings calls, so the input error swallows the elegant math.

Extra wrinkle: the dean's office has told us the fund gets folded into the main endowment in about six years if our record is unimpressive, and one 30% drawdown year would basically decide that. So our horizon may be cut short by people who don't care about our growth rate.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's vote? Approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasons the two objectors could actually push back on, not a primer on what Kelly is. I want you to take the "growth maximization is preference-free" claim seriously and tell me plainly whether you buy it; if you don't, say what work the log is doing that's being hidden. And deal with the six-year thing, because I suspect that's where the whole long-run story gets weakest, though I could be wrong about that too. Don't give me a bibliography or citations, I can't look anything up mid-meeting and I'd rather have your reasoning than guessed-at page numbers.

After the memo, add a JSON object with exactly these fields: "verdict" (one of approve / reject / approve_with_conditions), "conditions" (array of strings, empty if not applicable), "preference_free_claim" (object with "ruling" = yes / no / partly, and "reason"), "objections_addressed" (array of objects, each with "objection", "strongest_form", "how_the_recommendation_handles_it", "residual_risk_if_dismissed"), "horizon_problem" (how the possible six-year cutoff changes things, or why it doesn't), "what_would_change_my_mind" (the specific argument or piece of information that would flip you to a different verdict), "weakest_link" (where you think your own reasoning above is most likely to fail), and "confidence" (low / medium / high).
