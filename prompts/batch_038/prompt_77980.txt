I chair the Standards and Safeguarding Panel of a care group running eleven residential homes for people living with dementia, and I need help turning a philosophical distinction into something five laypeople can apply under time pressure. The panel is a nurse, two home managers, an HR lead, a family representative, and me; none of us has studied philosophy. We meet twice a month and decide what follows for a member of staff after an incident or a near-miss: coaching, retraining, redeployment, suspension, dismissal, or referral to a professional regulator. That is roughly forty decisions a year, six or seven of them involving a death or a serious injury. Each case gets about forty minutes of panel time, the written reasons have to fit on two pages, and those reasons must be simultaneously defensible to a grieving family, to the regulator who gets notified within twenty-four hours of any death, and to an employment tribunal if the person is dismissed. Dismissals need my countersignature and HR's.

Three years ago we adopted a written policy borrowed from patient-safety practice whose central clause says that an individual's fault must be assessed without reference to the outcome: two staff who commit the same lapse are to be judged alike, whether or not anyone was hurt. The point was to make people report near-misses. In practice the clause is coming apart from both ends. On the side of principle, the panel keeps feeling that it is not merely uncomfortable but somehow wrong to record the carer who left a garden gate unlatched and whose resident walked out and drowned in a canal as culpable to exactly the same degree as the carer who left the same gate unlatched and whose resident was found asleep on the patio an hour later — and when pushed, members cannot articulate why without contradicting the policy. On the side of practice, our HR lead observes flatly that we do dismiss people after fatal incidents and have never dismissed anyone after a near-miss, so the policy is describing a panel that does not exist. I have been reading Nagel on moral luck and Williams's companion piece, and the two things I want to keep are these: the intuition that we can be assessed only for what depends on factors under our control, together with the corollary that two people should not be assessed differently if the only further differences between them are down to luck; and Williams's suggestion that the driver who kills a child through no fault of his own nevertheless owes something — a distinctive form of regret, and a demand on him to make some response — that the identical driver who kills no one is not required to owe, even though it would be a mistake to describe that as extra blame, or as worse culpability. Our policy collapsed the second thought into the first and then discarded both.

Write me a decision procedure my panel can actually run on a case: ordered steps, what question each step asks, what counts as a sufficient answer, roughly how long each step should take, and which steps terminate in a finding as opposed to being handed to a vote of the panel or to me because they are matters of value rather than fact. It must state, at every point where the outcome is allowed to influence the decision, which door it comes through — as something bearing on what the individual is expected to say and do afterwards, on what we owe the family, on our estimate of systemic risk, or on sanction — and it must state where the outcome is excluded and why. It also has to handle two sources of luck that our current policy ignores entirely: the luck of exposure, since who was rostered, who was short-staffed, and which resident they were assigned to is decided by the company and not by the worker, so that one person's opportunity to lapse at all is a matter of institutional accident; and constitutive luck, since some of our staff are habitually inattentive and some habitually anxious through no doing of their own, and we presently sanction the second group's paperwork more harshly than the first group's judgment. Flag every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure, say what the procedure cannot settle at all, and tell me how a two-page write-up should be structured so that the family and the tribunal can both see which parts of our decision were outcome-blind.

Then run it on three cases. (a) Two care assistants in the same home, on consecutive nights, each fail to log a resident's fall because the shift has ended and they want to go home; the first resident is unharmed, the second's subdural haemorrhage is diagnosed three days later and she dies. (b) A senior nurse has been administering a controlled drug outside the recorded time window roughly twice a month for two years, which surfaces during a medicines audit; nobody was harmed, no complaint has been made, and her record is otherwise spotless. (c) A relief worker is found to have lied to a relative about a resident's condition in order to end a difficult phone call; the lie had no harmful consequences whatever, but it was deliberate, and it is the second time. At least one of these should produce an answer that my board and our family representative will refuse to accept, and I want you to say which one, say whether the resistance indicates a defect in the procedure or in us, and say what the procedure costs us in that case.

I want a committed answer rather than a survey of the options. Take a definite position on whether outcome should ever be permitted to alter a culpability score as such, or whether it can enter only through the other doors; take a definite position on whether an employer can require the kind of response Williams has in mind or can only invite it, and on what a panel should do when it is invited and refused; and if you think our existing policy is not merely incomplete but simply wrong, say so and say what should replace it. If your honest view is that the procedure I am asking for cannot be made truthful — that any workable version smuggles outcome-luck back in at every step while claiming to have sealed it out — I would rather be told that directly, with the reasons, than be given something tidier.
