In about three weeks I'm giving a 40-minute talk with 20 minutes of Q&A at a workshop on autonomy and everyday technology, and I'll be the only person in the building who works on AI systems. The room is maybe 30 people: mostly clinical psychologists plus two psychiatrists who do motivational-interviewing-style work with addiction and self-harm, two behavioral economists who run field experiments on commitment devices, a disability-rights lawyer who does supported decision-making cases, and a handful of HCI grad students. Nobody does alignment research, and one of the invited commentators has already written in the program thread that "paternalism" is a term we should have retired a decade ago.

Here's the view I'm defending. Call it disclosed friction. A consumer AI assistant should basically never refuse by default — it should add friction (a delay, a re-prompt, making the user type out the consequence before it goes through, an offer to revisit in an hour). How much friction scales with exactly two things: how irreversible the downstream act is, and whether the user opted into that specific check in advance, calmly, before it was relevant. It does not scale with how bad the system estimates the harm to be, which I know is the weird part. Every bit of friction is disclosed at the moment it's applied: the system says it's delaying and why. Covert friction — quietly re-ranking, quietly slowing down, quietly softening a request before passing it on — is ruled out as deception, and I want to argue it's worse than a flat refusal even where it works better. There's a very short list of outright refusals even against an explicit, informed, unhurried request, and I claim that list is about the company's complicity rather than about the user's own good. And the part I already know is shaky: when someone has self-bound in advance (don't let me send anything like this after 11pm, don't let me reorder), the system should sometimes release them from their own rule when the situation differs in a way the earlier self couldn't have forseen, and I want to say that release is governed by a published standard rather than vendor discretion.

Sketch of the argument: (1) most of the worst harms here run through acts that are fast, one-shot and hard to undo, while the user's own preferences about those acts are visibly unstable across time; (2) where a preference is unstable across time, honoring the contemporaneous request isn't neutral between the user's own temporal parts — it picks the present one, so "just do what the user asks" is already a normative choice and not the absence of one; (3) so the real question isn't whether to intervene but which interventions the user can see, anticipate and veto; (4) disclosed friction plus prior authorization gets most of the protective benefit of refusal without substituting anyone's judgment for the user's.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the eight to ten objections this specific room will actually raise, ordered by likelihood times damage. Tag each one with who's raising it, and phrase it the way that person would phrase it out loud, in their vocabulary and not mine — the clinicians will not say "autonomy violation," they'll say something else, and I want to hear the sentence before I hear it. For each: a reply I can actually deliver in under two minutes standing up, and one line on where that reply is soft, i.e. what I'd have to give up to make it airtight. Then tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes so the concessions aren't torn out of me at minute 65, which pair of objections turn out to be the same objection wearing two disciplines' clothes so I only answer it once, and the one reply that absolutely has to land or the proposal doesn't survive the session.

Two more things. I'm a decent speaker but I visibly wobble when someone says some version of "so you're saying the chatbot knows better than the patient" — build me something specific for that. And my guess about the question I'm praying nobody asks is "which of your rules would look different if the legal department wrote them, and how would anyone outside the company tell?" If that's really the worst one, say so; if there's a worse one, give me the worse one. Please don't pad this with the usual AI-ethics standards — this room is not going to ask about consciousness or trolley cases, and if one of those shows up on your list you've misjudged the audience.
