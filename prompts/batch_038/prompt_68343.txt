Having retired three years ago after four decades in condensed matter physics — most of them spent on transport coefficients and on the awkward business of getting thermodynamics out of mechanics — I have taken up philosophy with some seriousness, and the philosophy of social science in particular. I am currently reading my way through the long quarrel between methodological individualists, who insist that nothing social is real unless it can be traced back to the actions, beliefs, and situations of particular human beings, and the heirs of Durkheim, who maintain that social facts are external to individuals, coercive upon them, and irreducible in any useful sense. To an old physicist the quarrel looks both oddly familiar and oddly stalled, since we appear to have fought the same battle over temperature and molecules and largely declared it a draw.

A former doctoral student of mine, now an economic sociologist, assures me that the two debates are not merely similar but identical in structure, and that once this is seen the philosophical quarrel evaporates. His mapping runs as follows: individuals are the molecules, and a society's macro-level properties — the inflation rate, the median age at first marriage, the persistence of an institution — are pressure and temperature; the microstate of a society is the complete specification of who is doing what and believing what, and just as temperature is nothing over and above a statistical feature of such a specification, so social facts are nothing over and above statistical features of individual conduct. Yet, he continues, nobody concludes from this that thermodynamics is dispensable: the macro vocabulary is autonomous, it does explanatory work the micro vocabulary cannot economically replicate, and near a phase transition it delivers behaviour with no transparent micro-level reading. Hence his conclusion: the individualist is right that the social is fixed by the individual, the holist is right that the social level is autonomous and irreducible in practice, and a century of dispute has been verbal. He advises both camps to shake hands.

I should like a rigorous audit of that analogy rather than a general survey of the two positions. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what in the social case plays the role of the molecule, of the microstate, of the Hamiltonian, of the coarse-graining, and above all of the thermodynamic limit and the typicality assumptions that make the reduction go through — and then tell me where the mapping fails. I am especially interested in a ranking of the disanalogies: which are merely inconvenient, and which are fatal to the conciliatory conclusion my former student draws? I offer the following as prompts rather than as settled answers: molecules hold no beliefs about the pressure and do not alter their trajectories when the pressure is announced, whereas human beings do both; a macrovariable in physics is generated by a fixed statistical procedure over a well-specified microstate, and it is not obvious that the inflation rate, or the state, is definable in that way; and in my own discipline the reduction required additional machinery — an infinite-volume limit, a low-entropy past — for which there is no evident social counterpart. What does the analogy quietly import from statistical mechanics that the social case lacks, and what does it obscure about the possibility that the micro-level regularities of human conduct are themselves contestable, or not lawlike at all? Finally, if the analogy must be abandoned, is there a better one for the relation between individual action and social structure — and does abandoning it push one toward the individualists, toward the holists, or merely toward the view that my former student's handshake was premature?
