I have no academic background in any of this. I left school early, work with my hands, and have spent the last several years reading about probability and rational belief on my own in the evenings. The question I cannot stop thinking about is a plain one: when two people disagree about something, is there always a fact of the matter about which of them is being unreasonable?

What got me stuck is a result I kept running into, associated with Aumann, roughly to the effect that two people who are both fully rational, who are honest about what they believe, and who know what the other believes cannot simply settle at different estimates of the same thing — provided they started out from the same prior. The catch is that last clause. Everything seems to hang on the assumption that people who disagree began with the same view of the world before any evidence arrived. A friend of mine and I have argued for years about whether that assumption is a harmless simplification, a requirement of rationality itself, or simply false. Neither of us can say which, and when I went looking for how the question gets settled I found people pulling in so many directions that I could not tell whether they were even answering the same question.

So I would like you to build me a decision tree that maps that space. It is for a small self-taught reading group I meet with once a month — people like me, comfortable with the idea of odds and bets, but who have never seen a proof and get lost as soon as notation appears.

Between six and eight branch questions. Each one must be a concrete situation a reader could answer without already knowing which way the branch leads, not a check on which label they like. I want the branches built out of cases like: whether it makes any difference that the two people are disagreeing about a coin that has already landed rather than a season that has not yet happened; whether the fact that a person's starting view was handed to them by their parents, or by which life they happened to find themselves living, is a reason to think priors must genuinely differ, or a reason to think their disagreement is not a rational one after all; whether the question "what should I believe?" even has an answer when the asker is one particular person rather than a generic observer standing nowhere in particular; whether the whole picture changes if the two of them are limited in time, memory, and calculating power; whether it changes if each person's uncertainty is a range of odds rather than a single number and the two ranges overlap; and whether the result survives if they do not actually know what the other believes but only believe that they do. Add or drop branch questions as you judge best, but say why.

At least ten leaves. Each leaf carries the name of the position if it has one — the view that rational agents are required to share a prior, the view that priors may legitimately differ, the view that a shared prior is a fine idealization that turns out to do no work, the view that the whole debate rests on a picture of belief nobody can actually use — and "unnamed" if nobody really holds it. Under each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment: the thing its defender least likes having to say out loud.

Some combinations of answers will be logically available but empty. Point those out, and for each say whether the spot is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has thought to stand there. And if two leaves cannot really be separated by any question on your tree, say so plainly instead of implying otherwise.

Please keep everything in plain language: ordinary bets, ordinary arguments between two people, no formal machinery beyond the notion of odds. I care far more about the branch questions being genuinely diagnostic than about getting names exactly right, and I would rather have fewer, sharper questions than a complete survey.
