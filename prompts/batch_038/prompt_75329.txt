Last night I spent four hours arguing with a friend who works in health technology assessment, and we parted with each of us convinced the other had spent the evening evading the question. The nominal subject was interpersonal comparison of utility — whether one person's gains and losses can be weighed against another's on a single scale. I said the debate has been frozen for the better part of a century, and frozen in a peculiar way: on one side sits the methodological verdict inherited from the 1930s, according to which utility functions are ordinal, defined only up to increasing transformations of each person's scale, so that cross-person comparisons have no factual content and welfare economics must confine itself to Pareto-improvements; on the other side sit the philosophers and the distributional welfare economists, who point out that prioritarianism, cost-benefit analysis, distributional weights, and anything resembling a theory of fair allocation all quietly presuppose such comparisons, and who suspect that the impossibility verdict was won by definitional fiat rather than by argument — if you build "utility" out of individual choice behaviour, then of course it cannot be compared, but nothing follows about whether lives can be compared. Neither camp appears to have converted a single member of the other in ninety years, which strikes me as suspicious.

My friend's position is that the whole thing is a pseudo-problem generated by a badly specified estimand, and she argues from her own field. When the global burden-of-disease exercise stopped treating the disability weight as an index of lost well-being and re-specified it as an index of lost health, the numbers moved enormously — the weight for blindness fell from roughly 0.6 to roughly 0.2 — and a large part of the standing quarrel with disability advocates lost its sting, because the objection had been that the measure pronounces a disabled person's life worth less, and the re-specified measure makes no such pronouncement. She adds that subjective well-being research simply went ahead and treated answers to life-satisfaction scales as cardinal and comparable across persons and countries, and that those numbers predict things. On her diagnosis, then, the disagreement was never about metaphysics or about the meaning of a word; it was about what quantity a protocol is estimating, and once you fix that, comparability stops being a puzzle and becomes an engineering matter. My counter, which she thought was obscurantist, is that re-specifying the target from welfare to health does not answer the question whether well-being is comparable — it changes the question, and the change conceals a normative choice, since any health scale must still be anchored by trade-offs elicited from people imagining conditions they have never had. That anchoring is exactly where the familiar asymmetry bites: people living with severe disabilities routinely report good lives while members of the general public, asked to value the same condition, assign it a far lower score. If the anchor is imaginative projection rather than the subject's own experience, choosing between anchors is substantive, not technical. And the health-versus-welfare manoeuvre is unavailable precisely where the money goes — in end-of-life care, or wherever health loss and welfare loss come apart, someone must decide which of the two is being traded against a year of somebody else's life.

So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and certainly not a survey of the literature. Is this a verbal dispute about the word utility, with one side meaning a representation of individual choice and the other meaning a magnitude of well-being, such that both camps are right about their own term and the impasse is an artifact of a shared vocabulary? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading — an inferential question about what can be read off observed behaviour versus a metaphysical question about whether lives admit of better and worse on a common scale? Or is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, namely whether a common cardinal structure in fact obtains, which would be settled the moment we had an indicator everyone accepted and which no amount of conceptual analysis can settle in advance? Or is the real disagreement about standards of evidence and professional respectability, so that the impossibilists are making a claim about what may be asserted in a science rather than about the world? Having sorted that out, turn to my friend's estimand diagnosis directly: is the shift from welfare to health genuine progress, a change of subject that smuggles in a normative premise, or both at once, and does her appeal to subjective well-being measurement actually lift the ban or merely relocate it? Finally, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a measurement result, a formal theorem, a change of question — or make the case that none should be expected and say why. Argue from the positions as I have described them and from whatever you already know; I am not looking for references or a reading list.
