I am the Secretary to the Rules and Precedents Committee of a national federation of roughly three hundred affiliated local clubs. We are volunteers — the chair is a retired accountant and there is no lawyer among us — but our rulebook is sixty years old, has been amended perhaps two hundred times, and contains an amendment clause that permits the amendment of the amendment clause. Once a year, in a ninety-minute sitting before the Annual Congress, the committee rules on the admissibility of every submitted motion, roughly forty of them; each ruling is published to all three hundred clubs, who read it adversarially and spend the following eleven months arguing about it. This year's slate includes motions that our accumulated informal habits cannot handle, and I want a written procedure the whole committee can run: an ordered set of tests, each with a stated pass condition and a stated output, terminating in one of exactly three verdicts — inadmissible, admissible under a specified track, or refer — together with tie-break rules for cases where two of our own rules point in opposite directions, and an explicit mark at every node where the answer is not determined by anything in the rulebook and the committee is making a choice rather than finding a result. It has to be usable by someone who has never read a word of jurisprudence, in about ten minutes per motion, and the final product has to be one sentence read aloud plus at most three numbered reasons.

The relevant parts of the rulebook are as follows. Rule 101(a): any motion to add, amend, or repeal a rule requires two-thirds of delegates voting at Congress, on sixty days' written notice, with the text tabled in final form and no amendment from the floor. Rule 101(b): eight listed provisions are entrenched, and may be changed only by three-quarters of delegates plus ratification by two-thirds of affiliated clubs within ninety days. Rule 101(c): Rule 101, including the list in 101(b), is itself entrenched under 101(b). Rule 7 (entrenched): no affiliated club may be deprived of its right to withdraw from the Federation on sixty days' notice without its own consent. Rule 12: the committee's ruling on admissibility is final, and no Congress may consider a motion the committee has ruled inadmissible. Rule 3: nothing in these Rules limits the power of Congress to determine its own procedure. Rule 44: a rule repealed at a Congress may not be re-enacted, in the same or substantially the same terms, at that Congress or the next. Rule 205, a relic of 1968 that has never been used: the Rules may be suspended for one meeting by unanimous consent, and business transacted under suspension has the same effect as business transacted under the Rules. I am aware that this is precisely the combination that generates the familiar puzzles — whether an amendment clause can be applied to itself without either contradicting the rule that authorizes it or launching an infinite regress of ever-higher clauses, and whether what makes a change valid is ultimately an inference from that prior rule or simply the continued willingness of the membership to go on accepting the result. I do not want a survey of that controversy, and I do not want references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want to know which pieces of it must survive into a ninety-minute meeting of volunteers, and which can be dropped without cost.

Please run the procedure on the following, and end each application with the sentence I would read out.

- Motion A. Tabled under the ordinary track with proper notice: "That Rule 101(a) be amended by deleting two-thirds and substituting a simple majority; that Rule 101(c) be repealed; that this motion take effect on adoption notwithstanding Rule 44 and notwithstanding any other provision inconsistent with it; and that Rule 12 shall not apply to any motion concerning this amendment." A delegate has separately given notice that, should Motion A carry, he will table at the same Congress a companion motion restoring the two-thirds threshold for all rules except itself.
- Motion B. Two motions, B1 and B2, tabled together under the entrenched track with full notice and the required support, and formally flawless in every respect. B1 adds a new entrenched Rule 250: "Rule 251 may not be amended, repealed, or suspended by any procedure, including any procedure established by a later Congress, and any instrument purporting to do so is void from the outset." B2 adds a new entrenched Rule 251 in identical terms about Rule 250. Neither contradicts anything currently in the rulebook. I need the ruling now, and I also need to know what the committee says in year three when a motion arrives to repeal Rule 250, and whether the rulebook leaves any way out of the pair — suspension under Rule 205, Congress's procedural power under Rule 3, simple disregard by a later Congress, or nothing at all.
- Motion C. In 2014 Congress amended Rule 61 to permit proxy voting by affiliated clubs, on sixty days' notice and a two-thirds vote. A club has now produced a dated copy showing the notice went out on fifty-eight days. Every election to the executive in the eleven years since has been conducted with proxies, and none was contested at the time. Two motions are before us: one declares the 2014 amendment void from the outset and all subsequent elections void with it; the other declares the amendment valid and no longer open to challenge.
- Motion D, briefly. A motion to repeal Rule 12 and substitute: "The committee has no power to rule a motion inadmissible; all questions of admissibility are decided by Congress on a simple majority." Is the committee competent to rule on a motion that abolishes its own competence, and what becomes of the finality of that ruling if the motion carries?

In addition to the worked cases, I would like four things. First, the list of distinctions from the theory of amendment and entrenchment that the procedure must preserve — for instance, between a limit on how a rule may be changed and a limit on what it may be changed to; between the validity of the enactment and the validity of the content enacted; between a rule that is unamendable and a rule that nobody has yet amended; between retrospective and prospective effect; and between acceptance of a change as a fact about the membership and acceptance as a rule of decision the committee may apply — each with one line saying why it matters operationally, plus the distinctions the committee may safely ignore and the specific misfire each omission risks. Second, defended default rules for gaps and conflicts: when the rulebook is silent or two rules collide and neither is entrenched, does later-in-time prevail, does the higher threshold prevail, does the reading that avoids self-reference prevail, or does the reading that preserves the committee's own competence prevail? Third, what to enter in the precedent book, worded so that the precedent does not quietly entrench itself or bind a later committee that has better information than we do. Fourth, an honest account of the limits: the class of motions on which this procedure will output something indefensible, whether "refer" can be a real verdict at all given that Rule 12 forbids Congress from considering anything we have ruled inadmissible — so referral needs a trigger and must not become the escape hatch we reach for whenever the reasoning gets difficult — and where the committee is really exercising political judgment under the cover of a test. I expect at least one of these four motions to produce a ruling I do not want to publish; please say so plainly and give me the sentence anyway rather than softening it.
