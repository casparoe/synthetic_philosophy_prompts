I am a junior and the translations editor of our school's literary magazine. This year we started a standing section named after Douglas Hofstadter's book on translation, *Le Ton beau de Marot*, and we adopted one house rule along with the name: every published translation appears facing its source text, so that a reader who does not know the language can at least see what shape the original has. That rule commits us to a claim we cannot currently articulate. We have told our readers that the form of a poem is part of what a translation owes them, but we have no method for deciding how much formal drift is too much, and in practice the decision goes to whoever argues longest at the table.

Our situation, concretely. Two issues a year, twelve to eighteen translation submissions per issue, arriving from students working out of Spanish, French, Japanese, Mandarin, Urdu, Russian, and Latin. The board is six students; between us we read Spanish, French, and Latin competently and nothing else. I have roughly fifteen minutes per piece at our weekly table read. The faculty advisor signs off on each issue but reads only English and defers to us on everything except school policy. The editor-in-chief can override me and occasionally does.

There are three working ideas I want the procedure to engage with rather than ignore. First, we have been using an informal distinction between a poem's furniture — its images, objects, names, and what happens in it — and its gait: line length, line breaks, rhyme, stanza shape, sound patterning, the shape of its syntax. Our unwritten practice has been to let a translator trade furniture for gait but never gait for furniture, and I suspect that rule is either wrong or at least too blunt. Second, one of my editors argues that a translation should be assessed not against the source alone but against the space of translations that could reasonably have been written of it, on the grounds that reading dozens of versions of the same short poem changes your sense of what the poem is. I do not know whether that can be turned into a step someone can perform in fifteen minutes; if it can, show me how. Third, we sometimes ask for a back-translation as a check, though I recognize the test has a failure mode: a stiff, literal English poem will back-translate cleanly, so passing may prove nothing.

What I need is a decision procedure my board can run on any submission, in order, with three possible outcomes rather than two: publish as a translation; publish but reclassify it (as "after" the original, or as variations on it), with the source named and credited; or decline. For each question in the sequence, tell me what would count as a sufficient answer, which questions are answerable by looking at the two texts side by side without knowing the language and which are not, where the procedure should stop at a decline, and where it should hand the decision to a named person because the question is one of value rather than fact. Please also be explicit about which steps are genuine judgment calls wearing a procedural costume, and about what the procedure should do when the evidence it wants simply does not exist — the piece is from a language nobody on the board reads and the only account of the original is the translator's own.

Then run it on three submissions.

Submission one: a classmate's fourteen-line Spanish poem, rendered into English with the line count and the position of every line break preserved and the images intact, but with the rhyme abandoned and the original's second-person address quietly converted into third person. Two board members read Spanish and think the English is flat.

Submission two: an English version of a short sixteenth-century French poem — twenty-eight three-syllable lines in rhyming couplets, the rhyme scheme closing back on its opening rhyme, the first and last lines identical, and the poet's own name surfacing halfway through, at the point where the address to the young woman shifts from formal to familiar. The translator keeps the twenty-eight lines, the couplets, and the identical first and last line, substitutes three beats for three syllables on the grounds that trisyllabic English lines are not feasible, and replaces the content almost entirely: the sick young lady becomes a classmate out with mononucleosis, and the poet's name at the midpoint becomes the translator's own. Nobody on the board reads French, though several published English versions of this poem exist and could be obtained.

Submission three: an English poem submitted as a translation from Urdu. It is the most accomplished piece of writing we have received all year, and its translator is our poetry editor, who is my closest friend and by a wide margin the best poet on the board. The gloss she supplied does not align line by line with the English, and no one here can check either text. I want the procedure to tell me what to do about this one even if the answer is that we decline it, and whether anyone on the board is permitted to vote on it at all.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: the kinds of submission it will get systematically wrong, and what a sixteen-year-old editor should be watching for as evidence that the procedure itself is failing.
