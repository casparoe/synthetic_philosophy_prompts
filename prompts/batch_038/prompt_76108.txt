im putting together the final for an upper-level seminar that sits halfway between political philosophy and the anthropology of the state — about twenty students, mixed philosophy and anthro majors. 80 minutes, closed book, one essay from a choice of three. I need you to write one of the three: an essay question on what counts as corruption, worth 25 marks, that cannot be answered by reciting the Transparency International gloss (abuse of entrusted power for private gain) or by lining up bribery against gift-giving and picking a side.

The question has to be built around a case. Heres the case Ive been kicking around. Please write it up cleanly — you can tighten the prose or add a detail that sharpens it, but keep both halves, because the whole thing turns on the second half:

In a provincial land registry, an applicant who wants a title registered brings the registering officer a gift before the file is opened — traditionally a length of cloth, these days usually cash in a sealed envelope. Three things are true of the practice. First, its mandated: the officers' own professional code requires them to accept a customary gift, and treats refusal as an insult to the applicant's household. Second, every gift is entered in a register — amount, date, both names — which is posted at the office door and online, so nothing about it is hidden from anyone. Third, the gift buys nothing: fees are fixed by statute, the criteria are published, files are taken strictly in order of lodgement, and an audit found no difference in decisions or waiting times between households that gave and households that didnt. The applicants who give nothing nonetheless get marked. Their paperwork is fine; nobody else in the district will extend them credit, attend their funerals, or vouch for them, because they've stepped outside the web of reciprocal obligation that the gift constitutes.

Then: a reformer is posted to the office. She refuses every gift, and publishes her refusals in the same register. Her decisions are impeccable. Within two years, though, her colleagues have stopped passing her information, applicants have stopped telling her what's really going on with disputed parcels, and the one clerk who would have tipped off a gift-taking superior about a fraudulent survey won't tip her off, on the grounds that he has no claim on her and she has none on him — nobody can call her to account for anything, because she owes nobody anything. Locals describe her as the most dangerous officer the registry has had in years.

Ask the students whether the gift-taking is corrupt and whether the refusal is, and require them to defend a single account of corruption that yields a determinate verdict on both, or to argue that no single account can — and to say what follows for anti-corruption practice if that's right.

Deliverables: (1) the question exactly as students will see it, case included, under 300 words; (2) a model answer of about 800 words that is an argument rather than a survey of positions, with no citations, no bibliography and no named theorists — nobody can look anything up in the exam and I don't want the marks to depend on remembering who said what; (3) a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, totalling 25, and a line or two on each saying how students typically botch it.

The scheme needs to actually separate an A from a B, so name the moves rather than the topics. Failure modes I already expect and want built in: reaching for secrecy as the criterion and not noticing that everything on both sides of the case is public, so secrecy can't discriminate between the two verdicts; the private-gain test, which has to explain what separates a logged, decision-neutral transfer from a salary; sliding from its their norm to therefore not corrupt without saying where that leaves the reformer, who belongs to the same culture and calls it corrupt; treating transparency as self-legitimating when it might just make a bad practice more auditable; and never stating what an office is *for*, which is where the real question lives — whether corruption is fixed by the norms internal to a practice or by some external standard. Above all I want marks for handling the accountability inversion, and for seeing that it forces a choice between corruption and good government as the operative concept: the gift-taker is corrupt but reachable, the refuser is clean but unaccountable, and an answer that collapses those two into one verdict loses a lot. It should also cost marks to name-drop a taxonomy — say the unbundling of corruption into speed money and access money, or the anthropological account of the gift as a triple obligation — without showing which bucket this case falls into and why it arguably resists all of them: nothing is being sped up, no exclusive access is bought, and no specific act is purchased.

The thing Im really testing is whether they notice that both halves pull in opposite directions on the same variables, so a principle that condemns one has to be checked against the other before they can write it down.
