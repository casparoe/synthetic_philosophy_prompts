I'm on a working group at a European museum writing a policy for the Benin plaques we hold — about ninety of them, taken in the 1897 expedition and then bought piecemeal on the market over the next few decades, so most of our accession records say "purchased, Hamburg, 1908" and nothing else. Our senior curator reaches for the same comparison in every single meeting: a museum is a library. You don't ship a book back to the country its author was born in; what matters is that the text is readable by anyone who wants it, and now we can scan it and send the scan anywhere in the world for free. From that he gets three concrete positions, and he thinks the analogy earns all three: (1) we return only the items where provenence clearly shows violent taking, and keep the rest; (2) high-res digitisation plus open access discharges whatever is left of any claim; (3) saying these things belong to humanity isn't a dodge, it's just the open-shelves principle, and the people who object are the ones being parochial.

At the last session our counterpart from the partner institution in Benin City pushed back with basically the opposite comparison: these are not books, they are more like human remains — they were made for one particular court, they are that court's own record of itself, the court that commissioned them still exists, and we already accept without argument that skulls and grave goods get handed back to descendants rather than kept "for humanity." So they should be handled under the same rules we already apply to remains.

Can you audit both of these properly? I want the correspondence laid out explicitly — for the library one, what in our museum plays the role of the library, the book, the text, the reader, the acquisition desk; for the remains one, what plays the role of the body, the descendant community, the funeral. Then the disanalogies, and I want them sorted into the ones that actually destroy the use being made of the analogy versus the ones that are just interesting but don't bite. In particular, dig into what "it's a library" is quietly assuming: something about who owns things when nobody has a title, something about whether an object's meaning is separable from the object and can be shipped as a scan, and something about who counts as "the public" given that most of the people who'd use our open access can get a visa here far more easily than the people whose court made the plaques can. And crucially — grant him the analogy for the sake of argument and tell me whether (1), (2) and (3) still follow, or whether they were being smuggled in by the framing and a library would behave differently anyway. Libraries do deaccession, libraries do have interlibrary loan and deposit agreements, libraries do return stolen items — does any of that give him a version of his own conclusion he can keep?

Last bit: if both analogies are load-bearing in the wrong way, what would a better one be? I keep wanting to reach for something like a crown, or a national archive, or a witness in a trial, but I don't trust my instincts here — tell me which comparison would actually carry the reasoning without doing the smuggling, or whether the honest answer is that no analogy works and the argument has to be made some other way.
