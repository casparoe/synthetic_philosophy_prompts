Been chewing on Goodhart's law and the episode I keep coming back to is the one the law was actually born out of: UK monetary targeting. Rough version of the facts, and correct me where I've got them wrong — published targets for broad money from 1976 under the Labour government, then £M3 made the centrepiece of the Medium-Term Financial Strategy in the 1980 Budget; exchange controls abolished in autumn 1979 and the corset scrapped in 1980; £M3 then blew through it's target range spectacularly over 1980–81; the response being a sequence of widened bands, rebasings, £M1 and M0 brought in alongside, and eventually £M3 demoted to something like a monitoring range while M0 became the aggregate that actually did any work. By the mid-80s it was all over.

Here's the position I want you to go after rather than just explain back to me. I don't think this is a case of Goodhart's law at all — not of the version everybody quotes, roughly that a measure stops being a good measure once it's used as a target. Because the households and banks who made £M3 useless weren't gaming anything. They were reacting to interest rates, to deregulation, to new instruments; nobody was steering behaviour towards a published number in order to look good on it. The measure's informational content got destroyed by the policy environment moving underneath it, which sounds much more like a Lucas-style point than like specification gaming. On that reading the popular formulation smuggles in an agent who is trying to hit the target, and it leans on that agent harder than it admits.

So, in order: map the episode properly first — who the players were (Treasury, Bank, the money market, the press and markets interpreting the number), what counted as hitting the target, and what escape hatches existed. Then tell me what would have to be true for me to be wrong. Are there real candidates for gaming in there — banks reclassifying deposits to keep them out of the aggregate, say? And the one I expect you'll push: the target-setter itself metric-shopping between £M3, £M1 and M0 depending on which behaved. Is choosing which specification to publish a form of specification gaming, and if so, who's gaming whom?

Then the harder bit: can the law be restated so it covers both the adversarial cases and £M3 without collapsing into "proxies sometimes stop working", which risks nothing at all? If it can, say what it then predicts that the rival account — measurement decay under structural change — doesn't. And finally, since I work near AI stuff: does transporting the law from monetary policy to reward hacking preserve it or distort it?

British English please, and no need for references. Do tell me how confident you are in each of the main claims, and single out the point you're least sure of — I'd guess it'll be some detail around the 1981 Budget, but say where you actually think the thin ice is.
