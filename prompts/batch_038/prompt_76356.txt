I am writing a term paper for a philosophy of science seminar on the epistemology of thought experiments, and I have reached the point where I cannot responsibly evaluate another proposal until I know what I ought to be demanding of one. So please write the specification: what any account of how thought experiments can yield knowledge of the world must deliver before it counts as adequate. Fix the purpose as follows, since adequacy is relative to it — I want an account usable as a screening device, one I could apply to a thought experiment I had never encountered, in a field where I am not a specialist, and come away with a verdict on whether its conclusion is genuinely licensed by what the thinker already possessed rather than smuggled in.

Give me the requirements list, each with the case that motivates it. I would like the cases to include the failures and the awkward ones, not merely the parade examples: Galileo's two bodies strapped together, which appears to demolish a rival physics without a single new observation; Einstein's hole argument, which persuaded him to abandon a principle he later restored; Maxwell's demon, whose verdict has shifted as facts about information and its erasure entered a debate that had run for roughly a century without them; and those scenarios in which competent readers reliably split into opposite verdicts about the very same setup.

Alongside the cases, tell me which distinctions an account must not collapse — I have in mind at least the difference between a thought experiment and a merely illustrative example, and between felt conviction and warrant, though I expect there are others — and which findings from psychology and from the history of science it cannot contradict. Then, critically, sort the requirements into genuine constraints and partisan prejudices. I already suspect that insisting a thought experiment be reconstructible as an argument with only empirical premises is doing camp work rather than describing the target phenomenon, and I want to know where else the bias creeps in. If some subset of the requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly and show the derivation; if the list is itself the negative result, that is what I most need to hear. And identify the single requirement that disqualifies the largest number of live positions.

One further requirement I do not think the existing debate addresses, and I would like you to say whether it is legitimate or whether I should drop it: because the account is meant to function as a screening device, its tests must be applicable by a non-expert without reconstructive work that only a specialist could perform. State what that costs, and whether any candidate can pay it.

Finally, test the whole list on a case of my own devising. Suppose a thought experiment delivered as a bare numbered list — no imagery, no concrete particulars, no narrative — that readers nonetheless report experiencing as compelling in the same way they experience vivid scenarios, and that they defend against counterexamples by reinterpreting the premises rather than revising the conclusion. Must an account accommodate thought experiments with no presentational force at all? If so, what becomes of any requirement that appeals to the vividness or concreteness of the scenario in order to explain its evidential feel?

Only after all of that, a short scoring of the three leading positions — the reconstruction view, the Platonic or a priori-insight view, and the mental-modeling or simulation view — against the list. Keep that to a few lines; the criteria are where I want the thinking done.
