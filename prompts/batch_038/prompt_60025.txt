I've been collecting cases where I can't decide whether to say someone still knows how to do a thing. Gut calls first, then what I want from you.

- My grandmother had a stroke. She still knits complicated patterns without looking, but ask her how she does it and she gets frustrated and says nothing useful. She knows how to knit.
- My brother was a decent guitarist, didn't touch an instrument for 25 years, picked one up last week and was fine. Knows how.
- A coach I worked with could take a bad swimmer and make them good, and could diagnose anyone's stroke in two seconds, but he swims like a dog and can't demonstrate anything. I still say he knows how to swim.
- A kid who memorized the textbook steps for a surgical knot, recites them perfectly but freezes at the table. Doesn't know how yet.
- A woman who drove for years, was in a bad crash, is now terrified of being behind the wheel and physically can't do it. She still knows how to drive, I think — but I cant say what makes her different from the kid with the knot.
- A guy who has done the same commute ten thousand times but can't draw a map and is helpless if you start him anywhere else. Knows how to get to work? I'd say yes, though I feel like he's missing something a taxi driver has.

The pair I can't reconcile is the coach and the kid: neither can perform the thing, and I give opposite verdicts. My tentative rule is "they know how if they can do it when it matters," but that clearly breaks on the crash victim.

So: what principle, if any, gets all six right? Tell me what it predicts for a case I left out — a chess player paralyzed from the neck down who still calls out moves and beats strong players — and be straight about which of my calls you'd have to overrule to get something simpler.

Bottom line in your first sentence (the principle, or your best candidate), then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my cases back at me.
