I lead the autonomy team at a company that runs automated guided vehicles in a container terminal, and next month we're adding an experimental low-impact term to the planner's reward: alongside the term for completing assigned moves, a charge for reducing what remains possible afterward. Concretely, the vehicle is penalized in proportion to how much it shrinks the set of terminal configurations it could still bring about, and how much it degrades its own later ability to serve a short fixed list of secondary objectives (keep emergency lanes passable, don't damage other equipment, stay operable). One of our senior engineers thinks the whole approach is confused: choosing the counterfactual baseline against which shrinkage is measured, choosing which secondary objectives make the list, and choosing how to weight and discount them are each substantive value commitments, so we haven't avoided specifying what we care about — we've buried the specification in hyperparameters nobody will audit. I think he's moving too fast: the penalty tracks the structure of the future rather than its desirability, and structure can be measured without knowing what is good.

Write a dialogue between the two of us at a design review, each trying to bring the other around. We should be equally sharp and equally familiar with the planner's internals, and we should answer each other's actual moves rather than restate our positions. Have us work through, at minimum: the case in which the assigned task is itself the intended and irreversible change, so the penalty must somehow separate doing one's job from collateral damage; the case in which the vehicle can avoid all side effects only by refusing the task; whether preserving options is really value-neutral or is already one value among others; and whether any baseline comparison is arbitrary. Neither of us should be a strawman. Close with the disagreement narrowed to one specific decision about the form of the penalty term, and with each of us saying what would actually change our mind.
