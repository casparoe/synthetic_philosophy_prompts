So I got into a two-hour argument with my friend Nadia last night about Libya in 2011 and I genuinely can't tell which of us won, and I suspect we were argueing past each other about what the criteria even mean rather than about the facts. She used to do protection work for an NGO and her line is that the intervention was legitimate on the day Resolution 1973 went through — ten in favour, five abstentions from Brazil, Germany, India, China and Russia, nobody against — because what the Council actually authorized was all necessary measures to protect civilians and civilian populated areas under threat of attack, while "excluding a foreign occupation force of any form on any part of Libyan territory," so anything that went wrong afterwards is a complaint about how a valid mandate got executed, not about whether going in was right. My line is that the drift from saving Benghazi to hunting Gaddafi wasn't an execution glitch, it was latent in the mandate from the start, and that the abstainers said as much in the room: Brazil's ambassador explained the abstention partly on the grounds that using force might do "more harm than good to the very same civilians we are committed to protecting," which is a prediction, and a correct one.

What I want from you is a disciplined case analysis of the whole 2011 arc through R2P, and I mean the actual machinery of the doctrine, not the op-ed version. So: the 2005 summit language with its three pillars and its threshold of a state "manifestly failing" to protect its population, plus the precautionary criteria the Canadian-sponsored commission had laid out in 2001 — just cause, right intention, last resort, proportional means, reasonable prospects. Walk me through which of those the intervention satisfied on March 17th and which it stopped satisfying later, and be specific about the pivots: the African Union ceasefire roadmap that got rejected in the spring, the recognition of the rebel council as the legitimate interlocutor, the air campaign shifting from halting columns to hitting command nodes and then the family compound in late April (I think a son and some grandchildren were killed, correct me if I've got that wrong), the ground advisers, the convoy strike outside Sirte in October. Then I want you to take Nadia's defence seriously as a double-effect move — regime change as foreseen but unintended, the only available means of making civilian protection stick — and tell me whether double effect can carry that weight here or whether it collapses into the usual problem of describing your intentions narrowly enough that anything inconvenient becomes a side effect.

And then run it the other way, because this is the part we never got to before she had to leave. What does Libya do to R2P as a doctrine? Two things I want pressed on specifically. First, the reasonable prospects criterion looks like it can only ever be judged ex post, which means either it's a real constraint that nobody could have applied in March 2011, or it's an unfalsifiable rhetorical device you invoke afterwards to bless or damn whatever happened — is there a version of it that's actually action-guiding at the moment of decision, and if so what evidence would have satisfied it about a country with no institutions, no army that wasn't Gaddafi's, and a opposition made of defectors and volunteers? Second, the pillar problem: pillar one puts the protection duty on the sovereign state, and the intervention's whole point was to destroy the thing that was failing that duty, so after October 2011 who does the responsibility revert to, and does the doctrine have any resources at all for that handover or does it just go silent? Also tell me whether the Syria veto pattern that followed is best read as the system learning a lesson about reasonable prospects or as five states deciding that pillar three was a trap — because those two readings imply completely different verdicts on whether 2011 broke the norm or just revealed what it had always been.
