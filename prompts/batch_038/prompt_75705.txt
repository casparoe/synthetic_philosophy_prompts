You are acting as my senior clerk. The jurisdiction and case described below are entirely fictional, and every fact you need is stated here; do not cite or refer to real-world constitutions, statutes, or case law.

**Setting.** The Republic of Vessane adopted an entrenched bill of rights in 2009. Its Constitutional Court, seated since 2011, has nine members and may annul statutes in whole or in part. Under Article 151, the Attorney-General may refer enacted legislation to the Court for abstract review before it enters into force; private parties cannot. The Constitution contains, among others, these provisions:

- *Article 12* vests the judicial power in the courts and provides that no body exercising functions of a judicial nature may be created outside the judicial power.
- *Article 22* guarantees the right to assemble peacefully and without arms, subject to such restrictions as are necessary in a democratic society for the protection of public order.
- *Article 96* guarantees to everyone the right to have any determination of his civil rights and obligations made by a court that is independent of the executive and of the parties.
- *Article 148(3)* permits Parliament, by an express declaration in the statute itself, to provide that the statute or a specified provision shall operate for five years notwithstanding Article 22. It has been invoked once, in 2016, and never since.
- *Article 150(2)* empowers the Court, in place of immediate annulment, to declare a provision incompatible with the Constitution and to suspend the effect of that declaration for up to twelve months, leaving the provision operative in the meantime.

**The statute.** The Public Order and Assembly Act was enacted on 14 March and enters into force on 1 November. It was introduced after three nights of rioting in the capital, in which two people died; debate on the floor was closed by motion after eleven hours, and the two opposition parties voted against it. Its operative scheme is as follows.

Section 4 designates a zone comprising the parliamentary precinct, three ministries, the central rail interchange, and the two public squares adjoining them. Within the zone, any assembly of more than twenty persons requires a license. Section 7 requires thirty days' notice and directs the District Administrator — a civil servant appointed by, and removable at the pleasure of, the Interior Minister — to cap attendance at whatever figure he judges the available crowd-control capacity to support, and to refuse a license where he reasonably apprehends that the assembly will occasion violence, serious disruption to the life of the community, or damage to property. Section 7(5) provides that the Administrator's decisions are reviewable only by an internal tribunal constituted within the Interior Ministry, whose members serve at the Minister's discretion, and that no court has jurisdiction to entertain any application concerning such a decision. Section 12 provides that evidence relating to the anticipated conduct of an assembly is inadmissible in any proceeding under the Act unless the Administrator consents to its production.

The referring memorandum records that the Minister, in debate, relied on the unpublished findings of a three-person expert panel he had commissioned on crowd dynamics, and stated that the panel had confirmed the necessity of a thirty-day notice period and of attendance caps; the report has never been released, and the opposition was refused an opportunity to question its authors.

**The petitioner and the referral.** A civic association that has held a monthly vigil in one of the two squares for eleven years asked the Attorney-General to refer the Act. She has done so under Article 151, asking the Court to determine whether sections 4, 7, 7(5), and 12 are constitutional, and whether the Act is a valid exercise of Parliament's power under Article 22.

**The Court's own case law, which pulls in both directions.** In 2017 the Court upheld a statutory ceiling on third-party electoral broadcasting expenditure, reasoning that where a legislature acts on a prediction about future social behaviour, and the prediction is one on which reasonable legislators could differ, the Court's task is to satisfy itself that Parliament addressed its mind to the question and had some material before it — not to substitute its own forecast. In 2021 the Court annulled an emergency curfew order, reasoning that an official assurance is not evidence, that the government had been invited to produce the data underlying its risk assessment and had declined, and that deference to an assertion the Court is forbidden to test is not deference but abdication. Neither decision was unanimous, and the two have never been reconciled.

**The panel.** The matter comes before a five-judge panel. Two members incline to the view that the referral is premature because no license has yet been refused and no one's rights have yet been violated; three incline to hear it. Two members consider section 7(5) fatal in itself; two consider it severable from an otherwise permissible licensing scheme; one is undecided.

**Your task.** Draft the memorandum I will circulate to the panel on 2 October, in advance of the hearing on 9 October. It should run to roughly 1,500–2,000 words, be written in formal prose under your own headings, and do the following:

1. Open with a bottom line stated without hedging: the precise disposition you recommend for each of sections 4, 7, 7(5), and 12, the remedy you recommend for each (immediate annulment; declaration of incompatibility with suspension under Article 150(2), specifying the period and what Parliament must be told it has to do; or dismissal), and the threshold ruling on prematurity.
2. Explain why the Court may properly examine the ouster in section 7(5) and the evidentiary bar in section 12 at all, given that they concern the allocation of judicial power rather than the content of the right to assemble — and given the objection that a court created by the Constitution cannot use the Constitution to disable the legislature from organising tribunals.
3. Address directly the Minister's strongest argument, which is not that rights are unimportant but that the thirty-day period and the attendance caps rest on a predictive judgment about crowd behaviour that a court is institutionally ill-equipped to second-guess. Explain how far this argument succeeds as to section 7, and state plainly whether the unpublished and untestable character of the panel report distinguishes this case from the 2017 decision, brings it within the 2021 decision, or requires you to say that those two decisions cannot both stand and which should be overruled. If you would overrule one, say so and justify it.
4. Consider the argument, which two members of the panel find compelling, that the Court should leave section 7(5) intact because striking it down invites a confrontation the Court may lose: Parliament could re-enact the provision under Article 148(3), and the Court would then be unable to reach it. Assess whether Article 148(3) would in fact shelter section 7(5) from review, treating it as an open question of construction and giving your view on it, and explain what the answer implies for the prudence argument. If you conclude that the override would shelter the provision, say whether that changes your recommendation.
5. Devote a section to the strongest case against your recommendation as a whole, stated in its most persuasive form and in the voice of someone who accepts your constitutional premises but rejects your conclusion — not as a straw man and not as a survey of rival theories. Then say what, in your view, would have to be true for that case to prevail.
6. Identify, in two or three sentences, what the panel should decline to decide because it is not necessary to the disposition, and explain why restraint on those points serves rather than evades the judgment.

Write as though the panel will act on this document. Do not append a bibliography or a list of authorities beyond the Vessane materials described above.
