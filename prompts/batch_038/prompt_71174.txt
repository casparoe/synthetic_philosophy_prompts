Below is a draft I'm taking to a philosophy-of-mind workshop, and I'd like critical comments rather than encouragement. It's about Dennett's two-bitser — the coin-detecting device whose state is a detector of quarters here and, once the same machine is shipped to Bolivia, a detector of local coins that the machine cannot tell from quarters — and about whether that case really does what it is usually taken to do against Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality.

**Draft: "The Two-Bitser and the Relocation Test"**

1. Searle's Chinese Room argument is usually summarized as a claim about syntax and semantics, but its load-bearing premise is a distinction between two kinds of aboutness. Some states are about things in their own right; others are about things only because somebody treats them as being about those things, the way the marks on a page are about something only in virtue of our reading them. Dennett's two-bitser is aimed at this distinction. The device is trivially simple: it has two states, one of which goes off when a coin of the right size and weight passes the sensor. Nothing in the device's construction or in the causal history of its state makes it a detector of quarters rather than of the Bolivian coin it cannot distinguish from one, and so what its state is about can be made to shift by moving the machine, without any change in the machine. The lesson generally drawn is that there is no such thing as underived aboutness: all intentionality is derived, ours included, and the original/derived distinction is a myth.

2. My claim is that the two-bitser is doing something narrower than this, and that once you see what it is doing, it stops being an argument against Searle's distinction. The device is being used as a test: does the content ascription survive relocation to a new environment? Call this the relocation test. Hidden in its use as a refutation is an assumption — that a state with genuine, underived aboutness would pass the relocation test, carrying its content with it, owing it to nothing but what the state is in itself.

3. That assumption, though, is just the denial of externalism about content, and externalism is not what is at issue between Dennett and Searle. Twin Earth cases make precisely the same point about the relocation test: what a thinker's water-thought is about depends on which substance his community is embedded in, and relocating him changes the content. Nobody concludes from this that his thoughts are therefore not really about anything, or that they are about something only because an interpreter takes them to be so. Externalism is a claim about the determination base of content — about where the facts that fix content live — and not a claim about the ontological standing of content. So a state whose aboutness shifts with its surroundings is entirely compatible with its aboutness being underived. Searle's word for what he wants is unfortunate here, because intrinsicness in the sense of "not owed to anyone's interpretation" does not require intrinsicness in the sense of "fixed by the state's own intrinsic properties." Read against externalism, the two-bitser refutes a narrow-content thesis that the debate does not need Searle to hold.

4. To get from the relocation test to the collapse of the distinction, a further premise is needed, one the thought experiment is often read as supplying but does not actually establish: that the Bolivian relocation makes only a Cambridge change to the device. If the state's causal profile, its dispositions, and its history are all exactly as they were, then there is nothing left for the change in aboutness to be grounded in except the practices of whoever uses the state. Call this the interpretability premise. Once it is on the table, the relocation test has quietly become a different test — not a test for whether content is fixed by environmental relations, but a test for whether content is fixed by another agent's interpretive activity. The two tests come apart, and only the second threatens Searle. The thought experiment invites us to conflate them because, in the Bolivian case, both come out the same way.

5. Reformulating the distinction by locus of determination rather than by intrinsic versus relational property also explains why the two-bitser is a poor test case for a reason that has nothing to do with Bolivia. The device is so simple that its own history underdetermines any single pattern of aboutness: it has almost no dispositions, no downstream role, no learning, and no error conditions beyond the one it was built with. Simplicity, not relocation, is what makes its content indeterminate, and the indeterminacy should be expected to worsen as the device gets more trivial. But Searle never claimed that trivial devices have underived intentionality; his claim is restricted to systems with the right causal powers. The two-bitser is thus a limit case masquerading as a general argument.

6. Two consequences follow. First, if the distinction survives at all, it survives as a distinction about who fixes content rather than about whether aboutness is an intrinsic property — which is a real change of subject, and a welcome one, since it drains the debate of any suggestion that Searle is positing a mysterious property of matter. Second, and less comfortably for Dennett, if all intentionality is derived, the derivation must terminate somewhere. Either there is a terminal interpreter whose ascriptions are not themselves ascriptions, which reintroduces what was denied, or interpretation bottoms out in convention and pragmatic success, in which case original intentionality has not been refuted so much as declared an idle posit. The second option is defensible, but it too is a change of subject, and it implies that Searle and Dennett are not disagreeing about a fact.

7. So the two-bitser shows three things: that trivial devices are indeterminate, that content is externally determined, and that the interesting question is not whether aboutness is intrinsic but whether it is owed to an interpreter. Only the third challenges Searle, and he answers it badly — by appeal to consciousness and to the requirement that an unconscious intentional state be capable of causing a corresponding conscious one. That answer, and not the coin machine, is where the debate should be had.
