I'm auditing a seminar on the philosophy of economics this term — the last two weeks were Aumann's agreement theorem and the Milgrom-Stokey no-trade result, which I mostly followed. Afterwards my friend Ravi, who's writing a dissertation on social epistemology, said that whole literature is sitting on top of a much more general argument that nobody states out loud, and he sketched it on a napkin. I've cleaned it up as best I can:

(1) A price carries information only because somebody spent real resources — time, data, analyst hours — finding out something that isn't yet in the price.
(2) Somebody spends those resources only if they expect to earn them back, and they earn them back by trading against counterparties who know less than they do.
(3) If everyone treated the price as settling the question — if each of us just read the price off and formed our belief accordingly — there'd be no such counterparties left, so the spending wouldn't pay, so nobody would spend.
(4) General principle (this one is Ravi's): if a belief is produced by a process that would stop producing reliable beliefs were everyone to form their beliefs that way, then that belief isn't justified. He calls it failing the free-rider test — you're drawing on an epistemic practice in a way that would deplete it if it became universal.
(5) So beliefs taken straight off a price fail the free-rider test, and aren't justified.
(6) The same goes for any aggregator that runs on contributions: peer review (if everyone just accepted the consensus, nobody would check anything), polls, prediction markets used to decide rather than to measure, the wisdom-of-crowds move in general.
(7) Most of what any of us believes about anything we haven't personally checked is taken off some aggregator or other.
(8) So most of our beliefs are unjustified.

Ravi is completely unbothered by (8) — he calls it skepticism with an economic engine and thinks that's a feature. I can't swallow it. I trust the price of a stock I've never looked at more than I trust my own guess about it, and I trust that a paper in a decent journal survived some checking, and I don't feel like I'm caught in a contradiction when I say both. But I also don't want to just wave my hands at (4), because I think some version of it has to be right: I want to be able to say that the person who reads a prediction market and stops investigating, or the committee that outsources its judgment to a ranking, is doing something epistemically wrong even when the numbers are currently good. For what its worth I'd rather give up (8) than (4).

So walk me through where this actually breaks. If there's a step that isn't valid, or a term doing two different jobs between two steps, name it, give me both readings, and say why the plausible reading doesn't get you to the next line. If instead it's valid all the way down, tell me which premise is cheapest to lose and argue for that on the merits, not by pointing at how weird (8) sounds. Then — this is the part I care about most — price the options: what exactly do I lose if I reject (4) outright, is there a weakened (4) that still licenses the criticisms in my last paragraph, and does whatever the standard economist's reply is here actually stop the argument or just relocate it to (6)? And if there's a repaired version that ends in something I should actually believe, write it out with the premises numbered again.
