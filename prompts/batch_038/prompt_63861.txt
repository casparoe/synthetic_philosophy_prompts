so i run a tiny philosophy podcast — genuinely about forty listeners — and next month's guest is a philosopher who bites the bullet all the way down on epiphenomenalism. he's not just a garden-variety epiphenomenalist, he has a specific story for dealing with the usual objection (the one that gets called the paradox of phenomenal judgment): the worry that if qualia do nothing at all, then my report that i'm having a red experience can't really be *about* the experience, since the experience isn't doing any of the causal work that produces the report — i would have said exactly the same thing in a world where the experience never happened.

his answer, which is the whole of his book, is roughly this: one neural event N causes both the quale Q and the report R, so R and Q co-vary perfectly without Q causing R — and he thinks that's all you ever needed for R to be about Q. he likes the line that his reports are about qualia the way a smoke alarm's beep is about the fire: a second effect of a common cause, not an effect of the thing reported on. layered on top, he says reference is fixed by acquaintance at the moment of judging, and that acquaintance needn't be a causal relation, so Q's being inert doesn't threaten reference.

i want 8-10 questions that actually engage that machinery instead of letting him restate it, and every one of them needs a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. he's very smooth, so the follow-ups matter more than the questions do. please make sure at least one question lands on each of these:

- whether the smoke-alarm analogy gets him what he wants. a beep isn't knowledge of a fire. i expect a critic to say his reports are at best reliable *indicators* of Q and not judgments about Q at all, so press him on where he draws the line between co-variance and aboutness, and whether he can draw it without quietly letting Q cause something.
- the indeterminacy worry. if Q and N always come as a pair and only N does any work, what makes it the case that 'pain' refers to Q rather than to N, or to the pair, or to the property of being caused by N? i want this pushed as a determinacy problem and not just a 'but how could you tell' problem, and i want the follow-up to take on his acquaintance move directly: what could acquaintance possibly do to discriminate between two perfectly co-varying candidates?
- whether his view differs from zombie functionalism in any way anyone could detect. if the two say the same thing about every report anyone will ever make, including every sentence of his own book, what is left of the disagreement? ask him for a case where Q changes the content of a judgment without changing anything causally, and if he can't give one, follow up on whether he ought to just concede it's a verbal difference — and if he won't concede, what he thinks the difference consists in.
- self-undermining. on his own picture he'd have written the same book in a world where Q didn't exist, or where Q was systematically otherwise. get him to say out loud whether he thinks his own belief is sensitive to the experiences it's about, then follow up on whether that's a cost he'll happily pay or whether it's meant to be a reductio of the whole package.
