Before I read another proposed solution to the boundary problem, I want the specification such a solution has to meet. The context is not ideal theory: I want a principle good enough that a court or an electoral commission could apply it in advance of a decision to fix the electorate for that decision — say, a city deciding whether to site a waste facility just inside its border, where who is affected depends on which site is chosen, and includes people who do not yet live in the area and will move there only if a particular option is adopted.

Write the desiderata for such a principle, giving the case or consideration behind each. Among the things I expect the list to engage: the principle must not presuppose the very unit it is meant to determine; it must return a determinate electorate rather than absorbing the whole world into every local question; it must say what happens to people subject to a decision who are not members, and to members whom the decision leaves untouched; it must cope with a constituency that is itself a function of the decision, including future and merely possible members; and it must be applicable by fallible officials under the uncertainty and time pressure that real decision-making involves.

For each requirement, mark whether it is non-negotiable for any adequate principle or a commitment of one camp that others may reasonably reject — and identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, since if the list is partially incoherent, that is the finding I most want to see argued rather than papered over. End with the single requirement that eliminates the most candidates, and a compact scorecard of the familiar contenders — interest-based, subjection-based, and membership-based principles — against the list.
