**The materials.** Passages A–D are quoted below as they are normally transcribed, with original capitalisation preserved.

**A. Molyneux to Locke, Dublin, 7 July 1688** (heading and closing formulae included):

> A Problem Proposed to the Author of the Essai Philosophique concernant L'Entendement
>
> A Man, being born blind, and having a Globe and a Cube, nigh of the same bignes, Committed into his Hands, and being taught or Told, which is Called the Globe, and which the Cube, so as easily to distinguish them by his Touch or Feeling; Then both being taken from Him, and Laid on a Table, Let us Suppose his Sight Restored to Him; Whether he Could, by his Sight, and before he touch them, know which is the Globe and which the Cube? Or Whether he Could know by his Sight, before he stretch'd out his Hand, whether he Could not Reach them, tho they were Removed 20 or 1000 feet from Him?
>
> If the Learned and Ingenious Author of the Forementiond Treatise think this Problem Worth his Consideration and Answer, He may at any time Direct it to One that Much Esteems him, and is, His Humble Servant, William Molyneux

**B. The version Locke printed in the second edition of the *Essay* (1694), Book II, ch. ix, §8:**

> Suppose a Man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a Cube, and a Sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and t'other; which is the Cube, which the Sphere. Suppose then the Cube and Sphere placed on a Table, and the Blind Man to be made to see. Quaere, Whether by his sight, before he touch'd them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube.

**C. Molyneux's own answer, as Locke reports it:**

> Not. For though he has obtain'd the experience of, how a Globe, how a Cube affects his touch; yet he has not yet attained the Experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so; Or that a protuberant angle in the Cube, that pressed his hand unequally, shall appear to his eye, as it does in the Cube.

**D. Locke's agreement, immediately following:**

> I agree with this thinking Gent. whom I am proud to call my Friend, in his answer to this his problem; and am of opinion, that the Blind Man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say, which was the Globe, which the Cube, whilst he only saw them: though he could unerringly name them by his touch, and certainly distinguish them by the difference of their Figures felt.

*Part II — The design of the case*

4. What work is done by "of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness" in B, and by "nigh of the same bignes" in A? My worry, which I want you to evaluate rather than reassure me about, is that this clause is usually treated as a control on non-shape cues, but it cannot be one: a cube and a sphere of the same metal and near-equal bigness differ by roughly a factor of two in mass and in surface area, so if the clause were meant to equalise what the hands receive, it fails. Consider both readings of "bigness" (linear extent; volume) and say, for each, whether the clause is a control, a vividness device, or an under-specification — and whether the under-specification creates a real ambiguity in the case rather than a merely pedantic one.

5. Both A and B specify exactly two objects, one cube and one sphere, and demand that the subject "tell which is which." A subject with no visual knowledge whatever therefore succeeds half the time by guessing. Does the design of the case thus fail to separate *cannot tell* from *can tell*? Is there a reading of "know" in A, or of "distinguish, and tell" in B, that repairs this — for instance by importing a requirement that the subject be able to say why, or a requirement of certainty? Note that D adds "with certainty to say," a phrase absent from both A and B: assess whether that addition is Locke doing repair work on Molyneux's formulation, or merely his own idiom.

6. Examine the two prohibitions, "before he touch them" in A and "before he stretch'd out his Hand" in A's second question. Is each a restriction on when the verdict is delivered, or a restriction on what the subject may do — that is, a ban on exploratory movement and on visually guided reaching? If the latter, does the ban rule out the only route by which the answer could ever become "yes"? And is there an internal tension in A between the first question, which forbids touching, and the second, whose content is precisely about whether the subject could touch?

*Part III — The two versions, and the framing*

10. **The hypothesis I most want attacked.** My working thesis for the commentary is that the second question in A is not a redundant afterthought but changes the subject: the first question asks whether sight can deliver the same content that touch delivered, while the second asks whether sight can deliver information calibrated to the subject's own bodily capacities, since a sphere at a thousand feet is not reachable and knowing that is not a matter of identifying its shape at all. On this reading the pair of questions draws a distinction between object identification and action-relevant spatial information that is standardly presented as a much later development, and Locke's omission of the second question is not a simplification but a loss.
