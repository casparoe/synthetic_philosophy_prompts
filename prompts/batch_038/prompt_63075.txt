I keep the minutes for a volunteer-run workshop and tool library in a small town — a shared woodworking shop and a lending shelf of tools, all staffed by members on rota, all of it running on an honour system with a notebook and a tin for cash. After a bad autumn, in which two cordless drills, a set of clamps and a router simply never came back, and one member left a bandsaw with the guard off and the blade still running when he stepped out, the committee has split down the middle over a proposal to fit four cameras in the shop and to require a signed-in name against every borrowed item. I have been asked to write the proposal up for the general membership before the vote, and while taking notes I noticed that a single sentence is doing nearly all of the arguing, and that it is being made to do opposite jobs by opposite sides.

The sentence is "character is what you do when no one is watching." People in our group attach it to different famous names — a Christian writer, a basketball coach — and no one can actually say which, if any. Our longest-serving member, a retired schoolteacher, uses it against the cameras: on her account, conduct produced by a lens is not virtue at all, and a shop in which people behave only because they are recorded has ceased to be a moral community and become a supervised one; she adds that you cannot grow character if every act is observed, since the point of the unobserved moment is that it is the only one that counts and the only one you can practise in. The treasurer, who is the man who chased up the missing drills, uses the identical sentence for the cameras: if what really matters is behaviour nobody sees, then the whole difficulty is that we cannot see it, and fitting cameras simply abolishes the unsupervised space in which the thefts happened. A third member, younger, threw in an old story he remembered from somewhere — I believe it is in the second book of Plato's Republic, where a shepherd finds a gold ring on a corpse after an earthquake opens the ground, discovers that twisting it makes him invisible, and promptly uses it to seduce the queen, murder the king and take the throne — as a way of saying that every member, the decent ones included, would empty our tool shelf if they were certain no one would ever know, so the schoolteacher's faith in us is sentimental. It seems to me that all three cannot be drawing legitimately on the same materials, but I cannot say where any of the steps goes wrong.

Could you take that sentence apart and give me a list I can put into the write-up? What I want is the range of distinct, precise claims it might be making, from whatever is close to a truism through to the strong empirical thesis, and for each one: what it commits you to about the word "watching" — does it require a mind that can disapprove of you, or does a camera with nobody behind the screen count, or a recording that a stranger may look at next Tuesday, or God, or your own later memory of yourself; whether the claim is about evidence (unobserved acts are the best guide to what someone is like) or about what character consists in (unobserved acts are the only ones that make or show it); whether it concerns single acts or how a person turns out over years; and whether it is a description of people or a recommendation to them. Then tell me which of those readings are plausibly true, which are empty, which are straightforwardly false, and — this is the part I most need — which particular reading each of my three members has to be relying on for their argument to work, and whether any of them could hold their position on a weaker reading they would be happier defending. Finally, there is a result I half-remember from a radio programme, that people put something like three times as much money into an unsupervised honesty box for coffee when a picture of a pair of eyes is pinned above it as when a picture of flowers is up instead; if the slogan is supposed to explain that, say which version of it does the explaining, and whether a fitted camera is "eyes" in the relevant sense or something else entirely. I am not asking you to settle the camera question for us — the vote will happen whichever way I write — only to stop one sentence functioning as a conversation-stopper, and to make clear what we would each be signing up to.
