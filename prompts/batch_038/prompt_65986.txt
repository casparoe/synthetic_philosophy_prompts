I'm putting together a ~6,000 word paper for a small workshop on temporal experience and I've hit a wall, so I'd be really grateful for some honest comments on the argument sketch below. Bit of context: I'm a second-year postgrad, my background is in philosophy rather than psychology, and my supervisor is broadly sympathetic to Dennett, which is awkward because the paper started life as a critique of him and has ended up somewhere I'm not sure he'd object to. It's currently called "The Experiment You Can't Run", which I'm aware sounds a bit gimmicky. Please tell me if it does.

The sketch, as it stands:

1. Colour phi. Two differently coloured dots — say red up top, blue lower down — flashed in rapid succession at the right spacing and interval. Observers don't report two flashes; they report a single dot travelling between the positions, and they report it changing colour abruptly somewhere around the midpoint of the illusory path. Kolers and von Grünau ran the original studies, apparently after Goodman wondered what would happen if you put a colour change into apparent motion.

2. Why it's philosophically loaded. At the moment the dot is supposedly changing colour midway, nothing about the second dot has happened yet, and the same first dot could have been followed by a different colour, or by nothing at all. So whatever state carries the mid-path colour change cannot have been caused by the second flash in the normal way. That generates the two familiar options: either the experience of the interval is composed after the second flash arrives, all at once, so nothing is revised — the delay option, the Stalinesque one; or the interval was experienced one way and the record was subsequently rewritten, so the subject's report is a piece of post-hoc confabulation — the Orwellian option.

3. My actual claim, and the part I'm least sure of: this is a false dichotomy, and colour phi doesn't just fail to settle it, it shows the question is malformed. Both options assume that there is a determinate content to the interval — something it was like across those hundred-odd milliseconds — which either did or didn't get revised. I want to deny the assumption. On the view I'm gesturing at (I've been calling it the *unsettled interval*), what the subject experienced during the interval simply has no truth value independent of the discriminations that end up getting made. That is emphatically not the delay option with the serial number filed off: delay posits a determinate content delivered late, which is still a theatre, just with a buffer. My view says there was no settled content to deliver.

4. The underdetermination move, which is where the title comes from. To tell Orwellian from Stalinesque apart you need some readout of what the interval was like that is independent of the interval's eventual report — a second channel that could contradict the first. But every readout we have is itself a later discrimination. So the two hypotheses aren't merely underdetermined by the data we happen to possess; they're underdetermined by any data of that kind. Hence: the experiment you can't run. I think this is the strongest thing in the paper and also the thing most likely to make a psychologist roll their eyes at me.

5. A loose end I want flagged. I have a half-memory of a follow-up study in which observers could discriminate the colour phi display from a matched display where the colour changed only at the second flash. If that's real, a referee will reach for it immediately and say it shows the second flash's properties genuinely were absent during the interval, which favours Orwellian. My reply is that discriminability between two displays tells you the two displays produce different downstream states, not when those differences became differences for the subject — forced-choice performance is just another one of those later readouts, so leaning on it begs the question. But I may have the study wrong or invented it entirely, so please say clearly if it doesn't sound right to you rather than playing along.

6. Where I want to land. Not "Dennett is refuted". If anything the paper argues that the Orwellian/Stalinesque pair, as it's usually deployed, is a simplification of the view it was introduced to illustrate, and that colour phi pushes you towards something more radical than either horn — no settled record to delay and none to rewrite. I have a speculative coda suggesting the third view makes a distinctive prediction about confidence: if content is unsettled rather than late or revised, metacognitive confidence should be locally degraded during ambiguous intervals in a way neither delay nor revision predicts, since both posit determinate content by the time of the report. I might cut this.
