The question on the problem set was:

"Using a concrete three-judge example, explain the discursive dilemma (a.k.a. the doctrinal paradox). Then say what you take it to show about whether groups like courts or committees can be intentional agents. Roughly 400 words. 20 points."

Here's the answer one of my students turned in:

---

The discursive dilemma is basically the Condorcet paradox applied to beliefs instead of preferences. Say a three-judge panel has to decide whether a defendant is liable, and liability requires both that the defendant acted (call it P) and that the act caused the harm (Q), so the conclusion R is just P and Q together. Judge 1 says P is true and Q is true, so R is true. Judge 2 says P is true but Q is false, so R is false. Judge 3 says P is false but Q is true, so R is false.

The panel can vote two different ways. If it votes premise by premise and then applies the rule, the majority says P (2-1) and the majority says Q (2-1), so the group is committed to R. If instead it votes straight on R, the majority says R is false, 2-1. So two of the three judges personally think there's no liability and yet the premise route finds the defendant liable. Notice too that the premise-based verdict isn't anybody's own verdict: Judge 1 accepts R, but Judges 2 and 3 each reject R while accepting one of the premises between them. That's the paradox — the group's answer depends on whether you aggregate issue by issue or case by case, and there's no neutral way to choose between them.

This is the standard argument for premise-based voting. Voting on the premises guarantees the group ends up with a logically consistent set of judgments no matter what the agenda looks like, which is what you want if you think of the group as reasoning rather than just tallying. Conclusion-based voting looks more democratic in the one-person-one-vote sense, but it lets the group reach a verdict that the reasoning doesn't support. Pettit uses all of this to argue that groups can't really be intentional agents at all, since their belief sets come apart from what any member holds and can be outright contradictory. On his view only individuals have minds, and corporate belief is a useful fiction, which is also why the impossibility theorem in this area is usually taken to settle the group agency question. So the upshot for courts is that appellate panels should always vote on the premises, and objections about that being less democratic are beside the point.
