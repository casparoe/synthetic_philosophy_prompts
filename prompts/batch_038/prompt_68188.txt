I TA a second-year philosophy of maths course and I'm putting together the week 8 problem set, on the possibility that our basic axioms are inconsistent. I want one question, worth 25 marks, doable in about an hour, and I specifically don't want it answerable by reciting Gödel's second theorem or by name-dropping paraconsistent logic.

Here's the setup I want to build it around: next year someone publishes a machine-checked proof of 0=1 from ZFC. Two things the question should force students to wrestle with.

First, explosion. A trivial theory proves everything, so all our old theorems are still theorems — and yet the discovery would somehow gut the evidential value of the proofs we already have. I want them to say precisely what is lost and what isn't, and to explain why "well, if explosion holds then everything is now trivially provable, so nothing we ever proved is safe" is a bad argument — or, if they think it's a good one, to say exactly where its force comes from.

Second, downward transmission. ZFC proves Con(PA), so does a contradiction in ZFC threaten PA? I want them to seperate the logical point (it doesn't) from the evidential point (that ZFC proof was part of why we trusted PA), and then to say something about whether the fragment of maths actually used in physics could survive the loss of the big axioms.

So please write the question itself, a model answer of maybe 700 words, and a marking scheme listing the moves a first-class answer has to make with marks attached, plus the specific wrong turns that should cost marks — e.g. conflating inconsistency with unsoundness, or claiming an inconsistency would be self-concealing because any proof of one would be too long to write down, which in this scenario it demonstrably wasn't.
