Our philosophy-of-probability reading group has now spent six weeks on Lewis's Principal Principle and I've come out the other side more confused than I went in — not about what the principle says, but about why anyone thinks the argument around it is still live. Here's where we got stuck, and correct me if I've garbled it. PP says: if your evidence tells you the chance of A is x, and the rest of your evidence is admissible, your credence in A should be x. The trouble is the admissibility clause, which looks circular — admissible evidence is cashed out as evidence that bears on A only through what it tells you about the chance. Hall then argued that PP combined with Lewis's own best-system account of chance generates cases where following PP gives clearly the wrong answer, a bug he thought was bad enough to sink the principle, and he offered a repaired version, the New Principle, whose change is on the right-hand side: instead of setting the credence to x outright, you set it equal to whatever your credence in A would be conditional on the chance hypothesis itself, which lets you drop the restriction to admissible evidence entirely. Then comes the objection that NP is false in completely ordinary cases — learn the coin's chance of heads is a half, then look and see it come up heads, and NP still seems to want a half — plus the counter-reply that the objector is computing a conditional nobody claimed anything about. Running alongside all that is a second, separate argument: whether PP is supposed to be a priori, near-analytic, part of what makes chance the concept it is, or a substantive empirical claim about the world that could come out false. And there's a decision-theoretic line of attack claiming PP can't be a blanket requirement of rationality because it clashes with strict conditionalization in some setups, which I never could follow. My advisor wants ten pages on where this dispute actually stands and I dont want to write a survey.

My hypothesis, which I can't argue for yet: nobody is contradicting anybody. The fight is over what job the word chance is being hired to do — either it's a term whose meaning is fixed by the deference role it plays for credence, or a term whose meaning is fixed by a reductive story about the total pattern of events in the world — and each side's principle comes out right under its own conception, so PP versus NP, and a priori versus empirical, are really three different disputes wearing one trenchcoat. Is that right, or is it too tidy? Alternatives I want you to actually consider rather than wave off: that there's a genuine contradiction somewhere and the stalemate is just stubbornness; that the sides differ about standards of evidence — what could ever tell against a principle meant to be analytic; that they differ about which cases count as data, the bug cases versus coin tosses versus radioactive decay versus weather forecasts; that the real disagreement is about what an explanation of chance owes us; or that the question is ill-posed and there's a better-posed one underneath it. Diagnose it from what each side actually claims, not from the shape of the literature, and then tell me what would count as progress.
