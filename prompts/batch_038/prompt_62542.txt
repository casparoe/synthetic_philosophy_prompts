I am several drafts into a novel whose central object is a municipal pedestrian crossing, and I keep failing to make that artifact carry politics without letting a character deliver a speech about it. Before I revise further I want to see how a shorter and better work manages it, so: read Melville's Bartleby, the Scrivener through Langdon Winner's claim that artifacts have politics, attending to both of his arguments rather than only the famous one. Everyone remembers the Long Island overpasses built too low for buses, the case in which a designed object settles in advance who may pass; Winner's harder second claim is that some technologies are inherently political, requiring or ruling out particular arrangements of authority whatever anyone intends. Which of these, if either, describes the Wall Street office — the window commanding nothing but a brick wall ten feet away, the placement of the desks, the portable screen the lawyer stands up so that his clerk is out of his sight yet within reach of his voice?

My real question concerns a third possibility, and I want you to treat it as a proposal to be tested rather than a gift. Call it politics by refusal: an arrangement that stays politically inert while it is used as designed and acquires its meaning only when someone declines the script, so that the scrivener's habitual preference re-legislates the office from within it, the way a court's refusal to apply a statute re-legislates the statute. Does the novella bear this out, given that the lawyer answers the refusal by reconfiguring the world around it — moving his business to another building, abandoning the man to the emptied rooms, and ending at the wall of a prison yard rather than of an office? Tell me where the reading breaks, whether Melville's story ultimately vindicates or punctures Winner's optimism about designed things, and what any of this implies for whether an artifact's politics can live in the refusal to use it at all.
