I work on industrial control systems, and I have no formal training in philosophy, so please be patient with my framing. Last year my team spent roughly eight months reverse-engineering an undocumented device protocol: we captured traffic on the wire, hypothesized what each field meant, ran controlled probes against the device, and retained only those hypotheses whose predictions held up when we sent commands we had never sent before. I have since been reading about Davidson's project of radical interpretation — the interpreter who must work out what a speaker's words mean with no dictionary, no bilingual informant, and nothing but observed behavior plus the principle of charity — and a remark from our team lead during a design review has been nagging at me. His claim was that the two problems are literally the same problem in different clothes: utterances are the ciphertext, the speaker's meaning is the plaintext, the space of candidate interpretations is the keyspace, the interpreter's observations of the shared environment are the crib, and charity is simply a search heuristic — assume the far side is broadly right about the obvious things so that the search converges. On that basis he concluded that the philosophical worry about there being no determinate fact about what someone means is idle. His argument was that when two candidate decoders predicted our device's behavior equally well on everything we could throw at them, we declared them the same decoder and shipped, and nothing in the world distinguished them; so if indeterminacy in interpretation is real at all, it must be that same kind of harmless practical equivalence rather than a deep metaphysical difficulty.

Please audit the analogy rather than summarize Davidson for me. Concretely, I would like:

1. An explicit correspondence table. What plays the role of the ciphertext, the keyspace, the plaintext, the crib, the test harness, and — importantly — the specification document we never possessed but assumed existed? If some slot has no occupant on the Davidsonian side, say so plainly.

2. The disanalogies, sorted into those that actually bear on my colleague's conclusion and those that are merely surface differences. I suspect at least one obvious objection is a red herring — the complaint that people are more complicated than devices — while the damage may be done elsewhere, for example by the fact that we could reset the device, compel it to respond to arbitrary probes, and check our decoding against an environment whose behavior we already understood independently.

3. What the analogy quietly imports from our side of the comparison: that there is a designed artifact with a fixed specification, that the keyspace is finite and the key a self-contained object, that syntax can be pinned down before semantics, and that correctness is adjudicated by a check standing outside both parties. Are there further imports I have not noticed?

4. Whether the conclusion survives. Does the practical interchangeability of two candidate interpretations license the claim that there is no fact of the matter, or only that the difference makes no difference to us? And does the interdependence of belief and meaning — the way charity holds belief constant in order to solve for meaning — have any analogue in what we did, given that we could take the device's purposes as fixed by its engineering function?

5. If the analogy is a poor one, whether a better analogy exists from my own line of work — two processes that have to negotiate a protocol while already exchanging messages under it, perhaps — and what that replacement analogy would license instead.

One further thing I came across and cannot evaluate: Davidson apparently argued later that there is no such thing as a language in the sense of a shared body of learned conventions, motivated partly by malapropisms and by the gap between what he calls a prior theory and a passing theory. If that is right, does it make the code-and-cipher picture worse than my colleague supposed, or does it rescue some part of it?
