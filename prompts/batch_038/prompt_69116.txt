I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm reporting a piece about the way an argument conducted in the fourth century BCE has become a live question in developmental science. I would rather put a number on it than hand my readers the usual shrug.

The argument, briefly. Mencius held that people are born with incipient moral dispositions — the four sprouts, whose chief exhibit is the alarm and distress anyone feels on catching sight of a child about to fall into a well — and that these need only nourishment to mature into full virtue. Against Gaozi, who likened human nature to water whirling in a corner, running east or west according to where the channel is cut, he replied that water may be indifferent to east and west but not to up and down. Xunzi, a generation later, argued the reverse: what is so from birth is a bundle of appetites, and following them unchecked produces conflict and disorder; whatever goodness people manage is the product of deliberate effort — teachers, ritual, models, institutions — and so is achievement rather than nature. Modern scholarship adds a wrinkle I intend to take seriously: the two may not have meant the same thing by nature at all, Mencius using the term for the developmental endpoint an organism is expected to reach under the right conditions, Xunzi for the raw endowment present at birth, with both in fact holding that anyone can become good.

To keep the dispute from dissolving into that wrinkle, I am fixing the terms myself for the piece. Here is the stipulation I want you to work inside:

- **Arbitration.** The word nature means the raw endowment present at birth — Xunzi's stricter sense, which gives him home-field advantage.
- **Reading A (capacity).** Human nature is good if every normal human is born with a capacity that reliably issues in virtue under sufficiently ordinary conditions. Note that both sides seem to accept A, so A does not separate them.
- **Reading B (canalization).** Human nature is good if the acquisition of a working moral sense is channeled the way first-language acquisition is: generic social input suffices, the sequence is stereotyped across societies, and the endpoint converges on a broadly common core of other-regarding norms and motivations — culture supplies the trigger, not the design. The Xunzian rival is that moral competence is more like literacy: a cultural technology installed by instruction and imitation, whose content varies substantially across societies, which can fail to install at all, and which never becomes fully second nature.
- **Reading C (restoration).** Human nature is good if, were all cultural shaping stripped away, what remained would tend toward cooperation rather than predation.

Let **P** be this proposition: under the arbitration above, and taking reading B as the cashing-out of the dispute, the best evidence now available — infant and child studies, comparative primate work, the anthropology of norms and of norm psychology — favors the Mencian picture over the Xunzian one. P is meant to have a truth value: it says the direction of the evidence, not that the evidence is decisive or that anyone should feel settled.

Give me your credence in P as a number, and then decompose it. I want at least four terms, each with a probability and a single line of justification; add any you think belong. The ones I expect to see are: that B is a genuine, non-collapsing question distinct from A and C rather than a redescription of one of them; that the empirical literature is probative of B at all, given what looking-time measures, small samples, and heavily Western subject pools can actually support; that, given probative force, it points toward a channeled moral core rather than an installed technology; that a Mencian finding survives the arbitration, i.e. that early-acquired structure counts as being in nature rather than as the first layer of culture; and that resolving B this way would resolve the historical dispute rather than change the subject. Show your combination explicitly — whether you multiply, weight, or treat some terms as gates that a failure closes outright — and say why that is the right rule here rather than another. Then tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to: which one revision, upward or downward, moves P the farthest, and what specific finding or argument would produce that revision.

For my own calibration: my instinct is 0.6 toward Mencius, but I suspect that number reflects my sympathies more than the evidence. Please do not anchor to it; if your number lands somewhere else, tell me which of my terms you think I am implicitly scoring differently.

Two house rules. First, no citations, no bibliography, and no named studies unless you are confident in the specifics — my fact-checker handles sourcing separately, and I would rather have the shape of the reasoning than a reference list I cannot verify. Second, keep it under about nine hundred words.
