I'm giving a 40 minute talk at a satellite workshop attached to a consciousness conference, and I could really use a Q&A prep sheet beforehand.

The thesis. Heterophenomenology as it's normally laid out treats the subject's verbal reports as a flat, uniform data stream — you collect the text, you posit the world the text describes, you stay neutral on whether any of it is real. I want to argue that this is naive realism about testimony in disguise. My version, which I'm calling weighted heterophenomenology, says every report carries a per-subject, per-domain reliability weight, and that those weights have to be *earned* by deliberately catching the subject in confabulation: choice blindness setups, misattribution of arousal, lateralised presentation so you know which hemisphere got the information, that sort of thing. On my view the heterophenomenological world isn't a single description but a family of models indexed by those weights, and the interesting philosophical questions are about how the family collapses as your weighting gets better.

Two consequences I'm committed to and expect to be attacked for:

(a) A lot of what people report as genuine inter-subjective variation in phenomenology — how rich the visual field is at the periphery, whether there's such a thing as unsymbolised thinking, whether understanding a sentence has its own distinctive feel — is probably an artefact of differing report reliablity rather than differing experience. Some subjects are just better instruments.

(b) If you don't weight, you literally cannot distinguish a bad reporter from someone having an exotic experience, so the first-person datum does no explanatory work at all and the method collapses into psychophysics with extra steps.

The audience. Mostly cognitive neuroscientists who run introspection and awareness experiments and are temperamentally behaviourist — they'll be fine with (b) and horrified by (a) because their whole subfield publishes variation claims. Two experimental psychologists who do experience-sampling work and will object that my reliability weights are estimated in the lab but deployed on reports gathered in the wild, which is a different measurement context. And three philosophers: one card-carrying phenomenal realist who thinks heterophenomenology of any stripe changes the subject and will say so slowly, one who's broadly Dennettian but wants to know why I haven't just reinvented signal detection theory with a story attached, and one postdoc working on self-knowledge who thinks testimony has a special epistemic status that weights can't capture.

So: give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from *this* room, not the ones I'd get from a general philosophy-of-mind seminar. Order them by how much damage they'd do if I fumbled them. For each one, phrase it the way that person would actually phrase it — the neuroscientist will talk about construct validity and the phenomenal realist will talk about the explanandum, and I want to hear both voices — then give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing up, and a blunt note on where that reply is soft or where it's really just a promissory note about work I haven't done. Flag any pairs of objections that turn out to be the same objection wearing different clothes, because if I answer one well I want to bank the other.

Also tell me: what should I concede in the first five minutes so it doesn't get extracted from me at minute thirty-five, which single reply has to land for the talk to be a success rather than a draw, and what's the question I'm going to be praying nobody asks — the one that goes straight at the circularity of estimating report reliability with tasks whose interpretation already assumes something about what the subject was aware of.

British English please. And no reading list or references at the end, I've got plenty — I want the objections and the answers, nothing else.
