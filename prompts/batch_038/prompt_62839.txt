Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of two hours about Bernard Williams's integrity objection, and we did not get anywhere near an agreement. The fault line was this: whether the objection is structural — a complaint about the relation in which utilitarianism requires the agent to stand to his own actions — or whether it is, in the end, axiological, a claim about which of a person's projects are worth protecting and therefore capable of generating a complaint at all. My friend held one view, I held something like the other, and by the end we were each accusing the other of rebuilding utilitarianism inside Williams's vocabulary. I mention this only to explain why the essay I have to mark this morning has me hesitating over the pen. It turns on precisely that fault line. I am deliberately not going to tell you which side of last night's argument I came down on, and I do not want my reading to function as the standard: what I want is your judgement of the answer on its merits.

The question set was:

> "Williams's objection to utilitarianism, on the standard reading, is not that it asks too much of the agent but that it asks the agent to stand in the wrong relation to what he does. Consider the following variant of George's case. George accepts the research post, and years later explains that he took it because the restoration of a derelict chapel — the project on which he had staked his life — would otherwise have failed; the weapons work turned out to produce nothing lethal, and the chapel still stands. Contrast a George who took the same post, in the same circumstances, purely for the pay. Does the first George have a claim on utilitarianism's attention that the second lacks? Explain what would have to be true for the answer to be yes."

The student's answer follows in full. Our module caps these essays at 1,000 words, so concision should not be held against it.

> (1) Williams's complaint is not, in the first instance, that utilitarianism is too demanding. He is willing to concede that any morality worth the name will cost the agent a great deal. His complaint is about what the agent is required to do with himself in deliberating. In *A Critique of Utilitarianism* he argues that utilitarianism is committed to the doctrine of negative responsibility: I am answerable not only for what I bring about but for what I allow, and since a failure to act is itself a course of action with consequences, my responsibility spreads out over the whole of the state of the world. Once responsibility is spread that thinly, the only feature of my act that carries moral weight is the outcome it produces, and the fact that the act was mine falls out of the picture as irrelevant. That is what threatens integrity, because a person's integrity consists in his identification with certain projects — his 'ground projects' — which give him a reason to go on living and which organise everything else he does around them.
>
> (2) George is the test case. He has just taken his doctorate in chemistry, his health is poor, jobs are scarce, and he is offered work in chemical and biological warfare research. The utilitarian calculation tells him that if he refuses, another man with fewer scruples will take the post and pursue it with more enthusiasm, so that his refusal will produce worse consequences than his acceptance. Williams does not dispute the arithmetic. His point is that George cannot perform it without stepping outside the very commitments that make him the person whose life the question is about. A utilitarian can of course try to accommodate this — by treating integrity as a valuable state of mind to be maximised, or by building integrity-protecting rules into a rule-utilitarian scheme — but Williams thinks the accommodation is unstable, because it keeps George's projects alive only on sufferance, as items whose weight the calculus may revise at any time.
>
> (3) The same structural point is made by the one-thought-too-many passage later in *A Critique of Utilitarianism*, in which a man can save only one of two people in equal peril and saves his wife. Williams's remark is that if the man needs the thought that in situations of this kind it is permissible to save one's wife, he has had one thought too many: the right response is not to justify the particular attachment by appeal to a general permission but simply to act from it. The justification, on this view, is not merely unnecessary; requiring it is the mistake.
>
> (4) That shows what is doing the work in the variant. The chapel George and the paycheque George are structurally identical: each acts from something he is deeply attached to, and each is asked by the utilitarian to weigh that attachment impersonally against the consequences of refusing the post. If the objection were purely structural, the two would have exactly the same claim, and the question would be a trick. But it is not purely structural. The drowning case tells us why: the wife counts because she is his wife, and no impartial reasoning can add anything to that — but if the man on the shore were choosing his wife over the stranger simply because he happened to fancy her better, the thought would not be one too many; it would be the only thought there was, and it would be a bad one. Attachment confers a claim only where it is attachment to something that warrants it. The chapel is such a thing. The paycheque is not; it is a preference like any other, and there is nothing alienating about asking a man to weigh a preference against the destruction he will otherwise cause.
>
> (5) So the answer is yes, the first George has a claim the second lacks, and what would have to be true for that is that integrity is normative all the way down: a project generates a complaint against utilitarianism only if it is a project worth having. This also disposes of the charge that Williams's view is self-indulgent, since on his account only worthwhile projects produce the objection in the first place. Two further points. The variant stipulates that George feels no regret; that cannot matter, because Williams's objection concerns the structure of his deliberation at the time of the decision and not his retrospective attitude to it. And the fact that the weapons work produced nothing lethal is likewise beside the point, since the objection is about how George must think, not about how the world turned out.
