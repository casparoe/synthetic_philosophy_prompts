So I co-organize a little interdisciplinary workshop each spring with the econ department — this year's theme is the metaphysics of money, about 40 people, half philosophers, half economists who have never voluntarily read a book published before 1900 — and I've been talked into giving the two-minute welcomming remarks before the keynote. I want to open with a joke, and specifically a joke about Berkeley's economics rather than Berkeley's idealism, because everyone and their dog has already made the "so your balance only exists while the bank is perceiving it" joke and I refuse to be that guy. Please treat this as a real craft problem rather than a fill-in-the-blank nerd gag: you're a professional philosopher who also writes stand-up, someone who could do a tight five on early modern monetary theory and get laughs from a room of people who don't know what a scholastic is. Think carefully and show me the work.

Some raw material in case it's been a while. The Querist is that strange book made up of nothing but numbered questions, which he put out shortly after being made Bishop of Cloyne, and in it he pushes the line that money isn't wealth at all but a kind of ticket or counter whose entire job is to set people to work; that the real wealth of a country is the industry and labour of its people; that paper issued by a national bank beats gold and silver sleeping in a vault; and that what makes the tickets move anything is something closer to imagination and confidence than to metal. Biographical context that I think is comedy gold: he sailed to America to found a college in Bermuda, sat in Rhode Island for years waiting on promised money from Parliament that never showed up, came home, and then wrote hundreds of questions about how a country gets its money to circulate. If any of that is off, say so instead of building a punchline on it — I dont want to be corrected from the back of the room by a historian.

Here's what I want from you. First, brainstorm: four or five candidate angles, one line each, then tell me which one you'd bet on and why in a sentence or two. Then write the actual bit — stand-up register, but delivered aloud by a non-comedian in a hotel conference room, so it has to survive being read flat; hard cap around 180 words; one real punchline and at most one tag. The setup should smuggle in enough of Berkeley's actual view that people who have never heard of the Querist still get the joke and come away having learned something true, so nothing can depend on prior knowledge — but it also has to be specific enough that a genuine Berkeley scholar laughs at the accuracy rather than at me. No "walks into a bar," no tree falling in the forest, no tar-water, and no crypto gag unless you can make it genuinely surprising instead of the obvious Berkeley-invented-bitcoin move; frequent flyer miles, casino chips, gift cards, a bank teller, or the Rhode Island fiasco are all fair game as vehicles. After the bit, give me two alternative punchlines on the same setup in case the first one dies, and finish with a short, honest note flagging every line that stretches or misstates his view and exactly where an expert in the room would wince. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I have those — I just need it right and funny.
