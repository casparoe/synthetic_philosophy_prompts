You are a philosopher who works on bioethicism and personal identity and has thought hard about whether advance directives retain authority after severe dementia. Before giving a verdict, do the entailment work first: state briefly what each commitment below commits me to regarding the others, and only then deliver the audit.

I am drafting a position paper and want a consistency check before I circulate it. My package:

(1) A directive made by a competent, well-informed adult about her own future care should be honored even when she no longer remembers making it and can no longer grasp the decisions it governs.

(2) The ground is not that honoring it serves her present self, but that the shape of a whole life matters: her critical interests — the evaluative commitments that made her life hers — persist through dementia even when her experiential interests shift.

(3) What prudentially matters, though, is psychological connectedness rather than identity. In advanced dementia the connectedness between the earlier self and the patient is weak, and I am willing to say the patient is, in the sense that matters, largely a different subject.

(4) Present contentment is not a lesser good merely because it fails to cohere with a narrative; I reject discounting the demented patient's daily welfare on the grounds that she cannot appreciate its place in her life.

(5) I also endorse what I would call a self-abdication clause: language in the directive instructing clinicians to disregard it if the patient turns out to be consistently and evidently contented. I think people should be encouraged to write such clauses.

(6) A later revocation made while the patient still has decision-making capacity beats the original document, but the distressed protests of a patient who has lost capacity do not.

(7) Honoring a directive is not using the demented patient as a means to the earlier self's ends; the document is her own authorization.

Here is the case I need my package to handle. A woman with early dementia, still decisionally intact but flat and apathetic, writes that her old directive is withdrawn; asked what she wants, she says only that she does much care either way. Two years later she is incapable, sings most afternoons, greets visitors with pleasure, and develops pneumonia. Tell me what my package actually says should happen — including whether the self-abdication clause died with the document it was written into — and whether my principles can deliver that answer rather than my preferred one.

Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and which conflict is worst? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each genuine one say what has to give and what the cheapest revision costs. Please do not reassure me that the set is broadly coherent, and do not flag every surface friction as a real one.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, if I pressed it, would make you reverse one of your severity rankings or reclassify a tension you called genuine as merely apparent, and where in this audit you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
