My roommate's a phil major and he will not shut up about how Scanlon's contractualism "solves the aggregation problem" — that's his whole sales pitch for why its better than utilitarianism, since you only ever compare complaints one person at a time instead of adding up everybody's wellbeing. I sit on the ethics committee at a regional hospital and we've got a budget choice: fund a program that saves roughly two lives a year, or one that gets rid of chronic moderate pain for about 900 patients a year. He says the contractualist verdict is a gimme — numbers can't count, nine hundred moderate complaints don't add up to outweigh a single death, so obviously fund the lifesaving one. But I'm pretty sure I've read that Scanlon himself thinks you should save the five rather than the one when the harms are the same kind of thing, which looks exactly like counting numbers. So is he just smuggling aggregation back in through the side door and giving up the one advantage over utilitarianism he was selling, or is the five-vs-one case special in a way that doesn't apply to us?
