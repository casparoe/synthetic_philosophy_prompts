Some background first, because it explains why I am troubling with a question I last thought about seriously as an undergraduate, and why I would like an answer that is careful rather than consoling.

My wife and I have just had twin daughters. They are monozygotic: a single blastocyst was transferred on day five and then split, and the scans showed one placenta and two amniotic sacs, so the splitting must have happened after the blastocyst had already formed. Both children were conceived by intracytoplasmic sperm injection, which means that an embryologist looked into a dish containing several million sperm, chose one that seemed to be moving well, and injected it into one chosen egg. Two further blastocysts from that cycle remain frozen, and next week we are due to tell the clinic what we want done with them. I have found that I cannot stop thinking about how nearly every particular of my daughters' existence traces back to two laboratory decisions and one piece of chance: which egg, which sperm, and then the accident of splitting.

I would like you to audit a set of metaphysical commitments I have been accumulating, half-formed, over the past few months. I want to know whether they can all be held at once, and where they cannot. I am not asking for an essay on the ethics of embryo disposal, and I am not asking to be reassured; I am asking for an inspection of the structure. Here they are, as precisely as I can manage:

(a) I accept origin essentialism of roughly Kripke's kind: no person could have come from a different egg and a different sperm. Everything downstream of that is contingent; the origin is not. What convinces me is armchair reasoning of the familiar sort — the table that could not have been made from a different hunk of wood, together with the thought that if origins were not essential, two worlds could be alike in every qualitative respect and yet differ in which individual is which, and I do not know what that would amount to.

(b) It follows, and I believe it independently, that my daughters could have been born on another continent, been adopted at birth, been given other names, and lived wholly different lives, and would still have been the very same two people. Several friends have told me that this flatly contradicts (a). I do not think it does.

(c) I believe that the embryologist's selection of that one sperm settled which two people my daughters are. I also believe that there was nothing whatever about that sperm — nothing he could have detected, nothing qualitative about it at all — that made it the right one, and that had he taken the one lying beside it, neither of my daughters would have existed.

(d) At the same time I believe that a child arising from a different embryo, or from that neighbouring sperm, would still have been our child in the only sense of "same" that I care about, and I do not regard that as a softening of (c) or a figure of speech.

(e) About identity over time I am broadly Parfitian: personal identity consists in psychological continuity and connectedness with the right kind of cause; identity is not what matters; what matters is the psychological relation, whether or not it holds one-to-one.

(f) In fission cases I am content to say that identity is indeterminate, rather than to choose a branch or to double the number of people.

(g) Because a single transferred embryo of ours split into two children, I have come to think that the early embryo is not yet one determinate individual: until twinning has been ruled out, there is not yet a single someone. This is why I am at peace with the disposal of the two remaining blastocysts, and why I told the clinic's counsellor that no person had been destroyed by it.

(h) I accept the converse case too. If two early embryos fuse — as I understand happens, yielding one chimeric person with two cell lines — then the number of people is not fixed by the number of zygotes. I take this to reinforce (g).

(i) Finally, I believe that had our embryo not split, exactly one child would have been born, and that this child would have been one of my daughters, though I could not say which. I find my inability to say which entirely comfortable.

Please go through these and tell me which of them genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision would cost me — both in the metaphysics and, where it bears on it, in how I should think about next week's decision. If one single revision would clean up more than the rest, say which and why. Be explicit as well about the tensions that dissolve on inspection, since I would rather know that (b) is safe than be told about it twice. And if any of my formulations are simply unclear or misstated in a way that makes the question ill-posed, say so and tell me what you take me to have meant.
