The two camps, roughly:

Any statement like "A gains more than B loses" is either meaningless or an ethical judgment wearing a lab coat, and he thinks the compensation-test stuff in cost-benefit analysis proves it — the moment you have to actually decide whether to compensate, you're doing ethics, not measurement.

They agreed not to fight in the abstract, so the document has to be built around one concrete case, which is a lab protocol a postdoc here has been running. All the details you need are below — it's made up / unpublished, so don't try to recall anything about it, just reason from what I've written:

Call it the "Meridian" matching task. Each subject first fixes a personal anchor by recalling a specific bad experience of their own (the instructions use a bad toothache) and rating it. Then they do two kinds of task. (1) Vignette ratings: given descriptions of outcomes — a month of chronic back pain, a year without a car, a week of nausea — they report each one in units of the anchor. (2) Duration trades: how much time in outcome X they'd accept to avoid one unit of the anchor experience, run at several magnitudes. Findings so far, with 240 subjects:

- Within subjects, the duration trades are internally consistent in the right way — the implied trade ratios are stable enough that each person's scale is determined up to a positive affine transformation, i.e. up to their own zero and their own multiplier.
- Across subjects, anchor-relative ratings correlate about 0.8 with ratings from a totally different anchoring task (matching the unpleasantness of outcomes to the loudness of a tone). She says that's evidence the common scale isn't arbitrary. He says a 0.8 correlation between two self-reports collected from the same person in the same session is evidence about response style, not about a shared quantity.
- In one arm, subjects were shown the average anchor rating of previous subjects before answering. Their own anchor moved by a factor of about 1.4 on average, and the *shape* of their ratings barely changed — everything just got rescaled. His favorite result, obviously.
- A small subgroup (11 people) reports near-total flat affect on standard scales. Under the protocol they come out with welfare changes that are almost zero for everything, including outcomes everyone else rates as catastrophic. The protocol has no independent way to say whether they really are barely affected or whether their anchor is just scaled differently. Nobody on either side likes this result.

And the payoff case: a clinic has 100 courses of a therapy. Option one gives them to 100 people with a severe chronic condition, each getting a large anchor-relative improvement. Option two gives them to 2,000 people (over successive rounds) with a mild but constant condition, each getting a small anchor-relative improvement. Run through her aggregation, option two wins by a wide margin. Run through his framework, the question can't be asked at all, and the clinic should decide on something else entirely.
