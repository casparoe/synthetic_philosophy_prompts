Two years ago I sat through a resilience workshop my employer arranged, and the facilitator opened the day with a line that has been going around for decades: between what happens to you and what you do about it, she said, there is a space, and in that space sits your freedom to choose. It is usually forwarded with Viktor Frankl's name attached to it, though I have since read that the attribution is unsettled and that nobody has managed to pin it to anything he actually published. I did not object at the time; it sounded right, and it was clearly doing a lot of work in the room. She used it three separate ways that day: to say that we are not like conditioned animals who merely react, to say that no circumstance can take away your ability to choose your response, and, more quietly, to wave off any talk of determinism or of people being shaped by their upbringing. Later in the afternoon the same sentence was used on a man who said he had been unable to function after his divorce, and it was used to suggest that this was a choice he had been making.

Around the same period I read about the digger wasp that stings a caterpillar, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to check that everything is in order, and then comes back out and hauls it in. The experiment I read about says that if you move the caterpillar a few inches while the wasp is underground, the wasp comes out, finds it, drags it back to the threshold, and goes inside to check again, and will repeat this indefinitely. That story unsettled something in the facilitator's sentence for me, because the wasp plainly does not go straight from stimulus to response either. There is an interval. Something happens in between. It goes and looks.

So I would like the sentence taken apart. Please give me the different precise claims it could be making, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, and for each one say explicitly what is supposed to count as the stimulus and what as the response; what the "space" is on that reading (an actual pause in time, an internal rehearsal of options, a veto over an impulse already underway, a capacity to notice and reconsider, the mere absence of outside coercion, or simply the fact that an observer cannot predict what will happen next); and what "freedom" is being claimed (not being caused at all, being able to have done otherwise, being guided by reasons rather than by triggers, being self-governed, or being unpredictable). I also want a verdict on each reading: is it a platitude that nobody would deny, a substantive claim that is probably true, a substantive claim that is doubtful, or simply false — with a sentence or two of reasoning, not a label alone.

Then tell me, for each reading, whether it would actually deliver what the workshop needed: (a) that human beings are relevantly unlike the wasp, (b) that circumstances cannot take away your ability to choose your response, and (c) that determinism is therefore not a threat to that ability. I am especially interested in (a), and in whether any reading earns it. It seems to me that if all the sentence says is that something occurs between trigger and behaviour, then the wasp has that too, and the difference becomes one of how much goes on in the interval and how easily it breaks down when the situation is nudged — which is a much less comforting claim than the one being sold. I would rather be told that plainly than be handed the flattering version. Please also say which readings, if any, would license the use the sentence was put to at the end of the day, against someone describing himself as unable to function.

I would like the readings set out as a table with exactly these columns: Reading (a short name); Claim (the slogan restated in your own words so that it is something that could be true or false); What the "space" is; What "freedom" is being claimed; Status (one of: platitude, substantive and plausible, substantive and doubtful, false); Why (one or two sentences); Delivers (list which of (a), (b), (c) it delivers, if any). Aim for at least six readings, ordered from the most deflationary to the most ambitious. After the table, add three short paragraphs: the first naming the single reading you think most people who repeat the line actually intend and whether it is one that survives scrutiny; the second saying what the wasp case does and does not show about the sentence; the third telling me, if you think there is a nearby claim that is both true and worth saying, how you would put it in one line.
