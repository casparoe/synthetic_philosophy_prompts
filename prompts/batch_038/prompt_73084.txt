I write about science and ideas for a national magazine, and I have spent the past year in the comparative-history literature on why industrialization took off in Britain rather than in China's most advanced regions — the puzzle usually traced to Joseph Needham. Reporting on it, I have come across the same maneuver often enough that I assume it must have a name, but I have not found one, and I would rather not publish a coinage of my own if specialists already have a term for it.

The maneuver has nothing to do with which causes are proposed. It has to do with the comparison itself being adjustable. Three versions I have watched play out in print:

1. Space. An author identifies a British advantage — coal within reach of navigable water, wages high relative to the price of energy, a map broken into competing states — and a reviewer shows that the Yangzi Delta had something comparable. The reply does not drop the cause; it re-draws the region the cause belongs to. Europe becomes Britain, Britain becomes the north of England, or China becomes an imperial aggregate from which Jiangnan is subtracted as a special case. The comparison survives at some scale or other, and because there are many scales available, a sympathetic author can usually find the one where it survives.

2. Outcome. The question opens as a comparison of positions at a date — how prosperous, how mechanized — and when the positions turn out to be close, it becomes a comparison of slopes, sustained growth rather than levels; and when the slopes also look similar across a generation, it becomes a comparison of how long the slope was held.

3. Onset. When dating the divergence to 1800 looks arbitrary in light of the evidence, the same thesis is restated with the divergence beginning in the fifteenth century, or in the Song, or only after 1850. The causal story is unchanged; it is now measured against a different interval, and the interval was chosen after the objection was raised.

Each of these is defended, case by case, with better data than the version it replaced, and I do not think the authors are acting in bad faith. What troubles me is the aggregate effect: the thesis about why Britain and not China never actually loses, because losing at one specification licenses a re-specification, and every re-specification is locally reasonable.

So does this have a name? I am asking specifically about the pattern in which an explanation of the form why A and not B preserves itself by re-describing the terms of that comparison, not about bad argument in general. I do not mean simple goalpost moving — no evidential bar is being raised against the author, and the moves are usually improvements. Nor do I mean the ordinary way a scientist patches a theory after a failed prediction, since there the patch is at least supposed to be independently checkable.

What I want is the carving, not a label. Which established concepts come closest, from either the historical-method side or the philosophy-of-science side; how the thing I have described differs from each; which of them, if any, genuinely covers it; and whether comparative economic history has a term of art for it. Then the harder part: is there a defensible stopping rule? Some criterion a referee or an editor could apply to tell a genuine refinement — a re-drawn unit that pays for itself in new evidence and new predictions — from a rescue, where the unit is redrawn after the fact and has only to absorb an objection. If the pattern is real but unnamed, propose a term, define it, and give me a case that looks like it but is not. Finally, tell me whether the recurrence of this maneuver should make me doubt that the question why Britain and not China is well posed at all, or whether the question can be repaired by fixing the comparison in advance of the evidence. Please do not send me a reading list; I want the concepts and the distinctions, and I am content to track down the sources myself.
