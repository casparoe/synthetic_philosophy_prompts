Second-year value theory seminar, 90 minutes, about 25 of them, and week four is well-being. They've had Bentham and Mill on qualitative hedonism and they've all seen The Matrix, so half will walk in convinced the question is settled — reality is obviously better than the simulation, end of discussion — and the other half will think Nozick just proved feelings don't matter. I don't want to hand them the machine as a refutation of hedonism and move on to the next theory; I want them leaving with the sense that its a probe into what a theory of well-being is even supposed to be tracking.

So plan the session for me rather than explaining the material to me. Which moves go in which order and what depends on what; which variant of the machine should carry the weight — the original plug-in offer, the one where you wake up mid-program and are told you've already been in it for forty years, or the one where you're offered the machine for your kid instead of yourself — pick one and defend the pick. Tell me where to drop in the fact that the reversal version produces wildly different answers depending on what students are told their real life outside the machine is like, since that's the thing that stops them treating their own squeamishness as evidence.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually turn up: that desire-satisfaction and hedonism get hit by the same argument because satisfying a desire just means giving you the experience you wanted; that authenticity names a value all by itself; that Nozick's three reasons are three seperate arguments when they might be one wearing a hat. Where in the sequence I head off each one, one closing question that distinguishes the students who got it from the ones who just agreed with me, and what I should cut — I'm guessing objective list theories and the hybrid views — plus how to say out loud that I'm cutting it.
