So I'm a third-year philosophy grad student, nominally working on formal epistemology, but I've spent the last year half-distracted helping moderate a forecasting Discord where a bunch of us answer public question sets for fun. That's how I fell down the rabbit hole on the IARPA forecasting tournament, and now I have this itch I can't scratch. The facts as I understand them: roughly 2011 to 2015, several academic teams recruited online volunteers to give probabilistic answers to hundreds of time-indexed geopolitical questions, scored with Brier scores. Tetlock and Mellers' team won. Their volunteers were unpaid amateurs, and their winning recipe was to aggregate the crowd's numbers and then extremize the aggregate -- push it away from 50/50 -- which beat the plain average of the same crowd. The team also beat, by something like 25-30 percent, a prediction market run inside the intelligence community and staffed by professional analysts with access to classified information. And a one-hour training module in probabilistic reasoning measurably improved volunteers' accuracy for something like a year afterward.

What I want is that episode analyzed through the lens of accuracy-first epistemology / epistemic utility theory, with the mapping done properly before anyone hands down a verdict. Start by saying who the players actually are and what each one's option space looked like -- the individual volunteer choosing what number to report, the aggregator choosing a pooling rule, the tournament designers choosing which questions to ask and which scoring rule to grade with, the intelligence agency choosing what to do with the output -- because most of the confusion in my Discord comes from treating those four as a single agent. Then say what the theory tells each of them to do: proper scoring rules say report your honest credence, the dominance arguments say be coherent and update by conditionalization, and immodesty says your own credences are, by your own lights, more accurate than anything you could switch to for free. Then tell me where the episode resists that description. Three things bother me. (1) Extremizing helped, which looks like evidence the crowd was systematically underconfident -- but a proper scoring rule is supposed to be precisely the device that makes underconfidence self-defeating, so what does it mean that an outside algorithm had to correct a bias the incentives couldn't see? (2) Almost all the gains came from pooling other people's numbers, and every theorem I know in this area is an internal dominance result about one agent's credence function -- I don't think the theory has much to say about social aggregation beyond naive pooling arguments that immodesty then undercuts. (3) The question set was chosen by an intelligence agency for resolvability and decision-relevance, and the volunteers were explicitly coached to triage, i.e. skip the questions where they couldn't beat a dart-throwing chimp. So accuracy was measured on a hand-picked subalgebra with middling base rates, not on anybody's total belief state. Is that a fair test of veritism at all, or a kind of epistemic gerrymandering that flatters the theory?

And here's my half-formed hunch, tell me if it's dumb. The reason a crowd average beats the average member under a quadratic score is a variance decomposition: the error of the mean equals the mean of the individual errors minus the spread of the forecasts. And the reason the dominance arguments for probabilism work is a Pythagorean/projection fact about the same Euclidean geometry -- incoherent credence functions sit off a convex set and get beaten by their projections onto it. So the crowd effect and the coherence theorem are cousins, which is definately the part I find interesting. Does that make the tournament a real empirical vindication of accuracy-first epistemology, the same geometry showing up in the lab? Or is its an artifact of everyone using Brier -- would the crowd advantage and the dominance results both change shape under a logarithmic score, and does that show the theory is hostage to the scoring rule it picked? Then run it the other direction: which features of the episode the theory explains well, which it misdescribes, and which it is simply silent about -- say where it's silent rather than making it speak, and if you think some other framework (a pragmatic account of what credences are, a calibration-first view, whatever) explains the episode better, argue for that. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I know the literature and I'll fill that in myself; I want the analysis. Oh, and I already know extremizing is disputed as a practice -- I'm not asking whether it worked, I'm asking what it means about the theory that it did.
