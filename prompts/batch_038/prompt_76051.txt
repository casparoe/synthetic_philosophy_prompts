I teach a masters-level philosophy of mathematics seminar — 16 students, we meet once a week for 90 minutes, and by the session in question they'll have had exactly one week of intuitionistic natural deduction. They can do the → and ⊥ rules, they've seen on the board that ¬¬P doesn't get you to P, and nobody has touched Kripke models or Beth semantics yet. Half the room are philosophy people; the other half are CS people who keep saying some version of "isn't this just computability theory with extra steps," and I want to break that habit by making them run into the logic instead of hearing me describe it. So: design me an in-class game that lands three things at once — (a) that asserting an existence claim or a disjunction is a speech act that costs you something, because you can be called on to hand over a witness or commit to a disjunct on the spot; (b) that ¬A is not "I don't know A" but a positive general claim that no further information could ever settle A in the affirmative, which is much harder to make good on than it looks; and (c) that the price of excluded middle is a kind of omniscience — if you assert P ∨ ¬P for some arbitrary infinite object you've committed to being able to decide it, and that's a bet you should sometimes decline. None of those should be named during play. They should hit the walls themselves and we name the walls afterwards.

Some mechanics I already like, change them if you have better ones: information gets revealed in rounds and whatever a team asserts becomes locked in permanently — they may learn more later but they can never retract, so they have to be extremely careful what they say now; cashing out a claim triggers an "audit" where a skeptic team has to be able to execute what the claimants wrote, by hand, on an index card, no laptops, and a failed audit costs more then a successful one pays; and there should be at least one round built around a predicate that's trivial to state but hopeless to decide in the room — something in the spirit of Brouwer's weak counterexamples, an open or practically undecidable condition on the naturals — where the tempting move is to assert excluded middle for cheap points and the right move is to refuse. Give me the full rules, a point scheme that runs on tokens or a whiteboard scoreboard rather than money, team sizes, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes including how much of it the debrief needs, and the facilitator script for mapping each game wall onto the formal feature behind it. I especially want that mapping worked out for the lock-in rule, because my hunch is that it's secretly persistence in a Kripke model and that the audit is secretly the BHK clauses, but I'm not confident I could defend either claim if a student pushes.

Then the honest parts. What predictably goes wrong the first time this runs — my guesses are that the CS students will game the audit by writing something technically executable but comically slow, that the philosophy students will freeze and assert nothing at all, and that the token economy will collapse once teams start hoarding — and what I do about each, including a 45-minute fallback if I blow the schedule. And most importantly: what feature of intuitionism this game cannot reproduce, and what I have to supply by hand so nobody walks out thinking intuitionism is just classical logic minus guessing. I'm genuinely worried the whole thing teaches them epistemicism about logic, since every object in the game is a finite human procedure and so "there is no construction" collapses into "you couldn't find it in 90 minutes." If there's a round that keeps those two apart — that makes them feel a refusal to assert P ∨ ¬P as something other than ignorance — build it in and tell me what it's doing. If there isn't, say so plainly and give me the ten-minute talk that patches the gap.
