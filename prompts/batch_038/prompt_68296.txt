I cover science and ideas for a weekly, and I've got a piece stuck at the stage where I can feel the shape of a problem but can't get my hands on it. Hoping you can help me find the seam.

There's a report going round — embargoed, and the name doesn't matter for what I need — arguing that rich countries should stop spending on emissions abatement and put the money into growth and adaptation instead. The economist who wrote it walked me through the reasoning over the phone, and it's been rattling around in my head ever since, partly because I can't find the flaw, and partly because I can see its also doing damage somewhere I'm fairly sure he doesn't want it to.

Here's the argument, tidied into steps by me. He'd probably object to some of the tidying, which is itself part of what I want checked.

1. An act wrongs someone only if it makes that person worse off than they would otherwise have been.
2. Emissions and growth policy over the next two centuries will shift the detailed course of history — who meets whom, when people are conceived, which people come to exist.
3. So for each particular person alive in, say, 2200, the high-emissions pathway is among the conditions of their existing at all. On the low-emissions pathway a different set of people exists.
4. Life for those people, even on the hotter pathway, is worth living. His own numbers say so and the report concedes the point.
5. The alternative for each of them is never existing. Never existing isn't better for them.
6. So none of them is made worse off by the high-emissions pathway.
7. Therefore that pathway wrongs no one in 2200 — and the same goes for any policy whose consequences land more than a couple of generations out.
8. "Future generations" as such aren't the sort of thing that can be wronged. Only people can be wronged, and there's no one there to be wronged in the relevant sense.
9. So justice puts no constraint on how we trade off against the remote future. Climate policy has to be argued on other grounds: prudence, insurance, taste, the look of the thing.

Two things I can't live with.

First, run 1 through 6 backwards. My grandmother's choices, and her ancestors' before her, are conditions of my existing. Whatever they did — burned the coal, exhausted the soil, took land off people — if I'm here and my life is worth living, then by 5 and 6 nothing they did made me worse off. That seems to indemnify the entire past against complaint. And I've read this move pressed into service against reparations for slavery and dispossession: the descendants alive today wouldn't exist without the injustice, so they aren't worse off, so no wrong was done to them. If his argument is sound, that one is sound too. When I put this to him he said his was about climate and mine was about history, but he couldn't tell me which step does the separating, and I don't think there is one.

Second, the barrel case, which is my own. Suppose I bury a canister of something that stays lethal for ten thousand years, and it will definately breach in 2400 and kill or maim a few hundred people. Same structure: burying it shifts history, so those people wouldn't otherwise exist; their lives are worth living; so by 5 and 6 I've wronged nobody. I cannot accept that, and I've started calling it the barrel test, because it seems to me that any argument in this family either passes it or explains it away, and I've never seen one manage either.

If I have to choose, I'd rather abandon 9 than abandon the barrel intuition. But I'd also rather not jettison 1 wholesale, because most of the ordinary harm talk I write about for a living leans on something like it.

So, what I want from you.

You're a professional philosopher — population ethics and climate justice, and ideally someone who has had to explain this sort of thing to non-specialists, because that's basically my job. Think carefully. Before you commit to a diagnosis, brainstorm: list every place in 1–9 where something might have slipped, including ones I haven't thought of, and only then tell me which of them is actually load-bearing.

Then work through:

- Which step or steps you reject, and whether the trouble is that the argument doesn't hold together (an equivocation, a baseline that quietly changes sense between 1 and 5, a scope or modal slide) or that it holds together fine and one of the premises is just false. Tell me which of the two it is, because the repairs are completely different and I need to describe them accurately.
- Why the bad step looked fine when he said it out loud. I need this for the piece more than anything else, because my readers will feel the same pull I did and I have to be able to tell them what the pull is made of.
- What each repair costs. If I keep 1, what do I have to swallow elsewhere — and does the barrel case push me toward a non-comparative notion of wronging, a "worse off than they could have been" reading, something about harm to whichever people end up existing, or something else again? If I give up 1, what happens to the rest of ordinary moral thinking that leans on it? And be fair to him: is there a version where he keeps most of his conclusion?
- Whether the backwards run and the barrel case fail for the same reason. My hunch is that they don't, and that the difference matters, but I can't articulate it.
- Whether 2 is doing more work than it looks like. It reads to me like a contingent claim about how reproduction actually happens to work, and if it's contingent then 9 is contingent with it, which seems a flimsy thing to hang a policy conclusion on. Tell me whether that's a real objection or whether I've hold of the wrong end of it — and sketch what the argument looks like in a world where 2 is false, say one where future people are chosen from a fixed pool of embryos, or where population policy is deliberately set up not to change who exists.
- Whether 8 is a separate mistake rather than a restatement of 7.

One request: don't just tell me this is the non-identity problem. I know it lives in that neighbourhood, I've read enough around it to be tired of the name, and naming it isn't the deliverable — the mechanics are.

No reading list, no citations, no names-and-dates, no page numbers. You don't have the web and I don't want you guessing at references; I want the reasoning in your own words. British English please, that's house style. Gloss any unavoidable technical term in a single line, since neither I nor my editor is a philosopher. Aim for something between six hundred and a thousand words, with headers for the rejected step, the diagnosis, the pricing, and the two test cases, so I can work from it directly. And if you think he's right and I'm the confused one, say so plainly and show me where.
