I've spent twenty-two years drafting the instruments that let one person's earlier self reach forward and grab the wheel: revocable trusts that become irrevocable on a stated trigger, springing durable powers of attorney, health care proxies, dementia-specific directives refusing artificial nutrition, spendthrift provisions whose entire purpose is to protect a beneficiary from his own predictable future weakness. I also read the rationalist blogs and their descendants fairly steadily, and over the last year I've been working through the philosophical literature on advance directives — Dworkin's defense of precedent autonomy, with his distinction between experiential interests and critical interests; Dresser's reply that the demented patient in front of us has interests of her own that no longer speak in the voice of the person who signed the form; and the identity-flavored argument, which I first met in Parfit-adjacent form, that if psychological continuity is what makes a later stage the same *person*, then a directive executed by the earlier self may simply not apply to anyone now. I want to get clear on this dispute, and I want to do it in the only vocabulary I actually command. So here is my request. Please take on the role of a philosopher who works at the intersection of bioethics and personal identity and who is genuinely comfortable with fiduciary doctrine — not someone translating law into philosophy, but someone translating philosophy into law and reporting honestly on the losses.

The task: restate the Dworkin/Dresser disagreement, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary of trusts and estates and guardianship practice. Use my own terms — the settlor's intent, dead-hand control, revocable versus irrevocable, the capacity standard as functional and decision-specific rather than global, reformation and deviation, the substituted-judgment standard versus the best-interests standard, the court's parens patriae role, the no-contest clause, the spendthrift trust as a device for binding a future self. Map each substantive philosophical claim onto whatever legal notion carries it: the claim that a competent earlier self can authorize treatment refusal that binds a later, non-competent self; the claim that critical interests can outweigh experiential ones even when only the latter remain; the claim that honoring the directive is not a harm to the patient now; Dresser's claim that the person in the room has a welfare that the directive does not speak for; and the personal-identity objection, for which I suspect the closest analogue is the law's fiat that one legal person persists across radical psychological discontinuity, as with the ward who no longer remembers executing anything. Before you write the translation proper, spend a short paragraph listing the three or four mapping pairs you considered and rejected, and why — I want to see that you chose rather than defaulted to the obvious pairing.

Two constraints matter to me more than completeness. First, do not let the doctrine settle the question. If "the settlor's intent governs" smuggles in the premise that there is a continuing subject whose interests are served by being obeyed, say so explicitly rather than presenting it as a translation; likewise for the assumption that a directive is a conveyance of authority over *property* rather than over a living body, and for the fact that my field has already split the baby — the dead hand rules the estate, while guardianship law makes the ward's present best interests controlling and treats the earlier wishes as at most evidence under substituted judgment. Where the legal framework quietly resolves a philosophical question by definitional fiat, flag it as smuggling, not as translation. Second, give me the residue report, and treat it as the main deliverable rather than an appendix: sort the claims into those that translate cleanly, those that translate only with distortion you can name, and those with no expression at all in fiduciary vocabulary. For each item in the last two buckets, tell me which of two diagnoses you favor — that my framework is missing something it could in principle acquire (a concept of a person's good that is not reducible to either her preferences or her property), or that the philosophers' claim was less clear than it looked, and the residue is what is left when a distinction that was doing rhetorical work is denied a legal cash value. Finally, and this is the question I actually brought from practice: on the translation you have produced, are Dworkin and Dresser disagreeing about who decides, or about whose welfare counts? If your rendering makes those two questions come apart, say which one each of them is answering, and whether the client in my conference room — directive signed twelve years ago, now content, recognizing her grandchildren, unable to say what she would want — is being well served by a legal system that answers the first question and has no vocabulary for the second. No citations, no bibliography, and please do not invent case names or statutes; I want the reasoning, not the references.
