I convene a small reading group in the philosophy of the human sciences, and this term's topic has been the Darwinian theory of culture. Going into our second meeting I intended to put the standard falsificationist objection to memetics on a rigorous footing, so that we could stop trading intuitions about whether it is a science or merely a manner of speaking. I wrote out the following argument and expected it to settle the matter in ten minutes. It occupied the rest of the evening, because the thing proved considerably more than I wanted it to.

(1) A hypothesis belongs to science only if there is some possible observation about its subject matter that would count against it.
(2) Memetics holds that cultural items — tunes, doctrines, fashions, techniques, slogans, ritual requirements — vary, are transmitted from person to person, and differ in how effectively they get transmitted, so that the items prevalent at a given time are those that have out-replicated their rivals.
(3) For any such item, and for any pattern whatever in its prevalence — sudden spread, generations of stability, regional patchiness, decline, replacement by a competitor — that framework supplies an account: what spread was better at spreading than what was on offer, what held steady did so because transmission offset attrition, what vanished lost the contest.
(4) Hence no possible observation about the prevalence of cultural items is incompatible with memetics, and so, by (1), memetics is not science.
(5) Nothing in that reasoning depends on the cultural case. Given any population whose variants differ, reproduce differentially, and pass their differences on, the same after-the-fact account is available: the variants that increased were the fitter ones, those that decreased were less fit, those that held steady sat at an equilibrium.
(6) Hence, by the same route, the theory of natural selection is not science either.
(7) But (6) cannot be accepted. Selection theory issues quantitative predictions from parameters estimated independently of the outcome it predicts — that the response to artificial selection should equal the selection differential multiplied by the heritability, for instance — and breeding programmes fail, visibly and expensively, when it does not.
(8) Therefore either (1) is false, or the move from (3) to (4) is invalid: a theory's capacity to accommodate every possible outcome does not by itself cost it scientific standing.
(9) And therefore the objection at (4) fails, and memetic explanations of cultural change stand on exactly the same footing as selectionist explanations of biological change.

I cannot accept (9), and neither could three of the six people in the room, but none of us managed to say why without begging the question. Two replies were offered. The first was that genes are particulate and cultural items are not — items blend, are reconstructed by each learner rather than copied, and two people's "same" belief may share no identifiable structure — but this seems to answer a question (5) never asks, since (5) is about what a selectionist framework can accommodate once the outcome is known, not about the fidelity with which its units are transmitted. The second was that memetics has produced nothing anyone would risk being wrong about; but (8) has just told me that accommodating anything is not the vice, so I cannot appeal to accommodation to explain the idleness. One member thought (3) simply overstated, citing the historical work on nineteenth-century American communes, which found that communes imposing costlier demands on their members survived longer and that the effect was concentrated among religious ones; the rest of us could not agree whether that counts as memetics or merely borrows its vocabulary. The case we kept returning to was the demographic transition: fertility in industrialized societies has fallen well below replacement and stayed there for generations, which memeticists read as transmission success coming apart from reproductive success, and which their critics derive from ordinary evolved psychology — status competition and the trade-off between number of offspring and investment per offspring — operating in an environment with reliable contraception. My difficulty is that I cannot see which of those two readings my argument entitles me to prefer, and I suspect that is not an accident. If I am forced to choose, I would rather give up (9) than (7), and I would rather keep (1) than give up the thought that some disciplines are just storytelling wearing a lab coat; but I recognize that these preferences are precisely what is in question.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable when I wrote it down, since each of them did? Price the alternatives for me. If you keep (1), how do you propose to criticize explanations that genuinely do rationalize whatever happens, and what do you say to (7)? If you drop (1), what replaces it, and does the replacement still deliver (4) — or does it license (9) all over again? And if you think the argument is valid and (9) simply has to be swallowed, say so plainly and tell me what that implies about selectionist explanation generally. Finally, once this argument is off the table, what is a critic of memetics entitled to say? I want the diagnosis first and the general assessment second, and I would rather have one step examined closely than a survey of everything that has ever been said against memetics.
