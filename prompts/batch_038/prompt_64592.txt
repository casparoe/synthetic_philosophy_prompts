We're designing a hiring committee rule and I want your actual credence on whether it escapes the discursive dilemma, not a tour of the options. Setup: five members vote yes/no on three criteria — research, teaching, service — and the candidate gets hired iff all three draw a majority yes (so, the standard conjunctive agenda). Our fix, call it Rotating Supremacy: before each vote one criterion is drawn by lot and declared conclusive, and the committee's verdict is simply the majority on that criterion; the majorities on the other two get minuted but dont enter the verdict. Nobody knows the draw in advance, and the draw is independent of how anyone votes.

Give me a number for the claim that this procedure is collectively rational in the sense the dilemma is supposed to threaten — i.e. that the minuted judgment set can't come apart from the verdict — plus the decomposition. I want terms like: the probability that the recorded majorities can still contradict the verdict even with a conclusive criterion; the probability that randomizing the draw does any normative work beyond concealing which issue got privileged; the probability that consistency is even the right target here rather than something like responsiveness or non-dictatorship spread across the agenda. Rough number and one line of justification per term, then how they combine, then which term your final number is most sensitive to and what would move it.
