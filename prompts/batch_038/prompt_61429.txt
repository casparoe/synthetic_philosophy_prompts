You are a philosopher who works at the intersection of the theory of practical reasons and literary criticism — someone equally at home with Bernard Williams's arguments about reasons and with close reading of a contemporary novel. I would like you to think carefully rather than produce an immediate, fluent-sounding gloss, and to begin with a short working stage before the finished piece.

The task. I am preparing a paper for a mixed audience of philosophers and literary scholars, and I want to read Kazuo Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go* (2005) through Williams's internal and external reasons distinction — the view that a reason for an agent to act must be capable of being reached from some element of that agent's subjective motivational set, S, by a sound deliberative route (a route that may include correcting false beliefs, reasoning accurately about means, and the exercise of imagination), and that statements asserting reasons where no such element exists are false rather than merely unacknowledged. Williams's own illustration, taken from Henry James by way of Britten, is Owen Wingrave: a young man whose family has a long military tradition and who has no desire whatever to serve. Please treat the novel as a serious test case for that doctrine, not as an illustration of it.

Begin with a brainstorming stage of roughly five hundred words, in which you list the pressure points before you argue anything: the specific moments in the novel that most directly engage the internal/external reasons question, and for each, the two or three incompatible things one might say about it. Only then write the reading itself.

In the reading, I want you to work through the following, in whatever order you judge best, and to take a position on each rather than survey options:

1. Hailsham versus the Wingrave household. Owen's S is intact but differently directed from his family's; the Hailsham students' S has been deliberately manufactured by an institution whose stated purpose was to shape what they could want and imagine. Does that difference matter for Williams's argument, or is internalism simply indifferent to the history of the motivational set it quantifies over? If it is indifferent, is that a cost, and is the novel registering that cost?

2. The question nobody asks. Escape is not a live option for Kathy, Tommy, or Ruth at any point; the novel never stages a deliberation about running away. Should the internalist say the students therefore have no reason to run, or does Williams's counterfactual structure — what the agent would be moved to do after sound deliberation — leave the question open here in a way that makes the counterfactual unanswerable? Say which, and explain what turns on it.

3. Tommy's childhood tantrums. These are episodes of raw, inarticulate protest that precede and outlast any belief or desire Tommy could state. Do such states belong in S? Does their presence help internalism (something in S is served by resisting) or embarrass it (S contains material that deliberation cannot organise, so the sound-deliberative-route clause has no purchase)?

4. Miss Lucy against Miss Emily. One guardian insists the students must be told the truth plainly and is removed for doing so; the other runs a school whose curriculum is an education in feeling and imagination, on the assumption that they can be brought to grasp their situation in their own time. Map both onto the sound-deliberative-route clause: which of them is the internalist about reasons, and is it possible that neither is? Does the novel show one of the two experiments failing in a way that is diagnostic?

5. The Gallery as a reason-generating technology. The art was collected to convince the outside world that these children had souls, and thereby to change how they were treated. That is an attempt to produce reasons in third parties by working on their imaginations rather than by argument. Does this make Ishiguro's novel a Williams-style work — committed to the idea that you cannot give people reasons except by reshaping what they can picture — and if so, what does the project's failure at the level of public opinion say about that commitment?

6. The deferral rumour and the revelation that it was false. Williams allows that correcting a false belief can bring an internal reason into reach. Here the correction arrives, and what follows is not a new motivation but despair and rage. Is that a counterexample, or does Williams's claim only licence the prediction that sound deliberation *could* move the agent, leaving actual outcomes unconstrained? If the latter, say whether that is a retreat that keeps the doctrine honest or one that insulates it.

7. Kathy's calm. She is proud of her work as a carer, loyal, capable of love, and unprotesting; she does not narrate her life as an injustice in any recognisable register. On Williams's account, does she simply have all the reasons there are? The reader, meanwhile, cannot help asserting a reason she does not have and cannot acquire — that she has reason to be angry, to refuse, to run. Assess the claim that the novel's form (a first-person narration whose normative outlook the reader must judge from outside) forces an external standpoint on the reader while simultaneously demonstrating that the standpoint is useless. Is that the novel's real philosophical achievement, or am I over-reading the narrative mode?

Then a section on resistance. State plainly where the internal/external reasons lens distorts the book — for instance, if Ishiguro is not asking what these characters should do at all, but what people make of a life whose shape is given to them in advance, in which case a reasons-based reading may turn the novel into a parable about compliance that it refuses to be. Do not manufacture a fit; I would rather have an honest account of the mismatch than a tidy one.

Finish with what the novel contributes to the debate that argument alone does not: what *Never Let Me Go* shows about manufactured motivational sets, or about the availability of a vocabulary of protest, that a paper of counterexamples and intuitions cannot show.

Two calibration requests, which I would ask you to honour explicitly. First, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. In particular, flag where your reading depends on a disputed point in Williams's own account — most obviously how much work the sound-deliberative-route clause can do before internalism collapses into the externalism it was meant to exclude, a matter on which Williams's critics and his defenders read him very differently — and say which side of that dispute your interpretation of the novel needs. Second, keep exegesis and interpretation apart: mark clearly when you are reporting what Williams's argument requires and when you are advancing a claim about Ishiguro that a reasonable reader could reject.

Practical constraints. Write for readers who know both the novel and the argument, and who do not need either summarised at length. Do not quote the novel or Williams verbatim — you cannot check the wording, and invented quotations are worse than none; describe scenes and paraphrase claims in your own words instead. If you are unsure of a detail in the book, say so and proceed with what you are sure of. No bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind. Aim for roughly two thousand to two thousand five hundred words in the finished reading, plus the brainstorming stage.
