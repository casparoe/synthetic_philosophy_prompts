I'm a staff engineer on a payments platform, and my philosophy education consists of roughly one year of reading on my own — a used metaphysics anthology, a lot of reconstructing arguments badly, and no one to check my work against. Our on-call culture is saturated in dispositional talk: we write in postmortems that a replica is disposed to fall behind under write-heavy traffic, that the token store is fragile with respect to clock skew, that the retry path is disposed to amplify load. After reading about finks and masks I sat down to make that idiom rigorous, and instead I produced an argument that seems to show it is empty. I can't accept the conclusion, and I can't locate the error.

Here is the argument, with examples from my own domain. (1) To say that a system x is disposed to manifest M in circumstances C is to say that x would M if C were to obtain, and nothing more. (2) That can't be necessary. I can build a fink on a bench: a lead that is genuinely live, wired to a watchdog that kills the lead the instant a probe makes contact. The lead is live — that is precisely what live amounts to for it — and yet it would not deliver a shock if touched. Same shape, no gadgetry needed in the description: a fragile glass packed in foam is fragile, and would not shatter if struck. (3) It can't be sufficient either, by a mirror-image construction, though I don't know the standard name for this kind of case. A service with no rate-limiting logic whatsoever, sitting behind a gateway that sheds excess traffic before it ever arrives, behaves exactly as a rate limiter would: the conditional is true and the disposition is absent. (4) The obvious repair is to relativize. x is disposed to M in C iff, in a suitable proportion of the nearest worlds in which C obtains and x is left alone, x Ms; call the second conjunct the no-interference condition. (5) But which worlds count as ones in which x is left alone, and which proportion counts as suitable, is settled only by which of x's properties are responsible for M — and the only way I have to identify those properties is by observing what x does under test. (6) So (4) gives truth conditions for a disposition ascription only by appeal to the dispositions it was meant to analyze, and the circle is not removable: every candidate no-interference clause I can write down — no fink present, no mask present, the causal basis persists unaltered — is either itself disposition-laden or is defeated by a further case of the same species. I gather this is why restricting the clause to intrinsic bases does not by itself close the hole, since an antidote can be part of the object's own makeup. (7) A claim is factual only if some possible state of affairs would make it true rather than false. Given (6) there is no such state of affairs for the analysans in (4) that is not already described in the vocabulary of the analysandum: any world in which x survives the test can be read as x being fragile with no interference, any world in which x fails can be read as x being fragile with interference present, and symmetrically for not-fragile. (8) Ascriptions that fit exactly the same worlds say the same thing, and an ascription that fits every world says nothing at all. (9) Therefore nothing is disposed to anything: fragility, solubility, and disposition-to-shed-load are shorthand for bare regularities, and my postmortems have been describing behavior while pretending to describe properties.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a survey. First, tell me where the argument actually breaks and what the break consists in — I have a nagging suspicion about (7), where I seem to slide from a claim about analysis to a claim about which worlds an ascription is compatible with, and I suspect the word fit is doing two jobs there, but I may be looking in the wrong place entirely and would rather be corrected than confirmed. Second, explain why each suspect step looked perfectly innocent to me when I wrote it, since understanding that is what stops me from producing the next version of the same argument. Third, price the exits: for a reformed conditional analysis built on normal conditions or a proportion clause, for primitivism about dispositions as irreducible causal powers, for a view on which dispositions are genuine but only ever licensed ceteris paribus, and for eliminativism or quietism about dispositional predicates, say what holding the view costs and which of my numbered steps it forces me to abandon. If you think (8) is the cheapest casualty, say so and tell me what has to take its place, because as an engineer I have real sympathy for (8): I distrust distinctions that no test could ever expose. Relatedly, tell me whether the argument would generalize if it were sound — to counterfactuals as such, to functional ascriptions like this module's job is to validate inputs, to beliefs construed as dispositions to assent — and whether its generalizing or failing to generalize is itself evidence about the location of the flaw. For what it is worth, my own preference ordering is to keep (2) and (3), since I can build both devices, and to keep (8) rather than accept that there is a brute, unanalyzable fact about a wine glass. Tell me whether that combination is available at all, and if it is, what the least-damaged version of my practice would look like.
