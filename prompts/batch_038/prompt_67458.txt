QUESTION (25 marks): (a) Explain the distinction between brute luck and option luck, and why luck egalitarians think distributive justice should be sensitive to it. (b) Explain how Dworkin's hypothetical insurance device is supposed to put that distinction to work. (c) State one objection to the luck-egalitarian programme and give the strongest reply available to a luck egalitarian. About 500 words.

STUDENT'S ANSWER: Luck egalitarians think a distribution is unfair when how well off you are comes down to things you had no say in, and fair when it comes down to your own choices. Dworkin splits luck into brute luck and option luck. Brute luck is a risk that just lands on you and wasn't part of any deliberate gamble — being struck by lightning, being born with a condition that makes your life cost more to live, being made redundant because the factory shut. Option luck is how a gamble you deliberately took turns out — you buy shares knowing they might fall, you take a dangerous job for the higher pay, you drive uninsured and crash. The distinction matters because they want justice to be ambition-sensitive but endowment-insensitive: if two people start with the same resources and one works harder or takes a well-calculated risk, the inequality that follows isn't an injustice, but if one of them is born with something that makes their life costlier to go well, the community should make up the difference.

Dworkin's device for working out how much to make up is the hypothetical insurance market. Instead of asking what people deserve, you imagine everyone starting with the same purchasing power and the same limited information, and ask what cover they'd buy against being untalented, disabled, or otherwise badly placed. What people would actually pay then sets the level of redistribution, and what they'd insure against tells you what counts as a misfortune rather than a preference. The clever bit is that this converts brute luck into option luck — once cover is available, going uncovered is a gamble you took — so the state doesn't have to make intrusive judgements about who's a victim and who's just careless. Because you're predicting real risk attitudes, you could also test the scheme against actual insurance markets and see what people buy. And this is basically Rawls's difference principle given a luck-egalitarian form, which is why Rawls is really the founder of the whole approach, even though Dworkin is the one who named it.

For the objection, the obvious one is Elizabeth Anderson's: luck egalitarianism gives up on the people it thinks chose badly, so the uninsured driver who crashes or the worker injured in a job they took freely can be left to suffer, and it humiliates the ones who have to declare their own inferiority to get compensated. The strongest reply is that the insurance device already handles this. Everyone had the chance to cover exactly these risks at the start, so those people aren't abandoned by the theory, they're getting what they chose not to buy cover for. So Anderson's objection only bites on versions that ignore the insurance step. Her own alternative, democratic equality, turns out to be luck egalitarianism with a floor under it so that nobody drops below what they need to function as a citizen.
