I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy working at the intersection of metaethics and the theory of AI value learning. A chapter of my dissertation argues that coherent extrapolated volition is not a novel proposal but a re-engineering of Roderick Firth's ideal observer theory: the same move from actual human responses to the responses of an idealized agent, with the idealization performed by a machine rather than by the moral theorist's stipulation. My committee pushed back hard and said the analogy is backwards. I would like a careful audit before I revise the chapter.

Let me state the two views as I have them, and then the use I make of the comparison.

Firth's ideal observer, from his 1952 paper, is stipulated to be omniscient about nonmoral facts, omnipercipient (able to vividly imagine any experience from the inside), disinterested, dispassionate, consistent, and otherwise an entirely normal human being; an ethical claim is then analyzed as a claim about what such an observer would approve of. Note that last clause: the idealizations form a short, closed list, and everything else about the observer is deliberately left at the human baseline.

Coherent extrapolated volition, as the AI-safety literature has it, proposes that an advanced system's goals be set by what humanity's volition would converge to under extrapolation: if we knew more, thought faster and more clearly, were more the people we wished we were, and had developed in closer contact with one another — with the further requirement that the extrapolation be stable, so that the volition of the extrapolated agents would not itself call for revision by a second round of extrapolation. Nothing in that recipe is a list of idealizing properties; it is an open-ended process description.

Here is the work the analogy does in my argument. If coherent extrapolated volition is ideal observer theory with a better engine, then the objections that have accumulated against ideal observer theory transfer to it more or less intact, and the burden of answering them sits with the proposal's advocates rather than with me. The particular objection I press is one I have dubbed the empty-chair problem: strip an agent of interest, of passion, and of personal perspective until it is calm, impartial, and fully informed, and you may have deleted precisely the machinery that generates wants. What remains either approves of nothing in particular, or reports the preferences smuggled in by whoever wrote the idealization clause. Firth escapes this by way of the normalcy proviso; my claim is that extrapolated volition has no such proviso and therefore cannot.

My committee's reply is that the analogy inverts the two theories. Firth's idealizations are a finite stipulation chosen precisely to build impartiality into the analysis, and the normalcy clause is doing enormous work there; extrapolated volition is meant to be content-free, to include our moral and metaethical views among the things being extrapolated, and to be plural rather than singular — there is no lone observer but a population whose volitions are supposed to cohere. On that reading, the two share a vocabulary and almost nothing else, and my objection transfers only by equivocation.

Please audit the analogy rather than adjudicate the two theories on their independent merits. What I most want to know is whether the empty-chair objection survives the transfer.

Structure the answer with the following sections, in this order:

1. The analogy, stated precisely (about 200 words). Re-express both theories in a common vocabulary — an idealization function, an input, an output, and the use to which the output is put — so that the comparison and the differences can be stated without rhetoric.

2. Correspondence table (about 100 words of prose plus the table). One row per element of Firth's ideal observer — omniscience about nonmoral facts, omnipercipience, disinterestedness, dispassionateness, consistency, the normalcy proviso — against whatever in extrapolated volition is supposed to play its role, including rows where the honest entry is no counterpart at all.

3. Load-bearing disanalogies (about 600 words). The differences that genuinely threaten my transfer of objections, ranked by how much damage each does. For each, say what my chapter would have to add or concede in order to survive it.

4. Idle disanalogies (about 250 words). Differences that sound fatal in seminar — one observer versus many, an analysis of ethical language versus a specification of an agent's goals — but that on inspection do not interfere with the empty-chair argument. Say why they are idle.

5. What the analogy quietly imports (about 300 words). Features of the source that the comparison carries over into the target without argument: that idealization has a determinate limit; that the idealized agent is still recognizably a person; that the party specifying the idealization is neutral; that removing bias from the idealizer preserves rather than destroys the content of the responses.

6. Verdict (about 250 words). Does the empty-chair objection reach extrapolated volition through this analogy, through some other route, or not at all? State plainly what is left of my chapter's claim.

7. A better analogue (about 200 words). If extrapolated volition is not ideal observer theory with a better engine, what is it most usefully compared to, and what would that comparison license — and refuse to license — in its place?
