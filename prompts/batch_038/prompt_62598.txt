I'm teaching a ten-week upper-level seminar on space and time to thirteen philosophy majors — a couple have had one semester of classical mechanics, none have taken differential geometry — and next week's session is a hundred minutes in a room with bolted-down tables and no lab access beyond whatever is sitting in the department stockroom. The session is supposed to make Newton's bucket argument, and the substantivalism/relationism debate it anchors, felt rather than merely summarized. They will have read the bucket passage and Newton's companion case from the same Scholium to the Definitions in Book I of the Principia: two globes joined by a cord, revolving together in an otherwise empty region of space, where the tension in the cord is supposed to disclose their true circular motion. They will also have read a secondary selection setting out Mach's complaint that arguments of this shape illicitly extrapolate from cases where the rotating body has plenty of material surroundings to a limiting case where it has none. What I do not want is a demonstration followed by discussion. I want an activity in which the students commit themselves on paper, under scoring pressure, to claims about what their world contains, and then discover the cost of those commitments.

Here is the skeleton I have been toying with; develop it, repair it, or replace it if you think it cannot work. Phase one: split the seminar into small "surveys" of a possible world, each handed a deck of cards reporting nothing but relational data — distances and angles between bodies, rates of change of those distances, readings of instruments carried along with bodies — from which the survey must compile a catalogue of that world's distinct states. Two of the decks describe Leibniz-shifted counterparts: worlds that differ, if space is something over and above the bodies in it, but that generate identical card data. Phase two: each survey answers, for a fixed battery of cases, whether genuine rotation is present, and wagers points on its answer. The battery runs from the ordinary — a bucket of water swung on a rope in front of the class, plus a student on a swivel chair holding the rope with eyes shut, calling out whether they are turning — through to the limiting cases: the same bucket alone in an otherwise empty universe; the two globes and the cord with all other matter removed; the globes at rest in one deck and the cord under tension anyway. Phase three: reveal that the two counterpart decks scored identically everywhere it was possible to check, and let them argue about what that shows.

What I need from you: full rules and a materials list (assume index cards, a length of rope, a bucket, a swivel chair, a whiteboard, and nothing that costs money or requires booking a facility); a minute-by-minute timeline for the hundred minutes; a scoring scheme that makes the wagering bite without collapsing into trivia; and a debrief that maps particular moments of the activity onto particular joints of the argument — the inference from inertial effects to rotation, the shift, Mach's reply, and the difference between detecting rotation by the forces one feels and detecting it relative to distant matter. I also want an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I already suspect two failures. Someone will say that the student on the swivel chair simply feels himself rotating, so there is nothing left to dispute — which quietly begs the question against Mach, and I don't know how to defuse it without lecturing. Someone else will conclude from the tied scores that the whole controversy is verbal, which would be a worse outcome than not running the activity at all. Tell me how to design against both, including anything in the rules, the card decks, or the payoff structure that pre-empts them rather than leaving me to argue afterwards. Finally, say plainly which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply in my own voice — I assume it has to do with the fact that the live question is not which theory fits the data, since they tie, but which one explains and which is ontologically cheaper — and, if it can be done in two minutes without misleading them, sketch a bridge from the tied scores to the descendant of the shift argument that turned up in general relativity a few centuries later. If that bridge is a trap at this level, say so and give me the sentence I should say instead.
