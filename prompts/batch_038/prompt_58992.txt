I am in the third year of a doctoral programme in clinical psychology, and the empirical half of my dissertation is a new self-report measure of what my supervisor and I have been calling felt-but-disavowed affect: the extent to which people's avowed emotional states come apart from the dispositions their behaviour and their close relationships suggest. The item bank is now around two hundred statements; I write roughly twenty a week, two research assistants code them independently, and my supervisor signs off on the final bank before we take it to the ethics committee and pilot it on some four hundred respondents. Because the measure is intended to end up as a secondary outcome in a treatment trial, anything that looks conceptually confused will be attacked at review, and I would much rather catch those problems myself.

A philosopher friend read a sample of the items and told me that a fair number of them are Moore-paradoxical: they ask a respondent to endorse, in the first person and in the present tense, a conjunction of a claim about their own state with a denial of, or a belief that contradicts, that claim. Her point was that such a conjunction can be true and can even be rationally believed, but cannot be sincerely asserted, so an item of that shape is not putting a question the respondent can coherently answer, whatever box they tick. I think she is right about some of my items and wrong about others, and I cannot presently tell which is which without her.

So I would like you to turn that observation into a screening procedure my assistants and I can actually run. It must be usable from about a page of description, by people with no training in philosophy, in under two minutes per item, and it must terminate in one of a small number of verdicts — keep, rewrite, discard — with the criteria for each phrased as something an assistant can check rather than as a restatement of the theory. Please be explicit about which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which you are willing to drop, about where the screen will misfire and in which direction, about which of its steps are really my intuitions wearing a rule as a costume, and about the points at which a decision has to come back to me rather than to the procedure. Where an item must be rewritten, say what kind of rewriting preserves the construct without the incoherence.

Then run the screen on three of my drafts: "I resent my mother, though I don't believe I do"; "I sometimes feel afraid in situations I judge to be safe"; and "I am not jealous of my brother, but I am convinced that I am." The first is my best item — in the pilot it tracked the clinician ratings more closely than anything else in the bank — so if the screen condemns it, say so plainly and explain why its predictive success does not rescue it. Close with what the screen cannot do: the item-bank failure modes it is silent about, which I will have to police by other means.

Please write all of this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter rather than a manual: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables. If the screen has ordered steps, carry them in running sentences.
