Act as a philosopher of interpretation who's spent years in the intentionalism debates — I want adjudication, not a survey. Quick problem from my actual life: I'm one of five people on the board that runs a mid-century novelist's literary archive. We license adaptations, sign off on the annotations for the critical edition, and every so often we have to rule on what a passage means when somebody claims it means something she'd have hated. Our track record so far:

1. Two years after publication she told an interviewer that the famous line in ch. 3 was meant ironically. The line reads perfectly straight, the words can carry the irony, and the ironic reading is the one most critics prefer. We endorsed the irony and glossed it that way in the edition.
2. In 1994 a private letter surfaced, written the year the book came out, telling a friend that the uncle is her brother. The text doesnt resist it, but treating the novel as a roman à clef flattens its best scenes. We refused the gloss, and refused an adapter who wanted to lean on it.
3. A scholar argued from the surviving drafts that she abandoned an ending in which the protagonist lives. We said the abandoned ending has no bearing on meaning, but agreed to print it as an appendix.
4. In a lecture in the 70s she insisted the novel is not about the war. Everyone reads it as a war novel, and the war reading is what holds the thing together. We left the war reading standing and noted the lecture.

New case, vote in three weeks. We've just catalogued a private notebook in her hand that spells out what she meant by the knot in part three — the passage people have argued over for fifty years. The reading the notebook supports is one the text can obviously bear, and honestly its better: it untangles the knot. Nobody saw the notebook in her lifetime, and nothing in the published book points at it.
