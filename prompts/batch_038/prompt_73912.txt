So here's a view I've talked myself into and I want it pressure-tested, not cheerleded.

The bundle, roughly: (a) competitive elections systematically select for a distinct career class — people who are good at winning, fundraising and surviving primaries, which is only loosely related to governing well; (b) because they intend to stay in office indefinitely, their incentives bend toward reelection, party loyalty and donor management; (c) sortition — a randomly selected chamber, stratified so it actually resembles the population, with real powers, decent pay, professional staff, and hard rotation so nobody serves twice — cuts off the careerist channel at the root instead of trying to regulate it with term limits and campaign finance rules. So: a lottocratic legislature would make better decisions and be harder to capture than an elected one.

What would count against this? I don't want the "people deserve to choose their representatives" line — I've heard the legitimacy objection and I'm half convinced its a taste dressed up as a principle. I want the stuff that should actually move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward.

- Start with what's been tried. Citizens' assemblies and deliberative polls, the Irish one that fed into the abortion referendum, those Oregon-style panels that review ballot measures and write the pamphlet entry, the permanent citizens' council in the German-speaking bit of Belgium, ordinary juries, and Athens itself — which filled nearly everything by lot but elected its generals, and I genuinely don't know what to make of that. What do those cases show about who actually says yes to a summons, whether lay members get steamrolled by chairs, secretariats and invited experts, how far their views move and in which direction, and whether anyone with power has to act on the output? And separately: the US state term-limit experiments — who ended up holding the expertise once the veterans were forced out, and does that cut for or against my claim that careers are the disease?
- The formal side. What has to be true for a randomly drawn group to out-perform a group selected on ambition and skill (competence-of-the-jury style arguments, and their failure conditions when members are correlated or badly briefed), and does a big stratified sample inherit the conditions or dodge them?
- The accountability hole. Random members can't be voted out. What actually disciplines them, is "they have to justify themselves in public" a real mechanism, and what evidence would show it isn't?
- Then the uncomfortable part: is this thesis testable at all, or does it survive by converting every counterexample into a design requirement — you needed longer terms, you needed a bigger sample, it only had an advisory role, it wasn't paired with an elected chamber, the staff were the wrong kind? If the answer is always "you didn't do sortition properly," has it been insulated into unfalsifiability, and is that fatal for a claim being sold on the grounds that it would work better in practice? Tell me what a fair test would look like and what result from it would kill the view outright.
- And what does it predict that a defender of elections doesn't? If the two camps disagree about, say, how responsive policy is to the wealthy versus the median voter, or about what happens to a member's behavior in a term they know is their last, point at where the predictions actually come apart and say which of those are checkable with what already exists.

Finally, which of these would a serious sortition advocate concede counts against them — they know some of this — and which would they wave off, and are they right to wave it off? No reading list or citations, I just want the substance. And be blunt: if the honest answer is that most of what I'd need can't be known until someone runs this for real at national scale, say that, and tell me what I should believe in the meantime.
