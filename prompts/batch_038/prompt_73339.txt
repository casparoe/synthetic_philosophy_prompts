I cover AI and the general science-and-ideas beat for a weekly magazine — curious readers, no formal logic in the room, and my own logic course was fifteen years ago. Roughly four or five items a week land in my inbox whose entire hook is that some system evaluates itself: an agent that checks a planned action against its own written charter and only proceeds if the check passes, a model that "grades its own homework" and reports the score, two agents that cooperated because each one verified what the other would do, a robot that derives a proof its maneuver is safe before executing it. My problem is that I can't reliably tell apart (a) a genuine result, (b) a genuine result wrapped in language that implies far more than was shown, and (c) nothing at all. I do have a rough sense there's a standing formal difficulty with an agent trusting its own reasoning — something like: you can't get to "whatever I end up concluding is true" without already having established the thing, so a system that gates its actions on its own verdict hasn't obviously bought itself any reliability. If that's a garbled version of the real point, fix it before you build anything on top of it.

What I need is a triage procedure I can run on one of these items in twenty minutes, in order, with the questions I should be asking of the press release or preprint, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where it terminates in "kill it" versus where it hands the call to my editor because what's left is a news-value question rather than a truth question. It should tell me what to do in the two situations I'm actually in most weeks: the company won't answer follow-ups, and I can't inspect the system or reproduce anything. It should also be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume — I'd rather know which rung of the ladder I'm making up.

Constraints worth knowing. I get maybe three emailed questions to a research group before they stop replying, so if the procedure generates questions, rank them. My editor has less time than I do and won't be checking my reasoning, so anything the procedure flags has to be something I can state in one plain sentence. And our desk has a standing rule I don't want the procedure to quietly override: we don't run a capability claim whose only evidence is the claiming lab's own evaluation of its own system, unless the story is precisely that the evaluation is self-referential.

Then run it on three items:

1. A startup's demo where the agent writes out a justification that its planned action complies with its charter, and acts only if that justification is accepted. The acceptance is performed by the same model, same weights, different prompt. They call it self-verification and want it in the headline.
2. A preprint where two computationally limited agents playing a one-shot cooperation game cooperate at a surprisingly high rate, described as each agent having verified the other's behavior. Reading closely, the cooperation seems to depend on both agents knowing an upper limit on how long a derivation the other one will search, and that limit was set by the experimenters.
3. The one I don't want to hear: a widely used evaluation setup where a model scores outputs generated by models from its own family, and I've been circling a takedown arguing the whole thing is circular in the self-trust sense. If the procedure says that framing is wrong — that it's a different kind of problem entirely, not a proof-theoretic one — say so flatly, and tell me what the correct objection would be instead, because I'd rather be right than get the piece.

For each of the three: give me the verdict the procedure produces (kill / run it with a specific reframing / needs one more question before you can tell), the one sentence I could actually put in print, and which single question of my three I'd spend on it.

Finally, tell me where this procedure will misfire. I'm especially worried about it over-flagging — treating any use of the words verify, check, or validate as an alarm — and about the case where the "independent verifier" turns out to be the same company's bigger model, where I suspect the distinction the procedure leans on doesn't so much resolve as relocate.
