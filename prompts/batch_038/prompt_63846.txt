I'm three quarters through my fourth novel and I've hit a wall that isn't a craft problem, it's a thinking problem, so I'm going to dump the whole thing on you. The book is set in a Midlands town whose biggest employer is a distribution centre for an online retailer — 900 people, two shifts, everything measured in picks per hour. In the story the firm runs a two-year pilot that cuts the standard week from 45 hours to 38 at the same pay, mostly as a recruitment gimmick. And here's the situation I invented and now have to actually be able to account for, because a reviewer will eventually ask me whether I believe it: nothing gets better. Town-wide hours go *up*, because people take on second jobs, courier runs, unlogged half-hours at the end of shift. The two or three who genuinely take the free time and use it — one of them goes back to fishing like he did at twenty — become objects of quiet suspicion, as though they're ill, or gambling, or being managed out and not saying. A couple start businesses that lose money and everyone calls brave and everyone means it. The guilt lands hardest on the people doing nothing, not on the ones putting in sixty. And the shift manager who fought for the pilot ends up working more hours than anyone, unpaid, cheerfully. My agent thinks the middle sags, and I think the middle sags because I haven't worked out what is *causing* any of this.

My brother-in-law did a sociology degree and sent me a list of candidate explanations, which I've written up in my own words (please don't correct my glosses unless a gloss is so wrong that it changes the score):

1. Weber's Protestant ethic argument — a religious valuation of work as a calling outlives the religion and survives as a bare secular drive to accumulate, with idleness as sin.
2. Lafargue's The Right to Be Lazy (1883) — the working classes have been infected with a love of work that amounts to a vice, and the time machines free up gets turned straight back into more toil instead of leisure.
3. Russell's In Praise of Idleness — four hours a day would be plenty; the obstacle is a slave morality that makes leisure shameful, not any economic scarcity.
4. Keynes's 1930 essay on his grandchildren's economic possibilities — a very short working week was coming, and the genuinely hard part would be the leisure, because people trained for striving don't know what to do with a free afternoon.
5. The passage in Marx's late-1850s notebooks about machinery — capital drives necessary labour time toward zero but has to keep labour time as the measure of wealth, so it converts free time back into surplus labour.
6. The French 35-hour week (the Aubry laws of 1998 and 2000, with time off in lieu instead of pay) treated as a natural experiment — what happened to actual hours, to intensity, and to the gap between salaried professionals and everyone else.
7. Karoshi as a Japanese administrative category — overwork death recognised for compensation since the late 1960s, tied to a threshold of overtime hours; the interesting part being that a society has to build an official category before it can admit what work is doing to it.
8. Graeber's bullshit jobs thesis — a large share of modern work is experienced by the people doing it as pointless, and that pointlessness is a harm of its own kind.
9. Han's Burnout Society — an achievement society where the coercive boss is replaced by self-exploitation that is *felt as freedom*, so the victim and the perpetrator end up being the same person.
10. Textbook labour economics — the backward-bending labour supply curve and the substitution effect; note the pilot cut hours and not pay, so for the workers it's effectively a raise.

Now the ask. Score each of the ten from 0 to 10 for how well it explains the pattern above *as I've specified it* — not for how true or how interesting it is in general — one or two sentences of reasoning each, and then sort them into tiers (say A/B/C/D). I specifically want you to separate three things that tend to get mushed together: accounts the pattern fits nicely, accounts the pattern merely doesn't test either way, and accounts the pattern actively *contradicts*, with a line on what would have to be true in my town for it to be real evidence against them. Don't hedge by saying they're all partially right and mutually enriching — that's the answer I'm dreading and it's no use to me at a desk with a deadline. Then give me two numbers: the share of the novel's causal weight, from 0 to 1, that should sit on the single best-fitting account (with everything else — money, character, this particular firm — dividing the remainder), and if two of them end up tied at the top, tell me the one further thing I could have happen in the town that would separate them, which is really what I'm after. Bottom line in your very first sentence — which account wins and with what share — then the scores and the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question back to me.
