I am two-thirds of the way through a novel whose central conceit is a ranking, and a philosopher friend, being kinder than a reviewer, has given me an argument to show that the ranking is impossible. I should like to know where his argument goes wrong before I commit another year to the book, and I should like to know what the alternatives cost, because I suspect I am going to have to buy one of them.

The conceit is this. The novel has a hundred chapters and each chapter is a life, told entire and in the same flat register. The first belongs to a composer who gets forty years of the most intense creative activity a person can manage and dies at the height of it. Each subsequent life is very slightly less excellent in that respect — I have calibrated the losses so that any one of them, taken alone, would be too small for anyone to notice — and very slightly longer, so that the hundredth consists of several centuries of mild, undemanding comfort. There is a frame: a committee reads the chapters and ranks them, and the ranking runs unbroken from the first to the last, best to worst. The book is meant to be read as a descent, and the last chapter is meant to be the worst thing in it.

The third act turns on a vote I need to be genuinely unrankable. The chairman must cast the deciding vote between two of the lives, and the weight of the scene rests on there being nothing to discover: neither is better than the other, neither worse, and — this being the part my friend says is impossible — they are not equally good either, since that would imply a common measure on which they happen to coincide. I do not mean that the chairman cannot decide. I mean that his anguish is knowledge rather than confusion.

My friend says both of my commitments are impossible, and impossible for the same reason. He set it out in eight steps, which I give as he gave them, because I cannot find the exit and am not certain the exit is in one place.

First, is-better-than must be transitive. Were it not, an agent could be taken round a cycle — preferring this to that, that to the other, the other back to this — and, charged a trifling fee at each exchange, returned to her starting point with less than she began with. No coherent agent can be exploited in that way for arbitrarily small sums, so the relation is transitive.

Second, there are only three relations in which two options can stand: better, worse, or equally good. Incomparability is not a fourth but a disguise, for if two options were truly incomparable one could trade between them for nothing in either direction, and the fee could be levied as before.

Third, in a spectrum of the kind I have built, each adjacent pair differs only imperceptibly: the loss of quality at each step is too small to detect, and so is the gain in duration.

Fourth, a difference that could not in principle be detected cannot make one thing better than another. Betterness that leaves no trace anywhere — in no experience, no measurement, no report — is not betterness.

Fifth, therefore no life in the spectrum is better than its successor, and, the argument working the same way from the other side, no successor is better than its predecessor. By the second step each adjacent pair is equally good; and since equally good is itself transitive, all a hundred lives are equally good, and the first is exactly as good as the last.

Sixth, and independently: were the first life better than the hundredth, that superiority would have to arise somewhere along the chain. A difference between the ends of a chain must be composed of differences between its links, or it appears out of nothing. By the fourth step there is no link at which it appears. So the first life is not better than the hundredth, nor the hundredth better than the first, and by the second step they are equally good.

Seventh, the reasoning generalises. Any two options joined by a chain of imperceptible differences are exactly equally good; any two not so joined differ perceptibly somewhere, and a perceptible difference must favour one of them, so those are strictly comparable.

Eighth, therefore nothing is incomparable, and no spectrum has a top and a bottom that differ. My descent is a fiction, and so is my chairman's vote.

I find the conclusion intolerable and cannot say which premise I most dislike. Two observations, offered without confidence. I have cut eleven chapters from this manuscript and I am certain it is not exactly as good as it was, though I could not tell you which cut did the damage; and if the seventh step holds, my friend's own argument, being joined to any other piece of writing by imperceptible edits, is exactly as good as any review I have ever received. I recognise that neither observation is itself an argument.

I have read enough to know there are named exits — intransitivity, parity, imprecise equality, and the view that the continuum must be honoured whatever it costs at the ends — and I know that some philosophers think my hundredth life is on reflection the best of the lot, so that the descent is the illusion. If that is the price of consistency I want to know it in advance, since it would mean rewriting the book rather than defending it. Tell me which step fails, or, if none fails, which premise I should surrender and what surrendering it costs; tell me why the failing step looked sound, since it looked sound to me; and tell me whether a novelist may write a choice that nothing ranks, or whether that is only bad philosophy dressed as fate.

If you would, give the diagnosis in your opening sentences and justify it thereafter, and write the whole of it as continuous prose: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold, no tables. I shall be reading it in pencil in the margin of a typescript, and I want it to read like a letter.
