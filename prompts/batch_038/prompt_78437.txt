Last Saturday my brother-in-law — who works in criminal justice policy and is considerably more fluent at a dinner table than in print — spent the better part of two hours maintaining that the Norwegian prison system has effectively settled the philosophical debate about punishment. I came away half persuaded and half irritated, and unable to say which half was which, which is precisely the state I dislike being in. His summary ran, as nearly as I can reproduce it from memory, like this:

> "The Norwegians have shown that punishment is counterproductive. At Halden there are no bars on the windows, officers eat and play sport with the prisoners, and nobody can be locked up for more than twenty-one years. Because they treat people inside as citizens who happen to have lost their liberty rather than as objects of retribution, roughly a fifth of their prisoners are back inside within two years, against something like two-thirds of America's within three. That is the empirical refutation of retributivism: retribution says offenders deserve to suffer, and suffering turns out not to reduce reoffending, while harsh conditions actively make it worse. Deterrence is just retributivism with a spreadsheet — both are about inflicting pain to send a message, so once you see that the message doesn't work, both collapse together. What survives is rehabilitation as the only coherent justification for locking anyone up, and the respect-for-persons strand in Kant points the same way, since treating an offender as an end in himself just is the normality principle. Victims, on this account, are a distraction: the state's obligations run to the offender and to future potential victims, and to nobody else."

I am not a philosopher — I read a little, badly — and I have no wish simply to defend my own intuitions about desert, which I suspect are unexamined. What I would like is an audit I can actually use the next time the subject comes up. Please take his passage claim by claim and sort it into three piles: what is straightforwardly accurate about the Norwegian system and about the statistics; what is a fair if compressed simplification of a more careful position; and what a philosopher of punishment would reject outright. In particular I would like you to identify every place where he fuses two claims that the theories he is invoking keep rigorously apart — I suspect there is at least one in the sentence about deterrence being retributivism with a spreadsheet, and another in his treatment of what recidivism data could possibly show about a backward-looking justification. Say explicitly whether his conclusion follows from the corrected version of his premises, or whether he needs additional argument to earn it, and if so which argument. I would also like your judgement on two further points. First, whether a cross-national comparison of reoffending rates can bear the causal weight he is placing on it, given that the two populations, sentencing practices and post-release environments differ in so many respects at once. Second, whether his appeal to Kant's respect-for-persons idea actually supports him or quietly undermines him — because it struck me mid-argument that he is simultaneously claiming retributivism has been refuted by the data and that a Kantian retributivist must endorse the Norwegian model, and I could not work out whether those two claims can be held together, or whether the second one concedes the whole debate to me.

Finally, if what he is really committed to, once the errors are stripped out, turns out to be a recognisable position in the philosophy of punishment — some mixed or hybrid account, perhaps — please tell me what it is roughly called and what it obliges him to accept in the cases where pure rehabilitation is uncomfortable with itself, including the question of whether anyone may be held indefinitely for as long as he is judged dangerous. Please write the whole response in British English, and do not feel any need to supply references; I am after the reasoning, not a bibliography.
