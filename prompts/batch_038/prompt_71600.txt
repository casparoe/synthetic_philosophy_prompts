So I've been going down a rabbit hole on Avicenna's flying man — the thought experiment where you imagine a person created all at once, fully mature and healthy, suspended in air or in a void, eyes covered so he sees nothing, limbs arranged so they don't touch one another, no sounds, no smells, nothing coming in at all. Avicenna's claim is that this guy would still be aware of his own existence, that there's a self there, but he would not affirm the existence of his body or of any of its parts. People usually say this shows the self just isn't the body, maybe even that its something immaterial.

Here's what won't leave me alone. I volunteer at a clinic, and there's an entire diagnostic category, depersonalization/derealization disorder, where patients report something close to the flying man's situation from the inside: my body doesn't feel like mine, I feel like an outside observer of myself, my limbs feel alien or the wrong size, I can't take my own reflection as me. The diagnostic criteria build in that reality testing stays intact throughout — they're not under a delusion, they know the feeling isn't literally true. And yet it's filed as a dissociative disorder, a disorder of self-experience, and the treatment is psychotherapy aimed at restoring the felt sense of being in your body: grounding work, sensory re-engagement, attention retraining. Nobody tries to talk them out of a belief, because there isn't one to talk them out of. Meanwhile in the same diagnostic universe, a patient who reports that their arm is being moved by someone else, or that thoughts are being inserted into them, gets that counted as a delusion and a loss of insight.

So what does the first practice actually presuppose about the self? Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does filing "my body doesn't feel like mine" as a symptom commit you to the view that felt ownership of the body is constitutive of being a self at all — something like the negation of the flying man, where a self-awareness stripped of bodily awareness isn't a purer awareness of what you are but a damaged one? Or is that overreading, and could a committed dualist who cheerfully accepts the flying man run the exact same clinic and the exact same criteria, on the grounds that what has gone wrong is affect and not selfhood? Please separate carefully what the practice strictly needs from what it merely fits nicely with, and flag where it's underdetermined, i.e. where two or three rival theories of the self would license identical criteria.

Then: what's doing the work in the asymmetry with the passivity delusions? Is it the second-order judgment (the depersonalized patient doesn't endorse the experience as true), the attribution to an outside agent, the presence of distress, the functional impairment? And which of those is a claim about what a self is, rather than a claim about evidence or about harm?

And the uncomfortable last part. If Avicenna is right that the self's awareness of itself needs neither body nor bodily awareness, then the depersonalized patient is arguably reporting something veridical about what they are, and we're pathologizing the one clear view of it. Take that seriously: what would have to change — in the criteria, in what counts as recovery, in whether this belongs under dissociation at all — if a clinic actually assumed the flying man's conclusion? And is there a version of the practice that survives that assumption intact, say one that treats the disorder as a loss of felt agency and of affective contact with the world rather than as a disorder of selfhood? Tell me which of these you think is right and why, not just the menu.
