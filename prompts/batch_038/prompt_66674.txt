Been slogging through Leibniz's Monadology with two friends from my old reading group and we've hit a wall at the bit where he jumps from the principle of sufficient reason to God — the two short sections right after the necessary-truths/contingent-truths setup, somewhere around 36–37, though I might have the numbering wrong. I don't have the book in front of me right now, so here's the sense of it in my own words, not the wording of any translation — treat it as what he's saying, not as a text to comment on the phrasing of:

the sufficient reason, which needs no further reason, must be found outside this series of contingent things, however infinite that series may be; and that's why the last reason of things must lie in a necessary substance, in which the detail of all the changes exists eminently, as in a source; and this is what we call God.

Four things I can't get past, and I'd rather you push back on my framing than just answer it as posed:

1. What is "eminently" doing there? My translation used a word like that and it reads like a leftover scholastic term of art. If it is, what's the doctrine behind it, and what would it actually mean to say God contains the detail of the world's changes that way rather than, say, straightforwardly?

2. The infinite-series move. Every member of the series already has its reason in an earlier member, so what's left over that goes unexplained? Is he just assuming that an infinite regress is unsatisfying, or is there a real gap between explaining each member and explaining why there is this series of members at all? Say clearly whether the objection "you've explained every item, so there's nothing left to explain" is a fallacy, a fair reading, or a different question.

3. Why must the reason be outside the series rather than the series as a whole having a reason in itself? And does "necessary substance" here mean logically necessary or merely uncaused and dependent on nothing — does the argument need the stronger one, or does it work with the weaker?

4. Does anything in this stretch get him from "a necessary substance" to "one God," or is that smuggled in from elsewhere?

Now the part that's drivng me up the wall. Whenever this comes up today — the people pressing a Leibnizian contingency argument against the people saying the universe is a brute fact and there's no legitimate demand for an explanation of the totality — both sides gesture at this same passage and nobody budges, hasnt for decades. My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: they aren't really disagreeing about causation, or even about whether the whole needs a cause in the ordinary sense. They're disagreeing about what an explanation has to do to count as finished — one side wants a reason that terminates the regress, the other thinks termination is just an arbitrary stopping rule wearing the costume of a requirement — so it's a fight over standards of explanation rather than over how the world is. And I suspect there's a second, hidden question tangled up in it about whether an explanatory demand that makes sense applied to each part carries over to the whole collection.

Is that diagnosis right, or is there a substantive disagreement about facts or about the scope of sufficient-reason principles hiding underneath it? Give me the diagnosis with reasons drawn from what each side actually says, not a tour of the argument, and then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a shift in the question being asked — or make the case that nothing will and why.

One thing I'd ask: be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on it, on both the Leibniz-scholarship side and the contemporary cosmological-argument side, and which parts are genuinely live and contested. Don't hand me someone's disputed reading dressed up as the consensus.
