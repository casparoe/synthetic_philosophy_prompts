I am the head registrar at a mid-sized museum of decorative arts in Central Europe with roughly forty thousand objects, and I need help turning a metaphysical dispute into something two junior assistants can actually run. Our accession numbering descends from an 1898 register built on a single assumption: an object, once entered, stays numerically itself — one number, one label, one insurance value, one line in any restitution file, one continuous provenance chain. In practice that assumption fails several times a month. We consolidate, re-line, re-mount, replace missing mounts and hardware, reassemble disassembled pieces after exhibition, occasionally deaccession a component, and sometimes discover that a nineteenth-century restorer quietly combined material from two objects in a way nobody recorded. Between the registrar, two assistants and a conservator we make perhaps a dozen "is this still the object we entered under that number?" decisions a month. They are not academic questions. The answer fixes which accession number appears on a loan agreement, whether an insurance schedule lists one object or two, whether a restitution claim filed against a given number can be met by pointing at the thing standing in the gallery, and whether a catalogue entry's chain of custody survives intact. The people who must execute the decision have no philosophical training; one of them has already told me she finds the whole question tiresome. I have about three weeks to install something and about twenty minutes per case; a procedure that takes longer will be ignored, and a procedure that is ignored is worse than none, because it leaves a false record of our having been consistent. A senior curator countersigns outcomes but will not re-derive them.

A friend of mine who read philosophy at university maintains that our problem is precisely the debate between endurantism and perdurantism, and that this museum has been settling that debate by accident, differently in different departments, for a hundred and twenty years. I would like you to take her seriously enough to build the procedure. Concretely: write a stepwise decision procedure, executable in under twenty minutes by a non-specialist, that tells us (i) when to treat an altered, repaired, split or recombined artefact as the same object as the one already carried under an existing accession number, (ii) when to open a new number, (iii) when to close a number, and (iv) when to record a single object with two distinguishable careers under two numbers that cross-reference each other. It must be grounded in the actual metaphysics rather than in a generic checklist of factors. Along the way, the procedure should make explicit what commitment we incur when we let one accession number carry incompatible properties across decades — an object that is complete at one date and 60 percent modern at another — as against treating it as a dated history whose parts are the units of record; and it should say which of the standard moves in that debate correspond to which registry operations: the worm-style treatment of an object as its whole history, the stage-style treatment under which a dated condition record is its own unit and sameness is a matter of standing in the right relation to another such unit, time-indexed or adverbial attributions of properties, the problem of temporary intrinsics, and the various continuity conditions people argue over. Where a distinction from the debate does no work in a museum, say so plainly and drop it rather than including it for completeness.

The deliverable should be a numbered list of questions in order, each with what counts as a sufficient answer to it, and with two kinds of exit clearly marked: the point at which the procedure stops and hands the case to the curator because what remains is a matter of institutional values rather than fact — what a restitution claimant is owed, what the public is being told about the object in the case, how much of a collection's history we are willing to have discontinuous — and the point at which it will deliver an answer we dislike. It also has to live inside our collection-management system, which is old and inflexible: each record can hold one current description, an unlimited list of dated events, and a free-text field for relations to other records; it cannot hold branching or nested objects, and it cannot hold a property without attaching it either to the record as a whole or to one of those dated events. Please then run the procedure on three cases. Case one: a late seventeenth-century marquetry cabinet, its carcass original, whose decorative panels were replaced in the 1840s and again in 2019 after a small fire, so that on our current estimate sixty percent of its visible surface is under a decade old while the structure and the locks are original. Case two: two marble portrait busts from the same workshop, both broken and stored as fragments in the early twentieth century, reassembled in 1974 with some fragments from each used in the other; neither bust can now be restored to its own fragments without destroying the other, and one of them is subject to a pending restitution claim. Case three: a kinetic sculpture accessioned in 1968 whose motor, bearings and control unit have all been replaced by successors to the original fabricator, with the artist's estate issuing a certificate that the work remains the same work, and a variant of this case in which the estate refuses to issue any such certificate. Finally, flag explicitly which of your steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and state what the procedure cannot do: it should not pretend to fix monetary value, to settle legal title, or to resolve cases where the material history is simply unrecorded — which is most of our cases — but it should say what to do in that last situation. Please do not give me references or a reading list; I want something my staff can hold in their hands.
