I'm a third-year PhD student in cognitive science, and my dissertation is on whether visual phenomenology outruns what a subject can access for report and reasoning — the overflow debate. Almost everything in my protocol turns on how I treat introspective material, and my committee is split down the middle about what that material even is. My advisor's view is that a verbal report is a piece of behavior to be explained, and that any method which lets the subject's say-so constrain the theory of consciousness has already conceded the question; one of my other members works in the phenomenological tradition and thinks that a study which refuses to treat the subject's description of her own experience as data about that experience is not studying consciousness at all, only the disposition to talk about it. Before I write the methods chapter and have to defend a particular protocol, I want the specification that sits underneath that disagreement: the list of requirements that any method for bringing first-person material into a science of consciousness has to meet to count as a method at all, rather than a way of collecting anecdotes.

So: give me that list. For each requirement, I want the case or consideration that motivates it, whether it constrains the data-collection procedure itself or the licensed inference from data to theory, and an operational test — something I could actually check against a published protocol to see whether it is met or merely asserted. Make sure the list covers the cases a method has to get right: domains where introspective judgment is systematically unreliable coexisting with domains where subjects' practical self-knowledge is excellent; confidence or visibility ratings that come apart from forced-choice discrimination; the same subject describing an episode differently after a period of introspective training; partial-report situations where far more is retrievable on cue than is spontaneously described in the first pass; reports that conflict across occasions and across labs; and subjects who cannot report at all, whether because of age, species, aphasia, or disorder of consciousness. It should also cover the distinctions a method must not collapse: a report versus the behavior it is embedded in; being wrong about whether one is in a state versus being wrong about the character of the state one is in; failing to report versus failing to experience; the accuracy of a description versus its granularity, since a vague report can be true and a detailed one invented; and the claim about how things seem to the subject versus the claim about what is going on for the subject. Add the constraints a method inherits from being science: whether validation can be non-circular given that there is no third-person ground truth for phenomenality, what stability across experimenters and sessions requires, how reports from different subjects are made commensurable, and what the deliverable's grain is supposed to be when the report is produced hundreds of milliseconds after the episode and reconstructed on the way out.

Then do the sorting, which is the part I actually need. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as the standing prejudice of a particular camp — I suspect some of what each side of my committee calls a demand on any adequate method is really a substantive thesis in disguise, and I'd like to see which ones. Where a subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly and say which member of the set should be surrendered and why; if the list itself is where the result lives rather than in any protocol, that is fine, but show me the derivation rather than the verdict. And tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates, because I expect to be able to run the debate in my chapter off that one. Finish with a paragraph — one paragraph, no more — scoring four options against the list: heterophenomenology as Dennett frames it, neurophenomenology with its second-person interview protocols, descriptive experience sampling of the beep-and-describe variety, and the deflationary option that treats reports as no different in kind from any other behavior and so needs no special method. Please don't include references, a reading list, or attributions with dates; I know the literature and I'm asking you for the criteria, not for a reconstruction of who said what.
