You are a philosopher of statistics who has also spent years brokering structured disagreements between research camps, and I want to put that unusual combination to work. I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a feature about the way single-case predictions get anchored — specifically about a fight that has been running for months inside the small world of people who forecast the performance of big public projects. I have interviewed both sides and neither will say in print what it actually concedes to the other, so I have decided the piece should be built around a document that does not yet exist: the joint statement the two camps would have produced had they sat down for a proper adversarial collaboration. I would like you to draft that statement.

Here are the two camps, as precisely as I can render them. Call the first the base-rate camp. They hold that for any one-off prediction there is a defensible procedure: identify the class of past cases that the case at hand belongs to, take the observed frequency in that class, and treat that number as the starting point from which any inside-view model may adjust only on articulated grounds. They think the familiar failure mode is not bias in the data but bias in the forecaster, who always believes the case in front of her is special. Call the second camp the specificity critics. They hold that no procedure selects the class: every candidate class is a construction that already embeds a causal theory about which respects matter, plus an interest in a particular answer, and so the instruction to start outside quietly assumes the very judgment it was supposed to discipline. They point out that the classical demand for maximal specificity — the narrowest class that is homogeneous with respect to the outcome, in the lineage that runs from Hempel through Salmon, and Reichenbach's problem of the single case behind it — is either unusable, because the homogeneous class often has one member, or indeterminate, because homogeneity is judged relative to a mechanism one already believes in.

To keep this from floating off into the abstract, both camps agreed in principle to argue over one case, which I am giving you as my own composite from the reporting. Treat every figure as stipulated and argue only from it. A mid-sized city is deciding whether to adopt a cordon charge on vehicles entering its centre at peak hours. The transport model built for the city predicts a 15 percent reduction in peak entries. The available classes are: the four cities in the region that actually implemented a comparable charge, where the realised reductions were 8, 12, 17 and 22 percent; the 23 cities that formally proposed such a charge in the same two decades, of which only those four went ahead; a broader class of roughly sixty comparable urban demand-management interventions of similar budget, whose mean effect is 6 percent but which mixes cordon charges with parking reform, bus-priority schemes and one light-rail line; and the narrowest class that is arguably homogeneous on the mechanism the critics care about — cordon charges in cities with a similar river-and-ridge geography, similar transit capacity and similar pre-existing parking supply — which contains exactly one city, whose realised reduction was 13 percent. The wrinkle that I think does the real work, and that I want the document to face rather than dodge, is this: the class of implemented charges was selected on the way in. Cities whose own models predicted small effects, or whose politics turned hostile, never implemented, so the four-case base rate is conditioned on exactly the variable under dispute; and it is not obvious which way the conditioning biases it, because implementation selects both for optimistic predictions, which should inflate observed effects, and for administrative capacity and transit substitutes, which should inflate them for a reason that has nothing to do with optimism.

What I need from you, in order, is the statement itself. First the claims both camps can sign without wincing — including whatever they agree about arithmetic, about selection, and about what a base rate is and is not for. Then the residual disagreements, each written so that the camp holding it would accept your wording for its own position and the opposing camp would accept your wording for the position it is attacking; no straw figures, and no wording so vague that signing it costs nothing. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. For each crux, name the test, dataset, argument or concrete case both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, in numbers where numbers are possible. I would expect at least one crux to concern whether the selection on implementation biases the four-case base rate in a determinate direction at all, and at least one to concern whether there is any principled tie-breaker when two defensible classes disagree and neither can be shown to be causally homogeneous. Finally, tell me which portions of the quarrel you judge to be merely verbal — I suspect the disagreement about whether one can genuinely begin outside may dissolve once the two camps specify whether they are arguing about accuracy or about the justifiability of a number an official will later be held to — and say why, and where the residue of real disagreement lies after the verbal parts are stripped away.

Before you settle on the wording, spend one paragraph thinking out loud on the page about what each camp privately concedes is the strongest point in the other's case; I want that reasoning visible, but I do not want it to grow into the document. Do not adjudicate, do not pick a winner, and do not write this as a dialogue or a script. Do not cite literature, name studies, or supply a reading list; I have no way to check any of it and my readers do not care — work entirely from the case as I have described it.

Format is strict, because this will run as a quoted document inside my piece. Continuous prose only: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold, no italics, no tables, no section breaks. Write it the way one writes a careful letter, signposting the transitions in sentences rather than in typographical structure, so that the agreed claims come first, the disagreements second, the cruxes with their pre-registered predictions third, and the assessment of what is verbal last. Aim for something between twelve hundred and eighteen hundred words, and keep the register formal but readable by an intelligent non-specialist.
