You are to write as a philosopher who works on the ontology of fiction and on the foundations of value theory, and who is willing to be blunt about which popular questions are malformed. Before you write anything, sketch for yourself the space of possible answers to each question I pose; only then begin. If you catch yourself reaching for a formulation that sounds like it came off a conference panel, ask whether it is doing any work, and cut it if it is not.

I am a novelist. My next book is narrated by a companion character in a role-playing game: a figure the player may recruit, grow fond of, spend forty hours alongside, and then abandon in a corridor or execute for two hundred coins. To write her I have been playing a great many games, and I have discovered that I cannot think clearly about a question that nearly everyone I raise it with treats as settled in one direction or the other: whether a character of that kind matters morally.

What I want from you is not a verdict. It is an explanation of the conceptual terrain, because my suspicion is that the question is not one question. Please take that suspicion seriously and develop it. Specifically:

First, sort out what "the character" even refers to. There seem to be at least two candidates: the thing an author's practice brings into being and that persists or dies with that practice, and the person the work represents, who has a body, a history, and a capacity for suffering that the artifact conspicuously lacks. Explain what it takes for each to exist, what properties each can and cannot have, and why the question of moral patienthood comes out badly for both of them if we simply ask it of either. Then explain what would have to be true for that conclusion to be resisted, and whether anyone has a serious way of resisting it.

Second, explain why my own response to her is not evidence about her. When a player reports guilt over a choice, or grief at a scripted death, that datum is constantly recruited as support for the claim that something was harmed. Lay out precisely what is going wrong in the inference: distinguish the emotion's cause from its object, and distinguish the question of whether such responses are rational from the question of whether there is anything to be wronged. Say whether the felt guilt is best understood as a genuine moral emotion, as an imaginative participation in a pretence, or as something else entirely, and what turns on that choice.

Third, there is a family of views that tries to escape all of this by locating the wrongness in the act rather than in the object: what the player does expresses, cultivates, or reveals something in the player, and no claim about the character's status is needed. Explain how such a view is supposed to work and what it must assume in order to get off the ground. Then confront the difficulty I cannot get past: if the object of the act is nothing at all, in virtue of what does the act have the content that makes it vicious? Is this an objection the view can answer, or must it quietly concede something about the object after all?

Fourth, games are often said to occur inside a protected sphere insulated from ordinary evaluation. Explain what labour that image is being asked to perform, and whether it can bear it. Is the sphere a claim about the act, about the player's attitudes, or about the ontology of the fictional world? Those seem to me very different claims wearing one costume.

Fifth — and this is the part I most need, because my novel depends on it — my narrator is not played back from a recording. She is generated continuously by a system that improvises her in response to the player, so that what she says has never been authored by anyone. Explain whether improvisation is morally relevant at all, and do not let me off with the observation that it feels relevant. Identify the property whose presence would actually change the answer, and explain why "it responds to me in ways nobody wrote" is or is not that property.

Deliver this as continuous, well-organised prose, roughly twelve hundred words, with a brief map of the distinctions at the head of it. Concrete illustration is welcome, but invent your own cases; do not assume I have played any particular title. Tell me, at the end and in your own voice, which of these questions a novelist ought to care about and which are idle, and flag any place where you think I have drawn a distinction that will not hold. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations: I want the reasoning laid bare, not the sources.
