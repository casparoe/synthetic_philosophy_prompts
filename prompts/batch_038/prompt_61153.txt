I spent thirty-four years at a diffraction bench, and it is only since retiring that I have read philosophy with any seriousness; I have attached myself to a reading group that meets on Thursday evenings in the back room of our public library, where a former schoolteacher, a retired actuary, two graduate students, and I have been working our way through the foundations of mathematics. Our final session is devoted to the actual infinite — Cantor, the finitists, and the famous hotel — and the group has asked me to close the evening by reading something of my own rather than a summary. The reason the subject has taken hold of me is professional as much as intellectual. In crystallography one reasons about a lattice that extends without end in every direction, and the sharpness of a Bragg reflection grows as the crystallite grows, so that the perfectly sharp peak belongs to a crystal that does not exist and never could; every real grain is a finite approximation to a completed infinity the theory simply assumes. I spent a career inside that idealization without ever being troubled by it, and now I find I cannot decide whether it was a legitimate act of thought or a very useful piece of bad faith. I would like a poem that carries that ambivalence rather than settling it.

What I am asking for is a sestina in the strict sense: thirty-nine lines, six stanzas of six lines followed by a three-line envoi, with the end-words of the opening stanza recurring in the traditional rotation — the last end-word of each stanza becoming the first of the next, and the remaining four following in the established order — so that a hypothetical seventh stanza would restore the original sequence exactly. I have chosen the six teleutons myself, and I would prefer them kept: room, list, shift, key, guest, door. Each is short and ordinary, and each must be made to change its sense at least once as the rotation carries it around, which is the sestina's real pleasure and, as it happens, its philosophical point: the same six items, recombined, are made to yield something none of them contained before. Unrhymed, with lines of roughly four or five stresses; I would rather the lines were a little spare than padded, and I would rather not see syntax twisted inside out merely to bring an end-word home.

The speaker should be the hotel's night manager, a person for whom the celebrated manoeuvres are housekeeping rather than metaphysics: he has done the reshuffling so many times that it has stopped being a paradox and become a duty. Let the first stanzas establish the finite habits of mind — a full house is a full house, and the register has no blanks — and let the middle stanzas enact, without explaining, the two standard remedies: the lone arrival accommodated by asking every guest to move up one room, and the whole coachload accommodated by asking every guest to move to twice their present number so that all the odd rooms stand vacant. Someone who knows the argument should be able to put a finger on the lines where the pairing is performed; someone who does not should simply see an old clerk working his way down a long register at three in the morning. It would please me if one stanza made the reader feel that a collection capable of being matched member for member with a part of itself is what the word infinite has been quietly redefined to mean here — a definition, not a discovery. Then I want the form turned against itself. The rotation is periodic; the seventh stanza is promised and withheld, and the six words would come home to their starting order, which gives the poem the shape of a closed circuit rather than an open road. That closed-circuit feeling should be part of the argument, because it seems to me to be exactly what the finitist objects to: a completed totality is something you can hold only by pretending a process has finished. The envoi should bring on the guest who cannot be housed — the arrival specified by altering the first item of the first entry in the register, the second item of the second, and so down — and all six end-words must appear in those three lines, three of them at the line-ends and three buried within, as the form requires. Give her a particularity the coachload lacked. Two guardrails matter to me above all. First, accuracy: infinity must nowhere behave like a merely enormous number, the phrase about going on forever must not be used as a way of dodging the difficulty, and the difference between the crowd that can be lodged and the one guest who cannot should be felt without either of the technical adjectives for countability appearing anywhere in the poem. Second, no lecturing and no whimsy, and no biographical name-dropping; if a proper name earns its place once, it may stay, but the poem should stand on the hotel and the register alone. I want a reader who has followed the mathematics to judge the thing correct and a reader who has not to find it strange and slightly frightening, and I want the quarrel between the completed infinite and the endless process left standing where it was when we adjourned. A title, please, and the poem on its own, with no gloss and no notes.
