You are a formal epistemologist who has spent a career on the diachronic norms of belief — on how an agent's present degrees of belief ought to be constrained by the degrees of belief they expect to hold at some later time — and you are equally at home doing the reverse-engineering work of extracting an implicit decision rule from a line of case-by-case rulings, the way a lawyer reads precedent. Please think carefully and brainstorm before committing: list every candidate general rule the record below would tolerate, including ones that strike you as unlikely, and only then test each against the new case. I do not want a literature survey or references; nothing here requires looking anything up, and I want the analysis rather than the bibliography.

I run calibration for a twelve-person forecasting unit that supplies probability estimates to an infrastructure lender. Our chief forecaster — I will call her V. — has final authority over whether an analyst may revise a logged number, and her written rulings over three years have converged on a recurring question: what an analyst should do when they know their own credence is about to change. Her rulings, in my paraphrase:

1. An analyst at 0.5 that a permit challenge would fail was certain to learn the ruling the next morning, after which her credence would be either 0.95 or 0.05. V. ruled that her present credence, conditional on her future credence being 0.95, must be 0.95, and likewise for 0.05 — but that the unconditional number on the log stays at 0.5, since what the log tracks is the weighted mixture, with weights given by her present chances of landing in each state.
2. An analyst at 0.5 that a borrower would hit its commissioning date was about to spend six weeks living on the borrower's site. She said no new information would reach her — she had read everything there was to read — but that rapport and familiarity would take her to 0.9. V. ruled she must hold 0.5, and that before departing she was to write a memo to her future self setting out the reasoning behind 0.5 and instructing that self to treat the expected rise as noise.
3. An analyst would be thirty hours into a shift when a go/no-go call came up; she predicted that at that hour she would be at 0.85 that a shipment would slip, against a present 0.5. V. ruled that the fatigued judgment carried no authority at all: the call was to be made by a rule written and logged in advance, which the on-shift analyst merely executed.
4. An analyst at 0.55 that she would still want to be leading a two-year project was to spend a month working inside that team, after which she said she would simply know, one way or the other. V. permitted her to move her number in advance of the month, to the extent she was confident what it would become, and remarked that a question about one's own future attitudes is not the same kind of question as one about a permit or a shipment.
5. An analyst who had already decided, on grounds V. accepted as sound, to recommend against a loan planned to spend a weekend reading a deliberately one-sided dossier in order to harden her resolve. V. ruled the reading permissible, the resulting credence owed no deference now, and every document in the dossier to be logged so that at a later review the inputs could be reconstructed and whatever proved probative credited then.
6. An analyst expected an off-site to leave her at either 0.9 or 0.1 on a contested demand forecast. V. ruled that her present number must fall inside the range spanned by those two, and more precisely must equal their weighted average.
7. An analyst proposed to record as her own number the figure she expected a cross-departmental committee she sat on to converge on in a fortnight. V. ruled that this was not a case about an agent's relation to her own later opinion at all but about testimony from other people, and referred it elsewhere in the handbook.

Thursday's case is this. An analyst is to be seconded for eight weeks to a supplier whose entire business rests on a thesis our unit disputes: that a certain storage technology will be cost-competitive at grid scale within six years. She is at 0.25. Her self-forecasts have been well calibrated in the past, so the following are treated as settled for the ruling. She will receive no data bearing on the thesis — the secondment is on logistics — and she has already read everything relevant. She is nevertheless confident that by week eight she will be at 0.7, and she expects to experience the change as understanding rather than as persuasion: she says she will finally grasp from the inside the deployment constraints she now knows only on paper. She will be able to say exactly which briefings, site visits, and conversations did the work, because the supplier keeps a complete log of everything she is shown. She will retain her present reasoning in full detail, regards her present commitments as binding on her, and would honour at week eight any bet she writes now. Two alternatives are formally on the table: she can decline the secondment, or she can have her 0.25 locked into the record so that moving it requires an external reviewer's sign-off. And the secondment carries a second, separable question: at week eight she will be asked whether she wants the unit to continue covering the technology at all, which is a question about her own future preferences rather than about the world.

Please give me: (a) your prediction of V.'s Thursday ruling, stated as precisely as her rulings are — both the number she will require on the log and any procedural orders she will attach; (b) an explicit list of the candidate general rules consistent with all seven precedents, noting for each which precedents it explains, which it strains, and whether it is a complete rule or only a component that must be paired with something else; (c) what each surviving rule predicts for Thursday, marking where they genuinely diverge and where they merely sound different while landing on the same number; (d) your best single guess and the reasons for preferring it, including which precedent you think is doing most of the work and whether you expect V. to split her ruling between the factual question and the question about her own future preferences; and (e) the one further case I could put on her docket that would most cleanly separate the rules you cannot yet tell apart. Finally, if you conclude that the seven precedents genuinely underdetermine Thursday's number, say which principle the unit would be adopting by ruling each way — whatever she writes on Thursday becomes binding precedent for the next four years, and I would rather we chose it deliberately than inherited it by accident.
