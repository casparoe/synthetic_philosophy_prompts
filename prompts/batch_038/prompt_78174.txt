I chair the safety and technology committee of a regional blood service. We meet once a month for three hours and vote on proposals of a single recurring shape: a certain cost or a certain loss of supply now, against a small chance of a catastrophic failure later. We presently rank these by expected value and then argue informally about the tail, which leaves the argument unrecorded and unwinnable. Our regulator has asked for an explicit criterion, and I want to adopt risk-weighted expected utility: order each proposal's outcomes worst to best, then weight each step up not by the probability of that outcome but by a strictly increasing weighting function w applied to the cumulative chance of doing at least that well, with w(0)=0 and w(1)=1 (a convex w is the risk-averse case, and reduces to expected value when w is the identity). Our lay trustee board sets w, and it can answer only comparative questions of the form "would you accept a doubling of the chance that a contaminated batch reaches patients in order to preserve the whole reserve?" — never a functional form.

Write the procedure this committee can run, in ordered steps, with arithmetic our two clinical members could do in a spreadsheet. It must cover: how to get from the board's comparative answers to a usable w, and what to do when two defensible w's rank a proposal differently; how to treat a tail probability we can only bound by an order of magnitude; whether proposals decided in the same month may be scored one at a time or must be scored as a portfolio; and how the procedure handles a staged proposal with a review point at which our own preferences will have shifted. Mark plainly which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure.

Then run it on three proposals from a single agenda: (a) pathogen reduction on the platelet line — an extra €40 per unit and discards up 12 points, removing a residual contamination risk we estimate at 1 in 40,000 units issued with roughly one death per event, rolled out over two sites with a go/no-go review at month six; (b) closing one of two collection sites for the winter — saves €1.9m a year, with a 1-in-200 chance per season of a single-point failure that would leave an estimated 30 patients critically undersupplied; (c) a cheaper donor-screening assay whose dominant failure mode is a correlated class of false negatives we can bound only between 1 in 10^4 and 1 in 10^6. Note that (a) and (b) both draw on the same platelet supply. At least one result should go against our budget office; if none does, say so and explain why not. Finish with what the procedure cannot settle.

End with a single line of the form Verdict: (a) adopt/reject/escalate — EV winner changed or unchanged; (b) …; (c) …
