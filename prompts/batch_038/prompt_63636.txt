I am preparing a rendering of a short poem for a verse-translation workshop, and what I need is conceptual housekeeping rather than criticism of my drafts.

The argument that concerns me began the week we read Hofstadter's book on translation, the one built around a single tiny poem by Marot — twenty-eight lines, three syllables apiece, in rhyming couplets — presented again and again in a great many different English guises. Our convenor took the book to show that a poem survives the loss of its form. I took it to show the opposite. Neither of us can cite a page, and I am not asking for one. I am asking about a phrase that has been settling our disputes for a month: when either of us is losing, we say that a translator must be faithful to the spirit, not the letter.

The poem I am working on is sixteen lines of exactly six syllables, all sixteen on a single rhyme, closing on the word with which it opens. Its subject is comic — a man who cannot get a tune out of his head. The source language has a generous rhyme inventory, so the monorhyme barely registers there as a stunt; in English it reads as a trick by the fourth couplet. My constrained version was rejected with that phrase, and I was told to begin again. The looser version I then produced was praised, and I am still not persuaded it is the same poem. A fortnight later the same convenor invoked the identical phrase in the opposite direction, to insist that I retain a refrain I had wanted to cut as untranslatable.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that faithful to the spirit, not the letter might express in the context of formally constrained verse? For each reading, specify what the letter is taken to cover (lexical meaning, syntax, metre, rhyme, line length, stanza shape, the framing device of the repeated word), what the spirit is taken to cover (the poet's intention, the effect on a competent reader of the original, the norms of the genre, the poem's overall aesthetic character), whether the thesis is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it licenses the convenor's first move, his second move, both, or neither.

Then tell me which of these readings, if any, could serve as a decision procedure — something that would have settled the monorhyme question in advance — as opposed to a label applied afterwards to whatever one already wanted. Finally, the phrase has plainly migrated from statutory interpretation: assess briefly whether the distinctions that keep it disciplined there transfer to translation, or whether the legal pedigree is doing no work at all.

Please write in British English.
