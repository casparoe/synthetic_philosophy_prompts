My advisor is writing a thing on original vs derived intentionality — basically Searle's complaint against Dennett — and in seminar she's been handing out verdicts on whether various systems have genuine, underived aboutness. Here's the running list so far:

1. Dennett's wandering two-bitser — the coin checker built to spot quarters that gets shipped to Panama and can't tell a quarter from a quarter-balboa: **no**. Its states are about quarters only because we read them that way.
2. A frog's retinal ganglion cell firing at small dark moving specks: **yes**, really about flies.
3. That same retina, kept alive in a dish down the hall, firing at a dot on a screen and hooked up to absolutely nothing: **still about flies**. She wont budge on this one.
4. Tree rings as a record of past climate: **no**.
5. A bacterium steering up a glucose gradient: **yes**.
6. A large language model: **no**.
7. A chess engine that only ever gets positions in notation, no sensors at all: **no**.
8. A robot vacuum that builds its own map of the apartment by bumping into the furniture — nobody wrote that map, it learned it: **no**.
9. My smartwatch's heart-rate sensor, where the watch itself uses the reading to decide when to buzz me: **no**, derived just like the coin checker.
10. A net trained by reinforcement learning to catch falling pixels in a Breakout-ish game: **no**.

The case she hasn't ruled on is the one our artificial life group is bringing her next week. It's a digital-organism sim: strings in a toy chemistry, maybe 200k generations, and the only thing anyone specified was that they should replicate — no fitness function, no task, nobody told anything what to track. One lineage evolved a sensor whose internal state covaries tightly with the local concentration of the resource they're competing over, and when you scramble that sensor the organisms starve. Nothing outside the sim reads those states; there's no readout and no application, and the grant runs out in June so the whole thing probably gets deleted. Does that sensor state have original aboutness, or is it derived from us like the quarter detector?

What I want is your best guess at her verdict, and then the rule you think she's actually running. Don't just say biological naturalism or teleosemantics — state the rule as a test someone could apply to a system she's never seen, and show which of the ten verdicts above knock out which candidate rules. Then give me at least two other rules that fit every one of those ten just as well as yours does, and say what each of them predicts about the sim. And if the sim turns out not to discriminate between the survivors, name the case that would — something we could actually describe to her in a paragraph.

One thing I'd like you to press on: if she says no to the sim, she owes us an account of what a molecule-for-molecule silicon duplicate of the retina-in-a-dish would be missing. And if she says yes, she owes us an account of why the Roomba and the RL agent are still no, given that gradient descent is arguably a selection process too. Is that a difference in kind, or just a difference in how many generations we ran?
