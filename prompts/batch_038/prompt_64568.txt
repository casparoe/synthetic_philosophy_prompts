In September I became the calibration lead for a twelve-person forecasting group that enters long-horizon prediction tournaments. Among other duties, I draft and defend the protocol that governs how members report and revise their credences: members are scored with a strictly proper scoring rule on whatever number they post when a round opens, and the round's news flow — agency briefings, market moves, satellite imagery, retractions — arrives after posting.

At our protocol review last Thursday two senior members, Nadine and Elias, argued for forty minutes over a rule Nadine wants to add. I was asked to referee and to write the recommendation. I could not settle it, and I need an adjudication I can defend at Friday's meeting.

The proposed rule: at the opening of a round, a member must post not what they currently believe but their expectation of what they will believe at the close of the round, on the assumption that the intervening change comes from news they expect to receive. The exchange went roughly as follows.

1. Nadine: this is an instance of a general requirement of rationality concerning one's own future beliefs — if you now expect that your later credence in some claim will be x, and you expect the change to be produced by information you do not yet have, then your present credence in that claim, given that expectation, should already be x. Her support: whoever flouts this can be made a guaranteed loser. A bookie buys from you today, at a price matching your current credence, a ticket that pays out if the claim turns out true; tomorrow, having by then absorbed the briefings, you buy the same ticket back at a price matching the credence you expected to have. The payoffs cancel, the bookie pockets the gap, and you are poorer however the claim resolves (with the mirror-image pair of trades available if your expected later credence is lower than your present one). A sure loss is the signature of incoherence.

2. Elias: that guarantee is an artifact of how the deals are handed over. The vulnerability exists only because the trades are offered at different times, at different information states, with no view of the menu as a whole. Feed bets one at a time to a forecaster who updates flawlessly by conditioning on what she learns, and let the bookie see the same news she does, and you can pump her just as dry — she will rationally accept each trade when it is offered, and jointly lose. If sequential offering is enough to convict someone of incoherence, the concept of incoherence has stopped doing any work.

3. Nadine: the Dutch book is the surface argument; the substance is about planning. Suppose you expect to be moved by evidence you have not yet seen, and decline to be moved now. You are adopting a plan whose later stages you already regard as responsive to reasons, while refusing to be responsive to those same reasons in advance. Deliberately arranging to disregard later what you now concede will be your reasons is irrational, and the sure-loss result is only a symptom. There is also a clean derivation available: if you expect to update by conditioning on what you learn, the requirement falls out for free, since your present credence is already the expectation of your later, conditionalized credence. It is not an extra postulate bolted onto Bayesianism.

4. Elias: which shows where the force actually comes from, and it is conditionalization, not reflection. Not every rational change of belief is conditioning on new evidence. Expect to forget which of two nearly identical reports you have already read, and your later credence will legitimately diverge from what conditioning predicts. Expect to be posting numbers at the end of a thirty-hour shift, or on a medication you know flattens your judgment, and your later self is worse informed or worse at reasoning than your present self. In those cases the rational thing is to defer backwards, to the self that currently knows more — the precise opposite of what Nadine's rule commands.

5. Nadine: those are not counterexamples, they fall outside the rule's scope. The requirement is explicitly restricted to cases in which you expect the change to be driven by information you lack. Forgetting, fatigue, and sedation remove the antecedent and leave the principle untouched.

6. Elias: then for our purposes it is either empty or circular. The only way to determine whether an expected change is information-driven is to have already worked out what the information supports — that is, to have already performed the update. A rule applicable only retrospectively disciplines no one's deliberation. And in the cases where it does bite, it instructs a member to post a number she currently believes to be false, to a scoring rule that measures the accuracy of what she actually believes. That is the one thing a calibration protocol should never require.

Nadine's last word was that expecting to arrive at 0.7 while sitting at 0.5 is not a stable pair of attitudes to hold; Elias's was that forecasting your own psychology is not the same act as ruling on the evidence. Neither budged.

Please adjudicate the exchange as it actually unfolded, move by move, rather than summarizing the dialectic. I want committed judgments, not a balanced digest: where you think one of them is simply wrong, say so and say why, and where a point is genuinely contested among people who work on this, tell me which side you would bet on and roughly how strongly. No citations and no reading list — I want the reasoning, not the references.
