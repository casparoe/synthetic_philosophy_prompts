I'm slogging through Leviathan chs. 14–15 and stuck on the bit about covenants where neither side performs right away. Hobbes's claim, roughly as I read it: if neither party performs presently but each trusts the other, then in the condition of mere nature the covenant is void upon any reasonable suspicion of the other's non-performance, while where there's a common power over both with force enough to compel performance it isn't void. Then a bit later he says the laws of nature bind in conscience always but in effect only where there's security.

Help me figure out what work "void" is doing there. I've been sorting accounts of why promises bind into two buckets — call them transfer views (promising alienates a right you had, so breaking is a kind of contradiction with your own prior act) and reliance views (promising binds because it licenses the other person to rely and act on it, so breaking takes back what they built on it). Neither seems to fit Hobbes cleanly: he starts from transfer-of-right language, but then makes the covenant's force turn on whether performance can be compelled. So is the sword part of what makes a promise obligatory for him, or just an enforcement add-on to an obligation that's already fully there? And is "reasonable suspicion" doing moral work or merely prudential work — is the suspicious covenant not really binding, or is performing it just foolish?

Also, please flag explicitly where Hobbes scholars actually disagree about this versus where it's basically settled; I don't want a contested reading dressed up as the standard view.
