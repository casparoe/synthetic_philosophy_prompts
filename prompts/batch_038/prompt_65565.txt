I am putting together seminar materials on the use of unrealistic assumptions in scientific modelling, and I have found that the general debate does not get me to a settled view of my own. I know the instrumentalist thesis — that a model is to be judged by the accuracy of its predictions for the purpose at hand, that its assumptions may be wildly false, and indeed that the more significant a theory is, the more unrealistic its assumptions tend to be — a thesis Samuelson named the F-twist, declining out of courtesy to call it the F-fallacy. I also know the standard rejoinder: that a false premise cannot be doing any work in explaining a true prediction, so what is really required is a showing that the idealised factor makes no relevant difference. Each position seems to me to capture half of my own case-by-case judgements and to lose the other half, so rather than another rehearsal of the argument I should like to know what principle my verdicts actually add up to. Here they are.

A first-year mechanics problem in which a block slides down a frictionless incline: acceptable; adding friction would make it more realistic and, for the purpose of teaching the resolution of forces, worse. Planets treated as point masses under Newtonian gravitation: acceptable, and barely an idealisation at all, since a spherically symmetric body attracts anything outside it exactly as though its mass were concentrated at its centre — here the assumption is not false but restricted, and I want a principle able to register that difference. Ignoring the mutual perturbations of the planets to get a first approximation to an orbit: acceptable, because the errors are not merely small but are computed and subtracted by the same theory, and it is the success of the perturbation calculation that earns my trust in the starting point. The ideal gas law, neglecting molecular volume and intermolecular attraction: acceptable, because the deviation is orderly and signed, admits a two-parameter correction, and tells me in advance which way the model errs and when. A lattice of nearest-neighbour spins standing in for a real ferromagnet: acceptable near the critical point, where the microscopic details are demonstrably washed out and quite different models share the same exponents. The thermodynamic limit — infinitely many particles — in order to obtain a genuine phase transition: acceptable, even though no real system is infinite and a finite one has no true singularity, because finite-size scaling shows me the way back. Galileo's falling bodies with air resistance neglected: acceptable; indeed dropping a hammer and a feather together on the Moon seems to me to have confirmed the idealisation rather than exposed it. The Earth treated as a perfect sphere: acceptable for working out great-circle distances between cities, unacceptable for predicting the precession of a satellite's orbital plane, where the equatorial bulge is precisely the effect in question — same idealisation, opposite verdicts, and the only apparent difference is the question asked. The independence of irrelevant alternatives in the impossibility theorem about social choice, a condition no real voting procedure satisfies: acceptable, because the aim is not to predict the behaviour of parliaments but to display a structural feature of a class of aggregation rules, and the idealisation is what makes the structure visible. The Black–Scholes assumption of log-normal returns with constant volatility: unacceptable, notwithstanding that it prices liquid options well, that everyone knows the tails are too thin and that volatility clusters, and that there is a whole family of corrections available in the form of jumps, stochastic volatility and local volatility implied by the smile. My unease is that those corrections are fitted to the very option prices the model was supposed to explain, so the deviation is never measured against anything independent, and that the model fails hardest precisely where it is most relied upon. The representative agent in macroeconomics: unacceptable, because aggregation is where the interesting behaviour lives, and because results about aggregate excess demand suggest that almost any smooth aggregate function can be rationalised by some distribution of well-behaved individuals, so the individual-level assumptions purchase nothing at the level where the predictions are made. The assumption of no unmeasured confounding in an observational study in epidemiology: unacceptable, and for a third reason — it is not merely false but untestable in principle from the data available, and if it fails it can reverse the causal conclusion while leaving every prediction intact. And the spherical cow: unacceptable, though I want the principle to explain why that is a joke and the frictionless incline is physics, given that the two are formally indistinguishable.

My own tentative formulation is that an idealisation is licensed when, and only when, the modeller has available an argument — mathematical, experimental, or a matter of demonstrated robustness — that the factor omitted or falsified makes no relevant difference to the answer to the question actually being asked, with a merely provisional licence where that argument has not yet been given. Three things worry me about it. First, the ideal gas law and the option-pricing model look structurally identical — a false distributional assumption, good central predictions, a known and systematic correction — yet I judge them differently, and what seems to drive the difference is whether the correction is anchored to something independent of the data being fitted, and whether failure coincides with reliance; I should like to know whether those are two further clauses, one clause, or nothing at all. Second, the principle appears to license idealisations only retrospectively: Galileo had no demonstration about air resistance, the correction to the ideal gas law arrived decades after the law was trusted, and the assumption of molecular chaos was used long before anyone could say why it was permissible. Either most of the great idealisations were illegitimate when made, or my provisional clause is doing all the work — and if it is, it looks suspiciously like permission to assume whatever has not yet been shown to matter, which is the F-twist with a guilty conscience. Third, if legitimacy is relative to the question asked, and the modeller chooses the question, what prevents the principle from licensing any idealisation whatsoever for a suitably chosen question? So: state the principle, or two or three rival candidates, precisely and clause by clause, saying which of my cases motivates each clause and what would undermine it; apply it to at least three cases I have not listed, one of them from outside physics and economics; tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot reproduce, and rank those verdicts by how costly they would be to abandon — I suspect I shall have to give up either my acceptance of the ideal gas law or my rejection of the option-pricing model, and I want to know which surrender is cheaper and what else it would cost me; and finally say whether the resulting principle is materially different from judging models by their predictions alone. If it is, describe a concrete situation in which the two come apart — a model my principle condemns though it predicts well, or licenses though it predicts badly. Please argue from the cases themselves rather than giving me a survey of the literature or a reading list.
