Been arguing with my study group about Good's theorem — the result that if you're a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, free information can never be expected to make you worse off, since the average of the maxima is at least the maximum of the averages. Half the group thinks that's a deep fact about rationality. The other half thinks it's bookkeeping: the assumptions get restated with the word "theorem" glued on, and every counterexample just negates one of them. I want an essay that actually settles which camp is right.

To keep it concrete, here are the two cases we've been circling. Neither involves paying for information, since everyone agrees that's boring.

Case 1. A prize worth 10 utils sits behind one of two doors, you have no clue which, and you pick tomorrow. Tonight you're offered a free, truthful note saying which door it is. Stipulate though that your utility function has a suspense term in it: a flat +6 utils for being at credence 0.5 on a live question that gets resolved tomorrow, and zero of that once you know the answer. Refuse the note: 5 expected utils from the prize plus 6 from suspense = 11. Take it: 10 utils from the prize, no suspense = 10. So you should turn down free truthful information, and the only thing wrong with you is that your utility depends on your own credences — which is exactly what the theorem assumed away.

Case 2. The proof needs the law of total probability, Cr(X) = Σ Cr(X|Ek)·Cr(Ek). An agent whose credences are merely finitely additive can violate that: for some countable partition, her conditional credence in X comes out lower than her unconditional credence in X on every single cell. Condionalizing then drags her away from X no matter which cell she lands in. Give her a bet riding on X and a free report of which cell obtains looks like something she should decline.

So: write the essay, and answer the question of whether the theorem is doing real normative work or whether it's empty. Take a side. I don't want a both-sides tour that ends on "it depends what you mean by substantive" — commit and argue for it. Say which of the two cases you think is more damaging and why; say whether the standard repairs (assume utilities don't depend on beliefs, assume countable additivity) are genuine repairs or just relabelling the problem; and say what, if anything, is left of the slogan that you ought always to conditionalize on your total evidence. Also flag the step in your own argument you think is shakiest — I'd rather know where you're on thin ice than read something confidently wrong. And if you think either of my cases is misdescribed or my arithmetic is off, say so and fix it, but still answer the question.
