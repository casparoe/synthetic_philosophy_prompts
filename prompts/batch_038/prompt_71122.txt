I have been reading around the ethics of asylum over the past few weeks, in preparation for a tutorial essay, and the person who has shaped my thinking most is an old friend of mine — a former immigration caseworker who reads political theory avidly, argues with great confidence, and is rather given to summarising whole positions over dinner. Last month he gave me what he insisted was a faithful account of Michael Walzer's argument in the chapter on membership in *Spheres of Justice*, together with what he called the legal reality that makes most of the philosophy academic. I found it persuasive at the time, and I have been building on it since. A reading set this week left me uneasy, though: some of what he said fits with what I half-remember of Walzer, and some of it sits badly, and I cannot tell which is which. I should confess at the outset that I am temperamentally on the cosmopolitan side of this debate, so I have an obvious motive to be told that he is wrong; please do not humour me on that account. Here is his summary, as near to his own words as I can reproduce it.

'Walzer's starting point is that a political community is above all a distributive community. The first good it distributes is membership, and the reason admission matters so much is not that movement is intrinsically valuable but that everything else — the welfare arrangements, the language of public life, the sense of who owes what to whom — depends on who belongs. His master analogy is the members' club. A club decides whom to admit; no outsider has a claim to be let in; and that is not selfishness but a condition of the club being anything in particular. A state is the same, and the argument has nothing to do with property or territory, only with self-determination. From this he concludes that a state may in principle close its borders, even to people fleeing persecution, because the members' interest in reproducing their own common life outweighs whatever outsiders can claim. What is left over is a residual duty of mutual aid: help where you can, up to limits the community sets for itself. But that is charity rather than justice, which is why the familiar objection — that a state which helped produce the crisis, through its wars or its arms sales or the borders it once drew, must take in the people who fled it — never really bites on Walzer's own premises. At most it makes for a stronger case for generosity, and a state can always answer that the causal chain is too long to trace. The one concession he makes concerns people already inside. A state that has admitted guestworkers cannot simply ship them home once they stop being useful, and long residence ripens into a claim to membership — but he makes that concession prudentially, because mass expulsion would be politically and administratively impossible, not because the residents have acquired any right. And in any case the law has made most of this academic. The 1951 Convention defines a refugee as anyone who has fled their country because of persecution or war, and its central obligation, non-refoulement, forbids a signatory from sending such a person back. So once someone has arrived and cannot be returned, the question of who must take them in has been settled by treaty rather than left to the community's discretion, and what remains is only a question of numbers: how many a state can absorb without damaging the common life Walzer is so concerned about. That is why the interesting disputes are all about integration capacity, and why the communitarian wins the philosophical argument by default.'

Could you go through this claim by claim and tell me how much of it I can trust? For each, I want it sorted into what Walzer would accept as stated, what is a defensible compression that loses something important, and what he would positively reject — and where he would reject it, what he says instead. Two things in particular are nagging at me. First, whether the analogy my friend treats as the load-bearing beam of the argument is really doing that work, or whether Walzer uses it illustratively alongside something else that the summary quietly drops. Second, whether the summary ever reaches a conclusion Walzer holds by a route he would refuse — that is, places where the answer is right but the reason attributed to him is wrong, which for my purposes is just as damaging as getting the answer wrong. Please also separate out the legal claims and handle them on their own terms: what the Convention's definition actually requires, who it therefore leaves outside the category, and precisely what non-refoulement settles as against what it leaves wide open — most obviously, whether it tells us anything at all about *which* state owes protection to a person who has arrived, or about the people who never reach a border in the first place. Finally, tell me whether his closing move survives the corrections: even once the summary is repaired, does it follow that the philosophical question is settled by treaty and that the communitarian wins by default? I would like to finish with a short list of the parts of his account I can carry into my essay with confidence, and the parts I should strike out altogether.
