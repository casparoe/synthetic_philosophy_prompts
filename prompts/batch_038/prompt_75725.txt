I do quality-manual work for a small accredited calibration lab, and I've been asked to rewrite the section of our manual that justifies why our certificates are worth anything. The rules we operate under are strict in a way I find philosophically interesting. Every instrument we return to a customer has to come with a certificate showing an unbroken chain of comparisons from that instrument up to a national standard; an auditor will reject a certificate whose reference standard is not itself traceable further up. We are not permitted to establish the accuracy of a device by comparing it against itself, and we treat a "calibration" in which the reference and the item under test are the same instrument as worthless, whatever the numbers look like. We are also expected to use a reference whose stated uncertainty is comfortably smaller than the tolerance we are certifying to, so that the chain degrades only gradually on the way down. The implicit message seems to be that the reliability of a measuring source can never be established by the deliverances of that source, and that a source only earns credence by standing in a relation to something already known to be better.

But then I read up on what happens at the top of the chain and it looks to me as though the same institution does the opposite there. Since the base units were redefined in terms of fixed fundamental constants, there is no longer an artifact to appeal to; the unit is a definition, and each national institute has to realize it experimentally. The institutes then periodically compare their realizations of a given unit against one another, and the value each one's result is judged against is computed from the participants' own results — roughly, a weighted average of the group, with each lab's weight reflecting the uncertainty it has declared for itself. On the strength of that mutual comparison each institute publishes the calibration and measurement capabilities it claims, and accreditation bodies and regulators around the world accept those claims as authoritative. So the top of the chain, which is supposed to supply the independent warrant everything below it borrows, appears to be warranted by the agreement of the very sources whose reliability is in question — and each of those sources contributes to setting the standard by which it is itself measured, which is precisely the move we forbid our own technicians from making.

Can you reconstruct the implicit epistemology here? I'd like to know what the lower part of the practice strictly presupposes about how knowledge of a source's reliability can be acquired — it looks like a blanket prohibition on pulling reliability out of the source itself, on the grounds that such an inference would make warranted belief far too easy to come by — and whether the upper part genuinely violates that prohibition or whether something about its structure changes the kind of inference it is: several sources rather than one, no source having authority over the value it is compared to, uncertainty entering as a weight, and so on. Distinguish what the practice cannot do without from what it merely sits comfortably with, since I suspect several incompatible theories would license the same rules; tell me which rival accounts of evidence or of warrant the lower rules quietly rule out; and say whether the two halves can be made consistent, or whether the whole edifice is an instance of the problem of the criterion wearing a laboratory coat. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change — in the manual, in the audits, in the international comparisons — if we adopted the opposite view, that a source's reliability can only be established by evidence independent of that source, and separately if we adopted the view that it can be established from within. Please be explicit about where you are reporting settled ground and where you are taking a side in something people who work on this genuinely disagree about; I don't want a contested position handed to me as though it were the consensus.
