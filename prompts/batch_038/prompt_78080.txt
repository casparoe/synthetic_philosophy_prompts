I am a third-year PhD student in metaphysics, and I have notes for a paper I intend to present at a graduate workshop in the spring. I would be grateful for critical comments rather than encouragement, and I am not looking for bibliographic suggestions: I want to know whether the central argument holds together and whether it is genuinely distinct from positions already on the table. Below is the paper as it currently stands, followed by the specific points on which I want your assessment.

**Working title:** Closure Without Termination: Grounding Fixed Points and the Ungrounded Base

**Notes and argument:**

1. Grounding is standardly taken to be a strict partial order, and foundationalism about grounding adds well-foundedness: no infinite descending chains, so that the fundamental just is whatever has no ground. Infinitists deny well-foundedness; the debate is normally conducted as though that single issue settled the fate of fundamentality.

2. I want to distinguish two properties a grounding structure can have. *Termination*: every descending chain ends in an entity with no ground. *Closure*: every entity has grounds, and the structure suffices to fix determinate identities, truth-values, and explanatory contents throughout, however far the chains run.

3. My central claim is that the arguments for well-foundedness are arguments for closure that have been misread as arguments for termination. The desiderata invoked against infinite descent — no vicious circularity, no explanatory failure, no threat to determinacy — are conditions on how much work a grounding structure does, not on how long its chains are. If so, foundationalists have been defending the wrong property, and their infinitist opponents have been conceding too much by accepting that the property in question is termination.

4. I then argue that closure can be had without termination, by structures I call *fixed points* of the grounding operator: configurations in which nothing is ungrounded, some descending chains are infinite, and yet determinacy is generated by the structure taken as a whole rather than by any terminal base.

5. My motivating case is an ontology of pure powers, on which each dispositional property's identity is fixed only by its relations to others. The familiar objection is that this fails to make the identities of powers determinate; the most promising reply treats the whole network, not any individual node, as doing the determining. I claim that the natural development of that reply yields exactly a fixed point: every node is grounded, none is ungrounded, and the structure is nonetheless complete.

**What I would like you to comment on:**

1. Is termination/closure a distinction with real theoretical work to do, or is it a rebranding of the distinction between being ungrounded and being the ground of everything else? If the latter, my fixed point looks like priority monism with an infinitely descending base, and the paper has no independent thesis.

2. There is an obvious dilemma I cannot yet escape. Irreflexivity together with transitivity rules out loops: if a grounds b and b grounds a, transitivity yields a grounds a. So either my fixed point is a genuine loop, in which case I must abandon transitivity, which I do not want to do, or it is simply an infinite descending chain, in which case it is infinitism under a new name and the label adds nothing. Which horn should I take, and is there a third option — for instance, limit elements on the model of limit ordinals, or a restriction of transitivity to chains of a particular kind — that I am failing to see?

3. Is my claim in step 3 actually correct? Do the standard arguments for well-foundedness really presuppose that explanatory work requires termination, or does their force come from an indeterminacy worry that non-well-founded structures demonstrably trigger, in which case my distinction does not touch the strongest argument.

4. Is pure powers the best case for the view? I have considered the set-and-singleton case and the question of whether grounding facts are themselves grounded, but both are contested in ways that would take over the paper. I am also unsure whether my weakened asymmetry is distinguishable from the pluralist view that grounding is a cover term for a family of specific relations.

5. Overall: does this read as an original contribution, or as a restatement of existing anti-foundationalist work in more formal vocabulary? Please say plainly which, and where the weakest step is.

6. If the argument is salvageable, should the paper lead with the formal distinction or with the powers case? My current draft begins with the formal apparatus, and I suspect that is why it reads as bloodless.
