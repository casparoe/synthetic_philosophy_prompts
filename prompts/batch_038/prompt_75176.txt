After forty-one years at a national laboratory I have retired and taken up philosophy in earnest, and I am drafting a paper for our local philosophy society on the ethics of work and leisure. I would like help auditing an analogy that carries a great deal of weight in that debate. In management writing, and among my former colleagues, one hears constantly that a good employer is like a family: shared purpose, loyalty in hard times, and the observation that one does not clock out on one's family. It is then put to two uses — recasting very long hours as devotion rather than exploitation, with karoshi as the limiting case, and casting statutory limits on the working week, France's thirty-five hours being the usual exhibit, as a category mistake that treats a bond as a transaction.

Please take the role of a political philosopher fluent in both labour economics and the sociology of work, and think the problem through carefully before answering. Begin with a short brainstorming section in which you list candidate correspondences and candidate disanalogies and discard those that will not bear weight; only then give me the finished audit: (i) an explicit table of what maps onto what — who plays the parents, the siblings, the household economy, the inheritance, and what corresponds to being cast out of a family; (ii) the disanalogies, sorted into those that actually threaten the intended conclusions and those that are merely rhetorical; (iii) what the analogy quietly imports that employment lacks, and what it omits that employment has — the wage, exit, and the asymmetry between who may end the relationship and on what notice; (iv) whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table; and (v) whether a better analogy exists — a guild, a ship's crew, a joint venture with stated terms — and what each would license instead.

Two additions. First: I left in June, and the exclusion was complete within a fortnight. Nobody telephones, my badge opens nothing, and my office has already been reassigned. Say whether that is evidence the analogy was never sincerely believed, or evidence that it was never meant to describe the relationship's duration — and whether either possibility makes it worse rather than better. Second: tell me whether Weber's religious register of work as a calling and this familial register are in fact two distinct analogies, and whether the modern workplace has quietly exchanged one for the other at some cost. No references or bibliography, please; I want the reasoning laid bare.
