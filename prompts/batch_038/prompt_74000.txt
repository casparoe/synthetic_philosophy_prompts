I am working through Maimonides' account of the sacrificial cult in the *Guide for the Perplexed*, and I should be grateful for help understanding two passages. I am reading the old English translation by M. Friedländer (second edition, revised throughout, 1904). I have no Hebrew and no Judaeo-Arabic, and I am not after references or a reading list; I want the passages themselves thought through. Where a point genuinely turns on the underlying language, please say so and tell me what a reader in my position should treat as unsettled, rather than supplying a rendering you cannot stand behind. Please write in British English.

**Passage 1 — Part III, chapter 32.** The chapter opens not with law but with physiology. It describes how the brain is soft at the front and harder towards the back, how the nerves are too soft to set the joints in motion and therefore branch into fibres covered with flesh which become muscles and then sinews, and how newborn mammals are given milk because they cannot yet take dry food:

> "In a similar manner did God provide for each individual animal of the class of mammalia. When such an animal is born it is extremely tender, and cannot be fed with dry food. Therefore breasts were provided which yield milk, and the young can be fed with moist food which corresponds to the condition of the limbs of the animal, until the latter have gradually become dry and hard."

The chapter then turns to the Law:

> "Many precepts in our Law are the result of a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being. It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed."

Having set out the mode of worship that was universal in that period — animal sacrifice in temples containing images, bowing down to those images, and burning incense before them — it continues:

> "It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action. For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner; viz., to build unto Him a temple … to have the altar erected to His name … to offer the sacrifices to Him […]."

> "By this Divine plan it was effected that the traces of idolatry were blotted out, and the truly great principle of our faith, the Existence and Unity of God, was firmly established; this result was thus obtained without deterring or confusing the minds of the people by the abolition of the service to which they were accustomed and which alone was familiar to them. I know that you will at first thought reject this idea and find it strange; you will put the following question to me in your heart: How can we suppose that Divine commandments, prohibitions, and important acts, which are fully explained, and for which certain seasons are fixed, should not have been commanded for their own sake, but only for the sake of some other thing: as if they were only the means which He employed for His primary object?"

The ellipses are my elisions of the scriptural proof-texts that Maimonides interleaves. The chapter goes on to press this objection further and to answer it.

**Passage 2 — Part III, chapter 46**, where he returns to the sacrificial precepts in detail:

> "The precepts of the eleventh class are enumerated in the Section on Divine Service (Sefer ‘abodah) and the Section on Sacrifices (Sefer ha-ḳorbanot). We have described their use in general terms (chap. xxxii.). I will now proceed to give the reason of each precept separately."

> "Scripture tells us, according to the Version of Onkelos, that the Egyptians worshipped Aries, and therefore abstained from killing sheep, and held shepherds in contempt."

> "In order to eradicate these false principles, the Law commands us to offer sacrifices only of these three kinds: 'Ye shall bring your offering of the cattle [viz.], of the herd and of the flock' (Lev. i. 2). Thus the very act which is considered by the heathen as the greatest crime, is the means of approaching God, and obtaining His pardon for our sins. In this manner, evil principles, the diseases of the human soul, are cured by other principles which are diametrically opposite."

> "The idolaters did not offer any other bread but leavened, and chose sweet things for their sacrifices, which they seasoned with honey, as is fully described in the books which I named before: but salt is not mentioned in any of their sacrifices. Our Law therefore forbade us to offer leaven or honey, and commanded us to have salt in every sacrifice"

> "All this concerns only those who desire to sacrifice; for we are distinctly told that the omission of the sacrificial service on our part will not be reckoned to us a sin: 'If thou shalt forbear to vow, it shall be no sin in thee' (Deut. xxiii. 22)."

With both passages in view, please address the following.

1. Reconstruct the argument of passage 1 as a sequence of premises issuing in a conclusion, and state as precisely as you can what that conclusion amounts to for the sacrificial cult in particular. Is the claim that the sacrifices were commanded *instead of* abolition, or that they were commanded *as* the means of abolition? How much difference does that make to whether they have any value in their own right?

2. Explain what work the counterfactual prophet is doing — the one who would tell us in God's name not to pray, not to fast, and not to seek help in trouble, but to serve Him in thought alone. Maimonides expects his reader to find that prophet absurd. Is the absurdity meant to show that abandoning sacrifice would have been *impossible* for that generation, or that it would have been *undesirable* in principle? And is the analogy sound, given that prayer and fasting remain standing obligations in the Law while the analogy treats them as indisposable?

3. The physiological material at the head of the chapter is presented as the very same course of action ("a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being"). What does importing an argument from the gradual hardening of nerves and the nursing of newborn mammals license: a claim about a permanent feature of human nature and hence of religious pedagogy, or merely an illustrative parallel to a contingent historical predicament? Say why the choice matters for how instrumental the sacrificial laws turn out to be.

4. The objection raised at the end of passage 1 is that these commandments would then have been given "only for the sake of some other thing". Set that objection against the *Guide*'s wider insistence that the Law is not arbitrary and that no commandment lacks a reason. Does an instrumental rationale rescue the sacrificial laws from arbitrariness, or does it make their arbitrariness worse — in particular, could the details (which species, salt and not honey, no leaven, birds and then flour for the poor) have been otherwise, and what does the answer imply about their status?

5. Passage 2 refers back to chapter 32 for the general use of these precepts and then gives reasons of its own, in which the sacrificial species and accompaniments are chosen as inversions of what idolaters held sacred or abhorred. Is that an application of the rationale in passage 1 or a different kind of reason altogether? And does it dissolve or sharpen the apparent paradox of passage 1 — that idolatry is blotted out by forms borrowed from idolatry?

6. Passage 2 remarks that omission of the sacrificial service is not counted a sin, while passage 1 says the whole cult was commanded and transferred to God's service. How should these sit together: is the command conditional (if you offer, offer thus), and does that conditional structure weaken or strengthen the concession reading?

7. As I understand it, Maimonides also codifies the sacrificial cult in meticulous detail in his legal code, as law awaiting a restored Temple. If the rationale in these passages is accommodation to an ancient sacrificial milieu, what becomes of that codification, and does passage 1 commit him to sacrifice in messianic times? Lay out the options you think are available to him, say which the passages best support, and give your reasons.

Finally, please distinguish, for each of your answers, what the passages themselves settle from what they leave open; flag any place where my framing above misdescribes the text; and identify which of your conclusions would be most vulnerable if Friedländer's wording departs from the original.
