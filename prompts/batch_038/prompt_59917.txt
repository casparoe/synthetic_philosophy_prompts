I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently reporting a piece on why two Western legal systems that agree about nearly everything else in reproduction end up with almost opposite answers to the question of who a child's parents are. I want the underlying philosophy laid out properly rather than the usual set of talking points, and I have no access to specialist literature while I'm drafting, so I would rather you reason from the practices exactly as I describe them than direct me anywhere.

The material I am working with is this. French civil law provides that any agreement concerning procreation or gestation on behalf of another is null and void, and separately that maternal filiation is established by the designation of the mother in the child's birth certificate — in effect, the woman who gives birth is the mother. That second principle is what makes anonymous egg donation legally invisible in France: because the donor never gives birth, the birth rule never engages against the recipient, and the woman who carries the pregnancy becomes the child's legal mother automatically, while the donor acquires no status whatsoever. The same principle is what defeats French intended parents who arrange a surrogacy abroad: the surrogate gave birth, so on the French view she is the mother, and their agreement is a nullity. Under pressure from the European Court of Human Rights, the French courts have nonetheless settled on recording the child's legal link to the biological father while leaving the intended mother's status to be constructed by adoption, a process that can take years and that leaves the child, in the interim, with one recognised parent.

Italy has gone further. A 2024 statute amended its 2004 assisted-reproduction law so that Italians who arrange a surrogacy abroad can be prosecuted at home, on the reasoning that the offence is punishable wherever it was committed.

California represents the opposite architecture. A gestational carrier agreement is enforceable; a court issues an order before the birth declaring the intended parents to be the child's legal parents whether or not either is genetically related to the child; no adoption is required; and both eggs and gestation are openly bought and sold, in a market that supplies intended parents from countries where the practice is banned.

With that in front of you, I would like the following reconstructed.

First: what does each architecture presuppose about what makes someone a parent — genetic connection, gestation, intention, the contract, or the state's own act of recognition? Please name the criterion each system actually relies on at the points where the criteria come apart, and pay particular attention to the fact that France appears to use gestation against the intended mother while using genetics in favour of the intended father.

Second, the asymmetry I find hardest to explain to readers: the birth principle quietly validates compensated egg donation and invalidates compensated surrogacy, so that a woman may sell an egg but may not sell nine months of gestation. Does the practice require a substantive premise about which human capacities are inalienable — something about the difference between transferring a cell and having one's body occupied under contract — or is the philosophical work actually being done by something less principled, such as the enforceability of a contract against a person's body, the visibility of the market, the sheer difficulty of policing conduct abroad, or the fact that a surrogate is still present to be interviewed while a donor is not? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and be explicit about what the practice strictly presupposes as against what it merely fits comfortably with.

Third: what does the French settlement — nullity abroad, partial recognition at home — presuppose about whose interests the ban serves? If the ban protects the surrogate, the visible costs fall on the child, whose legal identity is what gets delayed. If it protects the child, then recognising the father by genetics while refusing the mother looks arbitrary. Does the practice instead presuppose a third, expressive rationale — that certain transactions must not be given legal effect within the territory regardless of consequences for the people caught in them? Say whether that rationale can be held coherently alongside a legal system that everywhere else treats the child's interest as paramount.

Fourth: what does extraterritorial criminalisation presuppose about the wrong in question — that it attaches to the actor's status as a citizen rather than to conduct within the state, and that it requires an identifiable victim somewhere abroad, or that it is the kind of wrong a state may prohibit without a victim at all?

Fifth: what does the Californian model presuppose about persons — that parental status can be created by declaration and transferred by agreement? — and what does it thereby rule out, in particular the idea that parenthood is a status one finds oneself in rather than a right one acquires? Is that model consistent with the same jurisdiction's refusal to let people buy their way into adoption? If there is a principled line between paying a gestational carrier and paying a birth mother to relinquish, state it; if there is not, say so.

Finally, tell me where these practices are philosophically underdetermined — where an inalienability view, a contractarian view, a care-ethics view, and a commodification-and-inequality view would all license the same rule, so that the law's choice is not in fact explained by any of them. And tell me what would have to change on the ground — who appears before a judge, what a registrar records, which party bears the risk when an arrangement collapses in the seventh month, what a child can learn at eighteen — if France and Italy adopted the Californian premise, or if California were made to adopt theirs.

I am not looking for a survey of both sides, and I am not looking for a verdict on whether surrogacy should be legal. I want the implicit theories made explicit, the internal tensions located, and a flag anywhere that my description of the law is doing philosophical work I have misattributed.
