Ok, this has been eating at me for two weeks and my wife says I either need to call it or let it go, so I'm going to try explaining it to a stranger and see if it sounds as dumb written down as it does in my head.

I share a private gravel lane with my neighbor Carol — about 300 feet of it, her place is closer to the county road, mine is at the end, both deeds carry rights over the whole thing. There's an old maintenance agreement from when the two lots got split that says costs are shared "equitably," which of course defines nothing. The lane is shot: washboarding, two real craters, soup in the spring. A contractor quoted $9,000 to regrade and pave, split between us however we agree, and the quote is good through the end of the month, which I know is pressure and I'm trying to pretend it isn't. What I can't get past is that I don't know what number to open with, or what would make a number principled rather than just a haggling position with a straight face.

Here's the thing I keep landing on. Figure out the most each of us would pay rather than leave it gravel, and then split the $9,000 so we each come out the same *fraction* of that maximum. So if paving is worth at most $5,000 to me and $7,000 to her, I pay 9,000 × 5/12 = $3,750 and she pays $5,250, and we each clear a quarter of the best we could possibly have hoped for. I have no idea whether that's a real thing someone worked out long ago or something I invented on the porch. But then two things scrambled it. First, Carol mentioned last week, totally in passing, that she's listing her house in the spring and her realtor told her a paved drive is worth real money at sale — shed probably go as high as $11,000 now, she said. Under my fraction rule her good news drops *my* bill to about $2,800. I noticed I was pleased, and then immediately queasy, because that means the rule pays me for something she found out, and it means she has every reason on earth to never say out loud how much she stands to gain. Which she may not have. And none of these numbers are checkable — "the most you'd pay" lives inside her skull.

Second, and this is the part I genuinely cannot reconcile. Carol's walk-away is much worse than mine. If nothing happens I bump over gravel in a pickup and grumble about it; she has a work van she's already torn the underside of, and no other way in. My gut says somebody with more to lose from no deal deserves a break — that's just decency, the needier person gets helped. But every version of the arithmetic I've tried runs the other way: the worse your fallback, the bigger your number, the more you end up paying, because you're the one who has to move first. So my kindness instinct and my fair-split instinct are pointing in opposite directions and I can't tell whether one of them is bogus, or whether I'm mashing together two different questions that shouldn't be in the same sentence. Carol, for her part, wants 50/50, $4,500 each, "it's one lane, it serves us both," and honestly on a muddy Tuesday in March I want that too.

So: what do I actually say to her? Is there a principled number here, and if my fraction idea is a real principle, does it survive her house going on the market — should I have said something when she told me, or was keeping quiet the same as cheating? I'd also want to know if the decency thing has any business in a split like this at all or whether it belongs somewhere else entirely. And please be straight with me about how confident you are in each piece and which part you're least sure of, because I can already tell that half of what I'm doing is looking for a formula to hide behind.
