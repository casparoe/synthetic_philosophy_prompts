I work part-time in a building where two of the labs do origins-of-life stuff, and I've gotten in the habit of sorting things into alive / not alive for my own amusement, and my list has stopped adding up. Where I've landed:

A mule: alive, obviously, even though it'll never have offspring. A tardigrade dried into a tun, metabolism basically at zero for a decade: still alive, just paused. An eight-cell embryo sitting in liquid nitrogen: alive. A flu virus on a doorknob: not alive. A prion: not alive — it's just a protein that makes its neighbours fold wrong, there's nothing in there that could be a thing. A flame: not alive, no matter how much it eats and grows and throws off sparks that start new fires. A single yellow slime mold blob: one living thing. A coral colony: a bunch of living things, not one. The bacteria inside an aphid — the ones that can't last a minute outside the insect and have thrown away most of their genome: alive. HeLa cells in a dish, which only exist because people keep feeding and splitting them: alive. RNA in a tube with a copying enzyme, transferred for months until it's a stub that copies itself faster than anything else in the tube, i.e. it evolves in front of you: not alive. And my sister's lab's pet thing, which I'll describe because you won't have run into it: a fat bubble with a short self-copying RNA inside. It can't make any of its own lipid, it scavenges whatever drifts past. It doesn't divide — the instrument squeezes it through a microfluidic chanel every couple of hours and that's what makes two of them. Over half a year the RNAs in those bubbles got noticably better at grabbing lipids. Everyone there calls it alive when they're talking to funders and not-alive when they're talking to each other. I say not alive, firmly, and I can't say why, because it does everything on my list except divide itself.

The principle I've been trying on is: X is alive iff X does the work of staying itself, and can fail at that work on its own account. It handles the mule, the flame and the prion nicely. It breaks on the frozen embryo (if that one dies, the freezer did it) and I suspect it breaks on the HeLa cells too — they don't do much of the work of staying themselves either, we do, and yet I'm not budging on calling them alive while calling my sister's bubbles dead.

So: what's the principle, or the handful of clauses, that does the best job on all this? If it has to overturn some of my verdicts, say which ones and why I should let them go — I'd rather be told than flattered. And tell me what it says about things I haven't listed: a mitochondrion, a pig heart kept beating on a perfusion rig, a brain organoid, a computer worm, the whole biosphere taken as one system, and a seed vault.

Lead with your criterion in a sentence or two before you get into the weeds, then go case by case. End with a last line starting Verdict: followed by the criterion in one sentence, or by "no single criterion" plus the two or three clauses it breaks into.
