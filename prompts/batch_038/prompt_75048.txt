I cover economics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a long feature on how a fight over one decimal place in a government report turned into a fight about whether moral claims can be scientific. Before I commit to a frame, I want the underlying analysis done properly, and I want it done in both directions at once: the theory applied to the episode, and the episode turned back on the theory.

The episode is the reception of the Stern Review on the Economics of Climate Change, published for the UK Treasury in October 2006, and specifically the exchange between Nicholas Stern and William Nordhaus that ran through 2007 and 2008, in the Journal of Economic Literature and elsewhere. The facts you need are these, and you should treat them as given rather than reconstructing them from memory: Stern's headline recommendation — spend around one percent of global GDP per year now rather than face losses of five percent or more — rested on a social discount rate of roughly 1.4 percent. That figure decomposed into a pure rate of time preference of about 0.1 percent, an elasticity of the marginal utility of consumption of one, and an assumption of per capita consumption growth around 1.3 percent. Nordhaus's critique was that the near-zero pure rate of time preference was doing essentially all the work, that it was inconsistent with observed market returns on capital and with the savings behavior of actual economies, and that substituting a rate calibrated to those observations — somewhere in the region of four to five percent, with an elasticity nearer two — reversed the policy conclusion. He characterized the choice as ethical rather than economic. Stern's defence was that the descriptive approach merely reads existing market behavior as though it embodied a considered moral view, and that on ethical grounds the future should not be discounted simply for being later. Others entered from further angles: Weitzman argued that a fat-tailed distribution of climate damages plus a higher elasticity could reproduce Stern's conclusions without the near-zero discount rate, and Dasgupta disputed Stern's parameter choices while insisting the whole exercise was unavoidably philosophical. If any of these attributions strike you as imprecise, work from the version I have supplied and say so, rather than substituting details you are unsure of.

The lens I want is the theory of interpersonal utility comparisons, and I want it used with some technical care rather than as a decorative reference to the Robbins objection. Please treat this as four movements.

First, the mapping. Identify, step by step, which components of the Stern arithmetic actually require a comparison of utility across persons and which do not. Here is the distinction I care most about, and I think most commentary blurs it: a pure rate of time preference compares the welfare of the same person, or the same continuing society, at different dates, which is an intrapersonal and intertemporal comparison; whereas the elasticity of marginal utility combined with an assumption about growth does genuinely interpersonal work, because it determines how much the marginal dollar of consumption is worth to a rich person in the present relative to a poor person a century hence. Equity weighting across regions — which the Review also employed, to reflect that damages fall hardest on the poorest countries — is interpersonal in the most direct sense of all. Sort these three out, and say for each whether it is a comparison of levels of utility, a comparison of units, or something that does not need comparability at all.

Second, run the standard machinery over the dispute. Robbins's insistence in the 1930s that interpersonal comparisons are value judgments and not positive claims; the Bergson-Samuelson social welfare function as the device for admitting those judgments openly instead of smuggling them; Arrow's impossibility result as in part a consequence of assuming away comparability; Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as what you get once cardinal, interpersonally comparable utility is granted; Sen's campaign against what he called the informational poverty of utilitarian welfare economics and for partial comparability. Say what each of these implies about the Stern-Nordhaus exchange — who is behaving consistently with the Robbins ban and who is not — and note that Nordhaus's own descriptive calibration is not obviously ethics-free, since reading revealed market behavior as a moral verdict is itself a substantive assumption.

Third, and this is where I want you to be honest rather than tidy: state where the theory goes silent. I suspect interpersonal comparison theory has nothing useful to say about the Weitzman move, because fat tails and risk attitude are not questions of comparability; nothing about whether the persons of 2200 are the same continuing subject whose welfare is being traded off, which is a problem about identity over time in the Sidgwickian and Parfitian vein rather than about comparing persons; and nothing about the standing of non-human welfare or of the possibility that climate damages are not fungible into utility at all. Say which of these gaps is fatal to using the lens at all, which merely limits it, and which the participants in the dispute exploited without noticing that they had left the framework.

Fourth, the verdict in the reverse direction. Does this episode vindicate Sen's contention that no practical welfare economics can proceed without interpersonal comparisons, does it vindicate Robbins's contention that once you admit them you have left science for ethics, or does it show that both framed the issue in a way that misdescribes what actually happened — for instance because the operative disagreement was about the units of utility and about attitudes to catastrophic risk, and only derivatively about whether one person's welfare can be weighed against another's? I am prepared to be told that my chosen frame is the wrong one, but only after you have shown that it is.

A few constraints. No bibliography, no reading list, and no citations beyond the handful of names and dates I have given you; I cannot check them and neither can you. Do not put words in anyone's mouth — paraphrase rather than quote, and if you find yourself wanting a quotation, describe the point instead. Where you are reconstructing an argument rather than reporting a documented position, mark it as such. Aim for something between twelve and eighteen hundred words, organized under headings, written for a reader who is intelligent and has taken one economics course but has never seen a welfare function.
