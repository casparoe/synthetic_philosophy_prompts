I am a third-year doctoral student in a cognitive science program, and this semester I am teaching the discussion section for a graduate seminar on analogy, concepts, and computational models of cognition. For week nine the reading is on the letter-string microdomain developed around the Copycat project — the slipnet, the coderack and workspace, the codelets, the parallel terraced scan and the temperature mechanism — together with the later FARG models that transplant the same architecture into other microdomains (Letter Spirit, SeqSee, Phaeaco, Musicat). The seminar is unusually mixed: half the students are in philosophy and have never seen a line of Lisp, and half are from the department's machine learning group, whose default reaction is that the whole enterprise has been superseded by anything trained on a large corpus. My instructor has asked me to circulate a handout in advance that functions as a sorting instrument: not a summary of the readings and not a survey of who says what, but a decision tree that takes a reader from their own unreflective verdicts about a handful of concrete letter-string cases to a named position in the debate about what models of this kind can and cannot show about fluid concepts. The point, as she put it, is to sort people by what they would say about the cases rather than by which camp they cheer for, so no question may ask anything as blunt as "do you think concepts are fluid?"

The cases I intend to use are the following, and you should build the questions around them (you may add one or two of your own if you think a gap needs filling, but flag any case you invent). (i) The familiar demonstration abc → abd with target ijk, candidate answers ijl, ijkk, ijk; (ii) the same demonstration with target iijjkk, candidates iijjkl, iijjll, iijjkk; (iii) the same demonstration with target mrrjjj, candidates mrrkkk, mrrjjjj, mrrjjjk; (iv) the same demonstration with target xyz, where the successor relation runs out at the end of the alphabet, candidates wyz, xya, xyz. Two further cases are my own constructions and I want them treated as such: (v) a null-rule demonstration, abc → abc, with target ijk and candidates ijk and ijl, which I expect to reveal whether readers perceive an identity rule at all or default to the transformation they have been primed with; and (vi) a transfer microdomain of my own devising in which the elements are arranged on a circle rather than a line, so that the last element does have a successor — my hunch is that whichever positions predict a change in verdicts on the analogue of case (iv) in this circular domain are the ones with real empirical content, and positions that predict no change are not testable there at all. I also want the instrument to be able to sort someone into a deflationary position I have been developing privately, on which there is no fact of the matter about the correct answer to any of these problems: acceptability is fixed by the conventions of the puzzle genre, by whether the demonstration is presented as a string pair or as a verbal description, and by the reader's assumption that a well-posed puzzle has a single elegant solution. If your tree cannot separate that position from some nearby one, say so rather than papering over it.

Please write the handout content as follows. Eight to ten questions, each presenting one case or one claim with two to four answer options, phrased so that a reader who has never encountered this literature can answer without knowing which position their answer supports; some questions should be verdict questions about the cases above, and at least three should be methodological (for instance, what one would conclude if a system with no innate conceptual network, trained purely on distributional statistics, reproduced the same pattern of answers; or whether a hand-built network of concept nodes and link lengths can count as evidence of conceptual fluidity at all; or whether the explanatory burden lies with the architecture or with the choice of microdomain). Then supply a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto six to eight positions, which must include at least: the view that models of this kind genuinely exhibit fluid concepts because the space of descriptions is explored under pressure rather than searched by lookup; the question-begging objection, on which the pre-built conceptual network and the inventory of codelets already encode the structure the model claims to discover; the view that the architecture rather than the letter domain carries the explanation, so the model must be judged in other microdomains; the systematicity or relational-structure rival account; the distributional-statistical rival in both its confident and its deflationary forms; a view on which candidate answers are graded by simplicity or elegance priors, making the dispute a normative one about descriptions; and my conventionalist position. For each position give it a short label of your own coining, and note whether it corresponds to a familiar name in the literature without citing anything — I will supply every citation and every quotation myself, so please do not quote from any primary text and do not report output frequencies or statistics attributed to the original implementations, since I will fill those in from the sources.

Format the response in this order. First, a design rationale of six to eight sentences: what the instrument is built to discriminate, what it deliberately leaves unresolved, and which single question does the most sorting work. Second, the decision tree as an indented outline, with each internal node labeled by question number and each branch labeled by the answer option that leads down it, and leaves labeled with position labels. Third, Table 1, one row per question, with exactly these columns: question_id; case_or_claim_as_presented_to_reader; answer_options (lettered A, B, C, ...); what_each_option_indicates (for each letter, the position or positions it is evidence for and whether that evidence is strong or weak); why_diagnostic (one or two sentences); positions_this_question_cannot_separate. Fourth, Table 2, one row per position, with exactly these columns: position_label; familiar_name_or_none; canonical_answer_pattern (the question_ids and option letters that yield it); defining_commitments; most_awkward_commitment (one sentence); nearest_neighbor_it_cannot_be_distinguished_from. Fifth, a JSON array of the unoccupied cells in the logical space you have constructed, each object having the fields cell_id, answer_pattern (a list of objects with keys question_id and option), why_nobody_is_there (one of "incoherent", "unattractive", "merely_unexplored"), diagnosis (two or three sentences), and whether_a_view_could_be_built_there (boolean plus one sentence of justification). Please be candid about any pair of positions your questions fail to separate, and do not pad the instrument with questions that every position answers the same way.
