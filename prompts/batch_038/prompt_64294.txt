I am a fourth-year undergraduate on a joint philosophy and history degree, and this term's seminar is on historical explanation. Our cohort is small — nine of us, meeting on Thursday afternoons in a room with a wall map of Europe in 1914 that nobody has ever taken down — and for week five the tutor has asked each of us to bring a scripted exchange rather than another précis, on the grounds that hearing a well-argued dispute read aloud does more for the group than a fourth summary of the same three chapters. I have drawn the topic of contingency: the proverbial lost nail and the lost kingdom, and the question of whether a small event can be genuinely world-changing or is only the occasion on which large forces discharge themselves. I have done the reading — Carr's impatience with counterfactual history, Fogel's econometric counterfactuals about the railways, Ferguson's edited volume, Lewis on the semantics of counterfactuals, and enough Braudel to know what the longue durée is supposed to explain — but what I lack is a clear sense of how the dispute actually proceeds once both sides are arguing competently rather than trading set pieces.

So: please write a dialogue between two historians of equal ability who hold opposing views on this question, set in our common room after the seminar, one of them standing at that wall map and the other with a laptop showing a phase portrait from a dynamics course she audited. Call them A and B. A is a diplomatic and military historian who works on Sarajevo and holds that the events of 28 June 1914 mattered in themselves: the change of plan was never passed to the drivers, the car was halted and stalled within metres of a conspirator who had already concluded that morning that the plot had failed, and none of this was written into the structure of Europe. B is an economic and social historian who thinks the assassination was a tripwire rather than a cause — mobilisation timetables, alliance entanglement, the fiscal strain of the arms race, a crisis culture in which 1912 and 1913 had already rehearsed the thing twice — and who regards A's example as an illustration of the error rather than of the thesis. Neither may be a strawman, and it should not be possible to tell from the quality of the argument which side you would have chosen.

Three things I want the dialogue to get into the weeds about, because this is what our seminar keeps skirting. First, B should press the semantic point: a counterfactual means nothing until one says which nearby worlds it quantifies over and what licenses holding the rest of the state of the world fixed, and A should not be permitted to wave this away but should give a real account of how her discipline constrains the choice — including whatever she can say about the Fogel-style strategy of holding observable magnitudes fixed, varying one parameter, and running the accounts. Second, I want the chaos idiom deployed and then resisted, since everyone in my cohort reaches for it and nobody has tested it: A may argue that historical systems exhibit sensitive dependence on initial conditions, but B's strongest reply is that divergence of trajectories is one thing and the fate of basins and attractors another — a system can separate exponentially from every nearby start while continuing to revisit the same regions, so the question is not whether removing the wrong turn changes the trajectory but whether it changes where the system ends up and how fast; A should answer with something about overdetermination, namely that the trivial detail was itself the residue of a hundred antecedent decisions, so that a world identical to ours except for one turn may not be a nearby world at all. Third, they should at some point separate the claim that this token event was contingent from the claim that events of this type are contingent, since I suspect half our seminar conflates them, and they should distinguish real counterfactuals from what our tutor calls a mirage counterfactual — one whose antecedent cannot be made coherently close to the actual world.

Have each of them concede at least one substantive point that costs them something, and let them interrupt, correct, and reformulate rather than deliver alternating essays. Name the methodological literature sparingly and only where a name does argumentative work; I would rather see the moves made than the references recited. Please do not end in a comfortable synthesis. End instead with the two of them stating as precisely as they can where the disagreement now lies and what would count as a discriminating case — and let them disagree about whether any such case is available in principle, given that neither can rerun the twentieth century. Aim for something in the range of 1,300 to 1,800 words, since we will be reading it aloud.
