I'm third-year in decision theory, mostly working on intertemporal choice, but I've been sitting in on this economics-of-risk seminar where the guy running it keeps reaching for an analogy I can't quite evaluate. It goes like this: taking a multiplicative gamble on the basis of its expected value is like accepting a round of Russian roulette for a billion dollars. The expectation is hugely positive — one chance in six of losing everything, five in six of walking away with a billion — and yet everyone agrees you shouldn't play, and the reason you shouldn't play is exactly the reason growth-rate people give for caring about the geometric mean instead of the arithmetic one. He uses it to argue that EV reasoning isn't just occasionally misleading but structurally bankrupt whenever your wealth compounds, and that anyone still defending expected-value maximisation in that setting is just refusing to look at the gun.

I half buy it and half don't and I can't tell which half is me being slow. So can you lay out the mapping properly — what in the roulette case is supposed to correspond to what in a repeated multiplicative bet (the chambers, the billion, the one-in-six, the fact that you only get the one life and only get to live it forwards) — and then go through where the mapping starts to leak? Two places I'm specifically suspicious about: (a) whether roulette is doing its persuasive work because of an absorbing barrier at all, or because of something about the value of a life that has nothing to do with growth rates and would sneak straight back in even if the growth-rate argument were false; and (b) whether the analogy quietly converts a one-shot case into an iterated one, since as far as I can tell the whole geometric-mean story is about what happens to a trajectory over many repetitions, whereas the gun fires once.

Also — does the "everyone agrees you shouldn't play" premise even survive contact with someone whose wealth is already essentially zero, or with a case where the catastrophic outcome is bankruptcy rather than death and the agent can earn labour income afterwards? That's the substitution I keep wanting to make and I don't know if it's fatal to the analogy or just a limitation.

Then tell me what's left of his conclusion once the disanalogies are on the table. Is EV-maximisation still in genuine trouble over compounding gambles, or was the gun carrying the entire argument? And if the analogy is bad, is there a better one — something with a real absorbing barrier that doesn't trade on death, where the time-average/ensemble-average gap can actually be seen rather than felt — that would make the same point more honestly? Bonus if you can say what the better analogy would license that this one doesn't, because I suspect the two aren't the same claim.
