ok so this is a weird ask and it's personal, bear with me.

My mum has been in a dementia unit for about fourteen months. She doesnt know me now — not even a flicker-of-recognition level, just nothing. She's still warm, she still laughs at music, she still gets distressed if nobody comes for a while. In two weeks there's a family meeting about whether to stop treating her chest infections and just keep her comfortable, and I'm the one who'll end up signing whatever comes out of it.

I've been reading around ubuntu stuff lately, the idea that you're only a person through other people — that personhood isn't something you're born holding but something that gets built and kept up between people, and so it can be more or less. And honestly I had two completely opposite reactions in the same afternoon. One: it feels like the truest description of what my mum is now. She isn't a self-contained individual anymore, she exists in us, in what we do and don't do for her. Two: it feels like it hands my family a permission slip. If personhood is graded and hers has gone down, what exactly is still owed to her, and who decides?

So — write me a dialogue between two people who disagree about this properly. One holds the relational view: personhood is constituted by relationships, it comes in degrees, it's achieved rather than possessed. The other holds the status view: personhood is a binary, non-graded standing that a being has in virtue of what it is, not in virtue of how it's currently being related to by anyone.

Rules I actually care about:

- Both of them are smart and both are decent. No strawman. The relational one isn't a cold communitarian happy to write off the unproductive, and the status one isn't a rule-worshipping robot reciting dignity at a case.
- They answer each other. When one says something, the other engages with that thing rather than with a convenient paraphrase of it.
- The status view has to face the infants-and-congenital-disability problem head on, and it doesn't get to escape by retreating to "it's the kind, not the currently exercised capacity" without saying what's doing the normative work in "the kind." Then the relational one gets to ask whether their own view could make the exact same move, and whether that collapses the debate.
- The relational view has to face the permission-slip problem head on: what is wrong with withdrawing care from someone whose relational personhood has thinned out? And it can't answer by pivoting to "abandoning her would degrade us, the carers" without eating the comeback that this turns my mum into a prop for our moral development — which on the other view is the textbook dignity violation.
- I want one real concession from each side somewhere. Not a politeness concession, one that costs them something.
- Near the end they should try to work out whether their disagreement changes any actual decision — the antibiotics, the feeding, the visiting hours, who gets to decide — or whether it's verbal. If it changes something they have to name the thing and say how it changes it.
- Please don't make it a West-versus-Africa culture clash piece. They're working the same problem from two angles and they both know the other's literature.
- No name-dropping, no references, no bibliography. I can't check any of it and I don't want to. Just the argument.

Around 1200–1500 words. Plain dialogue, give the two speakers names, no stage directions unless somebody's actually doing something.
