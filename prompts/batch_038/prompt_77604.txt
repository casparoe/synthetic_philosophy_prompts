Take the British East India Company between 1765 and 1858, and analyse it as a case study in the tool-versus-agent distinction as that distinction is deployed in debates about advanced AI — the claim that a system confined to advising, forecasting, and answering questions, with a human retaining every decision, is safer in kind and not merely in degree than a system that pursues objectives on its own initiative.

Some facts I am supplying so that the analysis does not have to rest on your recall of them. In 1765 the Company was granted the diwani, the right to collect the land revenue of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa; it already maintained its own army and had been making war and treaties for decades. It was overseen by a charter renewed roughly every twenty years, by a Court of Proprietors and a Court of Directors elected by shareholders, and, increasingly, by Parliament. Bengal suffered catastrophic famine in 1770 while revenue collection continued. The Regulating Act of 1773 created a Governor-General and tried to discipline the Company's servants. Pitt's India Act of 1784 established a Board of Control in London which was to "superintend, direct and control" the Company's civil, military and revenue acts, while leaving the Company in possession of patronage and day-to-day rule — a system of dual control that lasted until 1858. The same act declared schemes of conquest and extension of dominion in India to be "repugnant to the wish, the honour and the policy of this nation", and required civil and military officers to disclose their property in India and Britain within two months of taking up post. Warren Hastings was impeached and, after a trial lasting 1788 to 1795, acquitted. The charter acts of 1813 and 1833 progressively stripped the Company of its trading functions: by 1833 it had lost even its China and tea trade and had become, on paper, a purely administrative body — though the same act extended its charter for a further twenty years and thereby perpetuated its right to raise armies, wage war and govern conquered territory. In 1857 the Company's own sepoys mutinied; in 1858 Parliament abolished its rule and transferred India to the Crown.

Against that record, I want you to test a piece of apparatus of my own rather than simply apply the standard one. Call a system an *instrument* when it performs a specified operation on specified inputs and nothing happens to the world until a principal acts on its output; a *proxy* when it acts within a mandate, under recall and audit; and a *principal* when it sets ends of its own and the nominally superior party can only bargain with it. My conjecture is that organisations travel a ratchet — instrument to proxy to principal — and that each transition is driven not by any decision to grant agency but by the accumulation of capability that makes non-use costly: the army raised to protect factories turned commercial questions into military ones and military ones into revenue ones, until there was no commercial decision left that was not also a decision about force. Test this. Is the ratchet one-way, and where exactly in this record does each transition occur? Or is the real variable something else — the latency and cost of oversight, meaning how long it takes and what it costs the principal to detect and reverse a decision once made, rather than whether the system formally "acts"?

Be disciplined about the mapping before you draw conclusions. Who is the principal here: the Crown, Parliament, the Directors, or the shareholders? What is the mandate, and who wrote it? Which levers of control actually existed — charter renewal on a twenty-year cycle, the Board of Control's veto over despatches, impeachment, the property-disclosure rule, the threat of nationalisation — and what was the feedback interval of each? Could Parliament have shut the Company down cheaply at any given moment, and how did the answer change between 1765 and 1857? Which of the interventions listed above are properly understood as tool-ification attempts — retaining the capability while removing the discretion — and did any of them work? The 1833 act is the sharpest case: it removed the profit motive and left the army intact.

Then run the analysis in the other direction. What does this episode show about the tool-versus-agent distinction itself? Consider at least: whether it supports treating the distinction as one of degree along a single axis of discretion, or as a false dichotomy; whether it demonstrates that agent-like outcomes can arise from a population of instruments with none of them agentic, the 1770 famine being the candidate, produced by revenue maximisation plus drought plus the absence of any channel through which the suffering could reach the people setting the target; whether it shows the human in the loop to be not a control mechanism but a bottleneck whose competence degrades as the system's capability grows, since Parliament could only understand India through information the Company itself supplied; and whether "keep it a tool" is therefore a temporary equilibrium that holds only while the principal retains both comprehension and a cheap exit.

Be explicit about where the analogy fails, and rank the disanalogies by how much weight they carry. The Company was a legal person containing hundreds of thousands of human beings with their own motives, who could be bribed, sued, impeached, or could mutiny; an AI system is not decomposable into agents of that sort, so several of the control mechanisms whose failure is instructive here have no counterpart. Its principal, moreover, was a diffuse political body with collective-action problems of its own, which suggests the lesson may be about the impossibility of oversight by a committee rather than about tools and agents at all. Say what survives once the load-bearing disanalogies are removed, and what does not.

Finish by stating the single most important implication of the case for anyone arguing that a given capability should be released as a tool rather than an agent.

I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want your own analysis, and you should say plainly where the record is silent or where the theory simply has nothing to say, rather than forcing either to speak. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning thereafter, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
