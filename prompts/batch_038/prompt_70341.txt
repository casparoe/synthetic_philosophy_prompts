I help run a small Discord for people who are into weird-history claims — lost ice-age civilizations, ancient astronauts, revisionist chronologies, that kind of thing. It isn't a debunking server exactly; roughly half the people there want to believe and half want to dunk, and every couple of weeks the exact same fight happens. Someone posts a claim, somebody else types "that's pseudohistory" like it settles the matter, and then we burn three days arguing about whether the word fits instead of arguing about the claim itself. I'm not an academic and I've never taken a philosophy or historiography class, I've just read a lot on my own, and somewhere along the way I ended up with a few private tests I run things through. Problem is they live in my head as vibes and I can't get them into a shape other people could actually use mid-conversation, and half the time I catch myself applying them inconsistently.

What I want is a decision tree — a sorting instrument, basically a flowchart — that starts at "someone has just put forward a claim about the deep past" and ends at a named bucket. Maybe ten to fourteen question nodes and six to eight leaves, short enough that it fits on a phone screen. The important constraint: every node has to be phrased as a plain question that a total layperson can answer in one sentence without knowing where it leads or what the labels are. So no node can just be "is it falsifiable?" or "do experts agree?" or "has it been peer reviewed?" — those smuggle the verdict into the question and are exactly what my server fights about. Ask about what the claim says, what it would take to trip it up, what it predicts, how much of the existing record has to be wrong for it to work. Sort people by what they actually think about the cases, not by which word they like.

Here's the angle, which is the part I can't do myself. Three of my own tests have to be in there as real nodes, cleaned up and made into answerable questions: (1) what I call the ratchet — whether the claim only ever gains support and never loses any, so that every new discovery is confirmation and every awkward one is a cover-up or a dating error; (2) the dull-detail rule — whether the hypothesis lets you predict something boring and checkable that nobody who cares about the theory would bother looking for, like a pollen count or a wear pattern on a tool or a date in a shipping ledger, versus only ever predicting spectacular things like a hidden chamber or a sunken city; (3) the one-big-mistake rule — how much of the independent record has to collapse into a single error or a single conspiracy for the theory to hold together. Add whatever other splits you think genuinely belong and drop mine if one of them turns out to be redundant. The leaves need to include at least: settled mainstream history, a genuinely open question that experts themselves argue about, honest speculation that's just wrong, pseudohistory proper, outright hoax or forgery, myth being read as literal history, and something like "right for the wrong reasons" where a lucky guess got dressed up after the fact. For each leaf give one sentence on what it costs you to sit there, and then tell me which corners of your tree are empty — which combinations of answers no real position occupies, and whether that's because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Finally, run at least three cases through it end to end so I can see the path being taken: pick from Plato's Atlantis as he actually tells it, the Palenque sarcophagus lid being read as a man in a rocket, Fomenko's rewritten chronology, the Vinland Map, Piltdown Man, the Shroud of Turin, the comet-at-the-end-of-the-ice-age stuff that gets bolted onto lost-civilization stories, Göbekli Tepe, and the Denisovan finger bone — deliberately include one that the tree handles badly, because I want to know where it breaks. Flag any pair of leaves it can't really separate (I already suspect honest-but-wrong and right-by-accident are going to blur together, and so are hoax versus myth once the hoaxer starts believing their own story), and note any case that changes bucket halfway through the tree, since a few of these definitly flipped categories after new evidence came in.
