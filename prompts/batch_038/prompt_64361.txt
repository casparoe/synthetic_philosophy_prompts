Been reading Overcoming Bias and then LW since around 2009, and I've finally talked an actual philosopher of science into coming on my very small podcast. He's a Lakatosian — not the museum-piece kind who writes about Lakatos historically, but someone who thinks the methodology of scientific research programmes is still the best account of theory choice we have, and who's lately been trying to drag it out of physics and into the messier sciences (including, in his newest stuff, ML labs). I've read enough of the primary text to know roughly where the load-bearing walls are, and I do not want to spend the hour letting him give the seminar summary of hard core / protective belt / progressive problemshift. So: draft me eight to ten questions, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. I want the follow-ups to be specific enough that I can read them out cold.

Stuff I definitely want hit, plus a few things I'd like you to sharpen:

1. The empty-rules charge. If the hard core is irrefutable by a methodological decision, and appraisals only come decades later with hindsight, what does the framework actually forbid anyone from doing? He'll almost certainly answer that appraisals aren't rules of conduct — I want a follow-up that sits on that answer and asks whether a methodology that can never tell a single scientist to quit is still a methodology or just a nicer way of narrating what already happened.

2. Reflexivity, and I want him to be explicit rather than charming about it. What is the hard core of MSRP itself? Which pieces of the protective belt have been patched over the years — the retreat to long-run appraisal, the habit of explaining any mismatch between the rational reconstruction and what scientists actually did by pointing at 'external history'? Push him to say, on the record, whether MSRP appraised by MSRP is progressive or degenerating, and don't let him escape into 'too early to tell', because that reply is available for literally any programme at any moment.

3. Novelty. Theoretically progressive means predicting novel facts, but novel is defined by whether the fact was already used in building the theory — so novelty is a fact about the history of a theory's construction, not a property of the theory itself. Get him to walk a specific case where a prediction is novel on one reading and not novel on another, and ask which reading his own recent applications rely on.

4. Who's allowed to declare degeneration. My own suspicion, and I'd like it put to him properly: the people calling a programme progressive are basically always inside it, and the people calling it degenerating are always outside it. Is 'degenerating' doing any evaluative work at all, or is it just a marker for 'not my programme'? Can an outsider who rejects the hard core legitimately appraise it?

5. Popper. Is MSRP more than sophisticated methodological falsificationism with a longer clock and a different unit of appraisal? If he says the real difference is the unit, follow up by asking what changes in practice — what would a Popperian and a Lakatosian disagree about in a live dispute, where both have all the same data.

6. The comeback case. He'll want to point at some programme that looked dead for decades and then revived — my guess is Bohmian mechanics or something like it. Ask what MSRP was saying during the dead decades, and how the framework is distinguishable in hindsight from 'bet on whatever eventually wins'.

7. The ML application, which is the reason I booked him. Are the frontier labs one research programme or thousands of little ones? Suppose the hard core is something like 'more compute and more data buys you more capability', and every failed capability prediction gets absorbed into architecture choices, data mixtures, or the eval being wrong. I can see him calling that the protective belt working as designed; I'd call it unfalsifiable. What's the difference, in a field where the belt has repeatedly predicted successful recoveries and where the recovery time is measured in quarters, not centuries? And does 'novel prediction' even mean anything when the theory is a curve fitted to a scaling plot?

8. His extension into psychology and the squishy sciences generally. Does 'programme' do real work there, or is it redescription with a Hungarian accent? Concretely: what would a grad student in one of those fields have done differently in, say, 2012 if they'd been following MSRP?

9. Close with the question I always close with — what evidence, if it showed up, would make him give the whole thing up. If he can't answer that in one sentence, ask him again.

Ground rules: no softballs, nothing like 'what drew you to philosophy of science', and don't hand me questions he can answer just by naming a distinction. Every question should make him apply something to a specific case. Also — I'm going to be reading these off my phone in a room with bad acoustics, so keep each question short enough to say in one breath, and put the teeth in the follow-up.
