Last night a friend and I spent four hours on Fitch's paradox of knowability and arrived nowhere, so I would like an outside assessment, put as neutrally as I can manage.

My understanding of the argument — which my friend accepts — is this. Suppose every truth is knowable: for any truth, it is possible that someone at some time knows it. Suppose also, uncontroversially, that some truth is in fact unknown. Take such a truth and form the conjunction saying that it obtains and that no one knows that it obtains. By the first supposition, it is possible that someone knows that conjunction. But whoever knows a conjunction knows each of its conjuncts, and knowledge is factive; so anyone who knew this conjunction would know the first conjunct and would thereby make the second one false. Hence the conjunction is not possibly known; hence, by contraposition of the first supposition, it is not true; and since the truth in question was arbitrary, there are no unknown truths at all. That is absurd, so the supposition must go: some truths are not merely unknown but unknowable.

From this my friend draws three conclusions, and I suspect each is too strong, though I could not locate the overreach. First, she holds that the paradox establishes — without borrowing anything from the anti-realist's own commitments — that the world contains facts no possible investigation could ever reach, and that it therefore settles the dispute between realism and anti-realism in the realist's favour. I objected that the derivation is a modus tollens run on the anti-realist's own first premise, so the upshot is a dilemma addressed to her rather than a free-standing discovery about reality; but I could not explain why that should matter, since one of the two premises has to be surrendered either way. Second, she maintains that the familiar response of restricting the knowability principle to some well-behaved subclass of propositions is naked special pleading, the subclass being drawn after the fact and drawn precisely so as to exclude the troublesome instance. I wanted to reply that a restriction may be principled if independently motivated, and could produce no motivation. Third, she assimilates the unknowable truths to the true-but-unprovable sentences of Gödel's theorems, treating the paradox as a semantic incompleteness result showing that truth outruns proof generally.

For my own part I pressed a different line, which I now think was confused and cannot diagnose. I said that the troublesome conjunction is a sentence whose truth is destroyed by the very act of knowing it, so the paradox is a curiosity about self-undermining statements rather than a finding about the world. She replied that this misses the point, since the construction can be run on any proposition whatsoever provided only that it is unknown, so nothing about the content of the proposition is doing the work. One of us is wrong and I cannot tell which.

Could you say what the argument does and does not establish, and adjudicate her three claims together with my objection? I would also like the following settled so far as they can be. Which properties of the knowledge operator does the derivation actually exploit, and does an analogous contradiction arise if some other operator is substituted for knowledge — being verified by someone at some time, say, or being rationally believable? Does the argument imply that the complete truth about the world, the conjunction of everything that is the case, could not be known, or does something already go wrong with the idea of such a conjunction? And does the result bear at all on empirical inquiry — for instance, on whether a final and complete physical theory would be impossible in principle?
