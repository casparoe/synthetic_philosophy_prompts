Write a pantoum — that interlocking form in which lines two and four of each quatrain return as lines one and three of the next, and in which the closing stanza traditionally folds back lines one and three of the first — spoken in the voice of a rule that has been kept so long that no one remembers anyone deciding on it: which side of the road to hold to, or what word a thing answers to. Let the machinery of the form carry the analysis. Each stanza should be a fresh act of conformity built entirely out of what everyone already expects of everyone else, so that the poem generates itself the way the rule does and no line feels authored by anyone in particular. Then break the machinery once, deliberately: include a single line naming the mirror-image rule that would have served exactly as well — the traffic on the other side, the other word — and see to it that this line never returns. Keep it to nine stanzas or fewer. Do not name Lewis and do not expound the theory inside the poem; avoid the phrases social contract, common knowledge, and coordination equilibrium entirely. It should be possible to say of the final stanza both why the poem has to come back to a line it opened with, and why the one line that never came back is the reason this rule binds by precedent rather than by promise. A reader who knows the account should hear that the conformity is conditional — I will, if you will — and self-perpetuating; a reader who doesn't should simply find a good poem about a habit nobody ever made.
