My colleague and I spent most of last night on the original-versus-derived intentionality dispute and arrived at an impasse that I now think was never about the answers but about the question. He holds something close to Searle's line: a state is genuinely about something only if the aboutness is intrinsic to it, and no amount of outside interpretation can manufacture that; the Chinese Room is supposed to demonstrate the bankruptcy of the interpretive strategy. I hold something close to Dennett's: the two-bitser — a storage element whose two states are a one and a zero in one circuit and something else entirely once it is lifted out and wired into another — is supposed to show that there is no fact about what a state is about except relative to an interpretive context, so that the demand for intrinsic content is a demand for something unintelligible. What struck me this morning is that we both assumed, without argument, that where there is content it is determinate, and that the only live question is where the determination comes from. I suspect that shared premise is doing the damage, and that the distinction worth drawing is not inside-versus-outside but who or what fixes the standard against which a state can be wrong. Call it, provisionally, the autonomy of the semantic norm, and treat it as graded rather than binary.

Please turn this into a proposal for a three-month internal research grant, roughly two pages, addressed to a committee that includes both philosophers of mind and people who work on the internal representations of trained models. It needs the following.

First, state the question precisely enough that a referee can tell what a yes and a no would look like, and identify the specific claim I am asking the committee to fund. Second, explain why neither the intrinsicness demand nor interpretation-relativity settles it, and why the naturalist middle routes that ground content in biological function or in information-carrying relations do not simply absorb my proposal. Third, set out the method: I want a ladder of cases — a passive sensor, a feedback controller with a comparator and a setpoint, a system that revises its own setpoint in light of its error history, a trained model whose only error signals originate outside it, and a nonhuman animal — and for each rung, an account of what the two traditional notions say, what mine says, and where the verdicts come apart. The proposal should say explicitly which rungs are meant to do the work and why.

Fourth, confront three objections head-on rather than noting them in passing: that autonomy of norm is nothing but a count of feedback loops and therefore adds no distinction; that correctness is itself an intentional notion, so the account is circular; and that the whole apparatus collapses into the instrumentalism of the intentional stance. Fifth, say what my reframing does to the Systems and Robot replies. I would rather the proposal concede where it vindicates Searle than pretend otherwise.

Finally: month-by-month plan, the deliverable, what would count as the project having failed, and an honest assessment of whether three months is enough. Skip the bibliography entirely — I will add citations myself.
