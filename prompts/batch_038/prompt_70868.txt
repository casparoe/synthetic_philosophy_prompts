Teaching a seminar on why promises bind and I want to hang it on the first two chapters of Sense and Sensibility — old Mr Dashwood's deathbed request that John look after his stepmother and the three girls, and the talk with Fanny afterwards that walks three thousand pounds down to five hundred apiece, then to a hundred a year, then to the odd present of fish and game whenever they happen to be short. Every one of Fanny's steps sounds reasonable on its own, which is what makes the scene so uncomforable.

Two things I want the reading to get its teeth into. First: has John actually broken a promise by the end of it, or did he never make one with determinate content? He insists his father stipulated no particular sum, only asked in general terms that he assist them — so who gets to fix what was promised, and does it matter that the one person who could complain, or release him, is dead and will never hear how the negotiation went? Second, and this is the bit I care about most: John plausibly already had a duty of charity towards those women, so what is the promise supposed to add on top of that duty? Set out both candidate answers — that it hands the promisee a right she can demand, and that it's a device for pinning down your own future self-interest — and tell me which one Austen's scene actually bears out, or whether it quietly embarrasses both.

Don't flatten the novel into a case study, though. If the scene is doing something the theories can't hold, say what, and say where my framing starts to distort it.
