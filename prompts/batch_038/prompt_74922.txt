**The Beast-Machine Test** (working title)

Everybody remembers Descartes as the guy who said animals are machines and therefore feel nothing, and mostly remembers it as a monstrous thing to have said. What I want to argue is that the interesting part of his position isn't the denial, it's the test he uses to get there — and that the test is exactly why his position has aged worse than the one he was arguing against.

Start with what he actually does. In the Discourse he says the bodies of animals are machines whose movements follow from the arrangement of their organs the way a clock's movements follow from its wheels and weights. He doesn't deny that animals do astonishingly complicated things. He grants it and then says it proves nothing, because a clock keeps time better than we do and nobody thinks the clock understands time. Competence is not evidence of understanding. That's the move, and I think it's the strongest move in the whole thing, and it's still being made every day by people who are pretty sure there's nothing it's like to be a language model.

Then comes the load-bearing part. In his letters from the 1640s — one of them to the Marquess of Newcastle, I think, and another to an Englishman named More — he says the only reliable sign of thought hidden inside a body is genuine language. Not repeating words. Parrots repeat words. Real language means arranging signs on the spot to get your thoughts across to somebody else, in situations you were never drilled on. And he presses it with a nice piece of evidence: human beings born without hearing or speech still invent signs to communicate, while animals with perfectly good vocal apparatus never do. So the deficit can't be in the organs. It has to be in the mind.

Here's my problem, and its the reason I wrote any of this down. Notice what kind of claim that is. It's not a claim about an inner glow that only God can see. It's a public, behavioral, operational criterion — the sort of thing you could go and check. Descartes, who of all people wanted to ground knowledge in private first-person certainty, has committed himself to an outside test for other minds. That makes him look less like a mystic and more like a behaviorist, and it makes his position the kind of position that new data can break. And the data broke it. A grey parrot in a lab a few decades back asked for things it had never been trained to name and combined labels in ways nobody had set up. Apes used signs to request objects that weren't in the room. Now we have things that aren't even animals passing the language test so thoroughly that Descartes' own criterion would oblige him to hand them a soul.

Compare that with what he was arguing against. The older scholastic picture — going from memory here, I may be mangling it — sorts living things onto a ladder: plants have a vegetative soul, animals add a sensitive one, humans add a rational one. That system is mushier and I wouldn't defend it on its own terms. But it absorbs precisely the evidence that breaks Descartes. You can slide the dog up the ladder, or slide the parrot up, or add rungs, and nothing cracks. Descartes built a binary switch; the scholastics built a dimmer. Switches are more precise, and they are more fragile.

Two loose ends I can't tie up.

First, Huxley. In the 1870s he picked the automaton idea back up but tried to keep consciousness inside it, arguing roughly that animals and people are conscious automata — the feeling is real but it doesn't do anything, like a steam whistle that isn't connected to the wheels. That reads to me like a retreat that saves the word and loses the content, and it makes me wonder whether Descartes and Huxley actually agree about the science and are quarreling only about whether to keep a term that has no work left to do. If so, the whole centuries-long fight is about where you're allowed to stop explaining, which is a much better fight than whether dogs hurt.

Second, a follower of Descartes named Cordemoy — this is the part I'm least sure of — apparently reasoned that if you ever ran into a body that used words aptly in every situation, you'd have to conclude it had a soul, because nothing but a soul could manage that. If that really is his argument, then the criterion was built as a two-way street from the start: pass the test, get the soul. Which would mean my main point isn't a criticism of Descartes at all, it's a prediction he signed up for. I can't tell whether that makes him more impressive or more naive.

Last thing, and I don't believe it. People say Descartes kicked a pregnant dog, or that his people at Port-Royal nailed dogs to boards and joked about it. My suspicion is that most of that belongs to Malebranche, who pushed the doctrine much further, or is just later gossip with the more famous name attached. I've never seen a solid source. I'd like to know if I'm right to be suspicious, or if I'm doing the thing where you defend a thinker you like from stories you don't want to be true.
