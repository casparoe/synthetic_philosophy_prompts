I'm in a federalism seminar this term (US, Germany, Switzerland, EU, comparing how each one allocates power) and my reading group put together an argument last Tuesday that we can't kill. It's supposed to show subsidiarity is self-undermining: that any federation taking its own subsidiarity principle seriously can't actually assign any powers to anybody. Our TA said "cute" and changed the subject, which isn't an answer.

We stipulated two definitions first, and this is our coinage, not something we read. Call a level of government *competent* for a task when it can carry that task out for everyone the task affects. And call the *affects-all reading* the claim that the set of people a task affects fixes the scope of the task, so you can't handle a task unless you can answer to everyone it touches. Then:

1. Subsidiarity: a task belongs to the lowest level competent to perform it.
2. Competence requires the affects-all reading — a level is competent for a task only if it can perform it for everyone the task affects.
3. Every task any government performs affects at least some people outside its own jurisdiction: downstream pollution, tax-base flight, migrants arriving, prices moving across a border.
4. So no level below the top one in a federation is ever competent for anything.
5. But the top level spills over too, since it affects foreigners, and there's no world government competent for those effects.
6. So for any task with cross-border effects there's no competent level at all, and subsidiarity can't hand a task to a level that isn't competent for it.
7. So the only tasks a government may perform are ones that affect nobody outside its own jurisdiction.
8. By (3), there are essentially none. Subsidiarity honestly applied dissolves government, and every real federation is a standing violation of its own founding principle.

We've been calling (3) through (8) the spillover regress. The part that spooked us is that it also runs downward: the same steps say a city can't do anything, then a neighborhood, then a household, and it doesn't stop at the individual either, since my choices affect strangers too. So it seems to prove there's no legitimate agency anywhere, which we're pretty sure is wrong.

Here's my problem: I'd give up almost anything before (3). Spillovers are the only genuinely empirical thing in this whole topic and I'm confident (3) is just true, so I want to know where the slide happens instead.

So: which numbered step do you reject, and why did that step look unobjectionable when we wrote it down? What does rejecting it cost — what can the surviving version of subsidiarity no longer explain? And make sure your repaired principle can handle the two defaults real systems actually use, where Switzerland leaves the cantons with everything the federation wasn't explicitly given, and Germany lets the federation into concurrent matters only where a federal rule is needed to keep living conditions roughly equivalent across the Länder. Finally, what does your repaired principle say about a federal floor on corporate tax rates, designed to stop the states racing the rate down?
