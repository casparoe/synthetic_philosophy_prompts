Our group has been asked to write the policy governing when a long-running agent instance may be paused, copied, or terminated, and I have discovered I cannot say what my own view commits me to. Before drafting anything, I want the specification. Write the desiderata any theory of which computations matter morally must satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each: the verdicts it must return, the distinctions it may not collapse (between having status and having a certain amount of it, for instance, or between being a subject and implementing one), and the facts about computation it may not contradict.

Anchor the list to cases like these. A checkpoint is instantiated a thousand times in parallel, each briefly, then the copies are merged — does moral weight scale with the thousand and collapse back afterward? A process is suspended for a year of wall-clock time with its state intact on disk: suspended subject, or merely unrealized one? The same computation runs at a thousandfold difference in clock rate. A state is sharded across machines so that no single process ever holds the whole of it. And a computation is realized only incidentally, as the pattern cast by something whose parts were never arranged to compute it — which forces the question whether, on a loose enough notion of implementation, almost anything implements almost everything.

Then mark each item as non-negotiable or as a preference of some camp, say which requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I expect something breaks between implementation-independence and a determinate count of subjects — and which single requirement eliminates the most candidates. Skip the scorecard of existing views; the list is what I need.
