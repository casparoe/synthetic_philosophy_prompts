So our personal-identity reading group is about halfway through Reasons and Persons and we've been keeping a scoreboard of what the guy running it says about each case. His verdicts so far:

- you at ten and you now: same person, and what matters is there.
- going to sleep and waking up: same, obviously.
- the standard teletransporter, where your body is destroyed and an exact copy is switched on on Mars: not you, and it doesn't matter — he was pretty firm about this one.
- same thing but with a several-hour lag between the destruction and the copy being switched on: exactly the same verdict as the instant version.
- your brain divided and each half transplanted into a different body, both of them waking up with your memories and your character: what matters is fully present, twice over, and he said it makes no difference whether you turn out to be identical to one of them, both, or neither.
- every neuron in your brain replaced by a synthetic equivalent one at a time over ten years, psychology preserved throughout: you survive, and it matters.
- the teletransporter where the original isn't destroyed, so you and your Martian copy are both walking around: he said the copy's existence doesn't make your own situation any worse, and it's still true that pressing the button wouldn't give you what matters.

Next week's case on the table is one I made up to try and trip him. I'm offered a procedure that permanently erases every memory, habit, belief and trait I have and installs a complete psychology belonging to somebody who died years ago — dense, coherent memories of a whole life that wasnt mine, a different temperament, different attachments, the works. It's done on my own body and my own brain in a single sitting. Nothing is copied, nothing is destroyed, no branching. And then a variant where the same end point is reached the slow way: the same amount of erasure and replacement, but spread over months in tiny increments, so that on any given day I'm barely different from the day before, with all the normal kinds of causal connections holding at every step, and yet by the end there's no direct psychological overlap at all with who I started out as.

What does he say about the one-sitting version — is what matters still there, and would he tell me I should dread it about as much as my own death? And does he come out differently on the slow version, or the same? The bit I actually care about is which rule his scoreboard pins down. As far as I can tell more than one survives everything above: something keyed to the persistence of the brain or the organism, something keyed to continuity but only when it's caused the normal way, something keyed to the sheer degree of connectedness rather than its bare presence, and probably others I haven't thought of. Walk me through what each of those predicts for both new cases, say which one you'd bet on him holding and why, tell me whether my two cases are enough to settle it between the survivors — and if they're not, give me a third case to put in front of him that would be.
