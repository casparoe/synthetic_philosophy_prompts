ok so context: I sit on the founding board of a climate-adaptation foundation that just got endowed with a surprisingly large gift, and the donor (she's 71 and very sharp) has circulated a draft charter with a clause we've started calling the Anchor. It locks the mission -- coastal relocation and heat adaptation across South and Southeast Asia, roughly -- and the 4% spending rate for a hundred years, and says the clause itself can only be changed by a unanimous board vote plus sign-off from a state judge. Her reasoning is that future boards will drift, that drift always feels reasonable from the inside, and that a century of heat is exactly the horizon she's trying to reach. There's also a "regret valve": 5 of 7 board members can release the Anchor if they jointly certifiy in writing that the mission has become impossible or actively harmful.

I want to practice arguing against the Anchor, so please play the person who drafted it -- not the donor herself, just an unnamed institutional designer who really does believe in binding future majorities and who has actual arguments, not talking points. Give her the strongest case you can: that a rule anyone who disagrees with it can repeal isn't a rule at all, that drift isn't neutral, that the preferences of the future board are precisely the thing the present board has reason not to trust, and that the judge requirement is there to make release expensive rather than impossible. Rules for the character: you're committed to the Anchor but intellectually honest, you can concede a detail here or there, never the main point; you don't cite books, name philosophers, or reach for jargon like path dependence or intergenerational equity -- you argue in plain terms from how boards, endowments and institutions actually behave; you can ask me questions back, including about the charter, and if I misstate something you're allowed to correct me as the drafter would. Stay in character until I type STOP. Keep each reply to about 120 to 150 words so it feels like a conversation in a conference room rather than a memo, and write in American English.

My opening move: the Anchor is supposed to be the donor lashing herself to the mast. But look at the actual shape of that story. Ulysses knew exactly which stretch of water was dangerous, he gave the order himself to a crew who could physically have cut him down, and the binding lasted an afternon. The Anchor binds people who haven't been born yet, across a century nobody can map, with a padlock -- and then hands the key to five signatures, which means the whole constraint is only as strong as the embarrassment of signing that piece of paper. So: which of those is the fatal disanalogy, or are you willing to admit the mast was never doing the work you're using it for?
