I'm writing the docs for a numerical library at work and I got stuck trying to explain, in more than one sentence, why you never compare two floats with == and instead check whether the difference is under some epsilon. Everybody knows the rule; nobody I asked could tell me what it assumes.

Because look at what the practice actually is. The only thing I'm ever allowed to positively assert is that two values are seperated by more than the tolerance — that's the informative, checkable claim. "They're equal" is what's left over when I've failed to find a separating gap, and the rule says I'm not allowed to conclude it from that failure. Meanwhile IEEE 754 itself makes NaN unequal to NaN, so equality isn't even reflexive over everything in the type, and +0.0 == -0.0 comes out true even though 1/+0.0 is +infinity and 1/-0.0 is -infinity, so two "equal" things can be told apart by what happens to them. And addition isn't associative, so (a+b)+c and a+(b+c) can be different floats for the same a, b, c.

I keep getting told this is all just grubby engineering: the floats are approximations to exact real numbers and epsilon is a workaround for the approximation. But it doesn't feel like a workaround to me, it feels like the practice has quietly changed what a real number is. I dimly remember that constructive mathematicians (Bishop's crowd, if I've got the name right) invert equality and inequality for the reals — treating "you can exhibit a rational between them" as the basic positive notion and defining equality as the failure of that, precisely because you can't get from not-apart to equal. Is that what the epsilon habit is doing? Or is it something else, some operationalism where a "real number" just is the float plus the tolerance regime around it?

What I want is a reconstruction of the theory of the reals, and of equality, that the never-use-== practice presupposes. Please be careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely looks like — someone could argue the whole thing is perfectly compatible with full-blooded Platonism about real numbers plus a modest claim about our finite precision and ignorance, and I want to know whether that defence works or whether the practice has really committed us to something anti-classical. Then: is it internally coherent? IEEE makes equality decidable (every comparison returns an answer) but gives up reflexivity and Leibniz's law, while the constructive reading keeps equality a proper equivalence relation but makes it undecidable. Which of those two is the epsilon habit actually following, and does it matter that we seem to do both at once — treating == as meaningless for numerics, then happily using it on the very same values when they're flags or loop indices? And finally, tell me what would have to change in the code, in the correctness proofs, and in the standard if a team decided to take exact classical reals seriously and ban tolerance comparisons altogether — whether that's even achievable, or whether the hardware makes the other reading unavoidable.
