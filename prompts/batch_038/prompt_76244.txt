In Switzerland, if you're a man and you dont want to do your compulsory military service, you can switch to civilian service instead — hospital work, care homes, mountain farming, that sort of thing. Two details of how that works have been nagging at me. First, you don't have to give reasons. No essay, no hearing, no committee judging whether your conscience is genuine; you file the application and it's basically approved. Second, the civilian service is one and a half times as long as the military service you're avoiding, and the extra length *is* the proof: accepting the surcharge is what shows you meant it. Finland runs a version of the same idea — civilian service there is roughly twice as long as the shortest military tour — except that people whose conscience rules out serving the state in any form at all, armed or unarmed, end up serving prison time instead. Sweden sits at the other end entirely: conscription is gender neutral, the whole cohort is on the register and gets screened, but only a few thousand out of a hundred-odd thousand actually end up in uniform, so for most people the duty is discharged by being available.

So what theory of conscience is the price-tag model operating with? It looks like it assumes the state can't see inside anyone's head and shouldn't try — but then what work is the surcharge doing? Is it a sincerity test, where a conviction counts as real only if you'll pay for it, or is it a penalty for ducking a shared burden, and does the practice have to choose? If willingness to pay is the measure, the Finnish total objector is paying the most and its the one being criminalized — can the model absorb that, or is it a straight contradiction? Tell me where the setup is genuinely underdetermined, i.e. which rival accounts of what a conscientious conviction is would license exactly the same rules, and what would have to change — the multiplier, the application form, who gets called up at all — if you swapped in a reasons-based examination, or the Swedish availability model.
