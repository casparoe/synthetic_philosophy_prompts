Right, I've been going back and forth with a friend who reckons my position on price controls is a mess, and I'd like a proper audit of it before I write the thing up. Here's what I actually believe:

1. Low-wage labour markets arent competitive in the textbook sense. Employers have real market power — they're wage-setters, not wage-takers — and I think that's the best explanation of why study after study fails to find the employment losses the standard model predicts when you raise the floor.
2. So the minimum wage is good policy. It lifts pay at the bottom without the usual efficiency loss, and to the extent it does cost something, that cost comes out of employers' rents rather than out of the productivity of the marginal worker.
3. On low-wage work more generally I take the line that a worker who accepts a job because it beats her alternatives isn't exploited in the sense that matters. I don't want to ban voluntary work at low pay, and I'm unpersuaded by the argument that any wage a desperate person accepts is ipso facto unfair.
4. Rent control in supply-constrained cities is bad policy. It shrinks and degrades the stock over time, it locks sitting tenants into units that are too big or too well-located for them, and the costs fall on people who havent moved there yet and have no way of organising against it.
5. In general: if you want to help poor people, give them money rather than interfering with a price. A transfer raises their command over everything at once; a price control distorts one margin and manufactures a shortage.
6. But I don't accept the usual escape hatch, which is that a policy leaving identifiable people worse off is fine because the winners could in principle compensate them and nobody ever actually pays. And the transfers I'd want — rental assistance on the scale we'd need, a proper income floor — simply don't get funded, so I'm not prepared to hold out for them.

Go through these pairwise. Which ones genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do? For the real tensions, rank them by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be. For the apparent ones, say why they dissolve on inspection. Then tell me which single change to the list would repair the most damage. I'm deliberately not saying which commitment I'd be most willing to drop — work it out from the set itself.
