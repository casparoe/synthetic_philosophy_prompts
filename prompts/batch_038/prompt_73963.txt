I sit on the curatorial panel of a national history museum, and at Friday's meeting we must settle the opening wall text for a gallery on the outbreak of war in 1914. Three drafts are before us. The first spreads causality across the alliance system, the mobilization timetables, and the miscalculations of that July, and declines to identify a responsible party. The second puts the weight on Berlin, citing the unconditional assurance given to Vienna in early July 1914, the December 1912 conference of the Kaiser with his military and naval advisers, and the expansive annexation programme drafted in the chancellor's office in September 1914. The third distributes responsibility but weights it toward Russian mobilization and the French determination to recover Alsace-Lorraine.

Our director backs the second, arguing that the first's even-handedness is not neutral: it revives the interwar insistence that no one willed the catastrophe, and thereby makes the treaty's war-guilt article look like a victors' fabrication rather than a contested judgment. Two members reply that blaming a nation is a category mistake — the decisions belonged to a dozen ministers and generals, most of them dead, dismissed, or discredited within four years, while the entity held answerable in 1919 no longer existed, and the one that pays today never made them. A sister institution is mounting the adjoining gallery, so our text cannot flatly contradict theirs.

Draft the memo I will circulate beforehand. It should recommend one text or a definite synthesis, state the historical and curatorial reasons a divided panel could actually argue with, and take the category-mistake objection seriously on its merits — explaining what it would take for responsibility to attach to a state at all, and whether our draft meets that standard, rather than dismissing the objection as bad faith.
