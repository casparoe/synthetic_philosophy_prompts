I run a small decision theory seminar for first-year philosophy postgrads — 16 of them, one 90-minute session, and essentially no budget (I can offer the winner a tin of biscuits, which they've made clear they care about far out of proportion to its value).

I want an in-class activity that gets them to feel ambiguity aversion in their own choices and then takes that footing away from them. What I'm after: everybody makes the bets, has their choices recorded, and then discovers that the person who flatly refused an ambiguous bet twenty minutes earlier will happily take it once it's framed or paired differently. Whether that shift actually shows up reliably in a room of 16 is part of what I want you to tell me straight, not paper over.

Two hard design constraints:

- No urns, no coloured balls, no decks of unknown composition, no jar of sweets anyone could count. Half the group have already sat through the standard apparatus as paid participants in a psych experiment and they'll be smug about it. Build the ambiguity out of something else — ideally ambiguity about which decision problem they're even in, rather than ambiguity about a draw from a device whose workings they've been told. Thats the version that actually bites in real life and practically never turns up in a textbook example.
- A second stretch where refusing the ambiguous option turns out to be exactly right, because the unknown was fixed by a person in the room who gains from their getting it wrong rather than by chance. They should meet the two stretches back to back, with the same nominal odds on the table, so they can feel the difference between a bias and a good heuristic — and so someone in the room argues about whether that difference lives in the world or in them.

Give me the full rules and materials, the running order with timings, an anonymous recording method that lets me put everybodys choices up on the board during the debrief, a scoring scheme that produces a genuine winner without money, and the debrief itself. In the debrief I want specific moments of the activity mapped onto the risk/ambiguity distinction, onto whether what they did actually violates anything (I want them arguing about the sure-thing principle and about whether it's even the right axiom to invoke here, rather than nodding at it), and onto the second stretch. Then tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time you run something like this — above all the danger that one or two of them reverse-engineer the design and play along to be helpful, contaminating everyone else's data — and be frank about which feature of real-world ambiguity the exercise simply cannot reproduce, so that I know what I have to argue for verbally instead.

Be committed: pick one design and defend the choices inside it. Don't hand me a menu of three variants with a note on the trade-offs; I want your version and your reasons, including why you set the odds and the stakes where you did. Lead with your bottom line — a single sentence saying what the activity is and what it's meant to make them feel — and then the rest. No preamble and no restating what I've asked for. British English throughout.
