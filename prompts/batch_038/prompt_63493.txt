I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about attribution — pigment analysis, X-rays, the whole lab end of it — and the spine of the piece is the van Meegeren affair, which everyone's heard of and almost nobody actually thinks through. Here are the facts as I have them, so you don't have to guess at any of it: 1937, he passes off a big religious canvas as a newly discovered Vermeer, the Supper at Emmaus; Abraham Bredius, then the reigning Vermeer authority, writes it up in a major art journal calling it "a wonderful moment in the life of a lover of art" and "quite different from all his other paintings and yet every inch a Vermeer"; a Dutch museum pays a then-staggering sum for it. After the war van Meegeren is arrested for having sold a "Vermeer" to Göring during the occupation, and to dodge the collaboration charge he confesses the thing is his own forgery — then has to prove it by painting another one under supervison while the authorities stand around watching. He's convicted of fraud rather than collaboration, gets a year, and dies before he serves it. The Emmaus is still in that museum's collection, now labelled as a van Meegeren and shown as one: the most famous fake in Dutch art history.

What I want is that episode run through the achievement account of forgery — roughly the Denis Dutton line, that what's criminal about a forgery isn't that the object is any worse but that it misrepresents a performance, a thing somebody actually did — and an honest report on what it reaches and what it doesn't. Two spots especially. First, the demonstration painting: nothing about it is deceptive, it was made precisely so it could be witnessed, and it's by all accounts a genuine technical feat — so what is it, a forgery, a fake, an original, or something the categories don't have a slot for? Second, the Emmaus today, which has picked up it's own provenance and its own audience as a celebrated fake — has a forgery managed to become an original, and should the achievement account have predicted that or is it an anomaly? And fold in the awkward bit: the Göring deception was cheered as patriotic while the museum deception was treated as a crime against art. Same lie about performance, opposite verdicts.

Then run it the other direction — what the episode does to the theory. Does it confirm it, strain it, or reveal that the view was never really built for cases where the fake is admired publicly as a fake? Be explicit about the mapping: who is the deceived party at each stage, what exactly is misrepresented, and when the misrepresentation stops. Where the theory just goes quiet, say so rather than filling the hole. No bibliography and no reading list — I don't want names dropped, I want the argument, and you can be blunt about which parts of the story resist.
