My two roommates have been arguing about jaywalking for four days and it ended up somewhere I couldn't follow, so I want you to referee it move by move.

Context: Owen crosses against the light constantly, including right outside our building. Nadia never does, even at 2am on an empty street.

(1) Owen said jaywalking isn't a moral thing at all, it's a local rule like which side of the escalator you stand on — no victim, no wrong. Nadia said that's false, at a busy intersection you make cars slam their brakes and you endanger the person walking next to you.

(2) Owen said fine, but take the empty street at 2am — no cars, no witnesses. A rule that only binds when someone's watching isn't morality, it's just nerves about getting caught. Nadia said it's not about being watched, it's free-riding: he wants drivers to stop for him, so he cant exempt himself from stopping for them. Fairness, not fear.

(3) Owen said that only works if you're actually a member of the scheme. He brought up Copenhagen, where he jaywalked all week with zero guilt, and offered a distinction he was definately pleased with: there's "playing the game," where you're a participant and breaking a rule is cheating, and "walking through the stadium," where you're passing thru and there's no game for you to cheat at. Visitors get a pass because locals don't expect them to know. Nadia said that's backwards — visitors are exactly the people who can't be relied on to police themselves, so the convention matters more for them, and choosing to go somewhere means taking on the trouble of finding out how people behave there.

(4) Owen said her line proves way too much: if visitors are obligated to follow local conventions, then a tourist is obligated to pay the bribe at the checkpoint, to not make eye contact with half the population, to tip the server who's already drawing a wage. Local custom launders anything. Nadia said no — coordination conventions (which side, queue order, shoes off) are one thing, conventions that are themselves unjust are another; you go along with the first and you may refuse the second. Owen's closer: that distinction is doing all the work, and she can't draw it without a moral standard coming from somewhere other than the convention — so the convention was never what was binding her, and "conventions create obligations" collapses into "morality creates obligations, and conventions sometimes line up with it."

Rate each of the four moves — clearly wrong, partly right, clearly right — and say who's ahead at each point, not just overall. Specifically: does the stadium distinction actually do the work Owen thinks it does, and does move 4 defeat Nadia or just change the subject? And what are they both missing that would have ended this sooner?
