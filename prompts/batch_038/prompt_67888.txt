I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background whatsoever, so forgive how I'm framing this. Please answer in British English, keep the jargon down, and don't give me a reading list or citations — I just want the argument.

Here's the situation. I work on a consumer app (call it 40m monthly users). Nothing ships unless it wins an A/B test. Concretely, the rules we operate under:

1. Every change to the product goes into an experiment. You declare a primary metric beforehand (activation, D30 retention, whatever), run it, and if the variant beats the control at significance, it ships. No further approval needed. "The data said so" ends every argument in design review.
2. If the result is inconclusive, nothing ships and the control stays where it is. Which means most of the app — everything built before this policy existed — has never been tested against anything, and nobody would re-run it.
3. When a variant wins, we record the delta and the p-value in a spreadsheet we call the graveyard. We don't record why it won. There is definately no field for it, and when someone tried adding one it stayed empty about 95% of the time.
4. Separately, there's a "design principles" doc owned by a small committee. It says things like *respect people's attention* and *we don't use dark patterns*. That committee can veto but can't propose anything.
5. Our feed ranking is now mostly a model, tuned against a metric. Ask anyone on the ranking team why the feed surfaces what it surfaces and the honest answer is "it moved the number and we don't know what it learned." But in company comms we say the feed "reflects what our community wants."

What started the argument: a new joiner asked in a retro why onboarding has four steps and a mandatory phone number field. Someone said "variant C lifted activation 2.1% in 2019." And he said, right, but that's not an answer to my question — that tells me what happened, not why it's any good. Half the room thought he was being pedantic. The other half (me) thought he'd put his finger on something we can't articulate.

So:

- What does this whole setup presuppose about what a reason is? It seems to assume a choice can be justified by a process even though no person anywhere holds — or could hold — the reason for it. The reason lives in the machinery rather than in anybody's head. Is there a coherent view of justification that permits that? What does it have to say about who or what the reason belongs to?
- Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? For instance, the thought that a reason has to be something someone can act for and be answerable to.
- Are the parts consistent with each other? Rule 4 seems to assume something opposite to rules 1 and 3, and rule 5's "reflects what our community wants" sounds like a claim that a group has a preference, when all we actually have is a metric that we picked. Where exactly do they collide, and can a company hold both without bad faith?
- Rule 2 is the one that bothers me most. We say everything in the product "earned its place by evidence," but we have no way to tell the tested winners apart from the never-tested leftovers. What theory of legitimacy does that slogan need in order to be even approximately true?
- Where is all of this underdetermined? My guess is that several quite different philosophical positions would license exactly the practice we have. Tell me which ones, and what would settle between them.
- Finally: if we decided that a reason has to be someone's reason — held by an actual person who can state it and defend it — what would have to change in the mechanics above? Be concrete about which rule breaks first and what the graveyard spreadsheet turns into.

If there's an established name for the view that reasons can float free of anyone who has them, mention it once so I can look it up later, but don't build the answer around names.
