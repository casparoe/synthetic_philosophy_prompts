The Ladder DNA Borrows

In the fake genetics Hofstadter sets up, a strand is just a string of the letters A, C, G, T. You read it two letters at a time, and each duplet names an enzyme; a few duplets are punctuation. The payoff is a strand which, read from the right starting point, names the enzyme that copies strands — Replicase, I think that's what it's called. So the strand carries the recipe for the machine that copies the strand. And the book's point is that this is the same shape as Gödel numbering: a string which, once you decode it, is about itself.

I believed that for about fifteen years. Then I tried to write down precisely what the two cases share, and the analogy kept sliding around under my pen. Here's what I think is actually going on.

In the theorem, the numbering is a notational trick. Nobody has to build it. You assign numbers to symbols, then to formulas, then to proofs, and the assignment is basically arbitrary — any workable scheme will do. It exists on the page and in the head of whoever is doing the proving. What makes the theorem go is not the numbering but the fact that arithmetic is rich enough to represent syntactic facts as arithmetic facts: the relation x is a proof of y becomes expressible, and then the diagonal move produces a sentence which, read back through the numbering, denies its own provability.

In the cell, the decoding isn't notational. Something has to physically do the reading, and the readers are themselves built to instructions. DNA polymerase copies DNA, and the gene for DNA polymerase is in the DNA. The ribosome reads messenger RNA and its parts are transcribed from DNA. The synthetases — the machines that actually enforce which amino acid belongs to which codon — are proteins, encoded in DNA. And there is no molecule that is the code table. The table is smeared out across a few dozen different machines with different jobs.

The wrinkle I find genuinely interesting: the catalytic core of the ribosome is not protein, it's RNA. So the machine that assembles proteins is itself a nucleic acid. Which suggests the two levels aren't two substances but two roles that one kind of substance can occupy, and that the neatness of the picture in the book is partly an artifact of drawing DNA and protein as separate boxes.

So here's the distinction I want to make. Call it a one-stroke loop when a single system carries both the description and the means of reading the description, so you never have to step outside. The Gödel setup is one-stroke: the numbering is written down once by a human, and after that everything happens inside arithmetic, including the proof of the theorem. Call it a two-stroke loop — a handshake — when the description of the reader sits on one tape and the reader for that tape is described on the other. Neither tape is self-interpreting. Together they bootstrap.

My claim is that DNA is two-stroke and could not be anything else, because the translation table isn't a string that could be appended to the strand. It's a set of affinities and machines. And that matters, because people slide from DNA is self-referential in the Gödelian sense to conclusions about life containing incompleteness, or about molecules meaning things. If the loop is two-stroke, none of that follows. What does follow is weaker but real: the unit of self-reference isn't the strand, it's the whole cycle. Cell to cell, generation to generation. The loop in biology is temporal. You don't find it in a tube at an instant, you find it across a life cycle. That's a different thing from the Gödel case and it buys you no undecidability at all.

To check whether I was fooling myself I built a toy. Two tapes, alphabet {0,1}, read in pairs. On tape A: 00 means build the reader described on tape B, 01 means run whatever reader currently exists on tape A, 10 means copy tape A onto a blank tape, 11 means stop. Tape B has the mirror image rules. Start condition: one of the two tapes arrives with its reader already assembled, plus a supply of blanks. I ran it by hand for a few generations. Three findings. First, the pair does replicate. Second, I could never delete the start condition — every variant I tried had to be handed one of the two readers, or handed blanks and a hand to move them. Third, if I fold the start condition into the description of the system then yes, the system is self-replicating, but by that standard a rock is self-replicating if you count the sun.

Moral of the toy: you can't manufacture a one-stroke loop out of a two-stroke loop by adding tape. You can only get one by declaring the environment part of the system, and at that point the word self has stopped doing any work.

Last thing, a diagnostic I want to propose half seriously. When someone says domain X is isomorphic to domain Y, try the deletion test: state the correspondence precisely, then delete the source domain and see what the target loses. Any theorem you couldn't prove, any prediction you couldn't make, any calculation that got harder. If nothing is lost, the isomorphism was decorative — a way of seeing rather than a way of proving. I suspect most of the isomorphisms in that book are decorative in exactly this sense, including possibly this one, and I don't think that's a criticism, because the book is about ways of seeing. But it does mean you can't cash an isomorphism in for a theorem.

Where I'm least sure of myself. Maybe I'm arguing with a position Hofstadter never held — maybe the book only claims the two setups share a shape, and the shape claim is true and interesting, and this whole essay is a straw man in a lab coat. Also, the code being near-universal across life might mean there's a respectable sense in which the table is one shared object after all, in which case my smeared across machines line oversells it. And I don't know whether one-stroke versus two-stroke survives contact with real logic: the Gödel numbering is also handed in from outside by a human being, so is arithmetic really one-stroke? Maybe the only difference between the two cases is who does the handing.
