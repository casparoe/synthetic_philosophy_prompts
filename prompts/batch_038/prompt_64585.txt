I'm the community manager for a mid-size open source project — ~400 contributors, nearly all volunteers — and every couple weeks someone asks me to change or kill an unwritten practice. So far I've anwered by gut and gotten it wrong at least once (a maintainer forked over it). I'd rather use something like Lewis's account of convention: a regularity people keep to because they expect everyone else to keep to it, where some other regularity would have served just as well. Turn that into a procedure my three moderators (none of us have any philosophy background) could run in about half an hour on a disputed practice, with the output being: is this a convention in that sense or a different kind of norm, would a plain announcement from the maintainers actually shift it, and if not, what would. Build in the theory-breakers on purpose — practices everyone privately resents but keeps doing because they figure the rest still endorse them, and practices where the alternative isn't arbitrary at all because one option dumps the work on a particular group. Then run it on three of ours: design discussion has to happen in a public issue before anyone opens a PR; commit messages follow a prefix scheme nobody ever wrote down; and whoever originally wrote a file gets to approve changes to it. Tell me where the procedure is really just my judgment wearing a philosophical costume, how confident you are in each of the three classifications, and which one you're least sure about.
