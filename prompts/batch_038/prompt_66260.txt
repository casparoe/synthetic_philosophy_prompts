For three years I have been revising the fourth draft of a novel, and I have settled on a way of describing what the work feels like: I can never consult the finished book from anywhere outside the book, every correction is made with the very instrument that requires correcting, and no sentence is exempt from revision except provisionally, for as long as I am standing on it. At a festival panel last month a philosopher in the audience said that this is exactly Neurath's boat — a crew obliged to rebuild its ship at sea, plank by plank, using the remaining structure as support and whatever driftwood comes to hand, never permitted to beach the vessel and begin again from the keel — and that the boat is the canonical image of coherentism. From it he drew a definite conclusion: that the regress problem is dissolved rather than answered, that justification amounts to nothing but planks holding one another up with no plank privileged, that the foundationalist's bedrock is a harbor which does not exist, and that the coherentist therefore owes no separate account of how the system as a whole hooks onto the world, since staying afloat is the only test available from inside.

A second panelist said he was reading the image backwards. Her objection had two parts. The sea will sink a badly rebuilt ship, so the boat retains precisely the external check that coherentism is accused of discarding. And the boat depicts how a body of belief gets repaired over time, whereas the regress is a question about what entitles a belief to count as justified at a moment.

Please audit the analogy rather than the positions. I would like the correspondences set out explicitly — plank, sea, harbor, shipwright, buoyancy, driftwood, the rest of the ship as support — each mapped onto whatever is supposed to play its role in the epistemology, and marked where the mapping is silent. Then tell me which disanalogies bear on the conclusion he drew and which are merely decorative; what the source quietly imports that the target must deny, given that a ship has a builder and a function and that its failure is not a matter of internal inconsistency; whether the diachronic–synchronic point is fatal or can be repaired by recasting the voyage; and whether the boat, read strictly, is an image of coherentism at all, or of some hybrid — Haack's foundherentist crossword, perhaps, or Peirce's walker on the bog, or Sosa's raft set against the pyramid. Finally, and selfishly: as someone who manufactures self-supporting systems of sentences for a living, I have candidate images of my own — the successive drafts, the manuscript whose early chapters are only legible in light of the ending. Tell me whether any of these could carry the weight without smuggling in the answer, or whether every analogy for the regress does.
