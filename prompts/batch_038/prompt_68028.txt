So I got into it with my friend Marcus last night — three hours in a diner, they started stacking chairs on us before we wrapped — about advance directives in dementia, and we both came out angrier without either of us actually saying the thing we meant. Marcus is a hospice nurse so he keeps landing at the bedside: his line is that a directive is a dead woman's paperwork being enforced on a living stranger, and that the autonomy being honored belongs to someone who's no longer in the room. Mine is that overriding it is just paternalism with better branding — she planned for precisely this situation under good conditions, and you're trading one deliberate decision for a pile of behavior nobody can read. Then he brought up the Dutch dementia-euthanasia case and I ran out of road, which is why I'm here. Facts, since the point isn't the facts but the mapping: a woman in her seventies with advanced Alzheimer's, in a care home, had written a euthanasia directive years earlier when clearly competent, asking to be helped to die once she was in a nursing home and could no longer recognize her family, though the wording was murky about who gets to choose the exact moment. The doctor sedated her through her coffee; she woke during the procedure and struggled; her husband and daughter physically held her down while the doctor finished. The national review committee found due care had not been observed, on the ground that the doctor should have checked the wish with the patient herself since she was still capable of expressing something; a disciplinary tribunal then found the doctor culpable; an appeals tribunal reversed and cleared her, holding the written directive was sufficient and that re-confirming with a patient in that condition wasn't required; prosecutors declined to charge.

Take that episode and run it through four rival accounts of what an advance directive actually is — treat them as genuinely competing theories, not flavors of one thing. One: a standing command that remains in force until revoked. Two: a Ulysses-style self-binding contract, where the whole issue is whether a later self's changed preferences void the deal. Three: an appointment, i.e. a transfer of decision rights to a document or a person rather than a statement of what to do. Four: mere evidence about the patient's values, carrying no independent authority, to be weighed against everything else in the room. For each: what it says the doctor faced, whether her conduct was permissible, which of the institutional verdicts it lines up with, and — the part I care about most — whether it can explain the flip between the first tribunal and the appeal without just declaring one of them wrong. My suspicion, which I want you to attack rather than flatter, is that the appeal court quietly switched from model one to model three and nobody said so out loud.

Then bring in the philosophy and tell me where it goes silent, because I think most of it does. Dworkin's split between critical and experiential interests: does her critical interest in the shape of her whole life survive the dementia strongly enough to outweigh a present self who was frightened and struggling but not, as far as the record I've seen goes, miserable? Dresser's objection that we systematically underweight the welfare of the demented patient actually in front of us: does the physical restraint sharpen it, or does the restraint sit outside it entirely, since the people holding her arms weren't her agents and no theory of diachronic autonomy says anything about who gets to authorize them? And Parfit — if what matters is psychological connectedness rather than identity, and connectedness has largely lapsed, does that cut against Marcus or against me? I keep finding it cuts both ways and can't tell if that's a real indeterminacy or just me being sloppy. Also: in the argument I coined a phrase I think I need and I want to know whether it's doing work or only sounding good — the standing asymmetry. The earlier self gets to speak through dated, notarized, legible documents and named proxies; the later self can only speak through behavior in the moment, which everyone concedes is hard to read and easy to discount, and our institutions are built to hear the first voice and treat the second as noise. Is that a real structural feature of how we handle autonomy across time or is it paternalism redescribed with a fancy label? And did the appeals ruling narrow the asymmetry or entrench it?

Bottom line first, please: your verdict on which of the four models best handles this case in sentence one, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of what I asked. Label the models as you go so I can argue with specific chunks, and close with a short take on what the episode shows about the theory of advance directives in general — confirmation, an anomaly it can absorb, or a case it never anticipated, which is what Marcus thinks. If one of my framings is bad, say so rather than working around it. Oh, and briefly at the end: does any of this transfer to Terri Schiavo, where there was no writing at all, just a husband's report of things she'd once said, or is that a completely different problem?
