I teach a third-year philosophy of mind seminar, 16 students, and they've all read the Dennett material on the four kinds of creatures -- Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian -- but they've clearly just memorised it as a ladder from dumb to smart. I want a 90-minute in-class game that makes them feel what the ladder is actually tracking: where selection happens and who pays for a wrong guess, not how clever anybody is. Room has tables, index cards, a couple of dice, and a whiteboard. No projector, no laptops or phones, no real money.

Here's the spine I want. Four teams of four, all trying to figure out and exploit the same hidden structure for points, but each team runs on a different architecture that you build into the *rules* rather than into a description they read:

- one team is hardwired. they get a fixed strategy card they aren't allowed to deviate from, and I want it to pay off spectacularly in one phase and then fall apart in the next, so they get to experience a competence they can't revise
- one team can only learn from real outcomes -- every probe costs them points, but the cost buys them the true answer, so they converge and they pay for it
- one team gets a sandbox: free or nearly-free practice trials, except the sandbox differs from the real task in exactly one respect they cannot detect from the inside, so their internal model ends up confidently wrong
- one team gets handed an artifact at the start -- a note or table the sandbox team produced in an earlier phase -- and skips discovery entirely. Their borrowed competence should look like genius right up until the point where they inherit the artifact's error, and only then

Payoffs on paper, and the accounting has to be simple enough that nobody spends the session doing arithmetic instead of thinking. Give me a seperate one-pager of rules per role, the full hidden structure of the task plus what the real rules are (so I can run it without lying to them by accident), the materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief. They shouldn't hear any of the four names until the debrief -- till then they're just teams. The debrief should map specific things that will have happened in the room onto the four architectures, and onto the question of what a Gregorian creature is really doing when it looks smart, i.e. renting someone else's selection history and getting the errors that come with it.

Then the honest part: tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this -- sandbox team getting suspicious about why their trials are free, hardwired team getting bored and breaking character, artifact team finishing early and heckling -- and what to actually do about each. And be straight with me about which of the four architectures the game can only imitate rather than instantiate, given that every student in the room is already a language-using creature role-playing something else, plus what I'd have to supply myself in discussion to cover that gap. If 90 minutes isn't enough for all four teams, tell me what to cut rather than squeezing it.
