You are a philosopher of science with a working command of the formal literature on truthlikeness — both the older accounts that define closeness to the truth in terms of what a theory gets right and wrong among its consequences, and the later ones that define it in terms of the similarity of possible states to the actual one. Please think carefully before answering. It would help if you began by brainstorming, briefly and for your own purposes, the full space of candidate concepts that might already cover the pattern I describe below, before you commit to a verdict about whether any of them actually does. I would rather have a considered survey of near-misses than a confident one-word label.

Some background. I am not a professional philosopher, though I read in this area, and I have been trying to get clear on how one false theory can be closer to the truth than another. To keep myself honest I have been working with the crudest possible bookkeeping: write a theory out as a list of claims, count how many of them are right and how many are wrong, and compare the totals. I know this is crude, and I know a serious account must handle the fact that a theory says indefinitely many things rather than just the handful of claims it happens to be written with. But I do not believe the difficulty below is an artifact of that crudeness, and I will explain why at the end.

A clinical panel tests a sample for two antibodies, P and Q. The standard report form has two boxes: is P present, is Q present. Suppose the truth about this sample is that both are present. Now consider three rival reports, all of them false:

- Report T: P is present, Q is absent.
- Report U: neither P nor Q is present.
- Report V: P is absent, Q is present.

On my bookkeeping, T gets one box right and one wrong; U gets both wrong; V gets one right and one wrong. So T and V are equally close to the truth, and both are closer than U. This matches my intuition: T and V each get half the picture, U gets none of it.

Now suppose a different laboratory information system records the very same panel on a different form, whose two boxes are: is Q present, and were the two results concordant — that is, did they agree, either both present or both absent? These two boxes are interdefinable with the original pair; from the new boxes you can reconstruct the old ones and vice versa. Nothing has changed about the sample, and nothing has changed about what T, U, and V claim: they are the very same three possibilities as before, and the truth is still that both antibodies are present.

But the bookkeeping flips. In the new vocabulary the truth is that Q is present and the results are concordant. Report T says Q is absent and the results are discordant — both wrong. Report U says Q is absent but the results are concordant — one wrong, one right. Report V says Q is present but the results are discordant — one right, one wrong. So now U and V tie, and T is strictly worse than both. A report that was strictly ahead of another is now strictly behind it, and a report that was tied with a third is now strictly worse than it. The only thing that changed is how the same content was parcelled into boxes.

Two further observations that I could not resolve on my own. First, I checked whether the trouble arises from any change of notation at all, and it does not: simply reversing the polarity of a box, so that it records the absence of P rather than its presence, changes nothing, because every report loses or gains symmetrically. What does the damage is replacing a box with a compound one. Second, I tried to reassure myself that this is an artifact of counting conjuncts, and that a more careful account — one that measures the distance between whole possible states and the actual state — would be immune. I could not convince myself. To measure such a distance you first have to settle along which respects two states can differ and how much a difference in each respect counts, and that looks like the same choice of boxes all over again. I may be wrong about this, and I would like you to check it rather than take my word for it.

What I actually want is this. I keep needing to separate two things that I suspect are usually run together under a single heading. On one hand, there is the relativity of closeness to the truth to a genuine choice of subject matter: describing a gas in terms of temperature and pressure versus describing it in terms of molecular velocities is a substantive change of question, and it may be entirely proper that verdicts of closeness differ. On the other hand, there is what my example seems to show, which is not a change of question at all: two vocabularies that are interdefinable, that pick out the same states of affairs, and that leave the compared theories literally the same propositions, and yet the ordering comes out differently. The first might be a feature of the subject; the second looks like a defect in the measure.

So, with that in hand:

1. Does the second phenomenon have an established name? Is there a recognized adequacy condition on accounts of closeness to the truth that is meant to rule it out, and what exactly does that condition require — invariance under what class of reformulations?

2. Is the distinction I am drawing between a change of subject matter and a mere re-parcelling of the same subject matter a principled one, or does it collapse on inspection? If it is principled, what makes it so, and does either side of it have a name of its own?

3. What are the nearest established concepts, and how does the phenomenon I have described differ from each of them? I am aware that some obvious neighbours will be suggested — the classical result that showed the original definition of closeness to the truth could never be satisfied by any two false theories, the various consequence-based repairs, and the similarity-based accounts. As far as I can tell none of these is about a verdict changing while the content stays fixed, but I want you to tell me precisely where each of them stops and mine begins.

4. If nothing established fits, coin a term. Give me a definition, a diagnostic I can apply to a case, and the boundary conditions: what would count as an instance and what would not. Specifically, would a change of subject matter count? Would rewriting a claim from conjunctive into disjunctive form count? Would a change of vocabulary that is interdefinable but not expressible without compound sentences count? Where exactly does the phenomenon begin and end?

5. Is the phenomenon avoidable by restricting attention to a privileged class of vocabularies? If so, what makes a vocabulary privileged without simply assuming the answer?
