I am in the second year of a dissertation on individuation, and this term I have been auditing a seminar run by a visiting metaphysician whose own position I would like to characterise accurately in a chapter I am drafting. Her commitments strike me as unusually hard to pin down, so I have kept a log of her verdicts on the cases the seminar worked through, in order:

1. Black's case — a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres a mile apart in an otherwise empty, perfectly homogeneous space: not a possible universe. Asked why, she said there would be no fact of the matter as to which sphere is which, and she would not go further.
2. A universe containing exactly one such sphere and nothing else: plainly possible, and it contains one sphere.
3. A universe built on a finite, unbounded space in which antipodal points are identified, so that a description positing two spheres each a mile from the other is really a description of a single sphere from which a mile's straight travel brings you back to itself: possible, but it contains one sphere; the two-sphere description double-counts.
4. A universe with two exactly similar spheres and a lone third body, one mile from the first and two from the second: possible, two spheres. She was explicit that relational differences suffice here, so no intrinsic difference is required.
5. A universe with two exactly similar spheres in a space that has an intrinsic grain — a preferred direction — each sphere differently aligned with that grain: possible, two spheres.
6. Three exactly similar spheres at the vertices of an equilateral triangle in an otherwise empty homogeneous space: not possible as described. Someone suggested that each vertex bears to the others an irreflexive and symmetric relation, of the sort that has been proposed as a way of weakly discerning putative indiscernibles; she rejected the suggestion, saying that symmetry in the relations cannot manufacture a plurality.
7. A world just like ours except that two exactly similar elementary particles have exchanged roles: not a world distinct from ours, merely ours redescribed. She said she treats exchange symmetry as a redundancy in the description rather than a difference in what is described, and when pressed on whether that commits her to there being fewer particles than the description claims, she declined to answer.

Next week a cosmologist presents a toy model, and I would like to anticipate her response. The model is an exactly homogeneous and isotropic, otherwise empty spacetime containing two structureless point particles of identical rest mass following precisely mirrored trajectories, so that both the state and the laws are invariant under exchanging the two particles; nothing in the model distinguishes one from the other. The feature the cosmologist stresses, however, is that the model's gravitational field is exactly twice what a single such particle would generate, and that this doubling is not a bookkeeping artefact but an observable ingredient of the world the model describes, since it is what supplies the seed perturbations for the structure formation he wants to explain. He expects to be told that his model is metaphysically inadmissible, and he has said in advance that if the exchange symmetry is treated as a mere redundancy, the doubled field still has to be attributed to something.

Predict her verdict on this model and defend the prediction. Specifically: (i) lay out the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with all seven verdicts, of which at least two should differ substantively rather than stylistically — my own suspicion is that a hard modal constraint against indiscernible duplicates, a rule on which symmetric descriptions collapse to fewer objects, and a rule on which the failure is descriptive rather than metaphysical all survive the log, and that something which counts invariant degrees of freedom rather than objects survives it too; (ii) say what each candidate predicts about the cosmologist's model — whether it describes a possible world at all, and if so how many particles it contains and what bears the doubled mass; (iii) tell me which candidate you think she is actually running and which entries in the log you are leaning on, including what verdicts 6 and 7 each rule out and what they leave open; and (iv) propose one further case I could put to her in the seminar that would discriminate between your leading candidates, saying what each candidate would say about it. You are welcome to connect the candidates to positions you know from the literature, but please do not supply references, citations, or a reading list; what I need is the rules themselves and the reasoning that favours one of them.
