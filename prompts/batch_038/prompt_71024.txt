I am assembling the third week of an undergraduate political theory seminar on alternatives to electoral representation, and after some argument with a colleague I have committed to opening the session with Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery." The unit is about sortition, and I want the students to have something concrete in mind before we reach the claims I actually want to press: that election was understood by the founders of modern representative government as an aristocratic device, chosen over lot precisely because it picks out the eminent, so that the identification of democracy with elections is a historical accident rather than a conceptual truth; that there is a serious epistemic case for the lot, resting on the much-cited result that a randomly assembled but cognitively diverse body of problem-solvers can outperform a body made up of the individually best problem-solvers, and on the observation that campaigns select for visibility, fundraising capacity, and rhetorical performance rather than for anything we want in a decision-maker; and that the Athenian practice was not naked randomness at all, but lot for most magistracies drawn from a self-selected pool of citizens over thirty, annual terms that could not be repeated in the same office, a pre-entry scrutiny of citizenship and character that assumed competence rather than testing it, a compulsory audit at the end of the term, and election retained exactly where expertise was thought indispensable — the generals. My colleague thinks the story will make those stakes vivid. My worry is that it will make them too easy: I have twenty-two students, several of them student-government types, and I expect half the room to leave saying that Jackson has settled the question against random selection before we have begun.

So I would like a real critical reading rather than a summary. The village's procedure is recognizably a lottery — a two-stage draw, first by household and then within the household, conducted from a list that is carefully made up the night before by the same man who runs the square dances and the Halloween program and who is sworn in by the postmaster for the occasion — and yet almost everything that made Athenian allotment an institution of rule rather than of sacrifice is missing: there is no office, no term, no scrutiny, no audit, no reason-giving, and no possibility of refusal or re-draw. Work through what the story does with that subtraction. Consider the composition of the pool, which nobody argues about even though it is the only genuinely political decision in the story: married daughters draw with their husbands' families, and a small child is handed a slip and made to draw for himself. Consider Tessie Hutchinson's sequence of complaints — that her husband was rushed and did not have time to choose the paper he wanted, then that her married daughter should be folded back into the Hutchinson draw, then, only once the marked slip is hers, that the whole thing is not fair and not right — and say what theory of legitimacy that sequence presupposes: is the story endorsing the view that the only admissible objection to a lot is that the odds were mismeasured, and what would an objection to the purpose have looked like in that square? Consider also that the villagers are perfectly capable of reforming the means while the rationale decays: the paper slips that replaced the chips of wood when the population outgrew them, the perennial talk of building a new box that nobody acts on, the chant and the salute and the original paraphernalia long since lost, the neighboring village that has abandoned the practice entirely and is called a pack of fools by the oldest man in town, who remembers nothing about why it is done beyond a proverb connecting it to the harvest. Ask whether that is an argument that institutions outlive their justifying theories, and whether it can be turned against contemporary sortition proposals at all, given that those proposals are almost nothing but justifying theory plus a mechanism. And press the distinction I suspect the whole reading turns on: Jackson's lot allocates a cost, not an office. Both are chance-allocations of something no one wants to distribute by merit or by vote, and we do allocate burdens by lot elsewhere — jury service, conscription, the rationing of a scarce organ. Tell me what theory of fairness licenses allocating a burden by chance, whether that theory can be cleanly separated from the one that licenses allocating power by chance, and whether the horror of the story depends on running the two together. Relatedly, the village has maximal rotation and no career anything: nobody serves twice, nobody accumulates office, and it is monstrous. Does rotation do any normative work at all where there is no right of refusal and no exit?

Please give me criticism with a verdict I can act on. Tell me whether the story should be taught as a live test case for sortition, as a hostile caricature of it that I should frame with explicit warning labels, or as essentially orthogonal to it — a story about tradition, scapegoating, and complicity that happens to use a drawing as its machinery — and defend whichever verdict you reach against the strongest version of the other two. Be fair to Jackson in both directions: she said her aim was to shock readers into recognizing pointless violence and general inhumanity in their own lives, not to comment on methods of appointment, and the lot may be doing purely literary work, as the engine of a twist whose power depends on the reader's assumption that a lottery is a way of giving something away. Say plainly where you think I am over-reading, and where the story resists the political-theoretical lens rather than rewarding it. Point me to particular moments rather than retelling the plot, which my students will know, and do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or lengthy quotation from the text; I have the story in front of me and I am not asking you to source anything. End by telling me what would change your mind: which single consideration, if I pressed it, would move you from your verdict to one of the other two, and where in your own reading you think you are most likely to be forcing the story to carry a philosophical payload it was never built to carry.
