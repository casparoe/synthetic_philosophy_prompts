A workshop on vagueness ended, as such workshops do, with two participants agreeing to co-author a public statement of exactly where they stand. Draft that statement. The parties are, first, a committed epistemicist, who holds that vague predicates have sharp boundaries fixed by ordinary use, that bivalence holds without exception, and that our inability to locate a boundary is a purely epistemic limitation to be explained by something like a margin-for-error constraint on knowledge; and, second, a committed supervaluationist, who holds that a borderline utterance is neither true nor false, that truth is supertruth — truth on every admissible precisification — that this is precisely what preserves penumbral connections, and that vagueness is semantic indecision rather than ignorance.

To keep both parties honest, they have agreed to conduct the entire statement in terms of one constructed example rather than the usual bald men and heaps. The example: a certification panel of 99 trained inspectors classifies 1,000 consecutively produced ceramic tiles, arrayed in the order of a continuously varying surface measurement, as first-quality or not. Stipulate that each inspector applies the term with a sharp personal cutoff — inspector *i* classifies tile *n* as first-quality if and only if the measurement is at or above a threshold *c_i* — that the individual thresholds are spread across the range, that no inspector's threshold is published, and that the panel's official verdict on each tile is by simple majority. The official predicate therefore has a sharp boundary at the median threshold, while exhibiting exactly the profile that makes the sorites tempting: adjacent tiles can differ in the official verdict only through a single inspector's vote. Both parties must also address two variants. In the transparent variant, every inspector's threshold is published and known to all. In the waver variant, no inspector has a sharp cutoff at all: each is instead disposed to reverse their verdict on repeated presentations of tiles near their own threshold, so that the majority verdict drifts over time even with the same membership.

Produce the following.

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign: about the sorites argument itself, including which premise fails and in what sense; about the phenomena any theory owes an explanation (apparent borderline cases, the stability of clear cases, penumbral connections, higher-order vagueness, and the pattern of verdicts competent speakers give when required to classify every item in a series rather than permitted to abstain); and about what the panel example does and does not show, including whether it is a case of vagueness, a case of ignorance, or a case of neither. No claim may be one that either party would wish to annotate or qualify.

2. A disagreements table with four columns: the question at issue; the epistemicist's formulation, worded so that an epistemicist would sign it; the supervaluationist's formulation, likewise; and a joint classification of the difference as substantive, verbal, or a disagreement about which explanandum is central.

3. The cruxes — sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one party — and for each, the case, variant, argument, or experimental design that both accept as bearing on it, with each party's prediction recorded in advance. At least one crux must concern whether there is any non-circular account of what makes a precisification admissible, or, from the other direction, any determinate mechanism by which usage fixes one cutoff rather than another; at least one must concern the transparent and waver variants, and what the difference between them shows about whether vagueness can be identified with ignorance of a sharp boundary; and at least one must concern whether the two views differ on any question whose answer could in principle be discovered, given that both agree no speaker could ever be in a position to say which of them is right about a particular borderline tile.

4. A short section stating explicitly which parts of the dispute the parties jointly judge to be verbal — including whether "neither true nor false" and "true or false, but unknowably so" are doing the same work in the two theories — and which parts are not, with reasons.

5. For each party, what would count as evidence or argument against their own view, and an honest statement of whether there is anything both agree would actually end the disagreement.

6. A closing paragraph on what the two views jointly imply, and jointly fail to imply, for an institution deciding whether to write a numeric threshold into a rule.

Constraints: no survey of the literature, no adjudication, no winner. Each party's formulations must be ones that party would accept as a statement of their own position and would not accept as a description of the other's. Where a familiar objection begs the question against one party, the statement should say so rather than smuggle the objection into an agreed claim.
