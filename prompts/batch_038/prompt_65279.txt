Abhidharma-style reductionism about persons holds that a person is nothing over and above a causally connected series of momentary psychophysical events, and that 'person' is a mere designation imposed on that series, much as 'chariot' is a mere designation imposed on an assemblage of parts. Critics have pressed a disanalogy: the parts of a chariot all exist at once, so a single act of awareness can take in the whole collection the label is fastened to, whereas the moments of a mental series never coexist. By the time any designation is made, the moment designated is already past, and no single awareness ever encounters the series as a whole.

Please explain what is and is not at stake in this objection. Does it show that the reductionist owes a unifying subject after all, or only that the chariot is a poor illustration of a view that can be stated without that illustration? What precisely is the notion of a causal series being asked to do in the reductionist's account — and why is it not sufficient to reply that the awareness doing the designating is itself just another moment in some series? Finally, is this the same objection that gets pressed against Hume's bundle of perceptions, or a different one, and where exactly does the difference lie if so? I want the conceptual anatomy of the problem rather than a survey of the historical debate or suggestions for further reading.
