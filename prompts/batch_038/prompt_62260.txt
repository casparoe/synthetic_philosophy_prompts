I'm putting together a small zine about formal epistemology and I want a piece for it: a safety data sheet — the proper GHS 16-section kind — for a commercial product that lets a layperson update on evidence they aren't actually certain of. The premise is that it's sold as a concentrate to amateur rationalists who've been told to "just update on the evidence" without anyone mentioning that their evidence essentially never comes with probability one.

Invent the supplier, the product name, the address, the emergency helpline. No real people and no real companies, and please don't name or cite actual philosophers, papers or textbooks — I don't want a reading list smuggled into section 16, and I don't want you guessing at page numbers. Where a real sheet would give a CAS number, a concentration range or an occupational exposure limit, invent an analogue and make it earn its place (a "rigidity limit" would be the obvious move, but there's room to be cleverer).

Use the standard headings in order — Identification; Hazard identification; Composition/information on ingredients; First-aid measures; Fire-fighting measures; Accidental release measures; Handling and storage; Exposure controls/personal protection; Physical and chemical properties; Stability and reactivity; Toxicological information; Ecological information; Disposal considerations; Transport information; Regulatory information; Other information — with a signal word, a couple of pictogram descriptions, and parody H-codes and P-codes in section 2. You can compress the ones with least to say (ecological, transport) into a line each, but all sixteen numbers should be present and in order. Somewhere between 800 and 1000 words.

The humour has to come from getting the technical content right, not from the format being silly in itself. As a minimum, I want at least four genuine difficulties with this way of updating to show up as hazards, incompatibilities, storage conditions, handling precautions or first-aid advice — for instance: the user has to supply a partition that's mutually exclusive and exhaustive, and the sheet's advice on what to do when they can't; the conditional probabilities get held fixed through the update, which is exactly the wrong behaviour if the evidence turns out to be genuinely surprising; the user has to already know their new probabilities on the partition, which is often as hard as the thing they wanted the product for; repeat applications don't commute, so the order you take your evidence in changes where you end up; and ordinary conditionalisation is the limiting case for evidence that is certain, which should turn up somewhere as a cheaper, better-characterised, more stable competing product that this supplier is quietly snobbish about. Hit those or better ones, but a specialist reading it should conclude the writer actually understands the method rather than having heard the name once.

Deadpan all the way through: no winking at the reader, no "of course this is all a metaphor", nobody stepping out of the format to explain the joke. It also has to read as a real sheet first and a philosophy exercise second — someone with no maths at all should be able to follow it and find it weirdly plausible, in the way that a plausible-sounding document about a substance they've never heard of is plausible. Two specific bits I'd like if you can find room: first-aid instructions for a user whose credences have already gone to exactly zero and one and can't be revived by any further application; and something in section 16 about a previous formulation having been withdrawn, with the reason left tactfully vague.

British English please, including the spellings — -ise endings, rigour, behaviour, and so on — and put the supplier somewhere plausible-sounding in the UK or the EU.
