I'm helping run a grad seminar on the philosophy of AI this term and I've got to write the final take-home. The students have done a unit on machine consciousness and a unit on RL, so they know the basics of MDPs, value functions, and the general shape of the "maybe reward signals are functional analogues of pleasure and pain" proposal. What they haven't done is think hard about whether that proposal is even well-posed.

Write me one essay question, answerable in about two hours at 1200–1500 words, worth 25 marks. The stem should state in plain terms whatever technical facts the students need — I don't want this turning into an RL exam — but it should be built around at least two specific ones: (a) that for fixed discount factor and policy class, replacing r with a·r + b (a > 0), or adding a potential-based shaping term of the form γΦ(s′) − Φ(s), leaves the ranking of policies, and therefore everything the agent actually does, unchanged; and (b) that the quantity driving learning in temporal-difference methods is the reward *prediction error* rather than the reward, so in a fully predictable environment that error goes to zero no matter how much reward is flowing through.

The crux I'm after is this: if reward is valence, which of these quantities is the valence — and what happens to the view once you notice that reward's functional role fixes it only up to a scale and an offset, so the view looks unable to say whether the agent is suffering at all, only whether things are going better or worse than before. I want students to have to commit: is that fatal, does it force a retreat to some weaker structural claim about valence, or does the missing structure have to come from somewhere other than the reward function — and if it does, is the original view doing any work left? Wireheading should come in as a test case somewhere, but it shouldn't be the whole thing, and note that shaping transformations change the prediction errors while leaving behaviour alone, which is a nice way to make (a) and (b) collide.

Please give me the question, a model answer of around 800 words, and a marking scheme. The scheme should list five must-hit points with marks totalling 25, say what separates an A from a B by naming the actual moves rather than just "more nuance," and flag three or four mistakes that are especially common and should cost marks — for instance sliding between reward, return and value function as though they were one object, or treating reward hacking as if it settled the metaphysics. No bibliography or reading list; I'd much rather they argued from the concepts.

Oh, and I'm affraid the question can't just be a bare "critically evaluate the claim that..." — make it a concrete puzzle with a determinate shape so I can tell who's actually thought about it.
