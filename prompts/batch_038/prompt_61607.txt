You are a philosopher of mind with substantial experience serving on institutional animal-use review boards — someone who can hold an explanatory taxonomy of cognitive capacities and a regulatory decision rule in view at the same time without collapsing one into the other. What follows is a live case in my own institution. Please think it through carefully before you write anything, and treat the diagnosis as no less important than the recommendation.

I am a professor of philosophy, and I chair my university's animal welfare and ethical review board, which sets our manual of practice and hears appeals from the local committees of three institutes. Our manual is deliberately stricter than the national framework we operate under: that framework reaches vertebrates and cephalopods and no other invertebrates, whereas our manual extends its protections to "any non-human animal capable of learning from experience," and separately requires that a protocol use the least cognitively elaborate species adequate to the scientific aim. Both clauses were drafted fifteen years ago by a committee that had read Dennett's taxonomy of kinds of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian creatures — and thought it would supply a principled line. It has not.

The case before us. A comparative cognition group proposes a three-year study of aversive discrimination learning in three arms: honeybees, individually housed in small flight cages across repeated training days, with sucrose reward and a mild electric shock to the tarsus as the punisher; a salticid spider species, which the investigator defends as an upper-arthropod case on the grounds that these animals appear to evaluate a detour route before committing to it; and a woodlouse arm, described in the application as a lower-rung control showing only habituation and the crudest conditioning. Housing, enrichment, and endpoint criteria are specified for the bees but essentially absent for the spiders.

The board has split three ways over what our clause means. Reading A takes it literally: anything above the purely hardwired is covered, which sweeps in bees, spiders, and probably woodlice, and which strips the second clause of all content, since a boundary that admits nearly every animal gives us no way to prefer one species over another. Reading B holds that the clause was always meant to pick out animals carrying an internal model of their surroundings that can evaluate alternatives before acting — the rung at which an animal can be wrong in a way that costs it something, and at which confinement in a featureless cage can deprive it of the exercise of a capacity it has. Reading C wants a further tier for particular individuals whose capacities have been scaffolded by human training: our partner facility's two apes working with a lexigram system, an African grey, and a dolphin under a gestural command convention. The difficulty with C, as I see it, is that what these animals can be deprived of, and what they can be deceived about, is a property of their biographies rather than of their species, and our manual is keyed entirely to species lists.

Two objections from members I must answer. First, a comparative psychologist on the board argues that Reading B is empirically empty: talk of internal models and evaluation-before-action is, she says, a redescription of higher-order conditioning, the same animal deploys model-driven and habit-driven strategies interchangeably depending on training history and cost, and any line drawn there will be administered inconsistently across three local committees of non-specialists. She would keep the clause at Reading A and put all the normative weight on refinement — cage volume, foraging opportunity, social housing, shock parameters. Second, a colleague in ethics thinks cognition is simply the wrong axis: the only question that matters is whether the animal can suffer, and building a manual on a ladder of cleverness is both unfalsifiable in practice and a respectable way of ranking beings by their resemblance to us.

My own hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that the ladder is nested — every higher rung contains the lower ones — so it can yield a boundary but never an ordering, whereas our replacement principle needs an ordering. If that is right, the board has been arguing for fifteen years about where to draw a line when the manual actually requires a scale the taxonomy was never built to provide, and the persistent urge to read the rungs as degrees of moral worth is the urge to convert an explanatory taxonomy into an axiology.

Draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting, roughly 900 to 1,200 words, in the register of a document a committee could vote on. It must:

1. Open with a bottom line on the protocol — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and, if conditions, conditions specific enough that the investigator can tell whether she has met them: numbers, housing volumes, enrichment, shock parameters, humane endpoints, and the triggers that would bring the work back to us mid-study. The spider arm should not be allowed to proceed on the strength of an unspecified husbandry paragraph.
2. Include a diagnosis section, placed before the recommendation's supporting reasons, stating what the board's disagreement actually consists in and which of Readings A, B, and C could be administered by non-specialists without drifting into inconsistency.
3. Propose redrafted clause language for the manual, together with a decision procedure for contested taxa: who bears the burden of proof, what would count as evidence for or against placing a species above the line, and what the default is when the evidence is unsettled — which is the situation for most arthropods, including the two in this protocol.
4. Give the two dissenting members their strongest case in your own voice rather than a straw version, and say which of the two you take to be the more serious threat to the drafting exercise, and why.
5. Say what your recommendation costs us: which animals it leaves outside the manual that Reading A would have protected, and whether that loss is a defect of your proposal or a defect of the clause as written.

Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind, and do not attribute to any author a claim I have not already put in front of you. Do not restate the taxonomy back to me — I teach it every year — but do be precise about which rung each arm of the protocol plausibly occupies and how confident anyone should be in that placement. Begin by thinking aloud for a few lines about the possible readings of our clause and what each would commit the university to; you may fold that reasoning into the diagnosis section rather than presenting it separately.
