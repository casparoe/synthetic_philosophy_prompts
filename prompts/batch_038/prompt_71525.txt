I run conversational copy review at a mid-sized health insurer. Every new chatbot feature, template rewrite and guardrail tweak comes through me before it ships — call it 30-40 batches a quarter, some of them a single string. Right now our review is basically "does any sentence assert something false or legally binding," because that's what legal trained us on. But we keep getting burned by things the bot never actually says. A member asks whether their plan covers physical therapy, the bot answers that many plans in their area include physical therapy benefits — literally true, nobody commits to anything — and the member walks away believing they're covered, shows up to a clinic, eats a $900 bill. Or the bot declines to answer a drug-interaction question and the member reads the refusal as a warning.

Internally I've taken to calling these "shadow claims": the thing the user ends up believing that no sentence in the response actually states. I know this is roughly Grice's territory — implicature, the cooperative principle, all that — but I don't want a seminar and I don't want a survey. I want a screening procedure my reviewers can run. The reviewers are two content designers and a paralegal, no linguistics background, about fifteen minutes per batch, and legal signs off at the end, so anything the procedure escalates needs to come with a one-line statement of what legal is being asked to decide.

Please write it as ordered tests, in the order a reviewer should actually run them, phrased so someone who's never heard of Grice can apply them to a draft response. For each test: what to look at in the text, what counts as passing, what fix a failure licenses, and whether a failure means "block" or means "hand this to a human." Then answer these inside it, not in a separate essay:

- Which pieces of the Gricean machinery have to survive the simplification and which you're deliberately dropping, with reasons. I'd rather have four distinctions that hold up under a content designer than fifteen that get fuzzy in practice.
- Whether a disclaimer actually cancels an implicature or sometimes makes it worse. I have a strong suspicion some of our standard hedges are backfiring, and I want the procedure to be able to say so.
- Where the procedure is structurally blind — implicatures that only exist given what a particular user already knows or fears, which we can't audit in advance no matter how careful we are.
- Whether any of this applies to a machine at all. Grice's picture is a speaker with intentions and an audience who recognizes them; our bot arguably has neither. If the framework needs a patch to cover that case, tell me what the patch is and what it costs. If it doesn't survive, say that plainly instead of papering over it.

Then run the procedure on three drafts. All invented, but close to what we ship:

1. Member: "does my plan cover physical therapy?" Bot: "Many plans in your area include physical therapy benefits. You can view your full summary of benefits in the member portal."
2. Member asks about a prior authorization for a knee scan; the real status in the system is "pending, not determined." Marketing loves this one and has fought for it twice: "You're all set — just arrive ten minutes early and bring your ID!"
3. Member: "is it okay to take this antibiotic with the blood thinner I'm on?" Bot: "I can't tell you whether that combination is safe. Please check with your pharmacist before your next dose." Legal wants to append: "Nothing here should be read as a warning about any specific medication."

I expect to lose on number 3 and I'd rather find out here than in a complaint file. If the procedure clears something I think is terrible, or kills something I think is fine, say so and tell me why — don't soften it to match my expectations.

Format: give me the procedure first as a table with these exact columns — Step | What the reviewer asks | What they look at in the text | Pass condition | Fail action (fix / block / escalate) | Judgment call? (and if yes, what the human needs to know to decide). Then the three worked cases as prose. Then, after everything, a JSON array with one object per case using exactly these fields:

- `case_id`: "1", "2", "3"
- `literal_content`: what the response strictly says, nothing more
- `shadow_claims`: array of objects, each with `claim` (what the user is likely to come away believing), `trigger` (which maxim, sub-maxim, or textual device generates it), `confidence` ("high"/"medium"/"low"), `context_dependent` (true if it only arises given what this particular user already knows)
- `verdict`: "ship" | "ship_with_fix" | "block"
- `fix`: the replacement wording, or null
- `disclaimer_helps`: true/false/"depends", plus a `why` string
- `escalate_to_human`: the one-line question for legal, or null
- `procedure_cannot_settle`: what's left over that the tests can't decide

Valid JSON, no comments in it. And skip the citations and reading list — I can't chase them and my reviewers definitely wont.
