I'm putting together a session for an upper-level aesthetics seminar — mostly phil majors, basically zero literature training — and I want to use The Picture of Dorian Gray as the case study for the aesthetic testimony debate. Not the usual art-for-art's-sake-versus-morality take; the epistemology one. Can you find out on somebody else's say-so whether a thing is beautiful, or does the acquaintance principle bite and you have to go look for yourself?

Here's my worry, and the reason I want a real reading instead of a summary. Dorian Gray looks like a novel about testimony, but its testimony running backwards. Lord Henry hands out aesthetic verdicts constantly and never gives a reason for a single one, never revises, and nothing ever checks him — by Wollheim's lights he's a total fraud — and yet his testimony demonstrably works: it changes what Dorian sees when he looks at a sunset. Meanwhile the portrait is testimony about Dorian that isn't language at all, that only one person at a time can verify, and the plot is basically Dorian refusing to believe it. So which of the two does the book vindicate, and does the novel know it's staging that question?

What I want you to do is work through the actual testimony economy: Henry's talk in the garden, Sibyl's bad performance and what she says afterward about finally knowing what real acting is, the yellow book Dorian treats as an instruction manual for his own experience, Basil showing him what the picture has become near the end, and that moment where Dorian tells Henry the news of her death felt like a tragedy in which he took a great part but by which he has not been wounded. For each, say whether the Kant line — no grounds can compel an aesthetic judgment — illuminates it or just doesn't fit. And answer the question I keep getting stuck on. In Hume's story about the standard of taste the connoisseurs are vindicated when the key with the leather thong turns up at the bottom of the cask. Is there anything in Dorian Gray playing that role, some check that finally settles whose aesthetic judgment was competent — or is the total absence of one the point?

Then push back on me. I suspect the honest answer is that Henry is a character and not Wilde's mouthpiece, that a Gothic fairy tale with a magical portrait is the wrong place to hunt for an epistemology of testimony, and that Dorian is the one person in the book with first-hand acquaintance with everything and far too much of it, so calling him a victim of somebody else's testimony may be exactly backwards. If that's right, say so, and then tell me where the reading survives anyway. Related: the preface calls criticism a mode of autobiography and says it's the spectator, not life, that art mirrors — which sounds like a defense of the critic as testifier. Does the novel contradict its own preface?

No need to quote passages back at me, I know the book — just tell me which scenes to put in front of the class and why. And end with what the novel gives this debate that the Hopkins/Robson/Meskin articles don't: something you can only see by following one man through twenty years of taking other people's word for things.
