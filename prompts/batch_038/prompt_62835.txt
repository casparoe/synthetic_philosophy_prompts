I have been working through the formal dispute between utilitarians and prioritarians from a welfare-economics background rather than a philosophical one, and I want to test an inference I believe I have drawn correctly.

Here is my reconstruction of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. Each individual's preferences over prospects are representable by a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function, unique up to positive affine transformation; the social ranking over the same prospects is likewise von Neumann–Morgenstern representable; and the unanimity requirement — that society prefer any prospect that every individual prefers — together with anonymity forces the social utility function to be an unweighted sum of the individual ones. Prioritarianism, by contrast, ranks prospects by the sum of a strictly concave transform of each individual's well-being. A concave transform of the utilities is not an unweighted sum of them, so prioritarianism cannot satisfy the theorem's premises. Anonymity is plainly not the premise it surrenders: nothing in the priority view treats people differently according to who they are. So it must be unanimity.

Two questions follow, and I would like them answered directly rather than hedged. First: which unanimity-violating verdicts does the prioritarian have to live with, and how damaging are they? I have tried to construct a two-person case in which every affected individual prefers one prospect to another while the prioritarian social ranking prefers the second, and I have not managed it, which makes me suspect that any such violation must be exotic enough to count decisively against the view. If the examples exist, give me one with explicit probabilities and well-being numbers and tell me whether it is a curiosity or a pattern; if they do not exist, tell me exactly where my description of the theorem has gone wrong, since the linear-sum conclusion looks to me like a straightforward consequence of the premises I listed. Second, and less importantly: Arrow's theorem rules out any social welfare function that is unanimous, non-dictatorial, and satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives. If Harsanyi's result is compatible with Arrow's, is the sole reason that Harsanyi works with cardinal utilities instead of ordinal preference orderings, or does the reconciliation demand further assumptions?
