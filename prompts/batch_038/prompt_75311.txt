So I run a Wednesday night discussion group out the back of our local library — about fifteen of us, none of us ever went to uni, we just read stuff and argue about it. We've done six weeks on the Anthropocene and I want to set them a proper written thing to finish on, partly becuase otherwise it all turns into vibes and nobody changes their mind. Could you write me the question, a model answer of maybe 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that says exactly which moves a top answer has to make and which ones should cost marks? They can have a week and their notes, so it needs to be the kind of thing where looking stuff up doesn't help.

Here's what I don't want: a question you can answer by listing the candidate start dates. They all know the ones that get pushed — the bomb fallout layer in that lake in Ontario around 1950, the 1610 dip in CO2 that some people tie to the collapse of Indigenous populations after contact, the Industrial Revolution, farming way back. And they know the geologists formally voted the epoch down in 2024. So put the crux somewhere else. What's been nagging at me is that the geologists look like they were right by their own rules — they need something global and permanent you can actually point at in a layer of rock — and historians keep using the word anyway and nobody thinks that's stupid. So what are the two camps even disagreeing about? Is it a factual question about when humans became a planetary force, a question about what a period is for, or a question about who gets to do the naming and therefore who ends up blamed? I also think there's something in the fact that the marker that's easiest to detect isn't the cause anyone actually cares about — fallout versus coal versus colonisation.

Build the question round a short made-up scenario if that makes it sharper, with some friction in it, and plant one thing that should tempt people into a trap — like deciding it's all just politics and stopping there. No reading list at the end, they won't read it and I couldn't check it anyway.
