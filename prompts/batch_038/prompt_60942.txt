Every year our foundation's methods panel has to decide whether a grant proposal is allowed to claim that culture caused something. It got contentious a few years back when we funded a project revisiting Weber's protestant ethic thesis, and now the panel is roughly split between people who think values can be causes and people who think any appeal to a value is just a redescription of the outcome with a fancier label. Our rulings so far, for the record:

- Approved: variation across districts in how early factory-inspection laws got adopted, explained by which side of an old confesional border a district sat on, using a discontinuity design and 19th-century census shares.
- Rejected: a country's growth takeoff explained by a Confucian work ethic — no measure of the ethic anywhere, and no comparison case where the ethic is present without the growth.
- Approved with conditions: turnout gaps explained by a norm of civic duty, with duty measured by individual survey items; we made them show the item wasn't just turnout wearing a hat.
- Rejected: firms adopting a management practice explained by the professional identity of their managers, where identity was coded from interviews conducted after the adoption.
- Approved: a famine relief program that worked in one province and failed in the next, explained from archival records of what local officials wrote at the time about who deserved aid.
- Rejected: regional wage gaps explained by employer prejudice, where the prejudice was inferred from the wage gap itself.

Now the proposal in front of us. Within one European country, self-employment and small-business formation are higher on the historically Protestant side of an old internal border, still true today, controlling for income, schooling, urbanization and even soil quality across the border. The religious variable comes from 19th-century census denominational shares, so it's measured independently of the modern outcome. But there's no belief data at all — no surveys, no diaries, no sermons, no interviews — and the authors themselves note the gap is just as large in districts where religious practice has effectively vanished, which means the mechanism they offer (family socialization into an ethic of calling and worldly diligence) is neither measured nor plausibly still operative. They're asking for money to establish the effect, explicitly not to find the mechanism.

So: shortlist it, send it back with conditions, or reject? What I actually want is the reasoning. Which principles are our six rulings jointly consistent with — there's more than one, and I suspect at least one of them can't handle the relief-program case, which tells you something. Then say what each of those principles predicts about this proposal, give me your recommendation and which precedent is genuinely the closest analog rather than the most flattering one, and if the precedents really do underdetermine it, name the principle we'd be committing ourselves to by going each way. Last thing: tell me what extra detail about this proposal would discriminate between the principles, or what hypothetical eighth case would, if you think the panel should be building one deliberately.
