I sit on the science advisory panel of a funder that pushes animal-welfare money around across taxa. This cycle's shortlist includes crickets, farmed shrimp, octopus, some nematode thing a grad student is lobbying for, and — against my better judgement — a cnidarian proposal. Before we read one more proposal I want the spec, not the theories: what does an account of how consciousness is distributed across animals have to deliver to count as usable by us?

So write the desiderata list. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and mark whether it's non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp — because a lot of what gets demanded in this literature reads like camp prejudice to me, e.g. insisting the animal show something like self-awareness or a unified point of view before it gets counted. Tell me which of those I'm wrong about.

Cases the list has to explicitly cover, because our shortlist keeps generating them:

- the octopus arm: an octopus keeps most of its neurons out in the arms, and a detached arm will still orient and wrap around things. one subject or nine? does the arm get any weight of its own, and how would a theory even answer that?
- siphonophores and the other colonial animals: thousands of clonal zooids, some do the swimming, some the stinging, some the digesting, and the colony is the thing that behaves as a unit. where's the subject, and does treating it as one rather than as thousands change how much welfare we think is at stake?
- holometabolous insects — larva and adult share almost no body plan, most larval tissue gets broken down in the pupa. does the theory owe us an answer on whether the same subject persists, and does it matter practically given that the larval stage is usually the one thats farmed at scale?
- cryptobiosis and diapause: tardigrades in a tun, brine shrimp cysts, overwintering larvae. sentience that switches off for months and switches back on. is the requirement about persistence, or just about getting the aggregation right?
- fission cases like planarians, where one animal becomes two: does the counting-subjects question belong inside the theory of consciousness or over in the axiology? the list should say which, because we keep arguing about it.
- C. elegans: 302 neurons, mapped, extremely well characterised avoidance behaviour. your list has to say what a theory is required to do with the animal where we know the most and learn the least from knowing it.
- nociception-ish responses without anything like a central brain, and central brains without any obvious nociceptive machinery — so the requirement can't just be "it needs both."

Some requirements I already suspect belong on there, correct me where I'm wrong: it has to say what counts as evidence, not just what consciousness is; it has to come out the other end as something we can actually multiply by — weights or credences, not yes/no — and say what to do when it can't tell; it can't smuggle in vertebrate-specific machinery (opioid modulation, layered cortex, a particular kind of learning) while advertising itself as substrate-neutral; it has to survive convergent evolution, i.e. it can't lean on shared ancestry with us; and it has to be statable by people who disagree about the metaphysics entirely, since our panel has a global-workspace person, an integrated-information person, and a lab vet who doesn't care. If that last one isn't actually achievable — if any workable list ends up presupposing one camp's picture of mind — I want you to say so plainly rather than paper over it.

And the part I most want: if some subset of these requirements can't be jointly satisfied, show me the argument. My suspicion is that "must produce a determinate weight for every taxon on the shortlist" already fights with "must not go further than the evidence licenses," and that "must be substrate-neutral" fights with "must actually exclude something" — the more neutral your markers get, the less they rule out, until the theory says nearly everything with a nervous system is in. If that's a real dilemma and not just my confusion, that's the finding.

Last: tell me which single requirement does the most work knocking out candidates, and rank the list for us, because our protocol writers are going to cut anything that can't defend its place.
