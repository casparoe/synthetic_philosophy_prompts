I sit in on an informal AI-policy reading group, and last week two members got into a real argument about commitment races — roughly, the idea that when the prize goes to whoever makes a credible commitment *first*, you end up with a race to commit rather than a race to build.

Nadia (1) said the group should put its effort into pushing one leading lab to publish a hard commitment: a named capability threshold that automatically triggers a six-month pause, no discretion left in it. Her reason was that in a commitment race the first credible committer sets the terms everyone else has to react to, so getting a well-meaning lab in first is the highest-leverage move available.

Owen (2) said that gets the mechanism backwards. A promise to pause doesn't win anything — the prize still goes to whoever builds first, and a lab that was never going to commit just ignores the threshold. Worse, publishing it tells competitors exactly where the leader stands.

Nadia (3) replied she never claimed the pause wins on its own; the commitment is what makes a *mutual* pause negotiable at all, since nobody can agree to something they can't be held to. Owen (4) said a commitment that only pays off if others match is precisely the kind that isn't credible, and non-credible commitments don't set terms — so her proposal is either not credible or not conditional, and she has to pick.

Nadia (5) said fine, unconditional — but then Owen's alternative is doing nothing, and in a commitment race doing nothing isn't neutral, it hands the prize to whoever commits to *not* pausing. Owen (6) said thats the whole fallacy: there's only a race to lose if commitments are credible in the first place, and credibility is exactly what Nadia's campaign would be manufacturing.

Neither gave an inch and I couldnt referee it. Can you go through the six moves and say who's right at each one, then say who's more right overall and what both of them are missing?
