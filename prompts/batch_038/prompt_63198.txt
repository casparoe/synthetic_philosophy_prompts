I'm in a philosophy-of-social-science seminar and our take-home this week was basically "is Popper's case against historicism any good?" I went in pretty sympathetic — I like the Poverty of Historicism move where he says that what historicists advertise as laws of history are really just trends, extrapolations of what has been happening, and that unconditional prophecy is out of bounds for science. But working through it I wrote down an argument that starts from his own reasoning and ends up somewhere I really don't want to be, and my study partner says the argument is fine and I should just bite the bullet. Here's what I've got:

1. The growth of our knowledge is one of the main causal drivers of historical change — new techniques, new institutions, new beliefs about what is possible all come out of it.
2. If there were genuine laws of historical development, then knowing those laws plus the present state of society would let us predict the future course of history, at least in outline and at least in principle.
3. Nobody can predict, by any rational or scientific method, what our own future scientific knowledge is going to be, because having the prediction just is having the knowledge — you can't be handed today the content of a discovery nobody has made yet.
4. So we can't predict the future course of history, and so there are no laws of historical development. Historicism is dead.
5. But notice that (3) never actually mentions history. What it says is that a system whose behavior depends on its own future knowledge of itself can't be predicted from the inside. Any science whose subject matter includes its own practitioners has that property: psychology, sociology, economics, political science, and the parts of history that study decision makers who are themselves reading history and forecasting.
6. So by the same reasoning none of those sciences can have predictive laws either. Not weak laws, not tendency laws, not laws hedged with ceteris paribus clauses — nothing that would license saying what will happen.
7. That can't be right, or Popper is at war with himself, since he wants to keep piecemeal social engineering and the situational-analysis stuff he proposes for the social sciences, and both are supposed to be empirical, criticizable, and to support the kind of if-you-do-this-then-that expectation you'd need before you change a policy. He also wants to be able to say a social theory failed a test.
8. Therefore the argument meant to bury historicism also buries the human sciences and Popper's own method, so his anti-historicism is self-defeating.

My partner's reaction was: so what, maybe (6) is just true and Popper was inconsistent, or maybe he'd say the social sciences are predictive only the way weather forecasting is predictive and that's enough — and then he asked why I'm so attached to (7). I couldn't answer him cleanly. Later he muttered something about chaotic systems being unpredictable but still perfectly law governed, which sounded too easy to me and also like it conceded half the argument. What I want from you is a diagnosis, not encouragement: which step do you actually reject, and why did it look innocent when I wrote it down? If the slide from "we can't predict X" to "nothing lawful governs X" is the culprit, then say what is left of (4) once you block it, because (4) is the whole point of the exercise and I'd rather keep it than keep (7). If instead the cheapest move is to reject (2) — deny that laws of development would have to license prediction at all — say that plainly and tell me what a defender of Popper gives up by doing so, and whether "law" is then doing any work or is just a label for a well-confirmed regularity. And does reflexivity help or hurt? A forecast people react to can be self-fulfilling or self-defeating, and I keep coming back to things like the Lucas critique in macro, where models stop working once policymakers start targeting the variables they were built to explain, or Goodhart's law about measures turning into targets. Those are usually stated as *findings*, which is uncomfortable for (6), but they're also exactly the "the system knows about the prediction" phenomenon that (5) leans on. One more piece of context in case it matters: a classmate argued that demographic transition theory and the epidemiological transition look like laws of history that actually predicted things ahead of time, and the professor said that was question-begging unless you can show those generalizations survive being known — which I think is (3) all over again, just applied to one case. Rank your options for me at the end: which rejection is cheapest, which is most honest, and which one I'd be fooling myself with. And if your verdict is that I should keep (6) and give up on prediction in the human sciences entirely, then describe what such a discipline would actually consist of and whether that's a coherent picture or just an empty name for "we describe things carefully."
