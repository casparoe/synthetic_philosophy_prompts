I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine and I'm currently drafting a long piece on what an individual can properly be blamed for — a supermarket rotisserie chicken, a short-haul flight, a skipped ballot — and the philosophers I've been interviewing keep telling me, politely, that I'm arguing like an epidemiologist rather than like an ethicist. I think they're right about the diagnosis and wrong about the implication, because the desk I came from was public health: I spent six years reading cohort and case-control studies for a living, and the vocabulary I actually think in is exposure, dose and dose-response, relative risk, attributable fraction, population attributable risk, confounding, exchangeability, the prevention paradox, and Geoffrey Rose's distinction between sick individuals and sick populations. I also know the potential-outcomes apparatus — average treatment effect, the fundamental problem of causal inference, the impossibility of observing both futures for the same unit — and I use it without help.

So here is what I want from you. Take the standard moves in the debate over individual contributions to collective harm and restate them, one at a time, in that vocabulary — as faithfully as you can manage, not as a way of demonstrating that epidemiology already contains the answer. Four claims specifically. First, the inefficacy premise itself: because producers adjust to demand only in threshold-sized increments, my purchase of one chicken changes nothing about how many birds are raised and killed, and an act that changes nothing cannot be wrong on consequentialist grounds. Second, the expected-harm reply: there is no sharp threshold in the supply chain, so any single purchase carries some small probability of triggering another batch, and a small probability multiplied by an enormous suffering is still a morally serious quantity. Third, the participation or complicity reply: the wrongness of my act does not ride on its causal output at all, since I am taking part in a practice, and taking part can be wrongful even when it is causally inert. Fourth, the claim that the collective harm is entirely real — animals suffering, fisheries collapsing, warming accumulating — even though no individual act makes a difference, so the objection has established something strange rather than something exculpatory.

For each, I want to know what translates cleanly, what you had to bend to make it fit, and what has no expression whatever in my vocabulary; please keep the translation honest and not smuggle in epidemiological assumptions and then report them as findings. Then deal with the sentence my draft currently hangs on, which an ethicist friend says is too clever by half: that the inefficacy objection is simply the ecological fallacy wearing a moral costume, because it reads an individual-level null result as though it settled a population-level question. Is that right, or does the fallacy run the other way? And price out the harder suspicion I can't shake — that my framework has its own structurally unanswerable question, since unit-level causal attribution is out of reach by construction and epidemiology simply agrees not to ask it, which would make the philosophers' quarrel over whether my act harmed anyone less a moral problem my vocabulary is too thin to express than a measurement problem both sides have quietly abandoned. Answer in exactly five numbered items and nothing else, no introduction and no conclusion: items 1 through 4 corresponding to the four claims above, each naming its closest counterpart in my vocabulary and giving a verdict of clean, distorted, or untranslatable; item 5 answering the ecological-fallacy question and the measurement-problem suspicion together. One or two sentences per item — this is going into a sidebar, so it has to be tight.
