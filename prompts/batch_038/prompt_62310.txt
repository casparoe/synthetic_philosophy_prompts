I am sixty-one, and this July I retire after nineteen years as head of Ashcombe, a school of about six hundred pupils aged eleven to eighteen. I have to give the speech at the final assembly of the summer term, on the last Friday of June, in front of the whole school, the faculty, and a gallery of parents and alumni. That day is when we hold the Line. The Line is this: the entire school forms a single file from the chapel door down the drive to the river gate and stands in it without speaking while the head walks the length of it and greets every pupil individually, by name. It takes four hours. In some years it has taken four hours in the rain. Nobody leaves the Line; there is no written rule about this, and no sanction from the staff — the rule is enforced entirely by the disapproval of the other six hundred people standing there. It began in 1921 and our archive does not say why; there are two competing stories, one involving a train and one involving a punishment, and neither is documented. Last June a girl in the fourth form, who had been in and out of hospital since the spring, stepped out of the Line after two hours. Someone photographed her walking back up the drive alone, and the photograph went around the school for a week. She left us in September. I have known her since she was seven. In October I decided that this year's Line would be the last, and I have told nobody at the school yet.

Here is my difficulty, and it is not a sentimental one. I read Lewis on convention as an undergraduate and never once connected it to my job until this year; now I cannot stop connecting it. My understanding of the account, which you are welcome to correct or sharpen, is roughly this: a practice of this kind binds nobody by authority, and holds only because each of us expects the others to conform and therefore has a reason to conform ourselves; the particular form it takes is arbitrary, in that any number of alternatives would serve the same purpose just as well; and yet its arbitrariness does not make it optional, and its age adds nothing whatever to the reason for keeping it. What has kept me awake is the consequence I draw from it, namely that a head of school cannot abolish a convention from a stage. An order is not the sort of thing that dissolves an expectation held by six hundred people at once; if I simply forbid the Line, it will migrate, and it will migrate with a grievance attached. What dissolves an expectation of that kind is that six hundred people simultaneously come to know that six hundred people now know something else, and come to know a particular alternative that is obvious enough to be where everyone goes. So the speech cannot merely announce a decision. It has to be the mechanism: it has to manufacture common knowledge in the room and supply a focal point, in about eight minutes, in language an eleven-year-old can follow. I should add that I am the one person in that building with standing to do this. I wrote the history of the Line for its seventy-fifth year, I have told parents at open days that it is the finest thing we do, and I have walked its length nineteen times. My own daughter is in the third form and asked me in March whether she will ever get to stand in the Line; I have promised her the speech will not embarrass her.

Please write the speech — about eleven hundred words, spoken aloud, no stage directions beyond the minimum. It must announce that this year's Line is the last, and it must name what replaces it, concretely and in the same breath. I have a candidate: invert the thing, so that one adult stands still at the river gate for twenty minutes while the school walks past and is greeted, and any pupil who wishes to stop and say something may. I want you to either use that or propose a successor that meets three conditions I care about — that an alumnus would recognise it as a descendant of the Line rather than its replacement, that it is over in half an hour, and that it does not require any child to hold a position they cannot leave without being seen leaving. Then the constraints, which matter more than the content. It must not lie: I cannot say the Line was worthless, I cannot say that everyone always knew they were free to step out, and I would rather not say it will live on in our hearts unless you can make that something I actually believe. It must not insult the gallery, where my predecessor will be sitting — he is eighty-eight, in a wheelchair, marched in the Line in 1956, and defended it for forty years. It must not name the girl who left, and it must make clear why it does not, without naming her. The theory should be load-bearing and invisible: you may say the word convention, you may name Lewis once at most, and only if it earns its place; the moment this becomes a philosophy lecture to six hundred children I have lost them. Two members of the faculty have already told me privately that if the school withdraws its blessing they will hold the Line themselves next year, without us; the speech should leave them no grievance to organise around and should make their doing so look like a continuation rather than a schism, and if you think that cannot be done in eight minutes, say so plainly rather than straining at it. Finally, it should end with an act performed in the room that constitutes the switch rather than describing it. Nineteen years of prize-givings have made me allergic to uplift, so keep the register dry and let the argument do the feeling. After the speech, add a short note to me — a paragraph, no more — identifying the sentence or two where you think the common knowledge is actually produced, and the place where you think a room of teenagers is most likely to lose the thread.
