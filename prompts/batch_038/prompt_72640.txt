My paper defends an account of rule identity that I think dissolves the standard paradox of self-amendment, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the workshop where I present it — not a defense of the view, which I already have, but a rehearsal of what will be thrown at it.

The view, in brief. Legal and ludic rule-systems maintain a register: numbered rules in Nomic (the game Suber drafted for Hofstadter's Metamagical Themas column in 1982, with its immutable and mutable tiers and its requirement that an immutable rule be transmuted before it can be touched), articles in a code, Article V in a constitution. On my account, a rule's identity is its address in that register, not its content; amendment is an assignment operation that writes new content to a live slot. Four consequences, all of which I argue for in the paper:

1. What looks like self-amendment is not vicious self-application, because the authority invoked is the content occupying the slot at the moment of the write, while the content written takes effect only afterward. The two never occupy the slot together, so nothing is ever in the position of saying of itself that it is and is not in force.
2. Ross's puzzle — the claim, in his 1969 Mind article, that a constitutional clause authorizing its own replacement cannot validly exercise that authorization — presupposes what Suber calls the inference model, on which validity is transmitted from premises to conclusion. On the register view validity is not transmitted but is a standing property of occupancy: an amendment is valid if it is a well-formed write to a live slot executed by the procedure then in force. That is supposed to deliver the result without Kelsen's presupposed basic norm, without Hart's acceptance, and without Suber's see-saw device of two alternating amendment clauses.
3. The view is not merely deflationary. It predicts where real reflexivity trouble remains: precisely where a rule refers to its own content rather than to its address — self-entrenching clauses that describe what they entrench, sunset clauses whose expiry conditions are stated in terms of their own text, provisions declaring themselves unamendable insofar as they provide for some specified thing. Those are diagonal constructions, and for them the paradox bites. Hence a drafting norm: write change-procedures address-referring and never content-referring.
4. The corollary I find most interesting: a versioned rulebook that identifies provisions by content hash, as several legal-tech and DAO governance systems do, is not the same artefact as a slot-based register, because content-addressing makes every provision self-describing and re-imports the diagonal. Choosing the data structure of a code is choosing which self-reference paradoxes that legal system must live with.

The room will be about thirty people: mostly American constitutional law professors, several of them originalists whose live interest is Article V, self-entrenchment, and the clause forbidding the deprivation of a state's equal suffrage in the Senate without its consent; three legal philosophers working in the Hart–Kelsen tradition who know Suber's book closely; two formal people, one in deontic and dynamic epistemic logic and one who works on theories of truth and self-reference; a comparative constitutionalist who studies eternity clauses such as the one in the German Basic Law; and four or five computational-law researchers, one of whom has played in long-running online Nomics and knows exactly which of my predicted pathologies actually occur in practice. Forty-five minutes of talk, thirty of questions.

What I want: eight to ten objections, ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it, each labelled with the faction that raises it and phrased in that faction's own idiom — the originalist will not put the point the way the truth-theorist will, and neither will put it the way the Nomic veteran will. For each, a reply I can actually deliver in two minutes, an explicit note on where the reply is soft or where it quietly redescribes the problem instead of solving it, and a judgement about whether the right move is to concede something immediately or to refuse the framing. Tell me which objections are sport, asked to see whether I have read the literature, and which would leave the room thinking the paper commits a category mistake. At least one constitutionalist will demand a doctrinal payoff and another will say a children's parlour game cannot tell us anything about a constitution; give me a crisp answer to each. I especially want your assessment of the formal objection I fear most: that the address/content distinction is pragmatic rather than expressive, since in a finite, fully known register any address-referring rule can be reformulated as a content-referring one, so the diagonal is always recoverable and my dissolution is a notational convenience. Say plainly whether that succeeds, and if it does, give me the most honest ninety-second answer available. Also prepare me for the Hartians' collapse charge — that occupancy is itself a social fact and so I have smuggled acceptance back in under a new name — and tell me which single concession, made in my first five minutes, buys the most credibility for the rest of the hour.

Finish with the question I most hope nobody asks, and the best partial answer I could give it without abandoning the view. Please do not append a bibliography or invent references; everything I need should be phrased as it will be spoken in that room.
