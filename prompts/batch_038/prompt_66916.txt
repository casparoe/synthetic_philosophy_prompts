I TA a required first-year humanities sequence, and at our school that means half my section is acting and directing majors who needed the credit and grabbed the 9 a.m. slot. Next week is Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the theater -- the long one where he goes after d'Alembert's suggestion, in his Encyclopédie piece on Geneva, that the city would be better off with a playhouse. They'll have read an abridged version, I've got 75 minutes, fifteen students, whiteboard only, and honestly I expect the room to come in hostile in a personal way, like some dead Genevan is attacking their future paycheck.

Don't explain Rousseau to me, I've got the argument. What I need is a plan for teaching it. Which piece has to be in place before any of the theater material can land at all -- my instinct is that it isn't "here's what Rousseau thinks about plays" but something about what a small republic asks of its citizens, and that the Geneva/Paris contrast is doing work that people read right past -- and then the one example I should carry the whole section on. I was going to use something from our own campus: the student-run midnight movie series that the adminstration wants to formalize into a real screening room with a paid programmer and reserved seating, versus the all-school thing on the quad at the end of term where everybody's in it and nobody's really watching. Tell me whether that actually holds the weight or whether it quietly turns Rousseau into someone he isn't. What does a good carrying example here need to have, and what does it need to lack?

Then the misconception map, and specifically the point in the sequence where each one gets headed off rather than argued with at the end. The ones I'm bracing for: Rousseau is a Puritan who wants to ban plays; he's making a media-effects claim, so if you can't show that watching bad things makes people do bad things he's refuted; his festival alternative is just better theater with the roof off; and the one I suspect will be hardest, which is that the students who agree with him will agree for the wrong reason -- audiences are passive and get manipulated -- which is a very contemporary complaint and not his. And since I'm teaching actors: is there a way to put the objection to acting itself, the paid-pretending point, to them that doesn't collapse into "so you should feel bad about your major"? Close with one question I can ask in the last five minutes that would tell me whether they got the argument or just the vibe, and tell me what I should flat-out cut at this level and say out loud that I'm cutting.
