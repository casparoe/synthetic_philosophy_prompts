I am commissioning a piece of short fiction for a collection on distributive ethics, and I would like you to write it. The situation is the one philosophers since Nozick have called a utility monster: a maximizing allocation scheme confronted by one person whose capacity for wellbeing per unit of a scarce good is orders of magnitude greater than everyone else's, so that the arithmetic says give her everything, and the arithmetic is not obviously wrong.

Use the following setting, which you may flesh out but should not alter in its essentials.

The harbor city of Ostrane produces a fixed annual quantity of a synthetic enzyme, administered in monthly doses, without which its residents sicken and eventually die. Distribution is handled by the Ledger Office under a charter clause requiring whichever allocation maximizes total assay-verified yield. Yield is reported in welfare units by a standard assay calibrated against a reference panel of forty volunteers whose median yield was fixed by convention at 100 units. A new resident, Odile Sarr, returns a reading of 60,000 units per dose against that median of 100.

Three further features of the world matter to the plot. First, the charter permits recalibration only once every five years and only on a panel re-drawn by lot, so the scale is frozen within an allocation cycle; a technician can show that Odile's advantage is largely a product of the conventions used to convert honest instrument reports into a single cardinal number — the zero point, the unit, the transform — and that a monotone rescaling preserving every individual's own ordering of outcomes shrinks her claim to nothing. That demonstration will be correct and inadmissible. Second, Odile asks to be struck from the roll, but the assay records her anguish at receiving the doses as suffering, and suffering raises her measured need; the instrument has no way to register a waiver. Third, once her reading is public, other residents find ways to raise their own genuine readings — fasting, stimulants, conditioning — so that the city's total measured yield climbs while its inhabitants deteriorate.

Treat the instrument as honest: it reports accurately whatever it measures. Do not get the story out of its problem by making the assay faulty, the numbers fake, or the units meaningless. The difficulty lies in what the readings license once they are summed, and in the conventions that make summing possible.

Write the story as exactly five numbered scenes, no more than about 200 words each, dividing the material among them as you judge best. Observe these constraints: the argument about calibration must be enacted as a procedure — someone re-running the numbers on a transformed scale in front of someone who cannot use the result — rather than delivered as speech; no character may hold forth for more than a few sentences at a time; do not use the phrase utility monster, name any philosopher or book, or cite anything; and end on a decision that costs the people making it something they wanted to keep, not on a resolution that vindicates one side. No title, no epigraph, no framing introduction, and no closing note or moral — begin directly with scene one and stop where scene five stops.
