QUESTION AS SET

Q3 (15 marks). Paul is stranded in a desert, out of water, with no phone signal. A driver comes along who is willing to take him to the nearest town, but only on one condition: that Paul sincerely promise to send him twenty pounds once he gets there. The driver is an unusually shrewd judge of character — he can reliably tell a sincere promise from an insincere one, and he will simply drive off if he detects insincerity. Paul knows that once he is safely in town, sending the twenty pounds will serve no purpose of his whatsoever: he will never see the driver again, there is no realistic chance of his being found out, and nobody else's opinion of him is at stake.

(a) [4] Set out the case and say exactly what work the driver's shrewdness is doing. Would Paul face the same problem if the driver could not tell a sincere promise from an insincere one?

(b) [6] Explain what goes wrong for an agent who decides each act separately, at the moment of acting, on the basis of what best serves her interests then. Is the diagnosis essentially about the agent's lack of a way to bind herself, or is it about something else?

(c) [5] In Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle an eccentric millionaire offers you a million pounds if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a vial of mildly poisonous liquid at noon tomorrow. The millionaire has an infallible brain-scanning device that detects your intention at midnight, and he pays up the following morning whether or not you actually drink. The toxin will make you ill for a day but will not kill you. Does the toxin puzzle raise the same problem as Parfit's hitchhiker? Give reasons.

SCRIPT

(a) Paul is stuck in the desert and the only way out is a promise he has no interest in keeping once he's safe. The driver's shrewdness does the real work here: it's what rules out the easy option. If the driver couldn't tell a sincere promise from an insincere one, Paul would just say the words and never send anything, and there would be no puzzle at all, only ordinary cheap talk. So the shrewdness makes sincerity necessary, and sincerity requires that Paul actually be someone who will send the twenty pounds — you can't fake it, because faking it is precisely what the driver is on the lookout for. Without the shrewdness the case would collapse into a normal assurance problem, the kind you'd sort out with a contract or a deposit.

(b) Take an agent who decides each act at the time of acting, on the basis of what best serves her interests then. She is standing in town with the ride already paid for. Sending twenty pounds now benefits nobody she cares about and costs her, so she keeps the money. She ends up worse off than she could have been, and not because she miscalculated at any point — she calculated correctly at every point separately. That's the sense in which a theory can be rational act by act and still be self-defeating.

The way this usually gets put in the decision theory literature is that the hitchhiker is a case where evidential decision theory beats causal decision theory: the evidentialist sends the money, because sending it is good news about the sort of person she is, whereas the causal decision theorist sees no causal upshot and walks off with her twenty pounds. Worth noting that the question rules out moral reasons for keeping the promise, so this is a purely prudential puzzle and can't be dissolved by saying Paul simply ought to pay.

Is the diagnosis essentially about her inability to bind herself? I'd say yes. Paul has no binding device. If he could sign something with a penalty attached, or leave the money with a third party who'd release it only on proof of performance, the problem goes away — so what's missing is commitment technology rather than a new account of rationality. It's rational for Paul to want to be the sort of person who sends the twenty pounds, and equally rational for him not to send it when the moment comes, and I think that's the whole of the paradox.

(c) Yes, they raise the same problem. In both cases the payoff turns on your mental state rather than on your act — the driver cares about whether the promise is sincere, the millionaire's scanner about whether you intend to drink — and in both cases you know in advance that when the time comes you'll have no reason to do the thing. So the solution is the same in both: get yourself to be the sort of person who has the relevant state, rather than trying to produce the state directly. Kavka's scanner is just a more reliable version of Parfit's driver, which makes the case cleaner rather than different in kind. If anything the toxin is the easier of the two, since a day of feeling awful is a smaller price than twenty pounds and the effort of posting a cheque.
