My brother had his corpus callosum cut when he was 15 to stop the seizures, and I've been reading about split brains on and off ever since. Write me a dialogue between two people who disagree about what that leaves you with. One says that after the operation there are genuinely two subjects of experience in the skull — two points of view, two sets of perceptions. The other says there's still one person, whose experience is disunified and badly stitched together, but numerically one. Both need to be sharp: no strawmen, and nobody wins just by the other one running out of breath.

Set it in a testing room while a patient does the usual things — a word flashed to the left visual field so only the right hemisphere gets it, patient says out loud they saw nothing, then the left hand reaches out and picks the matching picture from a lineup. Also the cases where one hand buttons a shirt while the other unbuttons it.

What I actually want them fighting over is this: could anything ever settle the count? My own idea was that if one half can be honestly surprised by what the other half is doing, that definately looks like two of them in there. But I figure the other side will answer that surprise only proves there's no access, and that a single person can be cut off from their own contents too — like stuff you can be primed by without ever seeing it. Have them take that seriously and push past it rather than trading it back and forth. Is there any difference in principle between one disunified mind and two minds that an observation could detect, or is the whole quarrel about where to draw a boundary in something that has no boundary?

They should genuinely shift ground at least once each. End with a line starting "Verdict:" saying in a few words whose position comes out stronger — or that neither one does.
