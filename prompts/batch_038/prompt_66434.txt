So here's a thing I've been chewing on since a reading group session went sideways, and I want to get it out of the trolley sandbox entirely. Everyone I know who's read the DDE literature has read it through vignettes that were purpose-built to produce the dialectic — Foot's trolley, Thomson's loop, Kamm's several hundred pages of refinements — and I've started suspecting the whole intended/foreseen distinction is partly an artifact of the fact that the cases come pre-specified, with the causal structure already drawn in and no uncertainty about who's on the tracks. So: real episode, actual institution, actual document. Germany passed an Aviation Security Act in January 2005 whose §14(3) authorized the armed forces to shoot down, as a last resort, an aircraft being used as a weapon against human lives. Six people — four lawyers, a patent attorney, and an airline captain — brought a constitutional complaint, and on 15 February 2006 the First Senate declared the provision void (1 BvR 357/05). It went down on two independent grounds. One was federalism-flavored: the Basic Law lets the federation call in the military for a "grave accident," and the court accepted that a hijacked jet aimed at a building counts even though the disaster hasn't happened yet, but it doesn't let them show up with specifically military weapons. The other is the interesting one: shooting the plane down violates the right to life in conjunction with human dignity *insofar as innocent people on board would be affected*. The court's own summary of that reasoning is the sentence I want you to work on: "By the state's using their killing as a means to save others, they are treated as mere objects, which denies them the value that is due to a human being for his or her own sake." Note the limiting clause in the holding — it's about who's in the aircraft, not about what's in the pilot's head. For the American control case: on 9/11 there was a shoot-down authorization coming down from the White House, badly garbled in transmission, and at least two pilots went up with no live ammunition expecting to physically ram the aircraft; United 93 went down in Pennsylvania before anyone had to test it.

First half of the ask is the mapping, and I want it disciplined rather than gestural. Is shooting down a hijacked airliner the strategic bomber or the terror bomber? Walk me through it on the classic Anscombe/Foot formulation, then on Quinn's revision about direct agency and whether the victim is caught up in the agent's plan, then on Kamm's stuff, then on Thomson's deflationary reading, and say where each lands and whether any of them lands where the German court landed. Deal explicitly with the closeness problem — the obvious move for the shooter's side is that what you intend is the destruction of the aircraft-as-projectile and the passengers' deaths are merely foreseen, which makes this look like collateral damage and not like using anyone at all. Then deal with the move the other way: the passengers are literally inside the threat, which drags in McMahan's innocent-threat / innocent-bystander distinction, and they're also already doomed, which raises the separate question of whether killing someone who dies in four minutes anyway is a harm at all or just a relocation of death. Which of these frameworks predicts a rule keyed to the composition of the passenger manifest, and which predicts that manifest shouldn't matter one bit?

Second half is what I actually care about, and it's a test I've been calling the codifiability test, for want of a better name: a doctrine that can't be written down as a rule with an identified decision-maker operating under time pressure and radical uncertainty isn't a doctrine that can guide anyone, it's just a way of praising or blaming people after the fact. The German statute tried to write it down and got struck, and the court's replacement principle seems to be that dignity is not available for quantitative trade-off at all — no counting lives, no fifty versus three hundred — which strikes me as considerably stronger than anything DDE as such requires. Is that DDE with a Kantian multiplier bolted on, or a different doctrine wearing DDE's vocabulary? Taboo the word "dignity" and try to translate the court's principle into means/ends/causal-structure terms, and tell me if the translation loses anything. And notice that the court's own factual background included a 2003 incident where a lone confused man circled the Frankfurt banking district threatening to hit the ECB tower, and then landed safely half an hour later — same observable description as the 9/11 case, completely different moral shape. So does DDE survive contact with an institution that has to act on descriptions rather than on completed moral facts? Does the German episode confirm DDE, count as an anomaly, or reveal that DDE was never really the thing doing the work in public argument about these cases — that the work is being done by an absolutist rule about status that just happens to rhyme with it?
