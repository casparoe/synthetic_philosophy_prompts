I have spent about twenty years writing and maintaining software, and I have no formal training in biology or philosophy beyond what I was taught at school. Over the past year I have been reading around human genetics on my own, because my niece has recently been given a diagnosis with a genetic component and I want to understand what is actually being claimed when people speak about her DNA.

One of my main sources has been a popular podcast on gene editing. Its host leans on a software analogy constantly: your genome is the source code of you, genes are subroutines, mutations are bugs, the body is the runtime environment, and editing is debugging. He draws a definite conclusion from it. Because DNA is code, he says, complex traits are in principle refactorable, and the fact that environment and upbringing also shape those traits is a reason for patience rather than a reason for doubt — the environment is just input, and eventually you go and fix the program. He does not argue for this separately; the analogy seems to be the argument.

I have not been able to make the analogy settle down, and I suspect the problem is that the word 'code' is doing at least two very different jobs in what he says. Sometimes he means something narrow and, so far as I can tell, literally true: that there is a fixed table mapping three-letter sequences onto amino acids, that this table is much the same across nearly all living things, and that on his telling it was settled once and for all very early in the history of life and has not changed since. At other times he means something far larger: that the genome as a whole is a specification for a person, written in advance of that person, which development then executes. He moves between these without pausing, and I cannot tell whether the second claim is borrowing its plausibility from the first.

Could you audit the analogy properly for me? Specifically:

- Give me the correspondence table for the strong version. What plays the role of source code, compiler, interpreter, operating system, hardware, input data, bug, patch, and version history — and where does a slot have no occupant at all, or several competing candidates? I am especially interested in whether the interpreter slot keeps changing hands (ribosome, cell, mother, whole developmental system), and whether that instability is a sign the mapping is incomplete or a sign it is failing.
- Say what would have to be true for the codon table to count as a code in a strict sense — an arbitrary pairing between two vocabularies plus machinery that translates between them — and then say whether anything with that structure exists at the level of the whole genome. Does the narrow claim license the broad one, and if not, what work is the slide between them doing?
- Separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. At minimum I want these assessed: that a program is written once and run many times, whereas a genome seems to be copied many times and run once; that in development there may be no clean line between program and data; that calling something a bug presupposes a specification and a specifier, so I would like to know where the specification is supposed to come from if there is no author; and that code is authored first and only then executed, which may have no counterpart at all.
- Tell me whether a weaker and more honest version of the informational talk survives — a sense in which saying that genes carry information is not metaphor but a real, cashable claim — and what has to be given up to get it.
- Offer a better analogy for the same target, if there is one, and be explicit about what it would license and what it would not.

Finally, the bottom line I actually care about: once the disanalogies are on the table, does his conclusion survive? Can the software picture support the claim that editing the program is the route to changing complex traits and that environmental influence is a temporary obstacle rather than a reason to doubt the whole project — or was the analogy carrying that conclusion the entire time, with nothing left when it is withdrawn? I would rather be told plainly that my niece's situation is not a debugging problem at all than be given a flattering picture of it.
