You are a philosopher of biology who has worked both on the adaptationism and units-of-selection debates and on Popper's epistemology from the inside; treat what follows as a serious audit rather than a popular explainer. Please think carefully, and before you write the audit proper, spend a short opening section brainstorming candidate correspondences and candidate disanalogies, including ones you will later dismiss as merely verbal — I would rather see the field of options first than a polished list that has already been pruned. Write the whole answer in British English.

The context. I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who is a Popperian to his fingertips. His position is that natural selection is not merely *like* the method of conjecture and refutation: on Popper's account they are one and the same process at different levels of organisation, and my friend insists the identity is doing real argumentative work. The correspondence he laid out for me was this. The heritable variant — the new allele, the new morphology, the new behavioural disposition — is the bold conjecture. The environment is the critic. Death and differential reproduction are the refutation. And the genome is World 3: the objective, publicly available store of the hypotheses that have so far survived criticism, transmitted to the next generation instead of being published in a journal.

From this he drew two conclusions, and I could not get past either of them.

First, he said the analogy explains and indeed guarantees Popper's earlier verdict that Darwinism is not a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical research programme — the position Popper advanced in his intellectual autobiography in the mid-seventies and then partly withdrew in the Darwin Lecture he gave at Cambridge in 1978. His argument was that you cannot falsify a *method*, only claims; and if selection just is the method of trial and error-elimination wearing a biological costume, then demanding a crucial experiment of it is a category mistake. On his telling, the 1978 withdrawal was a lapse of nerve, and the analogy is what vindicates the original position.

Second, and more troubling to me. Popper holds that a refutation carries information and a confirmation does not; the falsifying instance is the informative event. Selection consists of nothing but refutations. Therefore, my friend said, an adapted organism is a body of genuine information about its environment — adaptation is knowledge in the strong sense, not a metaphor for it. He then turned this against specification-based engineering: any designed system that merely checks its outputs against a specification, without an elimination step that can actually destroy the design, cannot acquire the kind of information that evolution acquires, so verification-only approaches to building reliable systems are doomed to a shallowness that selection escapes.

My own objections from the night, which he swatted away and which I am not sure are any good: Popper himself distinguished the amoeba from Einstein precisely on the ground that Einstein can distance himself from his theory and criticise it deliberately, whereas the amoeba dies with its error — so if the analogy is strict, it seems to entail that the organism simply *is* its hypothesis and that nothing survives the refutation to learn from it, which removes exactly the feature Popper thought made the scientific case special. Second, an environment is not a critic: it does not compare an outcome against an expectation, it merely is what it is, and a dead organism reports nothing back to anyone; there is no deduction from a conjunction of hypothesis and basic statement, so the information-theoretic claim has nothing to bite on. Third, the genome is not World 3 in any sense that matters: it cannot be read off against alternatives, it is not open to criticism, and it stores only survivors, which is close to the opposite of a library.

What I want from you is an audit of the analogy — not a verdict on Popper, and not a summary of the two camps.

- Give the mapping explicitly; a table of correspondences is fine. Cover at minimum: the conjecture, the critic, the refutation, background knowledge, the growth of knowledge, and World 3. Where a slot has no honest occupant on the biological side, say so plainly rather than stretching to fill it.
- Separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his two conclusions from those that are merely verbal, or that a Popperian can absorb at no cost.
- State what the analogy quietly imports from the source domain that the target lacks — in particular anything concerning a subject who outlives the refutation, the retention of a refuted theory for later repair through auxiliary adjustment, and the possibility of learning *why* a conjecture failed rather than only that it did.
- On the first conclusion: does the analogy support it, or merely restate it? If the identity of selection with trial and error-elimination is itself a claim rather than a method, is there anything left to falsify, and can the analogy tell us? Also say whether your verdict changes if we take the later, more cautious Popper as the baseline rather than the earlier one.
- On the second conclusion: commit yourself. Is adaptation knowledge, information in Popper's sense, or neither — and does the anti-specification conclusion follow from the analogy at all, or from something else entirely, such as a claim about the cost and coverage of search that has nothing to do with epistemology?
- Finish by saying what, if anything, would be a better analogy for the relation between selection and scientific method, including the deflationary option that they are simply two instances of one abstract error-elimination schema, and that the schema rather than the analogy is all my friend was ever entitled to.

Be committed throughout. I want definite verdicts on each of the two conclusions, with your reasons stated, not a survey of options that leaves me to choose between them; and if you think one of my three objections is a bad one, say so and say why. Do not give me references, a reading list, or footnotes; where you are unsure whether a claim is genuinely Popper's, flag the uncertainty in the text rather than asserting it confidently.
