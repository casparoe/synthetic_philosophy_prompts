I'm a second year phd student in a wound-healing lab and at group meeting last week two of our senior people had an arguement that I still can't resolve. I was just taking notes, I wasn't a participant. Background: our field has two camps that both study "inflammation." Camp A (my PI's camp) treats it as a molecular state -- a panel of cytokines plus CRP from tissue samples, and a mouse counts as inflamed iff the panel crosses a threshold. Camp B (mostly clinicians) treats it as the old clinical picture -- redness, heat, swelling, pain, plus whether the thing responds to a steroid. Same word, different journals, and they constantly say each other's results dont replicate.

Here's how it went, roughly in order:

1. Dana (Camp B) said the two camps are talking past each other in the strong Kuhnian sense: 'inflammation' picks out a different kind for each camp, so neither can even fully state the other's central claim, and there's no neutral court of appeal to settle it.

2. Sam (Camp A) said that's overreach. The camps translate each other all the time -- they read each other's papers, cite them, and have disagreements that are obviously substantive ("your intervention drops IL-6 but doesn't reduce swelling"). Disagreement of that kind presupposes shared meaning, so at most we have one ambiguous word, not incommensurability.

3. Dana said Sam is describing precisely the thing Kuhn ended up describing in his later work: translation succeeds for most of the vocabulary and fails in the local clusters of inter-defined terms that matter, so what's left is interpretation rather than translation -- you get through a paper and systematically get the key term wrong. Being able to argue isn't evidence of shared meaning if each side is charitably re-reading the other's terms into its own scheme. That's the failure mode, not the cure for it.

4. Sam said that if that's the criterion the concept has gone empty. Any two groups who mostly agree and occasionally reinterpret each other would be incommensurable, so basically all of science would be, and the word stops marking anything. He added that the camps share a decisive standard: both would accept a study that measures the molecular panel and the clinical signs together against patient outcomes, and both have said so out loud. Shared method, shared statistics, shared trial design -- that's commensurability.

5. Dana said the shared study proves nothing, because when the results land each camp will read them through its own framework: if markers move and swelling doesn't, Camp A says the inflammation was treated, Camp B says it wasn't, and each calls that a confirmation of its own view.

6. Sam said that's just the theory-ladenness of evidence, which is weaker and far less controversial than incommensurability, and Dana is smuggling the strong claim in under cover of the weak one. Dana said Sam is doing the same in reverse: treating "we can mostly translate each other" as if it settled "we mean the same thing by it."

Neither budged. Our PI said he'd buy lunch for whoever convinces a neutral third party, so I'm outsourcing.

Go through moves 1-6 and rate each one -- clearly right, mostly right, mixed, mostly wrong, clearly wrong -- with a sentence or two of justification per rating, then say who's more right overall about our two camps. I'm also curious whether the camps are actually an instance of incommensurability at all or whether the right verdict is some third category. I have a vague memory that Kuhn himself distinguished more than one kind of incommensurability but I couldn't tell you which ones are in play here, so if the case splits across kinds, say so. And if you think what we've got is better described by a different label, tell me what that label would be and what a genuine case of incommensurability would look like by contrast.
