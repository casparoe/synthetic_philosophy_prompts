I am convening a two-day workshop at which two former students of mine — one now a committed evaluative-judgment theorist of emotion, the other a committed somatic-feeling theorist — have agreed, after roughly eighteen months of correspondence that neither would describe as productive, to attempt a genuine adversarial collaboration. Before they arrive I want to circulate a draft joint statement for them to argue over, and I would like you to write that draft, in American English. To fix the parties precisely, so that you are not tempted to soften either position: the first holds that an emotion token just is, or necessarily involves, an evaluative judgment with propositional content — to fear the dog is, in the central case, to judge the dog dangerous — and that the felt and autonomic accompaniments are downstream effects rather than constitutive parts, though they may be highly reliable. The second holds that an emotion token is a feeling of a patterned bodily change, its directedness derived from what typically occasions that pattern and from what the organism is thereby disposed to do; on this view an evaluative judgment may trigger, inflect, or be reported alongside an emotion, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient for one. A third view — on which emotions are non-doxastic appearances or perceptions of value, structurally analogous to stubborn visual illusions — is explicitly not a party to this collaboration, as we are taking it up at the following session. If you conclude that this third view would dissolve rather than resolve some part of their dispute, say which part and why, but do not smuggle it in as one of the two signatories.

The document should open with a numbered section of claims both can sign, and these must be substantive rather than anodyne: I want agreement not only about the target phenomena (that emotions are about things, that they can persist against the subject's own better judgment, that they admit of degrees, that episodes of the same nominal kind differ wildly in their autonomic and expressive signatures, that the taxonomy of emotion concepts is not obviously the same across languages, that whatever emotions are must be compatible with the facts of infancy, non-human animals, and the clinical neurology of affect) but also about what a successful account is being asked to deliver. Then a table of the residual disagreements, with three columns: the formulation the judgment theorist would herself endorse for the point in dispute, the formulation the feeling theorist would himself endorse, and a neutral formulation neither would repudiate in writing. Here I want you to do a piece of analytic work I have been pressing on them both, and it is the part of the assignment I most expect to be skipped. Separate three claims that their correspondence has systematically run together: an identity claim (the emotion is identical to the judgment, or to the feeling of bodily change), a dependence claim (the judgment is necessary, or sufficient, or neither, for a token of that kind), and a primacy claim (which of the candidate components comes first in the order of understanding, or in the causal order, or in the order of development). For each disputed pairing in your table, mark which of the three it is actually about, and identify any disagreement that evaporates once the separation is made. Where you think a disagreement is verbal, say so, and say under which stipulated reference-fixing for the term emotion — lay attribution and self-report, a functional profile, a physiological profile, the natural-kind question left open — it becomes verbal, and which stipulation each side would prefer. That preference is itself data, and I want it recorded.

The heart of the document should be a cruxes section. For each crux, state the case or body of evidence both parties would agree bears on it, and then record each side's prediction in advance, in a form specific enough to be wrong: not "the data will be ambiguous" but what pattern of results would show up and what each of them would thereby concede. At least four of the cruxes should be built on episodes of the following sort, which are the ones they have spent the last year trading, though you may substitute others you judge sharper: an acute panic episode with no object and no antecedent evaluation; a subject with a specific phobia who sincerely judges the stimulus harmless, describes his own reaction as absurd, and recoils all the same; a person with a high spinal-cord lesion in whom afferent visceral feedback is substantially interrupted, whose reports about the intensity of particular emotion kinds are themselves contested in the literature; a person with advanced dementia or dense amnesia who is visibly distressed by an unfamiliar approach and visibly soothed by a familiar voice, minutes after any memory of the encounter has gone; robust affective discrimination of stimuli presented under conditions that preclude identification or report; and a language community whose emotion vocabulary fails to align with our own in a way that is not merely a labeling difference. For each, say whether the two are disagreeing about the evidence or about what the evidence is evidence of, since I suspect the latter in most cases. Close with a short joint section on the costs each side is absorbing that they have not admitted to each other, one result or argument that both would accept as genuinely moving — not merely as interesting — and your own assessment of which crux is most likely to be settled by conceptual clarification rather than by further data. Do not adjudicate and do not declare a winner; the test of the document is whether both of them could sign it without wincing. You need not cite anything, and please describe empirical findings generically rather than naming studies, since the point is the argumentative structure.
