My sister has run design review at a small independent type foundry for something like fifteen years, and one of the things she actually signs off on is whether a glyph in a new face is the letter it claims to be — as opposed to a different letter, or a picture that happens to be sitting in the a slot. I've been keeping a list of her calls because I think there's a principle in there and I can't extract it. These are all lowercase a:

- Plain single-story a, round bowl, stem down the right side: yes.
- Same a but with the bowl left open where it should meet the stem, a real gap, its wide enough that you'd notice at a glance: yes in the display cut. When they scaled the identical outline down to 9 point she didn't reject it, she sent it back with a note to close the aperture before it ships.
- Double-story a with the top arc redrawn as a straight diagonal: yes.
- Perfect circle with a horizontal bar through the middle: no, that's an o with something on it.
- Single-story a mirrored, stem on the left: no, and she said it doesn't matter that anyone could read it.
- Blackletter a: yes. Italic teardrop a: yes. Somebody's handwriting where they start at the bottom and go counterclockwise: yes, and she was explicit that stroke order is not her business.
- An a built as an apple silhouette with the leaf doing the job of the stem, everyone in the room knew exactly what it was supposed to be: no.

The one coming up next month is the case I can't call. A designer submitted a text face where the double-story a has had its upper arc detached — the bowl and stem are there and connected normally, and the arc floats free above them with a visible gap. At 72 point you can see it's an a. At 12 point the arc is two pixels of noise sitting on top of what is unmistakably an o. She'll be shown both, and the designer's argument is that the face is meant to be used big.

So which rule is she actually running? Give me the one that best accounts for everything above, plus at least one other rule that fits every single one of her calls just as well but comes out the other way on the detached arc. Then tell me which way you think she goes and why. I want a real prediction with a confidence level attached, not a hedge. After that, name one further design — a single glyph you'd hand her — that would discriminate between those two rules, and say what each rule predicts she'd do with it.

And if your read is that the two rules aren't answering the same question at all, that one is about a shape and the other is about a writing system or a reader, say that outright rather than papering over it, because it would explain a lot about why these review meetings go around in circles.

Bottom line in the first sentence — your predicted verdict on the detached arc, yes or no — then the reasoning. No preamble and don't restate any of this back at me.
