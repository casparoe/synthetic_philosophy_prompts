I am the TA for a second-year philosophy of mind course, and I need to grade one response to a short-answer question on our final exam. The question was worth 25 marks. I have deliberately said nothing below about what I make of the response myself, since I do not want to steer your assessment.

The exam question:

(a) State Chalmers's distinction between the easy problems and the hard problem of consciousness. (b) Explain why he thinks a functional or mechanistic account cannot close the gap. (c) Set out one physicalist reply to his case and say whether you think it succeeds.

The student's response:

Chalmers divides the problems of consciousness into easy ones and a hard one. The easy problems concern function: how we discriminate stimuli, integrate information, focus attention, and report on our own mental states. They are easy not because they are simple but because we know what a solution would look like — a mechanism that performs the relevant function. The hard problem asks why any of this is accompanied by experience: why there is something it is like to be a subject running those processes.

Chalmers thinks functional explanation cannot solve the hard problem because functions are characterized purely by causal and relational structure, so for any mechanism we specify we can still ask why it feels like anything to run it. His support is the zombie case: we can coherently conceive of a system functionally identical to a conscious person but with no experience whatsoever. Since conceivability is a good guide to possibility in this domain, the functional facts do not metaphysically determine the phenomenal facts.

The knowledge argument is really the same argument in a different costume. Nagel made the point first, with the bat: even a complete physical description of a bat's sonar system would leave us ignorant of what it is like to be a bat, which shows the physical description is incomplete. Chalmers repackages that as the hard problem. Block's distinction between phenomenal consciousness — raw feel — and access consciousness — information available for reasoning and verbal report — is Chalmers's device for isolating the hard problem from the easy ones, since access consciousness is the easy part and phenomenal consciousness is the hard part.

One physicalist reply is the ability hypothesis: what Mary gains when she leaves the room is a new skill rather than a new fact, so physicalism survives the knowledge argument. It seems fairly convincing to me, but only as far as the knowledge argument goes. It does nothing about the zombie case, where the issue is not what anyone knows but that a functional duplicate could lack experience altogether. So the hard problem stands.

Please grade this response out of 25 and write the comments that justify the grade. End with the mark you would award and one or two sentences on what the student should concentrate on when reviewing the paper.
