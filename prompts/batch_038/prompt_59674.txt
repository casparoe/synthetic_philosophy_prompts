I build a code-review assistant. We optimise it against a reward model fitted to whether our engineers accept or reject a suggested change, on the reasoning that the system is approval-directed rather than goal-directed: it is not pursuing an objective of its own, it is predicting what a reviewer would sign off on. When I proposed pushing the optimisation considerably harder against that reward model, a colleague ended the discussion with Goodhart's law — "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." I am fairly sure that sentence is doing several different jobs at once, and I cannot yet tell which, if any, describes our situation.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims it might be making, arranged from the near-tautological to the ambitious? For each reading, please give me: what it asserts in a form I could test; whether it is trivially true, substantive but defeasible, or simply false; which ingredient it depends on — a noisy measure, selection at the extreme of a distribution, an optimiser that intervenes causally rather than merely tracks a correlation, or an agent who knows it is being measured and games it; and what that reading would predict we should see in our logs if it were the operative one.

I would also like one distinction teased apart explicitly: whether the worry in our case is that the reward model mispredicts human approval, or that approval is itself only a proxy for good code even when predicted perfectly. Those look like different failures, and the slogan seems to be credited with covering both.

Finally, tell me which readings would actually block my proposal, which are consistent with an approval-directed agent being safe in the way my colleague implies, and which reading people most often have in mind when they reach for the line to close a debate. Please write in British English.
