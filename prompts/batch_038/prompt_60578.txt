For thirty-six years I worked as a physical chemist on problems in which the Helmholtz free energy was a number I had good reason to trust: it was measurable, it did rhetorical work only after it had done quantitative work, and nobody thought it explained the behaviour of the accounting department. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy, and more recently theoretical neuroscience, and for two terms now I have convened a fortnightly reading group at the emeritus club of my former institute. Our present topic is the free energy principle and the predictive-processing picture of mind that is generally attached to it. The group is a mixed company: several chemists and a crystallographer who will not permit a loose use of the word "free energy" to pass unchallenged; two former students of mine who are now computational neuroscientists and who will spot a technical error instantly; and a contingent of retired engineers who confess that the formalism bores them. I have therefore decided to open the session with satire rather than with a summary, and I should like you to write the piece for me.

What I want is a parody of a staff handbook — specifically the "Frequently Asked Questions" section circulated to employees of a mid-sized firm whose leadership has adopted the free energy principle as its actual operating philosophy, not merely as a motivating metaphor. Roughly eight hundred words, organised as numbered questions and answers in the flat, benevolent register of corporate human resources, with a short glossary box and one troubleshooting block near the end. The firm, its officers and its inquirers may be invented; please do not name real persons, companies or laboratories, and please append no references or suggested readings, as the group has agreed to keep the session citation-free and I would have no means of checking them in any case.

The comedy must arise from the theory being applied faithfully, so that my neuroscientists laugh at the accuracy rather than wince at the mangling, while the engineers can still enjoy it as an ordinary workplace absurdity. Among the beats I should like handled: an entry on why employees are expected to report surprise and one on why the chief executive's forecasts are never themselves treated as prediction errors; a passage on the firm's flat insistence that revising one's model of the quarterly figures and revising the quarterly figures are interventions of exactly the same kind, which management finds administratively convenient; a description of middle management whose entire function is to explain away whatever can be explained away and to forward only residual error upward; a determination of which departments, contractors and outsourced providers count as internal states of the firm and which as its environment, together with the evident awkwardness of that determination; the treatment of curiosity and of new-market exploration as a budget line item traded off against the pursuit of preferred outcomes; an answer to the objection that an employee who truly minimised surprise would sit motionless in an unlit room, given in the manner the theory's defenders actually answer it and leaving a reasonable employee distinctly unsettled; an entry in which someone asks whether the policy could ever be shown to be wrong, answered with a technically correct restatement of the charge that the framework cannot fail, delivered as reassurance; and a closing note that the same principles account for the canteen's soup, the pension liabilities and the migratory habits of the local gulls, offered without embarrassment as a strength. I should also like the glossary box to make plain that the quantity being minimised is a variational bound on surprise computed with respect to a generative model, and that it is not the thermodynamic potential of my own discipline — while conceding honestly why the resemblance is more than a pun.

Two constraints govern the whole thing. The satire is aimed at a position and at a style of discourse, not at any person, and it should exaggerate only features the framework genuinely possesses: no invented technical vocabulary, and no caricature that a defender could dismiss as a straw man on the grounds that the theory never said what the handbook says. And it must remain deadpan throughout, never winking at the reader and never stating an objection in its own voice; if a reader cannot tell, without being told, which commitments of the framework each entry is exposing, I have failed, and so have you.
