Our philosophy-of-science reading group keeps a running log of what our prof (call her D) says whenever someone asks "but is that actually science?", and it's a lot less clean than you'd expect from a self-described Popperian. So far:

- General relativity: science, because it risked a specific claim about light bending and could have been killed by it.
- Freudian dream interpretation: not science, any dream whatsoever confirms it.
- Astrology: not science.
- Continental drift, before anyone had a mechanism for it: science anyway, it was making claims the world could have contradicted.
- Postulating Vulcan to explain the leftover bit of Mercury's orbit: not science.
- Postulating Neptune to explain the leftover bit of Uranus's orbit: science, because they said where to point the telescope and it was there.
- String theory: not science yet, but not pseudoscience either, and what she says she'd want to know is whether it's still throwing off new structure that could in principle be checked someday.

That last one is a hedge, and the hedge is why I don't think she's running plain falsifiability.

New case for next session, and I want to commit to a guess beforehand. An ecology group argued that a bunch of pond populations crashed because of a pathogen. Standard assays found nothing. They came back with a custom assay for a dormant form of it, detected it at 3 of 40 sites, and now predict that the 3 will keep crashing and the other 37 won't, over five years. Catch: the assay was tuned on those same 40 sites, so the prediction is close to guaranteed by construction, and there's no independent evidence the assay is picking up anything real. It is pre-registered though, and in the naive sense falsifiable — if the other sites crash too, theyre wrong.

What's she going to say? More interesting to me: which rule or rules fit all seven of those calls, which of them come apart on this one, and what single extra fact about the assay would separate the candidates most cleanly.
