A friend of mine who works on formal models of collective choice and I argued until two in the morning about a dispute that, so far as I can tell, has not budged in forty years, and I would like a diagnosis of it rather than a verdict on the merits.

The dispute is whether the discursive dilemma is a genuine problem for collegial decision-making or an artifact of how the problem has been formalized. One camp — running from Kornhauser and Sager's analysis of multi-judge courts through List and Pettit's impossibility results and the large literature that followed them — holds that premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation can come apart, that when they do the institution cannot avoid choosing, and that the choice is substantive: it determines what the group has judged, and therefore what it can be held answerable for. The other camp, which is my friend's, holds that the dilemma dissolves under inspection. His charges are three. First, the canonical setups assume the panel is unanimous about the doctrine connecting the premises to the conclusion, and real panels very often are not, so the puzzle quietly presupposes the agreement it should be explaining. Second, they assume the group has, or ought to have, a single consistent set of judgments across its whole agenda — a philosopher's demand rather than an institutional one, since tribunals routinely issue decisions that would not survive logical audit. Third, they treat a vote as the only route to a group judgment, thereby ignoring the sequencing, the drafting conventions, and the jurisdictional practices that in fact settle most contested cases before anyone tabulates anything.

The case we kept returning to was mine rather than the literature's. I sit on a five-member professional licensing panel. Revocation of a licence requires three findings under our governing rule: a material misrepresentation in the application, intent to deceive, and demonstrable harm to a client. On a recent matter, the members' considered positions were three-to-two for misrepresentation, three-to-two for intent, and three-to-two for harm — but not the same three each time, so that only two members accepted all three elements. Conclusion-based voting lets the licence stand; premise-based voting revokes it. My friend read that as vindicating his side: two members privately deny that harm is a necessary element at all, so the panel was never unanimous about the doctrine the paradox assumes, and in any event we did not take three separate ballots but deliberated to a single written decision, so the group judgment the paradox supposedly infects was made by the drafting of reasons and not by any aggregation function. I read it as vindicating mine: the disagreement between us was real, consequential for the licensee, and not resolvable by pointing out that our practice is informal.

There is a wrinkle neither of us could settle. Our decision must state the findings it rests on, and under conclusion-based voting that statement will assert findings no majority endorsed — which leaves the licensee unable to appeal them point by point, since each finding he would contest was supported by three of us and yet the outcome turned on none of them.

So what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal, with the two camps using the group's judgment to pick out different things? Is it a dispute about which cases count as evidence, one side treating formal toy models as data and the other treating actual tribunals as data? Is it a disguised normative disagreement about what a published, appealable decision owes the parties, in which case the impossibility theorems are largely beside the point? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading — one asking what a panel ought to do, the other whether groups are agents at all? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a theorem, an empirical study of how real panels actually vote and write, or a change of question? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
