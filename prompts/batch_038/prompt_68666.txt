Something keeps coming up in my life that I have no word for, and I want to know if it's a real thing or just me pattern-matching on three anecdotes.

My friend plays piano, improvised stuff, no sheet music, and she's genuinely good. Ask her afterwards what she did over some weird chord change and she'll shrug and say she doesnt know — fine, normal, everybody's heard that one. But the odd part: ask her to play something while narrating her choices as she makes them, and she doesn't just fail to narrate. She plays worse. Audibly worse. Same room, same three people, no audience, no stakes, she isn't nervous. It's the request for an account that does it, and she's back to normal two minutes later once you shut up about it.

Second case is me, unfortunately. I can tell pretty reliably which of two translations of a poem is better, and afterwards I can usually defend the call. But if someone asks me to state my criterion first and then judge by it, I invent a criterion on the spot and my picks go to hell — sometimes flatly reversed, where the criterion I just announced points at A and I still choose B, and I can hear myself choosing B.

Third, from work. A colleague who is uncanny in tense meetings: knows exactly when to go quiet and when to push, reads people instantly. We got him to run a session on how he does it. The session was okay, but everyone afterward said he'd seemed like a different and much duller person, and he told me privately he'd spent an hour making up rules he didn't believe.

Now, I've read enough popular philosophy of mind to know the standard line about machines doing clever things without understanding them — the chess program point, and the argument people build from it that understanding is overrated, that the cleverness lives in the doing and not in any grasping of reasons. Everything I'm describing points the other direction, and I can't tell whether that opposite has ever been taken seriously or whether I've stumbled onto something that's been sitting in plain sight.

So: is there an established concept for this — not for the plain fact that skilled people can't articulate their skill, which I know has been discussed for a century, but specifically for the articulation *degrading* the skill, so that comprehension isn't merely missing but actively corrosive? I want the nearest candidates and exactly where each one runs out. I can vaguely guess at things from sports science and from memory research, but I'm fairly confident none of them is this, because in my cases nobody is under pressure and nobody is trying to remember anything — they're just being asked for a report of what they're doing.

And if it isn't a thing, then name it and define it, but properly. Where are the edges? Because sometimes asking someone to explain clearly *helps* — writing it down, teaching it, talking it through — so what's the difference between the helping version and the wrecking version? Does the phenomenon survive if the person eventually absorbs the explanation and gets better at the skill, or does that mean it was never really the same thing? Are my three cases even one case, or is the piano one about performance while the translation one is about making up reasons after the fact? And does any of this bear on whether the original skill counted as knowing something at all, or is that a separate quarrel?

A bare label is useless to me. I want the carving: what it is, what it isn't, and where it stops.
