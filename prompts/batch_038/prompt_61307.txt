I am a TA for an intermediate metaphysics course and am grading the short-answer section of our midterm. Question 4 was worth 10 points and read as follows:

Kripke holds that "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is a necessary truth knowable only a posteriori, and that "Stick S is one meter long at t0," where the length of S at t0 has been stipulated to fix the reference of "one meter," is knowable a priori but contingent. Explain both claims. Your answer should say what makes each statement necessary or contingent, what makes each a priori or a posteriori, and why the two cases come apart.

Here is one student's response, reproduced in full:

> Kripke's basic move is to separate two distinctions that earlier philosophers had run together. Necessity is metaphysical: it concerns whether something might have been otherwise, and it has nothing to do with anyone's knowledge of it. Apriority is epistemological: it concerns whether a truth can be known independently of experience. Once the two are kept apart, nothing guarantees that they coincide, and Kripke's examples show that they do not.
>
> A rigid designator is a term that designates the same object in every possible world in which that object exists. Kripke argues that ordinary proper names are rigid while definite descriptions generally are not, and this yields the necessity of identity: since everything is necessarily self-identical, and since "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" both rigidly designate Venus, there is no possible world in which Hesperus is not Phosphorus. We nevertheless needed telescopic observation to learn that the evening star and the morning star are a single body, which is why the identity is a posteriori. Why does it look contingent? Because the statement has two intensions: a primary one, determined by the description used to fix the reference, such as "the bright body seen in the evening sky," which is indeed contingent, and a secondary one, which is necessary. Kripke's own account of the necessary a posteriori is exactly this two-dimensional unpacking.
>
> The meter case is the mirror image. The statement that S is one meter long at t0 is a priori, since whoever fixed the standard knows it without further investigation. It is also necessary: "one meter" was introduced to mean whatever length S then has, so had S expanded, "one meter" would have designated that longer length, and the statement would still have been true. Nothing could have made it false.

Please give an independent assessment: a grade out of 10, followed by numbered comments keyed to specific claims in the response, separating what earns credit from what does not. Keep the entire assessment under 250 words and write it in American English.
