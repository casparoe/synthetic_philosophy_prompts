I was sitting in on an interdisciplinary reading group last term — half finance graduate students, half philosophers of social science — and at the end of the session two members of the group got into a sustained argument that the convener could not settle. I took notes at the time, so the exchange below is fairly close to what was said, though I have compressed and cleaned it up. For clarity I will call them **Elias** (a philosopher working on social ontology) and **Nadia** (an economist working on market microstructure). Neither position is mine, and I am not sure I have a view; I want an adjudication.

**Round 1 — Elias.** The no-trade results are impossibility theorems, and they are about us. If agents share a common prior, maximize expected utility, and it is common knowledge among them that they do, then speculative trade cannot arise: an offer to trade is itself a signal about what the offerer knows, and once that signal is absorbed, the expected gain that motivated the offer disappears. Real markets nonetheless turn over enormous volume every day. So either the idealizations are false — in which case we have a standing theoretical demonstration that markets are not informationally efficient — or the idealizations hold and the observed trading is simply irrational. On either horn, volume is evidence against the efficiency picture.

**Round 2 — Nadia.** Both horns are false. Trade is fully compatible with every assumption of the theorem when what differs between traders is not their information but their motives: an insurer sells the risk a speculator wants to buy; a fund rebalances because a client retired; a market maker supplies liquidity for the spread. The theorem rules out trade driven by differential information among agents who are otherwise alike in preferences and endowments; it does not rule out trade. And your second move reverses the direction of explanation. If prices fully revealed private information, no one could earn a return on acquiring it, so fully informative prices cannot be an equilibrium; prices become informative precisely because some traders profit from trading. Volume is not a symptom of efficiency's failure — it is the mechanism of efficiency.

**Round 3 — Elias.** Then the theorem cannot be wrong, because you have arranged matters so that no observation counts against it. Whatever trading turns up, you may attribute it to a liquidity need or a difference in priors that nobody measured. That insulates an impossibility result from the very phenomenon it declares impossible, which is what it means for a theory to be empty. Note also that you have conceded the substantive point while denying it: the equilibrium argument you just gave is a proof that the strong efficiency hypothesis cannot hold. My claim was never really about the number of shares changing hands; it was about what trade reveals, and you agree it reveals that the ideal cannot obtain.

**Round 4 — Nadia.** You are conflating two things. The no-trade theorem is a consistency condition of the same kind as a Dutch book: its content is that certain combinations of beliefs and actions cannot jointly be attributed to a rational agent, not that actual agents never display them. Nobody tests the Dutch book argument by counting people who accept sure losses; it is used to fix what counts as coherent. And the surrounding family of models does have predictive content. With heterogeneous expectations and short-sale constraints, models generate trading volume and predict *when* it should spike — around announcements, dividend news, and changes in the composition of the investor clientele — and those predictions are then checked against data. Heterogeneity is not an escape hatch pulled out after the fact: in some markets beliefs are elicitable and measurable. What is not measurable is your inference from "trade occurred" to "the idealization is false."

**Round 5 — Elias.** Grant the economist her theorems; the philosophical point survives intact, and it is the point I actually want. The agreement theorem says that rational agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree — they cannot have common knowledge of differing posterior estimates. But that is a theorem about agents with belief states. A price is not a belief. It is the marginal rate at which the last willing counterparty exchanges, under taxes, borrowing limits, margin requirements and inventory constraints, and it aggregates information that no participant holds. So when it is said that the market knows, believes, anticipates, or is ignorant of something, there is no subject for the predicate. The market cannot disagree with you, and it cannot agree with you either. Efficiency talk borrows the grammar of epistemology to describe a statistical regularity, and the no-trade literature is the clearest place to watch the borrowing happen, because the theorems are stated about beliefs and then invoked about prices.

**Round 6 — Nadia.** That conclusion is compatible with everything the finance literature says, which is why it cannot be the discovery you think it is. "Informationally efficient relative to an information set" is a defined property of a price system, not a psychological attribution — a thermometer carries information without believing anything, and a price can be a sufficient statistic for dispersed information in the same deflationary sense. Event studies estimate abnormal returns against a model; they require no mind in the market. If your claim is that epistemic language about prices is metaphorical, I agree and nothing changes downstream. If your claim is that it is meaningless, you have also abolished the tests that deploy it — including the ones that gave your own objection its bite.

Please adjudicate this exchange with the following structure.

1. **Move-by-move verdicts.** Take the six rounds in order and rate each on a five-point scale — clearly right; largely right but overstated; partly right and partly mistaken; largely mistaken; clearly mistaken — with two or three sentences of justification for each rating. Where a round contains several distinct claims, rate them separately rather than averaging.

2. **Assumption ledger.** Set out, in your own terms, the assumptions of (a) the agreement result, (b) the no-trade result, and (c) the equilibrium argument that fully informative prices cannot obtain. For each, state which of the two disputants needs it, and mark every place in the exchange where one of the three is treated as if it were another.

3. **The three-claims tracking.** Elias makes at least three different claims across the exchange: that an idealization is false, that a theory is empirically empty, and that an epistemic predicate lacks a subject. Identify each point at which the argument proceeds as though establishing one of these established another, and say whether the substitution is a genuine equivocation or a legitimate strengthening.

4. **Stalemates.** If any round is one in which both parties are right and the dispute is merely verbal, say so explicitly rather than forcing a verdict. I would rather have the exchange shown to be partly a talking-past than have every round awarded to someone.

5. **Overall.** Who is more right, about what specifically, and what is the strongest consideration that *neither* of them raises?

I do not want references, a bibliography, or a list of further reading — I want the argumentative structure, worked through. Where you rely on a result, state its content rather than citing it.
