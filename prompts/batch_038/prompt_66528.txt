So here's a slightly odd request. I run an informal reading group and we keep going around in circles on the same argument: how much should you actually put on a bet when the odds favor you. I want to build a decision tree that sorts people by what they'd do with a series of concrete gambles, not by what they call themselves.

What I need: seven to nine questions, each one a specific bet or betting situation with two or three answer options. No question may name a position or a person, and no question should be answerable just by knowing the vocabulary — someone who's never heard of the Kelly criterion should still be able to walk the whole tree.

Make sure these get in there: a gamble offered exactly once whose payoff cant be reinvested; the identical gamble repeated indefinitely; a pair of cases that differ only in whether going broke is absorbing or you can always earn more and get back in; a bet where you estimated your edge from about ten trials, so your probability is itself shaky; and this one specifically — a repeated bet where each round you either gain 50% of what you staked or lose 50% of it, with probabilities 0.6 and 0.4. Please check my arithmetic before you use it: I'm claiming it has positive expected value but a negative expected log growth rate if you stake everything each round, so the EV maximizer takes it at max size and the growth maximizer refuses it or takes the other side. If I've got that wrong, repair the numbers rather than cutting the question — it's the most important node I have.

For leaves I want the real positions: bet the maximum whenever EV is positive; full Kelly; fractional Kelly; maximize expected utility of terminal wealth under some concave utility that isn't log; never accept any chance of ruin, however tiny; the time-average versus ensemble-average framing; plus whatever else the logical space forces you to add. Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then tell me which pairs of leaves this tree genuinely can't separate — I suspect full Kelly and log-utility expected-utility maximization collapse into the same verdicts on repeated bets and only come apart somewhere I haven't thought of, and I'd rather you say where than pretend they're distinct. Finally, list the combinations of answers that no position occupies, and for each say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered yet.

Audience is first-years in stats and econ who know what expected utility is but have never placed a bet in their lives.
