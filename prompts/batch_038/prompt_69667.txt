Consider the debate over the inefficacy objection — the argument that my own act cannot be wrong on grounds of harm because it makes no perceptible difference to anyone: this car journey, this steak, my abstention in an election with several million other voters. The familiar replies come in at least five families:

- probabilistic replies, on which the act does make a difference, only an expected rather than an actual one;
- threshold replies, on which harms arrive in steps and some token act is the one that crosses a step;
- cumulative replies, on which every act adds a real increment that is simply too small to be perceived;
- non-effectivist replies, on which wrongness never required making a difference — complicity, free-riding, integrity, character, the attitudes an act expresses;
- relocating replies, on which the primary duty is to join and sustain collective action rather than to abstain from the individual act.

My suspicion is that these families are not rivals answering one question, and that the debate has stalled because each of them is silently evaluating a different description of the act. Under the description *buy this steak*, the causal replies look unavailable and the fourth and fifth families become mandatory; under *eat meat*, or *participate in industrial animal agriculture*, the causal replies look far more promising. What is almost never examined is the description itself: what fixes the granularity at which the object of evaluation is picked out, and whether any account can specify that granularity without simply reaching for whichever description yields its preferred verdict.

I want to build an eighteen-month postdoctoral project around this diagnosis, and I need the three-page proposal the funding scheme requires. Please give it headed sections covering:

1. A precise statement of the research question, framed so that it has determinate possible answers rather than being an invitation to survey the field.
2. Why the five families above leave the question open instead of settling it — and whether any of them is not a candidate answer to it at all.
3. A candidate set of constraints on act-description: the causal structure of the harm; the granularity at which the agent genuinely controls her behaviour; the description under which the agent herself deliberates; and a pluralism on which all descriptions are legitimate and the choice is pragmatic. Say which constraint the project would take most seriously, and what consideration would decide between them.
4. A staged work plan across the eighteen months, saying concretely what I would be doing at each stage.
5. How the framework would be tested on three cases — a single long-haul flight, a single meal of factory-farmed meat, and a decision not to vote — and what a successful result would look like in each, given that the three cases differ in whether the harm is continuous, threshold-like, or mediated by a collective decision procedure.
6. Explicit failure conditions. Include the two I fear most: that the constraint on descriptions turns out to be consequentialist all the way down, so that the framework is a disguised restatement of the view it was meant to arbitrate; and that it becomes an unfalsifiable device for licensing any verdict an agent prefers.
7. A candid scope assessment of what eighteen months can deliver and what has to be left to a later project.
