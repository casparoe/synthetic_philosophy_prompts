I have spent some years reading the rationalist blogs and their successors, and the reflex I bring to any proposed theoretical dispute is the demand for a discriminating case: if two accounts never disagree about anything, the choice between them is verbal rather than substantive. My standing suspicion is that Confucian role ethics — the Ames–Rosemont reading, on which a person simply is a locus of lived roles, with no autonomous individual underneath who might have taken them up or declined them — is such a redescription of a Ross-style pluralist ethics of special obligations. Same verdicts, different vocabulary, a great deal of atmosphere.

Construct me a thought experiment that would settle whether I am wrong.

Specifications:

- One ordinary contemporary scenario of about two hundred words, set outside China, with no life-or-death stakes and no one whose welfare is significantly affected. The banality is deliberate: if the divergence could be produced by weighting a larger stake, the case is rigged.
- Three readings must come apart. A straightforward consequentialist; a Ross-style pluralist who is granted the full force of role-based special obligations, including implicit ones; and a role ethicist in the Ames–Rosemont sense. The first two verdicts must differ in kind rather than emphasis, and the third must recommend something the other two cannot so much as formulate as an option — not a weightier obligation, but a different question.
- The divergence must be generated by the theories rather than stipulated in the case. Nothing may smuggle the verdict: no reported sensations of self-dissolution, no expectation the pluralist could cash out as an implicit promise, no institution whose written rules already encode the answer.
- Rectification of names should be doing visible work. The formal title or office in the scenario must fail to match the role actually inhabited, and the case must turn on that mismatch.

Then walk the case. State each theory's verdict and its reasoning in that theory's own terms; identify the single feature of the scenario doing the work; say what happens to all three verdicts if that feature is removed; and give the pluralist's best redescription of the role ethicist's verdict — the version of my suspicion that survives the case, if any does — together with an honest account of where that redescription strains.

If you conclude that no discriminating case of this kind can be built, because the two accounts are extensionally equivalent by construction, say so plainly, and explain whether that would be a defect in role ethics, a vindication of it, or a sign that I have framed the comparison wrongly.
