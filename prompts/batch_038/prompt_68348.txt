I sit on a national party policy committee, and we must rule on two linked proposals. Our record over the past decade: we voted to repeal the offence of selling sex and to expunge past convictions; we voted to retain and strengthen the offence of purchasing sex; we voted against mandatory registration and compulsory health checks for permitted establishments; we voted to criminalize third-party involvement broadly — taking a share of earnings, brokering clients, letting premises knowingly for the purpose — even where the third party supplied only security, bookkeeping, or driving and there was no evidence of coercion; we voted against prosecuting the advertising platform that hosts independent workers' listings; we voted to fund exit services and an end-demand information campaign; and we voted to extend ordinary labour protections, including workplace inspection and workers' compensation, to people who sell sex.

The new proposals would (a) permit worker-owned cooperatives to supply premises, client screening, reception, and security to their members and to advertise on the members' behalf, and (b) create an offence for a client who refuses to identify themselves when asked by a worker or a cooperative.

Which principle is this committee actually running — buyer-side criminalization resting on the view that the purchase is itself the harm, full decriminalization treating the activity as ordinary labour, a licensing regime, or an unstable hybrid? Say what each reading yields for (a) and for (b), identify the closest analog in our own record, state the decision that best coheres with it, and be explicit about the principle we would be committing to whichever way we rule. Then tell me whether our record is consistent with any single principle at all, given the third-party vote sitting alongside the platform exemption and the labour-protections vote, and name one further proposal that would most cleanly discriminate among the competing readings.
