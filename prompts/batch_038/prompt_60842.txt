I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on knowledge and practical reason, and my supervisor has just sent it back with a criticism I cannot answer. Her point is that the standard teaching cases do not discriminate between the two views I am trying to contrast: in the familiar example where a couple decide not to deposit their paycheques on a Friday because the queues will be intolerable on Saturday, the practical pressure rises for everybody in the conversation at once, so the contextualist and the pragmatist about knowledge predict exactly the same shift in the students' verdicts, and the seminar ends with the suspicion that the dispute is notational. I want a case that pulls the two apart, and I want it built to a specification rather than dredged up from the literature. Please treat the following as a design brief, and note that stipulating the verdicts instead of generating them from the two views as they are independently formulated would count as a failure of the exercise, however natural it feels to write.

The scenario should be a single realistic paragraph set in an ordinary institutional or domestic world — a hospital corridor, an engineering firm, a household, a school — with no lotteries, no counterfeit barns, no deceiving demons, no sceptical hypotheses and no uncannily accurate predictors. Spell out the subject's total evidence explicitly, because it has to be held fixed across every version of the case; the only thing that may vary between versions is the practical situation, and that variation must not change the likelihood of the proposition's being false, nor supply the subject with any new information. Make the stakes asymmetric in the direction that is hard to construct: the cost of error falls on the subject, who must act, while the knowledge attribution I want judged is made by a third party who has nothing whatsoever riding on the matter and who is nonetheless fully aware that the subject has a great deal riding on it. Because contextualism is sensitive to precisely these details, stipulate the attributor's conversational context at length — who is speaking, to whom, for what purpose, what has been mentioned and what conspicuously has not — and ensure that no possibility of error is made salient anywhere in that conversation. Also ensure that the subject is not ruminating, not taking legal advice, not revisiting her evidence and not losing confidence: her situation is costly, but her own state of mind is steady, so that nobody can dissolve the case by saying she has raised the standards for herself. Then supply a control version: the same evidence, the same attributor and the same conversational context, with the sole difference that the cost of being wrong falls on somebody other than the subject, who is now a bystander with nothing at stake. Neither view should predict any shift between the main case and the control, and I want that stated plainly, since a design that cannot say what its own control is for is not a design.

After the scenarios, walk through the reasoning in full: what pragmatic encroachment says about the main case and by which route it gets there, whether that route runs through the link between knowledge and rational action or through a threshold for justification that moves with cost; what contextualism says about the truth of the third party's attribution in the stipulated context; and at exactly which sentence the two verdicts part company. Tell me what the subject herself should say when asked whether she knows, and whether the two views give different advice about the action she faces — if they agree about everything the subject and the attributor should do and should assert, I want to know that the divergence in my case is only apparent. Close with an honest assessment: identify the single feature doing the work, say what happens to the verdicts if it is removed, and give the strongest attack a committed contextualist would mount on the construction, for instance that the stipulated low-stakes context is unstable once the subject's predicament is common knowledge between speaker and hearer, or that no reader can survey the case without occupying a raised-standards context of her own. Say too whether the intuition the case elicits is really about knowledge or about the propriety of flat-out assertion. My students have met the classic bank and airport examples but none of the technical literature, so please do not cite papers or point them at a reading list; write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or a long email, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, and in British English.
