Explain why Leibniz holds that this is the best of all possible worlds. What does the goodness of the actual world depend on? Does his optimism imply that we ought to be indifferent to the evil we find in it?

Here's the student's answer:

"Leibniz's argument starts from God. There are infinitely many possible worlds, each a maximal set of compossible substances, and these worlds sit in God's understanding as ideas — they are possibles before any of them is actual, and their possibility doesn't depend on God deciding to create them. God's wisdom lets him compare all of them at once, and because he is perfectly good his will goes for the best. So the actual world is the one with the greatest variety of phenomena under the simplest set of laws: maximal richness of content for minimal complexity of ordering. On that criterion our world wins, and it wins comfortably — really the criterion just amounts to the greatest happiness for the greatest number of perceiving beings, which is why Leibniz gets called a proto-utilitarian.

Evil fits in because God isn't choosing pieces, he's choosing whole series. A world containing some sin and suffering can be better overall than one containing none, if those evils are conditions of greater goods that couldn't otherwise be had, the way a painter needs shadow and a piece of music needs dissonance. God permits evil but doesn't will it for its own sake, and the limits on creatures come from their own natures rather than being added by God. Since monads don't act on one another, the coordination of everything is pre-established in that original choice, and so the world's goodness doesn't depend on any causal give-and-take between things.

Now, God necessarily wills the best, so this world exists by necessity. Leibniz calls it morally necessary rather than metaphysically necessary, but that distinction doesn't do much work: a perfectly wise and perfectly good agent has his choice fixed in advance by the ranking of worlds just as surely as he'd be fixed by a contradiction. Either way there is no live alternative God could have taken, so the actual world is the only genuinely possible one, and the contingency is on our side — a matter of our not knowing the reasons.

On indifference, yes, optimism seems to imply it. If everything that happens is a required part of the best series, then working to reduce evil is either pointless, since the total is already fixed and my own efforts are just further parts of the series, or actively bad, since it interferes with an arrangement God judged best. The rational attitude is resignation. Leibniz's talk about justice as universal benevolence is best read as describing God's side of things rather than an obligation on us, since we are in no position to know which evils the whole requires."
