I host a small academic philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a moral philosopher who defends a strong version of the inefficacy thesis about individual contributions to collective harm. Because her position is unusual in its grounding rather than in its conclusion, I want the questions to engage that grounding directly rather than rehearse the standard exchange. Her view, as I understand it from the manuscript she has sent, runs as follows.

She holds that eating meat, flying long-haul, and similar acts of individual consumption make no causal difference to the harms at issue, and therefore cannot be wrong. Her argument for this is not the familiar one about probabilities and thresholds. Instead, she insists that the criterion of rightness is given by the actual consequences of an agent's act, not by their expected consequences, and she maintains that these are separate questions from the question of which decision procedure an agent should adopt. On that basis she rejects the expected-harm reply — the claim that a purchase which almost certainly triggers no restocking nonetheless carries a small probability of doing so, and so a nonzero expected harm — on the grounds that the reply covertly substitutes a decision procedure for a criterion of rightness, and that expected harms cannot ground wrongness any more than expected benefits can ground permissibility. She also argues that her thesis is not an a priori truth about agency but an empirical claim about the structure of particular harms: on her account, some harms are produced in discrete batches with reorder thresholds, so that most individual acts have actual consequences that are exactly nil, while the appearance of universal impotence is an artifact of confusing imperceptibility with absence. She supports the general skeptical lesson with a case of her own devising in which a thousand people each turn a dial by a hair's breadth, no individual turn making any perceptible difference to any victim's pain, while the victims collectively suffer agony; she reads this as showing that our intuitions about individual causal relevance are untrustworthy rather than as showing that the turners are causally relevant. Finally, she treats voting as a special case that she does not claim to have resolved.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for this guest, observing the following requirements.

1. At least one question on each of these issues: whether an actual-consequence criterion makes her thesis contingent and case-by-case, and how she could ever know that a given act made no difference; whether her dismissal of the expected-harm reply leaves her own view unable to guide anyone, and what an agent is supposed to do with a criterion she cannot apply; the difference between harms that are continuous in their production, such as emissions, and harms that are batch-produced, such as animal products, and whether an actual but imperceptible contribution counts as a contribution; what the dial case is supposed to establish, given that its natural upshot may be indeterminacy about which turners matter rather than the impotence of each; how voting differs, and specifically what about the discreteness of electoral outcomes could confer causal relevance that a single purchase lacks; and why she rejects an account on which an individual act is wrong as an act of participation in a collective harm, without individually causing it.

2. For each question, first state in one sentence the reply she is most likely to give, then give a follow-up keyed to that reply. The follow-ups should not merely restate the original question.

3. Do not write any question she can dispose of by appealing to the distinction between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure. Where that move is available to her, phrase the question so that it forces her to say what the criterion implies in the case at hand.

4. No generic material: nothing about her influences, her path into philosophy, or how audiences have responded to her work.

5. Order the questions so that the pressure increases, and make the final one a question about what would have to be true, or what evidence would count, for her to abandon the thesis.

6. Keep each question to two or three sentences and raise no more than two issues within it. Present the result as a numbered list, with the issue each question targets named in a short lead-in.
