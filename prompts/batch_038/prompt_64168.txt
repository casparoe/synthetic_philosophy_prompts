I'm teaching a seminar on social choice and I want to do something slightly perverse with it. Sen's own illustration of the liberal paradox is about a copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover: two people, a prude and a lewd type, three states of the world (the prude reads it, the lewd one reads it, nobody does), each person made decisive over one pair of states, and then Pareto turns it into a cycle. So the toy model was generated by an actual novel, and I want to read the novel back through the model.

Here's the reading I want you to pressure-test. Clifford isn't squeamish about books, he's squeamish about sex, but I think he's structurally the prude: he treats Connie's private life as one more feature of states of the world that he gets to rank, generously or meanly. Early on he tells her he doesn't mind her taking a lover so long as appearances hold and she comes back to Wragby and produces an heir; at the end, when she actually asks for a divorce, he refuses. He gives her the thing and then takes it back, which is what you'd expect if what she has is a slot in somebody else's preference ordering rather than a right. That's roughly Gibbard's complaint about the paradox -- that it only bites because rights are being modelled as decisiveness over pairs of social states instead of as protected choices, liberties you exercise no matter how anyone ranks the state that results. Does the novel bear that out, or does Clifford's psuedo-liberalism fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the model -- the heir business, the class horror of it being the gamekeeper, all that?

Second thing. Sen needs what the literature calls meddlesome preferences to get his cycle: the prude's ranking depends on what the other guy does. I want to argue the novel is a study of meddlesomeness as a social fact rather than a weird artifact of a made-up example -- Clifford, the nurse, the sister, the village, the rumours from Mellors's estranged wife that get him sacked. Does that hold up or am I stretching?

Third, the ending. Nobody gets a right there. Mellors loses his job and goes off to work on a farm, Connie's pregnant and unmarried and Clifford won't release her, and the book closes on a letter and a plan to be together in a couple of years. That looks like exit instead of voice: they shrink the society down to two people and walk out of the aggregation problem altogether. Is that an answer to Sen, or a cheat -- and does it get worse once you notice Lawrence seems to be recommending it politically?

Last thing, and be blunt. Half the book is about coal and machines and Clifford wanting to turn men into parts of a mechanism, and I have a nasty suspicion the real philosophical content there is alienation and I'm bolting a social choice toy onto it. Tell me if that's what I'm doing. No bibliography at the end please, I want your argument, not a reading list.
