I'm a third-year philosophy of medicine PhD student, currently stuck on my quals paper about the determination of death, and I've reached the point where I can't tell whether I have a view or just a pile of reflexes. So here are my gut verdicts. I want to know what principle they add up to, or which ones I have to throw out. No lit review please — I've read the usual suspects — I want the principle itself, stated precisely.

1. Ventilator, fixed pupils, no gag, no cough, apnea test positive, no flow on the angio. Dead. Not a hard call for me.
2. Same patient, but his leg jerks when they cut down on a line, and his sodium is stable so whatever's left of his posterior pituitary is still doing something. Still dead. Spinal stuff doesn't move me at all, and I can't say why it shouldn't if my story is about the body as a whole.
3. That patient is 16 weeks pregnant. They keep her on support for three and a half months; she heals a trach site, survives two pneumonias, mostly holds her own temperature, and delivers a healthy baby. Dead the entire time. And honestly the gestation makes me *more* comfortable with calling her dead, which I'm aware is backwards.
4. Woman in a vegetative state eight years: eyes open, sleep-wake cycles, breathes and swallows on her own, no awareness anyone can demonstrate. Not dead. But if I picture a hospital declaring her dead and taking her heart, I don't feel the horror I feel about the locked-in guy in the next bed. So my "alive" for her is apparently doing different work than my "alive" for him.
5. Locked-in, fully aware, only his eyes move: alive, obviously, and taking his heart would be murder.
6. Anencephalic newborn, brainstem working, breathing on her own: not dead. But I think it would be permissible to take her kidneys for another baby even though I know that's what finishes her. So for me "is dead" and "may not be killed for organs" come apart here, and I don't know whether that's a principled move or a dodge.
7. Teenager pulled out of a frozen-over pond, forty minutes of CPR, cold as a table, no pulse, no reflexes, flat line. Not dead. Keep working. I'd be furious if they called it.
8. Controlled donation after circulatory death: family withdraws support, the heart stops, the team waits five minutes, calls the death, opens the chest, and that same heart is beating in somebody else an hour later. Dead at five minutes.
9. Guy with a total artificial heart, no ventricles, walking around the ward, you can't find a pulse or a cuff pressure, talks to you the whole time. Alive, obviously.
10. In the abdominal DCD cases they now clamp and vent the vessles to the head before perfusing the organs, so the brain never gets blood back even though the pump could give it. I think the death happened before the clamp. I think that because otherwise the whole practice looks like killing, and I don't want it to look like killing.

The knot I can't untie: 7 and 8 look like the same physiology to me — no pulse, no brain function — and I give opposite verdicts. And if the difference is supposed to be whether circulation can come back, then in 8 it obviously can (the heart gets transplanted!), and in 10 the team is deliberately making sure the brain's can't. So reversibility isn't a fact about the body alone; its got something to do with what we've decided to do. But then death partly depends on our intentions, which I also don't want to accept.

My half-formed guess is: dead when the organism can no longer run itself as a whole on its own, without being propped up. That handles 1, 2, 5, 9. It breaks on 3 (that body was running an enormous amount of itself), on 7 (a cold body isn't running itself at all), and on 8, where I can't tell whether I need "permanently" or "irreversibly" stopped.

So: give me the best principle, or the two or three live candidates, with a clause tied to each case that motivates it. Then tell me what each candidate says about a hydranencephalic kid who's missing both hemispheres but cries when he's suctioned and lives to six, and about a patient on ECMO for two weeks whose whole brain is gone but whose own heart still beats when the pump is turned down. And be blunt about which of my ten verdicts each one forces me to retract. If the honest answer is that "death" is a policy concept and I should stop hunting for the biological fact, say so — and tell me what that does to 6 and 10, because those are the two I'd least like to lose.
