Two hours of arguing with my flatmate (econ PhD, second year, insufferable about social choice) and we got absolutely nowhere, partly because I'm not certain he wasn't making bits up as he went along. I'd like a second opinion on whether my own package holds together, not on who won the argument. The topic was Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal — I already know the proof and I can draw the Lady Chatterley cycle on a napkin, so please don't re-derive it or explain what the conditions mean. Just audit me.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

(a) Rights are side constraints, not goals. They screen states out; they don't get promoted. I think that's what actually dissolves the paradox — the cycle only appears because Sen builds the right-holder's decisiveness into the social ordering itself.
(b) The protected sphere has to be carved out of complete social states, describing what everyone is doing. I reject the fix where you redefine rights over narrow self-regarding features, because working out which features count as self-regarding is the question at issue.
(c) Pareto is unconditional for me. If literally everyone prefers x to y, society ranks x above y, full stop — even when everybody's reason is nosiness about somebody else's bedroom.
(d) Domain restrictions are cheating. I specifically don't buy the move where you say the paradox only arises because people have interfering, meddlesome preferences about each other: you can't solve a problem by assuming away the preferences that generate it.
(e) ...that said, I do think the paradox is shallow, and that it only bites when preferences reach into other people's private business. With purely self-regarding preferences there's nothing there at all.
(f) Rights are inalienable. No waiving, no trading, no forfeiture. Lewd has his right whether or not he feels like using it, and nobody can buy him out of it. This is the bit that stops my view sliding back into rights-as-goals through the back door.
(g) In the Lady Chatterley case I told him the right outcome is that Prude reads the book, because a rights framework that lands everyone in an outcome both of them rank below another one they could have had is broken. I thought that was a knockdown point; he said it wasn't and wouldn't say why, just smiled.
(h) I reject IIA anyway, so I figure Arrow-flavoured results don't get much grip on me in general.
(i) And I want, at the end of the day, a complete and transitive social ranking over whatever the rights leave on the table, because public justification needs something to point at. He claimed (a) and (i) can't both be true.

So: go through these and tell me which pairs genuinly pull against each other and which only look like they do — I'd rather have the false alarms identified than one long list where everything is equally bad. Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me downstream. Then tell me which single change to the package buys the most consistency, and be blunt about (g): was it the point I thought it was, or was he right to smile?
