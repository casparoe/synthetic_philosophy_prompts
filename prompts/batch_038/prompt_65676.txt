I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about choices that change who will exist, and my verdicts don't seem to hang together. Here they are.

1. My council rezones a district and builds a ring road. A century and a half from now, essentially nobody alive then is anybody who would have been alive otherwise. Nothing wrong here, and nobody wronged, even loosely.
2. The same council buries chemical waste on the cheapest site, knowing the containers will fail in two hundred years and badly injure whoever lives nearby then — people who, because of everything the decision sets in motion, would not otherwise have existed. Clearly wrong, and just as clearly not a wrong against any of them, each of whom will have a life worth living and would not trade it away.
3. A doctor tells a woman to wait three months before conceiving because of a transient infection; conceiving now would mean a deaf child with a life fully worth living. She waits and has a hearing child. Right decision — but I don't feel that she avoided wronging anyone.
4. A clinic holds two viable embryos, one of which would become that same deaf child with a life just as worth living, and transfers the other. Also right — except that here I want to say something was forgone, that the alternative would have been a wrong, though I cannot say to whom. Cases 3 and 4 look to me like the same facts, and I don't feel the same way about them.
5. A neighbour, given the same advice, didn't wait. Her daughter is nine, deaf, thriving, and would not want to have been anyone else. I think my neighbour did something wrong; I also think it would be offensive to tell her so.
6. A couple can stop at two children or go on to have twins, whose lives would each be somewhat worse — divided attention, more strain — but still clearly good, and whose existence means the single third child they would otherwise have had never exists. Permissible, I think, and none of my business. Yet this has the same shape as 2, which I condemned.

My own tentative principle is that an act is wrong when the lives it causes to be lived are predictably worse than the lives that would otherwise have been lived. That gets 2 but seems to condemn 6, and I don't see how to add a clause about numbers without begging the question.

Could you state a principle, or a small set of candidates, that delivers these verdicts? Say precisely what it is keyed to — it can't simply be "worse off than they would otherwise have been" — and then go clause by clause, saying which of the six cases forces each clause in. Tell me what the principle says about cases I haven't raised: a man with late-onset Huntington's who conceives knowing his child has a fifty per cent chance of a life that is worth living but cut short; a national tax policy that shifts childbearing earlier and so reshuffles who exists with no welfare difference at all; and a choice that predictably makes the lives that will be lived better than the alternative ones, where I suspect nothing is actually required. Finally, if no principle saves all six, say which verdict should go and why — I'd rather lose an intuition than acquire an epicycle for every case.
