I run a small cross-Atlantic reading group and two of us have been going round in circles for about a year on hate speech law. One side is the American near-absolutist: the state can't be trusted to define the hateful, the dignity injury is real but doesn't licence criminalisation, counter-speech plus social and professional sanction is the actual remedy, and every European reassurance about narrow drafting ignores who'll be running the prosecutor's office in ten years' time. The other is the European dignitarian: equal standing in public is a good the state has to guarantee rather than hope for, hate speech is an injury to status and not merely an unwelcome opinion, and the slippery slope is empirically overblown in the places that have run these laws for decades.

I don't want another round of argument. I want the joint statement the two of them could both put their names to after a proper adversarial collaboration.

To stop either of them reaching for a pet anecdote, fix the facts. Both sides must argue from this one stipulated record, and neither may supplement it with real-world cases:

Avenne — population about 9 million, one long-settled minority of roughly 12% distinguished by language and religion, a decade of very public argument about it. The provision: it is an offence to publish a statement, with the intention of stirring hatred against a group defined by ethnicity, religion or sexual orientation, which is capable of reaching an audience of more than five hundred people. Private talk and small meetings fall outside it. There's no requirement that violence followed, and none that anyone was actually persuaded. Maximum penalty: a fine or six months.

Twelve years of operation: 41 prosecutions, 19 convictions. Twenty-three of the 41 fall inside an eighteen-month window of rioting in one city. Of the other eighteen, five followed complaints from members of the targeted group, nine were begun by police or prosecutors on their own initiative, and four were brought by political figures against opponents. No custodial sentence was ever actually imposed; the median fine was around two weeks of median income. Over the same twelve years there were three successful civil claims brought by targets, and eleven occasions on which an employer or a university disciplined a speaker with no state involvement at all.

Neighbouring Corran — demographically similar, no such provision, comparable press freedom. Over the same twelve years, reported harassment offences against its own comparable minority rose by about a third, while survey measures of that minority's sense of belonging improved slightly. Neither jurisdiction has run anything resembling an experiment, and neither collects comparable statistics on non-criminal sanctions.

Now the deliverable:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign — including some about what hate speech does to its targets, some about what criminal law does to speech in general, and at least one about the state. Non-vacuous only: if a claim amounts to "both sides care about minorities", cut it.
2. A disagreements table, one row per genuine disagreement, with a separate column for how the near-absolutist would word their own position and how the dignitarian would word theirs, on the constraint that each side accepts the wording of their own column.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where an answer would actually move someone. For each: what case, comparison or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down beforehand. At least one crux should be of the form "what happens in Avenne over the following ten years if the provision is repealed tomorrow".
4. A seperate section on how much of this is merely verbal. My hunch is that "harm" and "censorship" are each doing two distinct jobs in this argument; if you agree, say exactly which rows of the table dissolve under the disambiguation and which survive it.

Then close with the residue: name the one disagreement you'd expect to survive even if every empirical crux came out in the near-absolutist's favour, stated so that both sides recognise it as thier disagreement rather than as a caricature of it.

No case law, no statutes, no citations, no reading list — I want the argument, not the docket.
