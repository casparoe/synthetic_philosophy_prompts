I'm TA-ing a 75-minute discussion section for a sophomore course on the meaning of life — 14 students, and this week they've read Schlick's short 1931 essay on the subject (the one that ends up with life as play and the claim that demanding a purpose for life as a whole is demanding something that couldn't in principle be supplied). My problem is that on the page the thing lands as a bumper sticker. Last time I taught it, two students said some version of "so he's telling us to relax and enjoy stuff, that's not an argument," and I never got them past that. What I want them to see is that there's a structural claim underneath: purposes always point further out, so a chain of them either runs on forever or bottoms out in something that isn't a purpose at all; life as a whole can't be a means to anything outside itself; and if you posit something outside — god, a cosmic plan, the fate of humanity a million years hence — you've just shoved the question one step down the line and turned life into an instrument. Same with the youth/play material, which reads to them as nostalgia: the claim isn't that adults are sad, it's that they've converted every activity into a means for a further means, and that the other way of doing things is a capacity that got suppressed rather than a property they lost. Relevant context: about half my section is pre-law or pre-med, so they have extremely well-developed purpose-chains and zero interest in being told the chains are the problem.

So design an in-class game or structured activity for those 14 students that makes them run into the regress themselves instead of hearing about it. My instinct — which you should feel free to throw out entirely and build something better — is a token economy where every action a student takes has to be licensed by pointing at some further action it serves, so nobody is ever allowed to just do a thing, and then a second phase where the licensing is quietly withdrawn and they find out to their own surprise which things they keep doing. Constraints: no lecture before they play, no more than one printed page per student, no real money, and the words play and meaning shouldn't be used as a thesis by anyone until the debrief. The payoff I'm after is two-fold: they should feel that the demand for a purpose of the whole isn't merely unanswered but unanswerable, and they should end up thinking Schlick's real move is "you've been mis-describing what you're doing all along" rather than "chill and enjoy yourself."

Give me the full rules and a materials list, a scoring scheme that works with counters or just slips of paper, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto specific steps of the argument — including where the suppressed-capacity claim is supposed to show up in their actual behaviour and how I'd point at it without announcing it. Then be honest with me about what will predictably break the first time I run this. I already expect one student to start gaming the licensing rule by inventing elaborate purposes on the spot, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a disaster or the best thing that could happen, so tell me how to steer it either way. And finish with a frank note on which feature of Schlick's view a game like this flat-out cannot reproduce, that I'll have to supply by hand afterwards.
