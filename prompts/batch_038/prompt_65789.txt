I'm trying to write down what would actually count as solving the combination problem before I read another panpsychism paper, and I want to pin it to a concrete case instead of doing it in the abstract.

The case: two people are joined by a bidirectional neural link. Early on it's low-bandwidth — a handful of channels, mostly motor to motor. Later it's high-bandwidth, always on, and eventually the two brains are running a shared task, with feedback loops that cross the link faster then any signal crosses the corpus callosum. I want a theory of consciousness that can tell me, at each stage, how many subjects there are, whose experiences they are, and what it's like to be the thing that spans both.

So: write the desiderata. What does a solution to the combination problem have to deliver to handle a case like that — and I mean the whole list, including the ones nobody argues about. The phenomena it has to explain (the unity of a single experience, the fact that experience has a structure that tracks the structure of what it's about, why there's something it's like to be the whole at all rather than just a heap of micro-experiences arranged the right way). The distinctions it can't collapse (being one subject vs. being aware of another subject; a subject having parts vs. a subject being a collection of subjects; degree of consciousness vs. degree of unity vs. degree of integration). The results it can't contradict (whatever split-brain and binding research actually shows, whatever the causal-closure / epiphenomenalism arguments force you into, anything about overdetermination). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it and say whether it's non-negotiable or just the taste of one camp.

Then the part I most care about: which of these are in tension, and is some subset jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that "the number of subjects is always determinate" plus "subjects genuinely combine to produce further subjects" plus "no experience is left over unowned" can't all hold together — either confirm that with an argument or show me where I've mis-stated it. And tell me which single requirement does the most work ruling candidates out.

Two extra constraints. First, don't smuggle the requirements in from human psychology: if a desideratum only makes sense for something with a body or a language or a memory of its own past, flag it, because my case is supposed to run for the linked brains and, in principal, for a mesh of coupled chips with no cortex anywhere in it. Second, if a short note on how the standard contenders do against the list falls out naturally, fine — but don't spend much on it, the list is the deliverable.

Finish by saying what would change your mind about the list itself: which requirement you'd give up if you were forced to, and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to break down.

Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the one thing a solution has to get right, in your view — then give the reasoning. No preamble and don't restate what I asked. A table is fine for the list, but I want the tension argument in prose.
