I am a graduate teaching assistant for our law school's second-year seminar on statutory interpretation, and my own coursework this term is in jurisprudence. The professor has asked me to draft the practice final that our student-led revision session will sit. Please take on the dual role of a legal philosopher of interpretation and a practising appellate advocate, and think carefully before drafting: begin by sketching, for yourself, the range of readings a competent student could give of the material below, and identify which of them are dead ends that a strong answer must refuse rather than merely underweight.

The question must turn on a fictional enactment I have written, so that no candidate can answer from memory of real authority. The Riverton Drone Safety Act, section 4, provides: (1) a person must not operate an unmanned aircraft within 200 metres of a hospital helipad; (2) subsection (1) does not apply to an aircraft operated by, or under contract to, an emergency medical service; (3) a person who contravenes subsection (1) commits an offence punishable by a fine of up to $5,000. The Act's preamble recites that its object is "to protect patients carried to and from hospital by air, and the crews who carry them, from the risks posed by unmanned aircraft." Three months before the incident in question, the helipad at Riverton General was closed for resurfacing; no aircraft landed on or departed from it during that period, and the hospital diverted air ambulances to a neighbouring airfield. The defendant flew a camera drone 150 metres from the centre of the pad for six minutes, filming the closed surface for a story about deferred hospital maintenance. No air ambulance was within two kilometres. He is charged under subsection (1).

The question should have two parts. Part (a) asks the candidate to decide the case twice — once as a committed textualist, once as a committed purposivist — and, crucially, to show that each theory's own internal resources (semantic context, the structure of the subsection (2) exemption, bright-line drafting, the absurdity safety valve; on the other side, the level-of-generality problem in stating the purpose, the mischief, the interest in keeping the corridor available) cut both ways rather than only one. Part (b) supposes that the legislature, learning of the prosecution, amends subsection (1) by inserting "(including a helipad that is temporarily out of service)" after "hospital helipad," and asks whether that amendment establishes what the original text always meant or changes what it means, and what the answer implies for the claim that statutory meaning is fixed at enactment.

Deliver: (i) the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, marked as a 75-minute essay worth 25 marks, with the two parts and their mark split stated; (ii) a model answer of about 700 words; (iii) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points whose marks sum to 25, each carrying a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong; and (iv) a short closing paragraph stating precisely what separates an A from a B answer, naming the specific moves each must contain — for instance, whether the candidate notices that the textualist and the purposivist may reach the same verdict for incompatible reasons, and whether they can say what that convergence does and does not prove about the disagreement between the theories.

Two constraints. Please include no citations, case names, bibliography, or reading list: the students sit this closed-book and I want the reasoning to stand on its own rather than on authority, and I do not want invented references. And do not use the "no vehicles in the park" hypothetical anywhere in the question, the model answer, or the marking scheme — every student reaches for it, and I want the practice paper to force them past it.
