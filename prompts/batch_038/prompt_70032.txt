For a conference paper I have reconstructed an argument against al-Ghazālī's account of causation in the Incoherence of the Philosophers, and the reconstruction has come out stronger than I intended: I do not accept its conclusion, but I cannot see which of the steps I am entitled to drop. Here they are as I have them.

(1) Al-Ghazālī denies that the link between what we call a cause and its effect is necessary in virtue of anything belonging to the two terms: when fire contacts cotton, the burning is created by God on the occasion of the contact, and God could create the contact without the burning, or the burning without any contact with fire.

(2) So on any occasion, which effect follows is settled by God's will alone, and that will is not determined by the natures of the things brought into contact.

(3) Where an outcome is settled by a will that nothing in the situation determines, then apart from what has been observed nothing counts in favor of one candidate outcome over another.

(4) In the case of fire and cotton, what has been observed is only that burning has followed contact so far; and that record is equally consistent with God's continuing the sequence, interrupting it, or replacing it with a different one, since by (1) nothing in the fire or in the cotton favors any of the three.

(5) When the available evidence is consistent with several outcomes alike and nothing further favors any one of them, expecting one of them rather than another is arbitrary.

(6) Hence expecting the burning on the next contact is arbitrary — and equally for food nourishing, water quenching, and the sun rising.

(7) The appeal to God's habit cannot repair this. A habit is just the observed sequence together with the assumption that it continues, and (2) through (5) deny that there is any ground for that assumption; citing the habit therefore restates the datum requiring a ground rather than supplying one.

(8) If no empirical expectation is better founded than its contrary, the position also removes its own footing: there is no better ground for believing that the sequence has been regular up to now, or that prophets occasionally work against it, than for the opposite. This is the force of Ibn Rushd's rejoinder, that a world stripped of causal connection is a world stripped of knowledge.

(9) Therefore al-Ghazālī's occasionalism is either self-undermining or committed to the claim that no empirical expectation is better founded than any other.

I would rather keep (1), which I take to be the honest reading of the text and not a retreat to mere agnosticism about necessity, and I would rather keep (7), since the replies I have had from defenders amount to reciting the notion of God's habit without saying why a habit licenses an expectation. What I cannot accept is (9).

So tell me which step you reject, and state it flatly rather than surveying the field and leaving me to choose: one cut, defended. Then explain why that step looked unobjectionable when I wrote it, since it did; what has to be added or clarified to block it; and what the block costs al-Ghazālī and the occasionalist generally. Be definite on two further points. First, whether what I have exposed is a defect in my reconstruction or a genuine vulnerability in the Ashʿarite position itself — I need to know which paper to write. Second, whether the argument, if it goes through at all, generalizes to any account that grounds the world's regularities in the will of a free agent rather than in the natures of things, Malebranche's included; if you think it does generalize, say what a theist who wants both a free creator and reliable induction is left with.
