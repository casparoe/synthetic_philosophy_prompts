For thirty-seven years I worked as a physicist, a good deal of that time on problems adjacent to defence, and only since retiring have I read philosophy with any seriousness. One feature of the law of armed conflict has arrested me, and I have not been able to make sense of it. A soldier taken prisoner may not be tried merely for having fought: so long as his acts were lawful, he is entitled to prisoner-of-war status, must be detained humanely, and must be released once hostilities end — and this holds whether or not the state he served had any right to be fighting at all. The same legal order nonetheless treats the planning and waging of aggressive war as a crime, one that by its nature only those in positions to direct a state's political or military action can commit, so that the foot soldier is immune from a charge that his own commanders are exposed to.

I should like an account of what theory of moral responsibility, and of liability to be harmed or punished, this pairing presupposes. Does the soldier's immunity rest on something like the moral equality of combatants — the thought that each side's fighters are equally permitted to kill and equally unpunishable for doing so — or can it be reconstructed without that, from the soldier's epistemic position regarding his own side's cause, from considerations of duress and role obligation, from the impracticability of adjudicating the justice of a war case by case in a criminal court, or from an incentive-based rationale concerning reciprocity and the humane treatment of captives? Is the leader–soldier asymmetry a coherent completion of whichever account you favour, or does it rest on a different and incompatible picture of agency? Please distinguish what these practices strictly require from what they merely sit comfortably with, note where several rival theories would license the same arrangement, and say concretely what would have to change in the treatment of captives or of commanders if the moral-equality reading were given up.
