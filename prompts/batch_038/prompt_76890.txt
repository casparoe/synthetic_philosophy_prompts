For the past two years our research group has been studying the data-management practices of a multi-site scientific consortium: roughly forty laboratories, fifteen years of shared history, one central repository. Three regularities hold with near-perfect compliance. Sample identifiers follow a fixed site-prefix, date, and serial format. Metadata workbooks present the same fields in the same order and the same units. Internal correspondence uses a stable set of abbreviations that appear nowhere in official documentation. Each is arbitrary in the sense that matters here: a rival format existed early on, was perfectly workable, and lost; we have found no measure on which the surviving format is superior.

The evidence we have collected is as follows. Newcomers conform within weeks, mostly by imitating shared templates; few members can state the rules explicitly, and several state them incorrectly when asked. Non-conforming submissions are rejected automatically by a validation script; the rejection message is impersonal and no interviewee reported being criticized, but resubmission costs a site several hours, several members volunteered that the repository staff notice who gets rejected repeatedly, and rejection counts appear in reports to the steering committee. In a survey, most members said they would favor migrating to a cleaner standard provided everyone else migrated, and almost none said they would migrate unilaterally. Conformity also persists where the shared pipeline never sees it: local notebooks, private drafts, internal messaging. Nothing validates these and there is no interoperability payoff. Finally, eight years ago a working group of five sites announced a revised identifier scheme; the validation script was updated six months later; within two years the whole consortium had migrated.

Our group has stalled on what to call this, and I have come to think the three positions are not engaging each other at all.

Position A says it is a convention in Lewis's sense: a recurrent coordination situation, an equilibrium in which each member's best response is to do what the others do, a workable rival that would have been an equilibrium too, and a mutual preference for conformity conditional on general conformity — which the survey supposedly displays directly. The migration is the mechanism Lewis describes: a salient precedent plus an announcement shifted expectations, and compliance followed with no change in anyone's material incentives. The validation script is downstream of the equilibrium, not constitutive of it. On the epistemic conditions, A insists Lewis treats them as idealizations that come in degrees, and that templates, onboarding documents, and the visible history of the migration supply mutual belief enough.

Position B says the survey shows the opposite of what A claims: a willingness to switch only if everyone switches is an expression of expected switching costs and fear of standing out, not a preference for conformity as such. What sustains compliance is anticipated cost — lost hours, visible rejections, exposure to the steering committee, and the unease several members described when contemplating a private deviation. The decisive datum is conformity where the coordination problem is absent altogether: a coordination equilibrium should dissipate once the coordination payoff disappears, and this one does not. The migration occurred only after the enforcement apparatus was rebuilt.

Position C says both over-intellectualize. There is no preference for conformity and no meaningful sanctioning, only the fact that templates, scripts, and downstream tooling were built around the format, so that deviation costs time and imitation is the cheapest individual path. Survey answers are post-hoc rationalizations of a cost calculation. Both 'convention' and 'norm' imply a regularity sustained by other people's behavior and attitudes; this one is sustained by software and by the sunk investments of forty sites. The migration was caused by an infrastructure upgrade, not by an announcement that made one option salient.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that we are not disagreeing about the consortium. A is answering why this regularity rather than the rival was selected and could be selected again; B is answering what keeps an individual member compliant when a one-off deviation is tempting; C is answering why the regularity resists change even though nearly everyone prefers the alternative. Selection, stability, and inertia are three different explananda, and each of us has fastened onto the piece of evidence that best serves a different one. I further suspect we disagree about evidential standards: A takes stated conditional preferences to reveal a disposition, B takes behavior in the absence of any payoff to be the only clean evidence, and C holds both to be contaminated by rationalization, so that no interview data could move anyone. And there is a question none of us can answer: how much recursive belief to attribute to members who cannot articulate the rules they follow perfectly well.

Diagnose this disagreement. Which is it — a verbal dispute over what 'convention' and 'norm' mean, a dispute over which explanandum is the target, a hidden empirical question about dispositions that our methods cannot reach, a clash of evidential standards, or bedrock intuitions about how much cognition and how much sociality to attribute to these agents? Support the diagnosis by reference to what each position actually says, not by a general survey of the theories of convention. Then tell me what would count as progress. Specifically: is there an intervention — an announcement without an infrastructure change, an infrastructure change without an announcement, an observed deviation with the rejection channel disabled, a survey instrument that separates conditional preference for conformity from expected switching cost, a comparison across subpopulations that differ in pipeline exposure — on which the three positions make different predictions? If so, design it, state each position's prediction, and say which result would falsify which. If you think no such intervention exists, explain why, and say whether the term should be split, so that 'convention' is reserved for regularities backed by a genuine preference for general conformity, or whether the impasse indicates that the question is ill-posed rather than merely difficult.
