I spent thirty-four years measuring reaction kinetics in a physical chemistry laboratory, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of time on my own, largely because the physics I already know keeps turning up in it. A former colleague sent me an essay from a general-audience science magazine about the block universe, and the passage that does all the argumentative work reads as follows:

"Presentism is the view that only the present moment is real; eternalism is the view that past, present, and future events are all equally real. Special relativity has settled the dispute in favour of eternalism. Because simultaneity is relative to a frame of reference, there is no fact of the matter about which distant events are happening now, and hence no way to draw a unique boundary around the present. The argument was pressed in the 1960s by Rietdijk and Putnam: if what is real must be the same for every observer, and each observer's 'now' picks out a different slice of spacetime, then the only non-arbitrary ontology is the whole four-dimensional block — as the geometry of Minkowski spacetime itself shows, since that geometry contains no privileged foliation. Two pedestrians passing on the street can disagree about whether events in the Andromeda galaxy are present, and both are right. It follows that the future is already there, which is why free will turns out to be an illusion: a block leaves nothing genuinely open. For the same reason, we should stop thinking of ourselves as objects wholly present at each moment and think instead of four-dimensional worms stretched out along the time axis. The one intuition presentism was built to protect — that time really passes — is a projection of our psychology. McTaggart made this point a century ago, and physics has since confirmed him. Presentists, meanwhile, still owe us an account of what makes sentences about the past true, when on their view there is nothing past left to make them true."

I should like an accuracy audit of this paragraph rather than a defence or a refutation of the position it recommends. Please go through it claim by claim and sort the claims into three piles: what a philosopher of physics would accept as stated; what is defensible only as a simplification, together with what that simplification hides; and what is false. Where a claim fails, tell me whether the failure concerns the physics or the metaphysics, since I am competent to judge the former and not the latter.

Three questions in particular. First, the essay asserts that relativity has "settled" the matter. State explicitly every premise required to get from the relativity of simultaneity to eternalism, identify each point at which a presentist could break the chain, and say for each such point what the presentist must give up or take on board in exchange — so that I can see whether any of the exits is dialectically free, or whether all of them carry a cost. Second, do the essay's two further conclusions — that fatalism follows, and that we are four-dimensional worms rather than wholly present things — follow from eternalism, or are they independent theses that have simply been bolted onto it? If they are independent, say what further commitment each would require. Third, what is McTaggart's argument actually an argument for and against, and is it the sort of claim that a physical theory could confirm in the sense the essay intends?

Finally, tell me how much of the paragraph's one apparent concession to the opposition — the difficulty about the truth of past-tensed sentences — survives as a live objection rather than as a challenge that presentists have well-known strategies for meeting, and whether, once the errors are stripped out, anything substantive remains for presentists and eternalists to disagree about, or whether the residue is in part a quarrel over how the word "exists" is to be used. I am inclined to suspect that the step from "the future is real" to "nothing is open" is a non sequitur, and that the appeal to McTaggart is decorative, but I distrust my own instinct here and would rather be shown the argument. I am not looking for references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the reasoning set out so that I can check it myself.
