I write first-person novels, so the metaphysics of the narrating "I" is not an abstraction for me; it is my principal instrument. My new book borrows the shape of Dennett's brain-in-a-vat narrative in *Brainstorms*: a surgeon splits a patient's cortex and distributes it between a refrigerated vault in Reykjavík and a simulation running on hardware in Singapore, forty milliseconds of lag between the two, while a radio-driven body walks the streets of Lisbon. My editor, who reads philosophy, holds that the premise is incoherent, and he has set his reasoning out as follows.

(1) Where a person is, is where that person's point of view is — which is why Dennett's narrator, brain in a vat in Houston, body somewhere out on the road, keeps reporting that he is in Houston, and goes on reporting it after the brain and the body are both destroyed and the backup simulation takes over.

(2) A person's point of view is constituted by whatever processes generate that person's stream of experience.

(3) So a person is wherever the processes generating that stream are being run.

(4) In my novel those processes are being run in two places at once.

(5) By (3), the person is wholly in Reykjavík and wholly in Singapore.

(6) Nothing can be wholly in two places at once; and he cannot be part in one and part in the other either, since (3) puts the person wherever the generating happens, and each site is doing enough generating to be doing it.

(7) Therefore my protagonist is either a scattered object or two persons, and on neither reading is there anyone who survives the operation — the novel has no narrator.

He presents this not as a quibble but as a reductio of (1), and says that if I keep (1) I owe him a principle for counting persons that does not multiply them every time their hardware is divided.

Tell me where the chain breaks. Name the single step you would reject first, explain why it looked unobjectionable to someone reasoning as carefully as he is, and then price the three repairs available to me: denying (1) and locating the person with the body; denying (6) and accepting a genuinely multi-located or temporally smeared subject; or conceding his conclusion and rewriting the book so that the voice on the page is not a person at all. I would rather keep (1) than any other premise, and I want to know exactly what that preference costs me — in the argument and on the page.

End with a last line of the form "Verdict: step N — <your reason in one clause>".
