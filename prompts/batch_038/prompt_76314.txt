I direct the policy analysis unit of a city council: a nonpartisan staff office of five generalist analysts, none of whom has econometrics training. Between local wage ordinances, rent regulation proposals, and occasional emergency price measures, roughly twenty-five proposed price controls reach us each session. Each one gets five working days, no modeling support, and a written analysis that will be read aloud in committee and picked apart in public by both business associations and tenant organizations, so it has to be defensible against a charge of bias from either direction. My office does not recommend; the committee decides what the city ought to value, and the city attorney decides whether we have authority to act at all, since state preemption of local price regulation is contested here and I do not want analysts reasoning their way into a legal position. Our standing instructions also forbid forecasting magnitudes we cannot support, so any framework we adopt has to work on the sign and the channel of an effect rather than its size. We currently have nothing: each analyst improvises, and two of us can look at the same ordinance and describe it in incompatible ways.

Please write the decision procedure we should be running instead. I want a sequence of tests that an analyst applies to a proposed control in a fixed order, and for each test: the question asked, the kind of evidence from a public record that counts as a sufficient answer, which answer terminates the analysis in a negative finding, which answer terminates it in an honest determination that the record does not support a conclusion, and which answer passes the proposal on. Tell me, as you build it, which distinctions from the economics of price controls have to survive translation into a checklist and which can be dropped without misleading a reader who never learned the theory, and why you drew the line where you did.

The procedure should open by classifying the market rather than the control, because our repeated failure has been to assume every price ceiling works the same way. It needs to determine whether price is actually the operative rationing device in the affected market or whether allocation already runs through waiting lists, lotteries, search, and selection by the party not on the constrained side; whether the control would bind for most transactions or only for marginal ones that nonetheless determine allocation; and whether it governs a fixed stock already in the city or a flow whose inbound supply responds to price, since a ceiling on the former and a ceiling on the latter are different policies wearing the same name. It needs to locate who ends up holding discretionary power over whom once price stops doing the allocating, and to treat that as an ethical finding in the memo rather than a footnote about efficiency, because in our experience this is the question the committee is really arguing about. It needs to separate a transfer between identifiable parties from a destruction of surplus that nobody receives, and to separate effects on present participants from effects on the people who would have been participants a decade from now and who appear at no hearing and file no comment.

It also needs the exceptions, stated as tests a generalist can actually run rather than as caveats. Where employers already possess wage-setting power, a floor can raise both pay and employment; give me the observable conditions that would let an analyst say whether that description fits this city and this sector, and what to do when the evidence is ambiguous. Where a ceiling falls on a fixed stock during a declared emergency, the familiar shortage prediction may not apply; tell me what would have to be true for that to hold and how to check it in two days. The procedure must force an explicit comparison against the alternative instruments realistically available to this city — transfers, subsidies, vouchers, supply-side regulatory changes — rather than against a frictionless ideal, and it must flag the exact point at which that comparison smuggles in a judgment about political feasibility, since whether an alternative is unavailable because it is unwise or because it is unpopular is not ours to decide. Mark every step that is a judgment call dressed as procedure. Mark every point where the analysis stops and hands the question to the committee because it concerns values rather than facts, and specify what the analyst should put in front of them at those points. Finally, give me the procedure's known failure modes: the classes of ordinance on which it will systematically mislead us, the ways short-run and long-run effects can diverge past the point where a single memo can hold both, and the situations in which the market benchmark itself is already so distorted by other interventions that there is no clean equilibrium to compare against.

Then run it on three proposals. First, an ordinance capping annual rent increases on existing tenancies at four percent, with the cap reset to market whenever a unit turns over, in a city whose zoning permits little new multifamily construction. Second, a city minimum wage rising over four years to a level that would be the highest in the state and, on our own rough estimate, near sixty percent of the metropolitan median hourly wage, applying only to employers with fifteen or more employees inside city limits. Third, an ordinance extending the cap in the first proposal to newly built rental units with no reset at turnover, introduced by the same sponsors as the remedy for the displacement the first ordinance is projected to cause. For each, show the tests being applied in order with the answers a competent analyst would record from the public record, and state the resulting finding. At least one of these should come out in a way its sponsors will not like, and I want you to say which, why the procedure forces that result, and how an analyst should write it so the finding survives the hearing. If the honest output on any of them is that the record cannot support a conclusion, say so and specify what evidence would settle it. Close with a plain statement of what the procedure cannot do — the questions it appears to answer but does not.

Do not include citations, a bibliography, a reading list, or verbatim quotations from economists; paraphrase everything, and write on the assumption that neither you nor the reader can look anything up. The finished product has to be auditable: a journalist should be able to follow it step by step, and an advocate who disagrees should be able to name the single test they contest.
