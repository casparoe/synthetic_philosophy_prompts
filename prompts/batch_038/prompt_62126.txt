My housemate is drafting a long post attacking the whole "science can finally tell us how to live" genre, and the engine of it is an analogy I can't evaluate. He says deriving an ought from an is would be like violating conservation of energy: a valid argument can't manufacture content that wasn't in the premises, so if every premise is purely descriptive then nothing normative can come out the far end — and anyone who thinks otherwise is smuggling a normative premise in and dressing it up as data. He treats this as a law rather then a rule of thumb and thinks it disposes of the naturalists in one move.

Two people objected at our reading group and I couldnt referee. One said the law is provably false, and gave this: from the plain descriptive claim that tea drinking is common in England you can validly infer the disjunction of that claim with the claim that all New Zealanders ought to be shot, by disjunction introduction. Then he pressed a dilemma — either that disjunction counts as normative, in which case you've already got an ought out of an is, or it counts as descriptive, in which case adding one more descriptive premise denying the tea drinking and running a disjunctive syllogism gets you a flat out ought. The other objector said the analogy is aimed at a Hume who never existed, since Hume's complaint was about authors who drift from is-statements to ought-statements without noticing they've changed the subject, not about what's formally derivable.

Can you audit the analogy? Spell out the correspondence table — what plays the energy, what plays the closed system, what plays the law itself, what "content" is supposed to mean here, and who exactly the smuggler is. Then the disanalogies, separated into the ones that actually bite against the use he's making of it and the ones that are just noise. Does the one-move conclusion survive once they're on the table? And is there a better analogy — I keep thinking dimensional analysis fits better, you can't add metres to seconds, but that seems to imply a legitimate conversion factor might exist, which could be worse for him than the version he started with. Last thing: does the analogy, on its own terms, condemn going the other direction too, ought to is? He says it doesn't and I can't see why not.
