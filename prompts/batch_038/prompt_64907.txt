I run an internal forecasting program on a commodity desk — 14 analysts, roughly 35 questions a week (recession calls, OPEC decisions, refinery outages, election stuff), and the numbers go straight into a position-sizing and limits model, so a bad 0.9 is expensive. Management's new ask is that I flag "irrational" probability estimates before they hit sizing. My first thought was the Dutch book story — Ramsey and de Finetti, coherence: if your degrees of belief don't obey the probability calculus, then there's a package of bets, each of which you'd accept at your own quoted prices, that loses you money for sure no matter how things turn out. Which sounds like exactly the check I want, except the theorem's assumptions are its fantasy down here. Nobody accepts every bet at their quoted price. There are stake limits and transaction costs. And half the time I'm not sure two analysts are even answering about the same event.

So: write me a procedure I can actually run. Ordered checks, what counts as passing each one, what triggers a flag, how big an inconsistency has to be before it's worth bothering anyone given the stakes, and where the procedure stops and hands the thing to a human because it's a judgment call and not a calculation. I get maybe 20 minutes per analyst per week, nobody on staff has read any of this, and the output has to be a one-line reason code I can defend to the analyst being flagged — two of them are senior and will absolutely push back.

Hard cases it has to survive, all real-ish:

- A quotes 0.75 for the Fed cutting at least twice next year, 0.6 for a US recession being declared next year, and 0.2 for both, and she also tells me the cuts are certain if there's a recession. Maybe that one's obvious. Tell me what to actually do with it.
- B won't give points, he gives ranges (0.3 to 0.5). He can't be booked at all, which makes him look like the most rational guy in the room, but his numbers also can't feed the sizing model or be scored sharply. Flag him, excuse him, or force him to commit?
- C is impeccably coherent and has been badly calibrated for two years. I dont want the procedure calling her irrational, but I also don't want it staying silent.
- D announces an update rule ("if the rig count drops below X I go to 0.9") and his prices today plus that rule would let a counterparty book him across the two dates. In practice no counterparty can actually bet with him at both dates. Flag, downgrade, or ignore?
- Two analysts answered what looked like the same question about "default" — one meant failure to pay, one meant a distressed exchange. Any conjunction test run across them is meaningless, so I assume the procedure has to start somewhere before the arithmetic.

And be honest about which of the steps are judgment calls in a procedure costume, what the thing structurally can't catch, and why bookability should matter to a desk that never actually bets — i.e. whether the reading on which incoherence matters because it produces bad decisions rather than because you'd literally lose money (the Christensen move, as I understand it) does better work in my setting than the money-loss reading. Then tell me what would count as evidence the flags are worth anything at all: do they predict something downstream, or am I generating busywork?

Run it on three cases, including one where the right answer is "leave it alone" and one where it flags somebody senior and I have to have an awful conversation.
