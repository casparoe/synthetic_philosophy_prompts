**The blanket is paid for**

1. The theory as I understand it, in case I've got it wrong. Your brain isn't a camera, it's a guessing machine. It carries a model of how sensations get caused and it spends every waking moment comparing what it expects to feel against what it feels. The gap is a prediction error. Perception is what happens when you shrink that gap by changing the expectation; action is what happens when you shrink it the other way, by moving so the world matches the guess — that's the active inference half. Friston's free energy principle pushes this further out: a thing that hangs around long enough to be worth calling a system is a thing whose sensations stay, on average, inside the range it expects. Surprise, in the technical sense, is how improbable your sensory data is under your own model, and you can't actually compute it, because that would require knowing all the causes of your sensations. So you minimize a stand-in, variational free energy, which is always at least as large as surprise and equals it in the limit where your guesses are exactly right. Stay inside your own expectations and you stay alive.

2. The boundary problem. None of that means anything until you say what the system is. The answer given is a Markov blanket: a set of states — sensory ones coming in, active ones going out — that screens inside off from outside, so that internal and external states are conditionally independent once you know the blanket. Cells have them, organs have them, bodies have them, and the framework is happy to nest them, blankets inside blankets. I accept the nesting. That's not my complaint.

3. My bit. The blanket gets described as a fact about a system, something it either has or doesn't, like a shell. But a blanket is work. Holding a boundary where it is costs energy that is therefore not going into modelling anything. A cut has to be closed. Your gut contents have to be kept on the correct side of a wall by an immune system that is expensive to run. Your keys live in the same bowl so that the hallway stays predictable. So I want to say the interesting variable isn't "how much surprise does this thing minimize" but "what fraction of its budget goes into keeping the boundary in place instead of into using it." Call it the blanket tax. Different creatures pay wildly different rates. A spider builds part of its boundary out of its own body and hangs it in a tree: the web is cheap to make, it does a lot of predictive work, and losing it is close to losing the spider. A human pays the tax in language, money, calendars, keys, institutions — infrastructure whose only job is to keep the boundary where it was yesterday.

4. What I think this buys. The dark room objection says: if the goal is to minimize surprise, why don't organisms sit in a dark room and stay there? The usual reply is that a creature like me already expects itself to be out foraging, so a dark room is itself deeply surprising for that creature. As far as it goes, fine. But the answer was hiding in the question. Nothing in the machinery says why any particular set of expectations is the one you have; you can write down a perfectly well-formed model that expects a dark room. The blanket tax gives you something to say: expectations are worth having in proportion to how much boundary-maintenance they save you later, and a model that expects a dark room doesn't pay for the machinery that keeps it around. What kills it isn't the principle, it's that dead systems stop minimizing anything. So the free energy principle does the local accounting, and something else — selection, history, whatever you want to call it — sets the exchange rate.

5. The worry I can't shake. If a blanket is something you pay for, it's also something you can redraw whenever the theory runs into trouble. Web too weird? Include the web. Money too abstract? Include the bank. And a framework that lets you move its own boundary to fit the data isn't a framework, its a choice of description. Maybe the answer is that the right blanket is the one where the accounting balances out. But then I've defined "system" as "thing my bookkeeping works for," which is uncomfortably close to going in a circle.
