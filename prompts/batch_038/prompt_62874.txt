I am a novelist without any formal training in philosophy. I have read around the problem of evil, but I would not trust myself to detect a bad inference dressed in good prose, which is precisely what I am asking you to help me with.

In a late chapter, a character of mine — a hospice chaplain, intelligent, sincere, and emphatically not a strawman — must explain to a mother why her son's death does not, as he sees it, refute God. I built his speech out of an argument in an essay I read in a religious quarterly, because I wanted the real thing rather than my own paraphrase of a caricature. A theologian whose judgment I respect read the scene and pronounced the argument sound and fairly stated. I should like to know whether she was right, since if the speech contains errors I cannot see, better-informed readers than she will see them.

Here it is as my chaplain delivers it, following the essay closely:

"There are two very different claims that get run together under the heading 'the problem of evil.' The first is that the existence of a good and all-powerful God is logically incompatible with the existence of any evil at all. Hardly any philosophers hold that claim now, believing or not, because it can be answered: a being of perfect power and perfect goodness could have reasons for permitting evils that it would be worse, all things considered, to prevent, and the freedom of creatures is plainly one such reason — a world containing free beings who sometimes choose badly is worth more than a world of harmless puppets. The second claim is weaker, and is what people usually mean: that there is so much pointless suffering that God probably does not exist. But notice what that claim asks of you. It asks you to look at a child dying and to know that no good depends on it. We are not in a position to know any such thing. We cannot survey the whole of a single life, let alone the whole of a history, and an evil that looks gratuitous from inside one moment may be indispensable to a shape we cannot see. This is only what any reader of a novel already grants: the humiliations of the middle chapters are justified by the book, and the author, who holds the whole in mind, is not thereby cruel. God is the author of a story whose every part is answerable to the whole. So what survives of the problem of evil is not intellectual but emotional. It is a cry of pain, and pain deserves comfort rather than argument — but it is not evidence."

Three things, in this order.

First, an accuracy audit, sentence by sentence: what a philosopher of religion working on this problem would accept as it stands, what she would accept only with qualifications that change its force, and what she would say is simply false. I am especially interested in whether the passage keeps apart two things I suspect it is fusing — the claim that we cannot know God's reasons, and the confident account of what God's reason is — and in whether its description of what the weaker claim "asks of you" is a fair description of that claim.

Second, the analogy. As the person who does in fact torment characters for the sake of a whole, I find the comparison flattering and faintly alarming, and I want to know what it will and will not carry: what a philosopher would press as the disanalogy, and whether anything survives the pressing that my chaplain could honestly still use.

Third, the conclusion. Does the claim that what remains is feeling rather than evidence follow from anything true in the passage, or does it depend on the parts that fail? And then, practically: what is the smallest set of cuts and substitutions that would leave the speech both honest and strong — the best argument an intelligent believer actually has, still spoken in the register of a man comforting a bereaved mother rather than a man winning a debate? Please also tell me which of the flaws, if any, are the sort a thoughtful person would naturally commit, so that I can judge whether keeping them is characterization or only my own error wearing his collar.

I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a reading list. I want the audit itself, argued rather than sourced, and I would rather you mark plainly where you are uncertain than smooth it over.
