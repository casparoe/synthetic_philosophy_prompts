Six weeks ago I was seconded to a small foresight unit inside a national infrastructure regulator. My first deliverable is an annual register of low-probability, high-consequence risks, and the template our office inherited requires a single point probability beside each entry plus a confidence figure glossed as the analyst's subjective certainty. That confidence column has been populated for years with numbers no one can justify, and reading Keynes's Treatise on Probability convinced me the office is missing a distinction Keynes treats at length there: between the probability that a body of evidence confers on a conclusion and the weight of that evidence, a second dimension that can increase while the probability stays where it was, and that Keynes took to be irreducibly comparative rather than numerically measurable.

I need a two-page proposal for an eighteen-month postdoctoral project, though I would also welcome your candid judgment on whether that horizon is realistic for what I have in mind. The question: can Keynesian weight be explicated so that it does genuine normative work in the reporting of judgment, without collapsing into something we already have? I want the proposal to take the three candidate collapses seriously rather than wave them away: weight as the spread of an interval in an imprecise-probability model; weight as a second-order distribution over first-order probabilities; and weight as nothing over and above the quantity of evidence, so that it is measurable by counting. My own half-formed idea, which I would like you to stress-test rather than assume, is that weight is best read modally, as the degree to which a probability judgment would remain stable under the addition of admissible further evidence, and that this reading is what makes sense of Keynes's resistance to treating bodies of evidence as aggregates, since which further evidence counts as admissible depends on the whole of what one already has.

Please write the proposal itself: a precise statement of the question together with what a determinate answer to it would settle; why the existing positions leave it open, and in particular whether the imprecise-probability literature has in fact already answered it; the method, specifying what I would do and in what sequence across the eighteen months, and why a formal-plus-exegetical approach is preferable to the obvious alternatives of pure history of ideas or pure decision theory; what a successful result looks like, including whether the deliverable should be a representation theorem, a defensible reporting standard for bodies like mine, or both, and what would count as the project failing; and a sober assessment of scope, naming the components most likely to balloon. I will supply the references myself, so please omit any bibliography and feel free to name positions and arguments without citation.
