I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of economics — fourteen students, and our final session is on revealed preference and the welfare interpretation of utility. I would like to close it by handing out a short poem for the students to read aloud and then argue about for ten minutes. It should be spoken by utility itself, in the first person, near the end of its career: an entity that was once a felt magnitude (cardinal, additive, comparable across persons, the sort of thing one imagined summing) and that has since been reconstituted as a bookkeeping consequence of the shape of someone's choices — something that now exists if and only if those choices have a certain form, and that no longer claims to be inside anybody.

Form: terza rima, aba bcb cdc and so on, twenty-one to twenty-seven lines, in loose iambic pentameter. In place of the conventional single closing line, the final line should return to the rhyme sound with which the poem opened, so that the chain of rhymes closes on itself into a circle. That closure is not decorative; it is the poem's subject. A cycle is the one configuration in which the speaker cannot exist, and the form should let the reader hear the circle complete at precisely the moment the speaker explains why it must never complete.

The content should be carried entirely by the speaker's own account rather than by exposition:

- The money pump, concretely: three bundles, three separate occasions of choice, a coin surrendered at each exchange, and a man who ends where he began with less in his pocket.
- The cycle must be invisible in any single pairwise comparison. Each choice looks defensible on its own occasion, and the trouble appears only when choices are set side by side across different menus — which is why the consistency condition has to be strong enough to look across contexts. Let that be felt, not stated.
- What the speaker is now forbidden to say: that anyone was mistaken about his own good; that someone chose against his own interest for the sake of a principle or of another person; that one person's gain may be weighed against another's loss. Render each as a grammatical or existential incapacity — something the speaker's mouth will not form — rather than as a list of grievances.

Constraints: the word "utility" must not appear anywhere in the poem, though you should give it a title, and the reader should be able to work out who is speaking before the end. No verbatim quotation from any real text, and no lines invented and attributed to a real person; allusion only, if you allude at all. No archaic inversion, and no diction that turns it into a period-piece ode.

It has to work twice over: a philosopher of economics should find the argument right rather than merely name-dropped, and a student who has never heard of revealed preference should find it a compelling, faintly sinister monologue. After the poem, add a separate note of three or four sentences for the margin of the handout, marking the line at which the rhyme closes the circle and identifying the technical point that corresponds to each of the three forbidden sentences.
