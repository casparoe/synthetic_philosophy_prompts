I teach historiography and I want to practice the move I keep failing to make land with my students, so you're going to eat it from the other side. here's my opening:

your own rule -- that you're obliged to record what people say, not to believe it -- is the thing that undoes you, becuase every sentence in your history is one of two kinds. either it's somebody's story that you passed along, in which case it's evidence about what that person or that city believed and not about what happened; or it's your own reasoning, in which case it's evidence about how a Greek of your century thought, which is again not about what happened. so the book is a first-rate source for studying you and your informants and a worthless source for studying Xerxes. take the bit where you work out that Helen must have spent the war in Egypt, on the grounds that otherwise Priam would have handed her straight back rather than lose his sons and his city over one boy's appetite -- that's you assuming a king reasons the way you'd expect a king to reason. it tells us about your political imagination and nothing whatever about Troy. so either start vouching for things, and you're credulous, or keep your hands clean, and you never reach the events. pick a horn.
