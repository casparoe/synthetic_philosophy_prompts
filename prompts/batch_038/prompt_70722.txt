Our reading group has been slogging through Parfit's hitchhiker case and I built up an argument on the whiteboard that now seems to demolish every candidate answer at once. Someone please tell me where it breaks.

Setup: the driver only stops for people who'll actually pay, and he's dead right about what you're going to do — no acting, no bluffing, he just sees your intentions. The ride is worth $100 to you, paying is $20.

(1) What you have most reason to do is whatever best advances your own interests, all things considered.
(2) At the ATM in town, whether or not you promised and whether or not you meant it, not paying leaves you $20 better off.
(3) So at the ATM you have most reason not to pay.
(4) Given (1) and (3), the driver sees you won't pay.
(5) So he doesn't stop, and you walk.
(6) But walking is worse for you than paying $20 for a $100 ride.
(7) So you now have most reason to make yourself into someone for whom (3) comes out the other way — someone whose paying isn't hostage to what pays off at the ATM.
(8) Yet at the ATM that person pays against their own interests, by (2) and (3), which (1) says they have no reason to do.
(9) A theory that tells you to acquire motives it then condemns acting on is self-defeating by its own lights: follow it and you end up worse off than if you'd ignored it.
(10) By (5)–(7), self-interest fails as a guide exactly where it was supposed to be strongest — it can't even get you the ride.
(11) And a theory that can't get you the ride, when being ride-gettable is the whole point of the case, is refuted, not merely inconvenient.
(12) So there's no consistent account of rationality here: (1) refutes itself, dominance reasoning strands you on the highway, and there's no third option that survives both.

The conclusion I can't accept is (12), obviously, and the step I'd fight hardest to keep is (1). Here's what I want from you:

- Is there a hidden flaw — an equivocation between steps, a shift in the scope of "reason," a slide from what you'll do to what you'll have reason to do — or is the reasoning valid and one of these plausible premises simply has to go? Don't assume I've told you which kind of problem this is.
- If you think it proves too much, say so concretely: run the same structure on some other case and pull out a second conclusion that's even more obviously false, and use that to locate the bad move.
- Price each rejection. What do I give up if I keep (1) and drop (9)? If I drop (2)? If I deny that the driver's accuracy in (4) licenses anything? If I say (11) is just false and self-defeat isn't refutation — what do I then owe about what a theory of rationality is supposed to be for?

Please write the whole thing in American English.
