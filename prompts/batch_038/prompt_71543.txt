Almost every account of the world that anyone has ever held has been false, and yet we rank falsehoods constantly: we say that one of them was closer to the truth than another. I want to know whether that ranking obeys a principle, and I have therefore been assembling my own verdicts on particular cases in order to see whether a pattern emerges. The cases are below, in roughly ascending order of difficulty. Most of the verdicts feel secure to me; one triple I know sits badly together; one case I have deliberately left undecided.

1. Two false cosmologies. The Almagest's system of deferents and epicycles is false. So is the rival I will invent for comparison, on which the planets are lights fixed in a rigid dome that rotates once a day. I judge Ptolemy decisively closer to the truth: it reproduced retrograde motion, got the periods right, and predicted positions well enough that the tables derived from it stayed in use for centuries. The dome theory gets little more right than that the planets cross the sky. Note that neither account entails the other, so I cannot explain my verdict by saying that one is logically weaker.

2. Two false chemistries. Phlogiston theory and Lavoisier's oxygen theory are both wrong in detail; Lavoisier listed caloric and light among the simple substances and held that all acids contain oxygen. I still judge the oxygen theory closer to the truth, and by a wide margin rather than a narrow one. But I want to insist on something else here: I would not rank a third chemistry higher merely because it came later and was equally detailed. Recency does no work in my ordering.

3. Padding a truth. A physician writes: this patient has influenza B. True. A second writes: this patient has influenza B, has two kidneys, and two plus two equals four. Everything in the second is true and strictly more is said. I judge the second no closer to the truth than the first — trivial padding neither improves an account nor damages it.

4. Padding with a truth that matters. The physician instead writes: this patient has influenza B and is allergic to penicillin. Both conjuncts true. Here my intuition reverses: this account does seem closer to the truth than the bare diagnosis. The only difference I can find between this case and the previous one is that the added conjunct bears on the question the diagnosis exists to answer.

5. Padding a falsehood. "The Earth is flat" is false. "The Earth is flat and grass is green" is also false, but says strictly more that is true and nothing more that is false. I judge the second closer to the truth than the first. I recognize that this verdict, held together with 3 and 4, is unstable: on my view, conjoining a truth to a falsehood improves it even when the added truth is as irrelevant as the colour of grass is to the shape of the Earth, while conjoining a truth to a truth does not improve it even when the added truth is as pertinent as a drug allergy is to a diagnosis. I have tried to surrender 5 and found I cannot; I have tried to surrender 3 or 4 and cannot either. Please tell me which of the three should go, and argue for it rather than merely noting that dropping it would simplify things.

6. Precision among truths. The true mass of a sample is 2.500 kilograms. Account A says it lies between 2.4 and 2.6 kilograms; account B says it lies between 1 and 4 kilograms. Both are true and B is entailed by A. I judge A closer to the truth. So I do not believe that all true accounts are equally near, nor that weakness is an advantage in itself.

7. Precision among falsehoods. Same sample. Account C says between 2.6 and 2.8 kilograms; account D says between 4 and 6 kilograms. Both false; C is closer. This seems to me the least controversial verdict on the list, and I would regard any account that cannot express it as disqualified.

8. Two vocabularies for the same day. One meteorological report describes a day in terms of temperature, pressure, humidity and wind speed, and is broadly accurate. Another describes the same day as close, muggy, and breaking towards evening, and is also broadly accurate. I judge the first closer to the truth, but I suspect that what I am actually judging is the vocabulary rather than the content, and I do not know how to tell the difference.

9. The case I have left undecided. Account B (between 1 and 4 kilograms, true) against account C (between 2.6 and 2.8 kilograms, false but narrowly so). I genuinely do not know which I think is closer to the truth. Something in me says a truth must beat a near miss however capacious the truth is; something else says B is so uninformative that it barely engages with the world, whereas C risks a great deal and lands close.

What I would like from you:

(a) A single account — a rule I could apply to a new pair of cases without further intuition-pumping — that delivers as many of verdicts 1 through 8 as possible, stated precisely enough that I can see what makes one account nearer than another, rather than a restatement of the ordering itself.

(b) For each clause of that account, the case or cases that forced it in.

(c) Its verdict on case 9, and whether it delivers that verdict with confidence or only by a tie-breaker. Also its verdict on a case I have not listed: a historian writes that Caesar crossed the Rubicon in January of 49 BCE and that it was raining that morning, where the crossing and the date are right and the weather is invented; a rival writes only that Caesar crossed the Rubicon in 49 BCE, with no month and no weather.

(d) An honest statement of which of my verdicts the account cannot accommodate, and which single one you would advise me to abandon.

(e) Whether the relation that survives is two-place — account and world — or three-place, involving also a question or a vocabulary. If three-place, say what fixes the third place, and whether that makes "closer to the truth" interest-relative in a way that would undercut what I said in cases 1, 2 and 7.

If the view you arrive at has a standard name in the literature, mention it in passing, but the deliverable is the account itself, in terms I could use.
