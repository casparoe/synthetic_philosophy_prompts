I've followed the hedonistic imperative literature for years, and there's one analogy that keeps doing heavy lifting in the threads I read. Someone defending the abolitionist program — recalibrating the hedonic treadmill so that nobody's baseline sits anywhere near suffering, eventually by genetic and pharmacological means — will get the standard objection: pain is information, and if you abolish it you abolish the warning system, so people will scald their hands and never notice the tumour. The reply, delivered with great confidence, runs like this. Suffering is a smoke alarm. It is a detector that natural selection installed and calibrated for inclusive fitness rather than for our wellbeing, and it is a bad detector: it sounds when there is no fire (chronic back pain, grief with no object, dread of a future that never arrives) and it stays silent through the slow fires it ought to catch. What an alarm contributes is the information it carries plus the response it triggers, and neither of those requires that the alarm be excruciating. So we can in principle keep the wiring, the flinch, and the learned avoidance, turn the aversiveness down to the level of a mild notification tone, and lose nothing of protective value. The conclusion being licensed is that the information objection to abolitionism is not a real trade-off but a confusion — that whatever residue of badness remains in a world without suffering is merely an engineering difficulty, not a principled objection. The same defender adds that congenital analgesia, where people are born unable to feel pain and injure themselves badly, is no counterexample, since that is pulling the alarm off the wall rather than turning down its volume.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on abolitionism as such.

First, give me the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the alarm, the smoke, the detector, the calibration, whoever set that calibration, the house, the house's owner, the information channel, the response, and the act of recalibrating. A table is fine.

Second, separate the disanalogies that bear on this intended use from the ones that are merely entertaining. I'm particularly interested in two. Does the analogy survive the thought that a smoke alarm's unpleasantness is wholly instrumental and could be anything at all, whereas the aversiveness of pain may be what makes it a signal with motivational force — that is, whether the notification and the tone are separable is exactly what's in question. And what does the picture import from the fact that alarms are artefacts built for a user whose interests lie outside the alarm? Who is the owner in the target case, and does the analogy get to assume that the sufferer and the owner are the same party?

Third, tell me what the analogy assumes about the relation between a signal's function and its phenomenology, and whether the wall-mounted-detector imagery is hiding how much work that assumption does.

Fourth, judge whether the conclusion holds: that the information objection dissolves into an engineering problem. If it holds only in part, say which part, and identify the residual objection the analogy cannot absorb — including whether the congenital analgesia reply is legitimate or whether it concedes that volume and information are coupled.

Fifth, if the alarm is the wrong source domain, propose a better one for the same target — hedonic tone as a detector channel with adjustable gain — and state what the replacement licenses and what it conspicuously fails to license.

Argue for your verdicts on each point rather than balancing them. But finish by saying what would change your mind: which consideration would make you grant that the smoke-alarm analogy does carry the abolitionist conclusion after all, and where in your own reasoning you think it is most likely to give way first.
