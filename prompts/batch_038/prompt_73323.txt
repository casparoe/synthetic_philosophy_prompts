**Artifacts don't have politics. They have revocation costs.**

The standard example is Robert Moses. Winner tells the story that Moses built the overpasses on the Long Island parkways low enough that the buses carrying poor and disproportionately Black New Yorkers out to Jones Beach couldn't clear them — the exclusion wasn't an accident of engineering, it was the engineering. The politics is in the concrete.

The response, from people like Bernward Joerges, is that the story doesn't survive contact with the records: the clearances weren't unusual for parkways of that era, buses did in fact get to the beach, and the anti-bus intent gets imported into the bridges from Caro's biography rather than found in anything Moses's own people wrote down. (I'm reconstructing this from memory and would like to be told where I've mangled it.)

Notice that both sides are fighting about the same question: did a value get *into* the artifact? Encoded by a designer, or attributed by an observer afterwards. I want to say that's the wrong axis, and that the fight over Moses's intentions is downstream of a question neither side is asking.

The question is: what happened to the question that the concrete settles?

Before the parkways existed, how people without cars got to the beach was an open, recurring political question — routes, fares, schedules, subsidies, argued about and re-argued every year by people who were actually affected by the answer. After they existed, it was a question that had been answered once, by one man, in a form that costs tens of millions of dollars and a decade of permitting to un-answer. That is the political property. Not the value inside the artifact but the **revocation cost** it imposes on a decision that used to be revocable.

Call these things **standing orders**: an artifact issues a standing order when it converts a question that would otherwise be reopened periodically into one that stays shut, and raises the price of unshutting it.

The nice thing about this framing is that it dissolves the Moses fight rather than picking a side. Those overpasses are political whether or not Moses disliked bus riders, because either way they moved a transit question out of the arena where it gets renegotiated and into the arena where it doesn't. Intent tells you who to blame. It doesn't tell you whether the artifact is doing political work.

But standing orders differ in a second respect, which I think is where the real action is: whether they keep paying for themselves. Some are self-enforcing — a culvert height, a road network, a default setting that nobody has the incentive to touch. Some expire unless somebody keeps funding the enforcement — DRM, an import regime held up by inspectors, a moderation classifier that has to be retrained against a moving target. So you get a two-by-two: cheap-to-revoke versus ruinous-to-revoke, crossed with self-maintaining versus needs-a-budget. Every cell has members. The self-maintaining, cheap-to-revoke cell is where most "design choices" live and where the politics is genuinely trivial; the ruinous, self-maintaining cell is Moses.

The trouble, and I think this is the most interesting thing in the draft, is that the cells aren't stable. A maintained artifact becomes self-maintaining the moment everyone forgets the maintenance is happening — a blocklist nobody updates silently hardens into a de facto standard, and the standing order outlives the order-giver without anyone deciding that it should. So the grid is really about rates of decay, not types. I don't know how to write that section yet and it may be the whole ballgame.

Where this bites practically: it changes what a design review is for. You stop auditing a system for hidden values and start auditing it for which decisions it takes off the table, in whose favor, and what it will cost to put them back on. Two systems with identical values in the head of the designer can differ enormously in what they foreclose.

Obvious objection, which I'm going to preempt because I know someone will make it: this is just corrigibility with a humanities budget, or just path dependence and increasing returns wearing a trench coat. My answer is that both of those are about a single agent's relationship to its own future options, whereas a standing order is about one party's capacity to bind parties who were never at the table and will never get a vote. Whether that's a real distinction or a rhetorical one, I honestly can't tell.

The question isn't whether artifacts have politics. It's whether anyone can still vote on it.
