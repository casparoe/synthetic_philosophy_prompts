The student's answer:

---

Diamond and Tainter both want a general theory of societal breakdown, but they locate it at different levels. For Diamond, Rapa Nui is the paradigm case of what he calls ecocide: the islanders felled the palm forest that covered the island, lost the timber needed to move the moai and to build ocean-going canoes, and then, with the soil exposed and rainfall diminished, could no longer feed themselves. The population fell from perhaps fifteen thousand to a few thousand, and by the time the Dutch arrived the society had degenerated into warfare and cannibalism. Diamond's unit of analysis is an environment plus the choices a society makes about it; collapse, for him, is the failure of those choices.

Tainter's unit is different. In *The Collapse of Complex Societies* he argues that societies add layers of complexity — bureaucracy, infrastructure, specialized roles — in order to solve problems, and that the marginal return on each added layer eventually declines until maintaining the system costs more than it yields. His case studies are Rome, Chaco Canyon, the Maya, and Rapa Nui. On Tainter's account collapse is not a catastrophe imposed from outside but an economizing move: when complexity stops paying, societies rationally choose to shed it, and for most individuals the result is an improvement, since they escape taxation and elite extraction. That is the deep difference from Diamond. For Diamond, collapse is what happens when people get their environment wrong; for Tainter, it is what happens when a society has been solving problems the wrong way for a long time and finally stops.

Rapa Nui is where Tainter's model looks stronger, because the archaeology does not support Diamond's version of events. Hunt and Lipo have argued that the Polynesian rat gnawed the seeds of the island's palm and prevented the forest from regenerating, so that the settlers did not so much destroy the woodland as inherit a landscape the rats had already ruined. They have also shown that the statues were rolled on logs rather than dragged on sledges, which required far less timber than Diamond's transport model assumes, and that the population at European contact — two or three thousand — was probably not much below its pre-contact peak. The catastrophe on Rapa Nui was therefore not ecocide but contact: introduced disease, the Peruvian slave raids of the 1860s, and then sheep ranching, which took the remaining land from the survivors. Diamond gets the timing wrong, and the timing is the whole argument.

The Maya make the same point about the word itself. What ended in the ninth century was the southern lowland city-states — Tikal, Copán, Palenque — where kings stopped raising dated monuments and the courts dispersed, while the north, at centers such as Chichén Itzá, went on building. The drought record exists, but most Maya archaeologists now treat it as an artifact of low-resolution sediment cores, and purely environmental explanations of the Maya are discredited. The same is true in the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE: the palaces of Mycenae, Hattusa, and Ugarit burned and Egypt's New Kingdom fell in the same wave, the Hittite world left no successor states whatsoever, and Greece lost writing for centuries. What these cases show is that "collapse" almost always means transformation — the people, the language, and the gods continue while the administrative shell that housed them does not. The concept should probably be retired from the literature altogether, and retiring it would also relieve us of the implication that modern societies face the same fate.
