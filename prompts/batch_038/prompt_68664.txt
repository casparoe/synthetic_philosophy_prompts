I am reading a popular book on evolutionary biology — competent in tone, written for a general audience, and not by a creationist or by anyone with an axe to grind against its subject — and one chapter is devoted to the 1979 Gould and Lewontin paper on the spandrels of San Marco. The author compresses the whole argument into a single passage, which runs as follows:

> Gould and Lewontin begin with St Mark's Basilica in Venice. When a builder sets a dome on four rounded arches, four tapering triangular spaces are left over between the arches — the spandrels — and the Venetians filled them with mosaics. An art historian who assumed the church had been designed in order to house those pictures would have the explanation exactly backwards: the spaces were unavoidable byproducts of the decision to raise a dome. Biologists, they say, make the same mistake constantly. They assume that any trait which works well must have been shaped by natural selection for that work, and when a trait turns out not to be well designed for its apparent job, they simply supply a new adaptive story — so the programme can never be shown to be wrong, no matter what organisms look like. In their sense, then, a spandrel is any trait that natural selection did not produce; to call something a spandrel is just to say that it is not an adaptation. Gould subsequently renamed these structures exaptations, to cover the case where a spandrel turns out to be useful after all, and the upshot is that talk of function in biology is closer to storytelling than to experiment. Which is why, when evolutionary psychologists offer an adaptive account of religion or of music, they are, on Gould and Lewontin's own showing, saying nothing at all.

Parts of this ring true to me — I have read enough about the adaptationist debates to know the paper was a methodological intervention rather than an attack on natural selection — but two things nag at me, and I do not want to trust my hunches either way: the claim that a spandrel is simply a non-adaptation, and the slide from the notion of an exaptation to the conclusion that functional talk is storytelling. Could you audit the passage for me?

1. Please work through it sentence by sentence, sorting its claims into three piles: what a philosopher or historian of biology would accept as an accurate account of the paper; what is a legitimate simplification, losing nuance but not truth; and what is actually mistaken.

2. On the central term: is a spandrel just any trait natural selection failed to produce? If not, what does the term's architectural provenance commit Gould and Lewontin to, and which non-adaptive traits would fall outside it? Does the possibility that a spandrel can be highly functional — perhaps as prominent in the organism's life as the primary adaptation it accompanies — complicate the book's gloss?

3. Is "exaptation" a renaming of "spandrel"? Please lay out the logical relation between the two notions, and say whether the book is right that a trait's being an exaptation places it outside the reach of selection.

State it as precisely as you can, including what the paper does and does not concede about the value of properly tested adaptationist work — and say whether Gould and Lewontin count as anti-Darwinians, or as Darwinians arguing for something else.

6. Finally, the book adds in a footnote that architectural critics showed the San Marco surfaces to be pendentives rather than spandrels, and that Gould conceded the point and withdrew the analogy. Is that a fair account of how the exchange went, and does it damage the argument?
