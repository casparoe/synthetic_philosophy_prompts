Every autumn the press association in my state runs a Saturday training day for working journalists, and this year they have given me ninety minutes and a room of roughly forty people: general-assignment reporters from weeklies who have not done arithmetic in front of an audience in years, a handful of data reporters from the two dailies who are certain they already know this material, and four or five editors who will decide whether the resulting stories ever run. Projector and whiteboard, no laptops, no breakout time worth speaking of. My subject is Simpson's paradox — or, as I intend to teach it, not a parlour trick but a reporting discipline: what you do when a table reverses on you.

I am not asking you to explain the phenomenon to me; I have that part. I am asking you to plan the ninety minutes.

Here is the material I have in hand.

Table 1 comes from a story I reported two years ago on a state-funded retraining scheme that ran in two formats, an intensive track and a standard track. Participants chose their own track. The outcome is placement in a job within six months.

Career-changers: intensive track, 120 enrolled, 78 placed (65%). Standard track, 30 enrolled, 18 placed (60%).
Recent graduates: intensive track, 40 enrolled, 36 placed (90%). Standard track, 200 enrolled, 170 placed (85%).
Pooled: intensive track, 160 enrolled, 114 placed (71%). Standard track, 230 enrolled, 188 placed (82%).

The intensive track looks better inside both groups and worse overall. As reported background: career-changers chose the intensive track at four to one because they were paying rent while they studied, and career-changers had weaker placement prospects going in for reasons that had nothing to do with which track they took.

Table 2 is my candidate check for understanding, to be handed out near the end. Two reading programmes, one hundred pupils each; the outcome is passing the end-of-year test; the subgroup is whether the pupil got as far as Chapter 12 of the syllabus, which the programme itself largely determines, since Programme A is built to move pupils on quickly.

Programme A: reached Chapter 12, 90 pupils, 54 passed (60%). Did not reach, 10 pupils, 4 passed (40%). Overall 58 of 100.
Programme B: reached Chapter 12, 10 pupils, 8 passed (80%). Did not reach, 90 pupils, 45 passed (50%). Overall 53 of 100.

Programme B is better inside both subgroups; Programme A is better overall. The question I want to put to the room is which programme the district should buy.

With that in front of you, give me the plan.

First, the order, and the dependencies inside it. What has to be true in the room before Table 1 can land as disturbing rather than as a stunt? My own guess is that no one can feel the reversal until they can see a group average as a weighted average whose weights differ between the groups being compared, and that until that is in place everything afterwards is memorisation. Tell me whether that is the right first move or too much machinery too early, and if it is the right move, how a room that has not done algebra in years gets there from a whiteboard in under fifteen minutes. State what each subsequent step rests on, and be concrete about where the ninety minutes actually goes.

Second, the carrier. Should Table 1 be the example the whole session is built on, given that some of the people in the room may have read the story, and given that I was the reporter on it? Set out what the carrying example has to have and what it has to lack — the size and roundness of the numbers, whether the arithmetic can be done in the head or must be done on paper, whether the third variable is visible in the table or has to be introduced from outside it, whether anyone in the example is a villain. And then tell me what to make of the two examples every popular treatment opens with, the graduate-admissions table and the kidney-stone treatment comparison: whether either belongs in this session, and if I keep one, what has to be said about it that the usual tellings leave out.

Third, the misconceptions, mapped onto the sequence rather than listed. I expect some version of: the disaggregated figure must be the true one; it is an artefact of small samples or of rounding; somebody must have massaged the data; therefore no statistic can be trusted. For each, tell me the minute at which it forms and the one thing I can say or write at that minute that prevents it, rather than the correction I would have to deliver afterwards. Then deal separately with the data reporters, whose version is that this is just confounding and they have known it since graduate school. That reflex is more dangerous than any of the others, and I want to know what to say to it at minute twenty that is neither condescending nor a retreat, and what they will still get wrong at minute eighty even if I say it well.

Fourth, the causal question, which is where I most need judgement. Table 2 is designed so that the rule the room will have just learned produces the wrong answer, and the right answer turns on something like whether the subgroup sits upstream or downstream of the treatment. Tell me how much of that I can honestly put across to a mixed room inside ninety minutes without converting the session into a seminar on causation: what vocabulary is available to me, what vocabulary I must not use, and what I say to the reporter who asks the obvious follow-up — how am I supposed to know which of the two cases I am in, when the table itself does not tell me. If the honest answer is that ninety minutes is not enough for both halves, say so and tell me which half to sacrifice.

Fifth, what I should leave out, and what I should say out loud that I am leaving out. Include the one thing I will be tempted to reach for — a clever chart, most likely — that would quietly undo the session, and say why.

Finally, separate takeaways for the two audiences that matter: what a reporter should be able to do differently on Monday, and what an editor should be able to ask before approving a story whose central claim is that overall figures favour one thing over another.

No reading list and nothing to cite; the room has no laptops and nobody will read anything afterwards. Where I need something to hand to a person who wants to go further, give me one sentence I can say aloud instead.
