You're a philosopher who works on collective action problems and who's done a lot of guest-prep for other people's podcasts — help me out here. Next week I'm interviewing an ethicist on my small show and her position is an unusual one. She grants the causal premise that the "no individual act makes a difference" crowd denies: my one long-haul flight, or my habit of buying the cheap chicken, does on her view genuinely cause harm to actual people, and she thinks the threshold / expected-harm style arguments get the causation roughly right. But she thinks that concession does almost no moral work — that the interesting failure in this literature was never about causation, it's about the step from "you caused some harm" to "so you have a reason not to."

Write me eight to ten interview questions. No warm-up softballs, nothing like what drew her to the topic. I want questions that turn on the content of her view, and I want at least one each on: (a) the aggregation puzzle — if each tiny emission causes a tiny real harm and harms add, why isn't the sum just my reason; (b) whether she can keep herself from saying that a million people each doing a small harm is somehow fine; (c) the voting analogy, and whether she treats skipping a vote and booking the flight the same way; (d) what she owes to the listener who says "fine, I'll do what actually changes outcomes, which is nothing"; and (e) whether her account only yields duties at the institutional level, and what a purely individual duty would even look like for her. She's fast and will try to retreat to "I'm not denying the causation, I'm denying the inference," so give me a follow-up for each question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.

Spend a few sentences brainstorming where a view like that is actually most exposed before you write the questions. And don't hedge at the end: commit to which two of the questions are the load-bearing ones, say why, and tell me her best escape route if she comes well prepared.
