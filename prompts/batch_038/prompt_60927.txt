I am opening a two-day workshop this Thursday called Reading the Weights, and I need the opening remarks. About thirty-five people will be there. Roughly half are mechanistic interpretability researchers who spend their working lives inside transformer models looking for features, circuits, and activation directions; roughly half are philosophers of mind who work on folk-psychological explanation and have never opened a weights file. Two people from the university's communications office will be sitting in the back, because the institute funding the workshop has asked — not required, but asked firmly, in writing — that our public-facing materials not describe the models as conscious, sentient, feeling, or understanding. I have five minutes at a lectern. I should mention that I have just come back from eighteen months of sabbatical, that I have not given a talk to a mixed room in that time, and that a colleague warned me last week that the entire framing of the workshop is a category error, which I do not believe but cannot quite dismiss.

What I want the remarks to do is use Dennett's three stances as the actual load-bearing structure of the speech rather than as an opening name-drop that is then abandoned. The claim I want made, without ever sounding like a lecture, is that the two halves of the room are not disagreeing about the same object so much as describing it from different elevations: one half is working almost entirely in the design stance — architecture, training procedure, superposition, induction heads — while the other is working in the intentional stance, asking what a model believes, wants, or is trying to do, and both treat the physical stance as something they could consult in principle but never actually do. The place I want the speech to land is the criterion Dennett proposed in Real Patterns (1991), on which a pattern is real just in case finding it yields a description that compresses and predicts better than the exhaustive physical account would. That criterion is doing a very specific job for me here: it is what lets me say that a regularity in a model's behaviour can be a genuine feature of the world without my having to say a single word about anyone's inner life, which is precisely what the communications office has asked us to avoid. The constraint should feel enabling in the speech, not like something being worked around.

Some concrete material and constraints. The workshop opens with a live demonstration, which the remarks must introduce: a colleague will steer a model's answers by amplifying one internal direction that was isolated from its weights, and we are doing it in front of the room. In rehearsal it worked twice and failed once, and the failure was completely indistinguishable from the successes to anyone watching from the third row. I want that fact used, plainly and without melodrama, as the speech's central example of why the question of whether a description is real or merely convenient is not a seminar-room luxury. Second, I need one passage of about thirty seconds in which the very same model is described successively in all three stances, with the transitions entirely unannounced — no "now from the physical point of view," no signposting at all — so that the audience feels the change of register rather than being told about it. Third, the speech must contain a genuinely strong concession to the interpretability side: the objection that the intentional stance is just a predictive instrument and that treating its attributions as tracking something real is a philosopher's old habit dressed up in new vocabulary. Make that objection good enough that a sympathetic listener could think I have abandoned my own position — because I have not, and the recovery should come from the compression criterion rather than from assertion. Fourth, do not resolve the dispute about whether Dennett was a realist or a closet instrumentalist about belief. Three people in the first two rows hold opposite views on this, and the workshop's second session is devoted to it; the remarks should sharpen the disagreement and hand it to the room, not settle it from the podium.

Length and register: about seven hundred words, written to be read aloud, with sentences a non-specialist can follow on first hearing and no clause that requires a second pass. Warm, since several people in the room are old friends, but not sentimental. No citations, no reading list, no titles beyond the one I have given you, and no stage directions beyond a single note if a pause is genuinely required. Please do not end with a summary of what the two days will cover — I will do that afterwards from notes — and do not use any of the four forbidden words anywhere in the text.
