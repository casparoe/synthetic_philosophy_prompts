I'm a second-year masters student in philosophy and I also work about 15 hours a week as a program assistant at a small private funder — handful of staff, grants mostly $20-150k, mostly academic groups doing biosecurity and AI-risk-adjacent stuff. Over the last year we've started getting these emails where a grantee asks us, basically, "should we post this publicly?" A preprint, a lab protocol, a checkpoint, a dataset. The program director has me triage them because she doesn't have time, and my current procedure is vibes plus forwarding anything that scares me. There's maybe two or three of these a month now, and it's only going up.

In my risk and collective action seminar we did the unilateralist's curse — the thing where you have N agents who each care about the common good, each with their own noisy estimate of whether some initiative is net positive, and each of them acts whenever their own estimate crosses the line. Then the more agents there are, the more likely it is that somebody acts on a false positive, so the bar for acting has to get *higher* as N goes up, and higher still when the harm is hard to undo. That's uncomfortably close to my situation: the set of groups that could plausibly do the thing in question has gone from like six to forty-something in three years, they know each other, and any one of them is a Slack message away from just posting it themselves.

Could you write me an actual decision procedure I can run on one of these requests? I mean: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where it just stops at "not yet / don't," and the points where it hands the thing up to a human because it's a values question and not a facts question. I have to be able to do it in an afternoon, with no wet lab and no ML background — that's me, unfortunately — and I want you to flag which steps are secretly judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.

Wrinkles I don't know how to handle. Please build these in rather than designing around them:

- The curse story assumes everybody's estimates are about equally noisy. Here it's usually the opposite: the person asking has spent two years on the thing and I've spent forty minutes on a Zoom call. When does their expertise legitimately override the "if N people each think it's fine, somebody is wrong" logic, and how do I tell that apart from someone just being in love with their own result?
- Somebody will post it anyway. For at least two of the live cases, competing groups are visibly working on the same thing. How should the procedure treat "delaying doesn't change whether, only who and when"? Is that ever a reason to go ahead, or is it exactly the reasoning the curse is warning about?
- Being reviewed is itself a risk. Last spring a team got spooked when we asked for a delay and preprinted over the weekend. So running the procedure has a cost, and asking a question can change the answer it's trying to find out. Does it need a fast branch where I just decide without investigating?
- I have a nagging feeling that us being gatekeepers is its own unilateralism — we're one actor with a noisy estimate making the call for everybody. Should the procedure acknowledge that, and if so does it change any answers or is it just decoration?
- The threshold supposedly scales with N. Give me a rule of thumb a non-mathematician can apply for how much the bar moves when the number of capable groups goes from 10 to 50, or tell me flat out that I can't responsibly approximate it and should use something coarser like a reversibility test.
