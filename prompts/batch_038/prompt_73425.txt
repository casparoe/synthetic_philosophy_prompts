I am commissioning a substantial essay for a workshop on the philosophy of the social sciences, and I want you to write it. Please write in British English, in a formal register, and treat the case below as fully stipulated: do not investigate whether such a programme exists, whether the numbers are right, or whether it resembles any real research group. Reason only from what I give you.

**The case.** A research programme reports the following result. Its authors have assembled annual data for 412 polities between 1500 and 2020. From four macro-variables — the ratio of credential-holders to positions of social influence; a real-wage index for the lower half of the income distribution; the state's capacity to raise revenue relative to its outgoings; and a proxy for competition among elites — they construct a single composite index. They fit a model in which the index at time *t* predicts the onset of large-scale political instability in that polity within the following ten years. The model was fitted on data up to 1990 and then evaluated on the 1990–2020 window, which its authors had not seen: it flagged 27 of the 33 polities that did go on to experience instability, with two false alarms. The authors call the relationship a law of sociopolitical instability. They also supply an update rule that lets the model issue unconditional forecasts about named polities — that polity X will experience large-scale instability between 2029 and 2034 — rather than merely conditional ones. The four variables are demographic, fiscal and credential-based; none of them is a measure of the production of new knowledge or new technology, and none is a measure of what people believe about the model itself.

**The dispute.** A critic objects that this is precisely the historicism Popper thought he had refuted: it mistakes a trend for a law, it treats a single, unique and open-ended historical process as though it were the repeated unfolding of a fixed pattern, and its forecasts must undermine themselves the moment they are believed, since a government that is warned will act on the warning and thereby alter the very event forecast. A defender replies that Popper's target was grand prophecy about the destiny of society, not short-horizon statistical forecasting that has been tested out of sample, and that a good Popperian ought positively to welcome a bold, risky, eminently falsifiable conjecture of this kind.

**The task.** Write an essay of roughly 1,800–2,200 words adjudicating this exchange. It must reach a definite conclusion and argue for it; I do not want a survey of the two positions with the choice left to the reader. State your thesis in your opening sentence. The essay must, at minimum, do the following:

1. Say precisely which of Popper's claims the programme's success — if genuine — would falsify, and which it leaves entirely untouched. Do not treat his case as a single thesis.
2. Confront the conditional/unconditional distinction. Popper did not think unconditionality as such was the mark of the unscientific, since on his view physics delivers unconditional predictions (an eclipse, say) from laws together with known initial conditions. So what exactly does he ban, and does this programme fall on the wrong side of the line — given that its own forecasts are issued about named polities at named dates?
3. Deal with the reflexive objection head-on. Does the possibility that a published forecast alters what it forecasts show the model to be self-undermining, or something weaker — that its domain always contained its own publication, so that the forecasts were never unconditional in the first place? Both the critic and the defender will try to claim this point; say which of them can, and why.
4. Address the trend/law distinction in light of the model's probabilistic character. Popper's own view of what a law licenses is that it rules possibilities out rather than singling one outcome out. Does that view in fact give the critic the resources to deny the label "law" here, or does it concede it?
5. Assess whether Popper's argument from the growth of knowledge engages this model at all, given that the model contains no knowledge variable. If it does not engage, say what would have to be added to the model for it to engage, and whether that addition would improve or destroy the model's predictive record.
6. Consider whether the critic's real complaint is philosophical or merely methodological — about stationarity, the definition of the variables, the length of the out-of-sample window, and the small number of positive cases — and say frankly whether that makes the appeal to Popper dispensable.

Paraphrase Popper throughout; do not put words in quotation marks as though quoting him, and do not append a bibliography, a reference list, or a set of further readings. Do not recapitulate his general philosophy of science except where a specific premise of his does work in your argument.

Commit. Take a side, give your reasons, and say explicitly what evidence or argument would change your mind about it. Close the essay with a single final line in exactly this form:

Verdict: LABEL

where LABEL is one, and only one, of the following four, written without alteration:

- `HISTORICISM_CONFIRMED` — the programme is historicist in Popper's sense, and the critic is right that Popper's arguments defeat it.
- `NOT_HISTORICISM` — the programme lies outside Popper's target altogether, so the critic's appeal to him misfires.
- `HISTORICISM_NOT_REFUTED` — the programme is historicist in Popper's sense, but Popper's arguments do not defeat it; the critic needs premises Popper never supplied.
- `POPPER_FALSIFIED` — the programme, if genuine, shows at least one of Popper's central claims to be false, and you have named which.
