Every time our animal-ethics reading group gets into a fight about what to actually eat, somebody says "eat lower on the food chain" and that ends it. Last week Dave used it twice, ten minutes apart, pointing in opposite directions, and nobody catched it. First it was why he'd switched from tuna to sardines — way lower on the chain, so less harm done. Then he used the same line to justify going back to chicken from beef: chicken's lower on the food chain than beef, obviously. I mumbled that a cow and a broiler both eat mostly plants so I'm not sure what "lower" is even doing there, and that one steer is a couple hundred kilos of meat while one bird is barely a kilo, so if you're counting dead bodies he's got it backwards — but I didn't have the pieces lined up and he just said the slogan again, louder.

So: lay out the different precise claims "eat lower on the food chain" could be making. I want a menu, not a lecture — the literal ecological one about trophic position and energy transfer, whatever efficiency thing people have in mind, a counting-individuals one, a suffering-weighted one, and something covering land use and the field animals that get killed in crop harvests. For each, say whether its just a truism about ecology, genuinely substantive and defensible, or false, and say which reading people who repeat the slogan unthinkingly probably mean. Then tell me which readings actually earn Dave his sardines and which actually earn him the chicken — and what's going on rhetorically when one sentence gets recruited for both jobs and they can't both be right.
