I've been reading the rationalist-blog stuff on memes on and off for something like fifteen years — the sequences-era bits, the arguments about whether memetics is a real research programme or just a very appealing metaphor, the dual-inheritance-versus-memetics flame war — and I've never once managed to make the debate feel concrete to myself. I think I came at it from the wrong end. My actual working life is compartmental models of infectious disease transmission: SIR and SEIR, force of infection, basic and effective reproduction numbers, overdispersion and superspreading, serial intervals, the virulence–transmission tradeoff, variant emergence and the messy business of defining a lineage, waning immunity, immune escape, quasispecies and error thresholds. I'm properly fluent in that. I am not fluent in biology, and I'm not fluent in philosophy.

So here's the job: take memetics — the Dawkins/Dennett version, where you explain culture by positing discrete self-replicating items under selection — and re-express it in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Not an analogy, not a restatement along the lines of ideas spreading like a virus; the actual conceptual claims translated. The ones I most want handled: what is the infectious unit here, and is there even a well-defined one? What plays the role of the susceptible host, and what makes someone susceptible to a belief in a way that has anything to do with being susceptible to a pathogen? Dawkins' three criteria for a successful replicator — longevity, fecundity, copying fidelity — what are their counterparts in my toolkit, and is there a genuine error-threshold analogue, i.e. a fidelity below which a meme stops being a lineage at all? What is virulence in this setting, and what is the meme's-eye-view claim — that what's good for the meme can be bad for the host — actually asserting once you say it in my terms rather than in the gene's? And what does a functional explanation like "this belief persists because it's good at persisting" amount to: does my field have a version of that inference, and is it any less circular than the biological one?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Sort every claim you translated into three buckets: translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. For the second and third buckets, say what got lost and whether that's a deficiency in epidemiological modelling or a sign the memetic claim was vaguer than it looked when the philosophers stated it. And watch yourself for smuggling — it would be very easy for you to quietly load in assumptions that belong to my framework (mechanistic transmission, a crisp infectious unit, no within-host evolution, hosts who don't reason about whether to become infected) and then hand them back to me as though memetics had committed to them. Flag every place you had to do that.

Two things I've argued about on forums for years without getting anywhere, and I want your actual verdict on both rather than a summary of the disagreement:

1. The reconstructivists — Sperber's crowd, the epidemiology-of-representations people — say cultural items are never copied at all; each mind regenerates them from cues, and stability comes from the way human cognition keeps landing on the same attractors. Ive always found that position annoying, partly because they got there first with my own discipline's name. But it now strikes me that their picture looks a lot more like within-host evolution and a quasispecies than like a clean transmissible lineage, and if that's right then the reconstructivists are the ones being properly epidemiological, while the memeticists are importing a genetics analogy that doesn't survive contact with the actual structure of transmission. Is that a real insight, or am I flattering myself because the vocabulary is mine?

2. The meme's-eye view. My field has a well-worked-out version of host-versus-pathogen conflict in virulence evolution, with an actual equilibrium. Does that analogy support the memetic claim, or does it collapse at the point where the host of a meme has reasons, evidence and an intentional stance towards the thing infecting them, and the host of a pathogen doesn't? I want to know whether that difference is one of degree — something my models could absorb by adding a choice-theoretic transmission term — or something structural that kills the translation.

Please write the whole answer in British English. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations at all — I own the books and I don't want to chase references, I want the translation and the audit.
