Ok so I've got an argument I built for my quals prep group on distributive justice and it's driving me nuts, because I don't actually believe the conclusion and I can't locate the bad step. Target is luck egalitarianism — roughly, the view that a distribution is unjust insofar as it reflects people's circumstances rather than their own responsible choices. Here's what I wrote out:

1. Luck egalitarianism holds that an inequality is unjust exactly to the extent that it reflects brute luck rather than choices for which the agent is responsible.
2. A choice is one the agent is responsible for only if the choice is not itself the upshot of factors for which the agent isn't responsible.
3. Every choice is the upshot of the chooser's character, capacities and tastes together with the options they can even see as options.
4. Character, capacities, tastes and the perceived option-set are all constitutive luck — none of them are up to the agent in the sense 2 demands.
5. So no choice is one the agent is responsible for; there is no such thing as option luck, only brute luck.
6. So by 1 every inequality whatever is unjust, and luck egalitarianism collapses into strict equality of outcome.
7. But strict equality of outcome can't be right: it licenses taking from the person who saved to compensate the person who gambled it away, and making people with cheap tastes pay for people with expensive ones.
8. So luck egalitarianism is committed either to 7 or to abandoning 1 — the distinction the whole view is built on can't be drawn.

I'd rather loose 6 than 1, honestly, and if I have to sacrifice a premise I'd like it to be 4, since 4 is where I think the argument gets it's punch.
