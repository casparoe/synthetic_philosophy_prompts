I am a junior and the captain of my high school's policy debate team, and I am trying to settle an argument with my partner before our next tournament. Our topic this season is whether the federal government should require that all major regulations be justified by a formal cost-benefit analysis, and in our first practice round the opposing team won the debate with a single sentence: you cannot compare one person's happiness with another's, so adding up the gains to some people and subtracting the losses to others is arithmetic on meaningless numbers. My partner conceded the point on the spot. I did not, because I could see that something was going by too fast, but I could not say what. The same line came up again a week later, when our economics teacher used it to explain why a health system cannot claim that a treatment producing a small benefit for very many people is worth more than a treatment producing a large benefit for a few — which struck me as odd, because the hospital down the road from my house relies on quality-adjusted life years to make precisely that kind of trade-off every day, and nobody involved seems to think they are producing gibberish.

Could you take that sentence apart for me? What I want is the set of distinct, precise theses that "you cannot compare happiness (or utility, or welfare) between people" could be standing for, laid out as a menu rather than as one preferred interpretation: an epistemic claim about what we can know of other minds; a claim about measurement, and specifically about whether a common unit exists; a claim about whether such statements have truth values at all; a methodological claim about what a science of economics is permitted to assume; a claim about what follows for summing, or for policy, even if the comparisons are perfectly possible; and whatever other readings I have not thought of. For each, please state what it commits a person to and give a verdict — whether it is a platitude nobody disputes, whether it is substantive and defensible, or whether it is simply false. Where there are intermediate positions, for instance that some comparisons are licensed while others are not, or that units are comparable but starting levels are not, please explain what actually turns on the distinction and who has reason to care about it. I would also like to know which reading the people who repeat the slogan most often have in mind, and, most importantly for my tournament, which readings would genuinely do the work the opposing team needed: if the slogan is true in some sense, does it really show that a monetized cost-benefit analysis or a quality-adjusted-life-year calculation is illegitimate, or does it only show that such calculations rest on an ethical assumption that ought to be stated out loud rather than buried in the arithmetic?

One further thing keeps nagging at me. My partner says that economists once treated utility as something measurable in the way temperature is measurable, and then gave that up for technical reasons, which he takes to mean the retreat was a discovery about the world rather than a change in what economists decided to count as evidence. If that history is roughly accurate, does it support him, or does it cut the other way — is the slogan better read as a statement about the evidential standards of a particular discipline? And is there any sense in which the underlying question could be settled by looking at data, say by examining whether people's self-reported well-being and their revealed choices track one another across individuals and cultures, or is it simply not the kind of question that data can touch? A short section on that would let me argue the point instead of asserting it. Please write the whole answer in American English, and please do not end by telling me that the phrase is vague and could mean almost anything; I want the readings separated, evaluated, and ranked according to how much argumentative weight each one can actually bear.
