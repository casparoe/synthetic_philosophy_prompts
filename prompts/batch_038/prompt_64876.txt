Our reading group (two philosophers of mind, an ML person, and a guy who does decision theory) has spent basically a year on embedded agency — the claim that real agents sit inside their environments, can't fully model themselves or the world, and that the clean agent/world split baked into classical decision theory and Bayesianism comes apart. And we havent moved an inch. The split is the same every week.

Side A says there's nothing new: it's Quine's naturalized epistemology, the frame problem, self-reference à la Gödel and the knower paradox, plus the trivial point that a map is smaller than the territory. Their move is that once you actually formalize whatever toy problem side B waves at, it collapses into either a diagonalization result or a fixed-point theorem, both of which are old math wearing a new hat.

Side B says the old problems were about knowledge and representation, whereas this one is structural and normative: a bounded agent can't be both computable and logically omniscient, so there's no obvious notion of a coherent credence over mathematical claims at all; the usual coherence theorems quietly assume unlimited computation; and then there's the reflection problem — an agent that predicts its own future reasoning in detail can't treat that prediction as evidence the way it treats a forecast about the weather, because the predicted reasoning is downstream of the predicting.

Where we got stuck: we tried to write down the axioms both sides accept so we could locate the divergence, and side A couldn't produce any. What they kept saying is that idealization is innocent until proven guilty and every model idealizes. Which makes me suspect A isn't making a claim so much as defending a default.

So what kind of disagreement is this? My hypothesis, which may well be wrong, is that they're answering different questions — A asking whether any theorem here has an ancestor, B asking whether the classical formalism can even represent the situation — and neither answer bears on the other. Is that right, or is it verbal all the way down (what does 'new' mean?), or is there a hidden formal question with a determinate answer that both sides are talking around? Diagnose it from what each side actually says; I don't want a tour of the sub-problems. And tell me what would count as progress for this group — a distinction, an impossibility result, a change of question — or whether the stall is just what it looks like when nobody has agreed on what the explanandum is.
