I am a graduate student in a program that straddles philosophy and economics, and this term I am in a seminar on the foundations of distributive justice whose syllabus pairs Nash's 1950 formulation of the bargaining problem with Kalai and Smorodinsky's 1975 alternative and then jumps ahead to the strategic models that are supposed to underwrite them. The final paper has to take a side in a dispute that, my instructor warns, most students write around by summarizing both axiomatizations and then congratulating themselves for seeing the merits of each. I would like you to write the core argument of that paper for me. Please adopt the standpoint of a cooperative game theorist who is equally at home in the philosophical literature on fairness, who is willing to commit to a conclusion, and who treats the axioms as substantive claims about what fairness requires rather than as moves in a game.

Here is the material as I understand it, and you should correct me where I have it wrong. Both rules take the same data: a convex feasible set of payoff pairs, a disagreement point, and therefore a Pareto frontier. Both satisfy efficiency, symmetry, and invariance to positive affine rescalings of the utility scales. They differ in the fourth axiom. Nash adds independence of irrelevant alternatives, and the resulting rule selects the point that maximizes the product of the two parties' gains over disagreement. Kalai and Smorodinsky drop independence of irrelevant alternatives in favor of individual monotonicity: if the feasible set expands in a way that raises one party's best attainable payoff without raising the other's, the first party should not end up with less. Their rule selects the point where the frontier is crossed by the straight line running from the disagreement point to the pair of ideal payoffs, which amounts to demanding that each party surrender the same fraction of what it would have to surrender to fall all the way back to disagreement. The two rules coincide whenever the problem is symmetric, so the entire dispute lives in the asymmetric cases, which are the ones worth arguing about.

I want the essay to work through a concrete case rather than hover at the level of axioms, and I want it to use the following one, because it does not behave the way the standard telling of the story predicts. Suppose the disagreement point gives each party nothing, each party could obtain at most 100 if the other accepted nothing at all, and the frontier is kinked, running from (100, 0) through (50, 75) to (0, 100), the kink standing for some asset that is worth far more in the second party's hands than in the first's. My own arithmetic gives (50, 75) for the Nash point and (60, 60) for the Kalai–Smorodinsky point. Now suppose the first party turns up an outside arrangement that would bring her 150, but only under conditions that leave the second party with nothing, so that the frontier becomes the line from (150, 0) through (50, 75) to (0, 100). I get (75, 56.25) for Nash and something close to (79.4, 52.9) for Kalai–Smorodinsky. Both rules therefore move in the first party's favor, and Nash moves further: the second party loses about 18.75 under Nash and about 7 under Kalai–Smorodinsky. That is the reverse of the usual complaint, according to which Nash is the rule that perversely refuses to reward a party for newly available opportunities while Kalai–Smorodinsky obligingly responds. Part of what I need is an account of why the usual complaint misfires here, and of what the two axioms are actually sensitive to: dominated alternatives on the one hand, ideal payoffs on the other. Note also that comparing the two rules' responses is delicate, since they already disagree before the expansion, and I would like the essay to say what the right way of comparing them is.

With that case in hand, please weigh the rival intuitions and reach a verdict. On one side there is the thought that a fair recommendation ought not to leave a party with less merely because possibilities opened up for it, even possibilities it could never have combined with a decent deal for the other side. On the other side there is the thought that a rule which responds to options no one could ever jointly choose is open to manipulation, since a party can inflate its ideal payoff with theatrical alternatives that change nothing about what the two of them can actually get together, and there is the further worry that indexing fairness to what each side could extract if the other capitulated completely rewards those whose position is already strong. Address as well the deflationary reconciliation that tempts me: the Nash point has strategic foundations in alternating-offer models whose equilibria approach it as the incentive to settle becomes negligible, whereas Kalai–Smorodinsky looks like a recommendation addressed to an arbitrator rather than a prediction about parties, so perhaps the two are answering different questions and neither is refuted. I want you to say whether that reconciliation works, bearing in mind that implementation results exist for the Kalai–Smorodinsky point too, and then to commit: which rule should a fair-minded third party actually use in a case like the one above, and what should that third party say to the party who loses out? Argue against the strongest version of the position you end up rejecting, not a caricature of it, and do not close on a non-committal note; a qualified verdict is acceptable, a refusal to give one is not.

Please think the problem through carefully before writing, and check the geometry and arithmetic of anything you assert, but do not show me the scaffolding: I want the finished argument, not an outline or a list of considerations. The deliverable must be continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter to a colleague, with no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, and no displayed equations; any notation should be worked into the sentences themselves. Somewhere in the range of 1,500 to 2,000 words would be right.
