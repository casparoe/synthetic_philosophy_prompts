I am putting together a session for a mixed metaphysics and philosophy-of-physics reading group, and I have grown rather tired of running the debate about laws of nature entirely on thought experiments — coin-flipping worlds of pure chance, Humean mosaics of featureless points, and the like. I would prefer to take one real episode and pull it apart properly, because I think history does some of the work that intuitions are supposed to do here and does it better. The episode is the pair of campaigns run by Urbain Le Verrier in the middle of the nineteenth century, which look to me like something close to a controlled experiment in what laws are doing when they are used to forecast something novel. You may take the following as given, and please do not spend any time on the astronomy itself: in the 1840s the observed path of Uranus deviated from the tables computed under Newtonian gravitation, and Le Verrier — independently of John Couch Adams — inferred that an unseen outer body was responsible, calculated where it should be, and had it found at the Berlin Observatory in September 1846 within roughly a degree of the predicted position. In 1859 Le Verrier applied exactly the same reasoning to a residual anomaly in Mercury's orbit, an excess in the advance of the perihelion of about 43 arcseconds per century, and inferred an unseen inner planet, which he named Vulcan. Vulcan was hunted for decades and never found; the anomaly was eventually accounted for in 1915 by general relativity, which showed in passing that Newtonian gravitation was not the true law of the matter but at best a limiting approximation. Same reasoner, same method, same auxiliary move from a residual to an unseen source, and then two verdicts as different as it is possible to imagine.

Please analyse that pair through the Humean versus anti-Humean distinction about laws, and I would like the mapping to be disciplined rather than impressionistic. Start with the Humean side: identify what the mosaic of local matters of fact actually is in each case and at each time, what the candidate best systematisations were before 1846 and before 1915 respectively, and how the balance of simplicity against strength is supposed to shift once Neptune is in and once Vulcan is not. Then ask the hard question — whether the best-system account can say anything substantive about why the Uranus inference was well warranted and the Mercury inference was not, given that the two inferences are formally identical and given that on this view the fact that a regularity is a law is partly constituted by the very facts being forecast. I want that circularity worry pressed as sharply as it can be, and I want to know whether it bites differently in the two episodes or equally in both. Then do the same from the anti-Humean side, taking seriously at least two varieties — a necessitation-between-universals view and a view on which laws are primitive dynamical features of the world that govern — and ask what either of them has to say about a law that is not true yet reliably generates correct novel predictions. If Newtonian gravitation was never really a law, what was it doing when it put Neptune within a degree of where Le Verrier said to look, and does the anti-Humean owe an account of that or is the success simply not their problem?

Two further things, and then a verdict. First, the counterfactual: both episodes trade on conditionals of the form "had the unseen body not been there, Uranus or Mercury would have tracked the Newtonian tables". Spell out how each side is entitled to underwrite that conditional — direct nomic support on one view, re-running best-systematisation on a modified mosaic on the other — and say whether the Humean machinery yields anything like the conditional that astronomers actually needed, or something that backtracks in an unhelpful direction. Second, note that Newtonian gravitation is a non-local, action-at-a-distance affair, which sits awkwardly with a supervenience thesis formulated in terms of local matters of fact distributed over spacetime points; tell me whether that tension is fatal to using this episode as a test of the Humean view at all, or whether the view can be recast so that the episode still bears on it. Finally, give me your assessment of what the pair shows about the debate itself: is it a confirmation of one side, an anomaly for it, or a kind of case that neither position ever really anticipated? Please be explicit wherever an account is simply silent rather than pressing it to speak, and do not send me a reading list — I know the landmarks and want the reasoning, not the references.
