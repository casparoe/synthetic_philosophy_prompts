So, three weeks from now I'm giving a work-in-progress paper at my department's political theory seminar — forty minutes to talk, thirty for questions, and the thirty is never enough. I want to stress-test the thing against the people who'll actually be in the room rather than the readers I imagine for it, because I've got so used to my own framing that I can no longer tell which parts sound like argument and which parts just sound like me.

The paper is about the boundary problem. My view, roughly: the circularity is genuine and not a pseudo-problem — you can't democratically settle who belongs to the demos without already presupposing a demos, and appealing to constituent power or to a pre-political people just launders history into normativity. But it doesn't follow that actually existing democracies are illegitimate. What follows is that their boundaries are permanently provisional. The criterion for who's in has to be moral rather than procedural — I end up with something like: anyone actually coerced by a body's rules has a standing claim to a say over them — and legitimacy comes not from having drawn the boundary correctly but from having an institutionalised, enforceable route for reopening it whenever a new class of subjects can show they're bound. So every real demos is at bottom unjustly constituted and under a permanent obligation to enlarge itself, and that's a feature rather than an embarrassment. The last third is spent arguing this beats the all-affected principle (which either makes the demos global or needs a threshold of affectedness, and any threshold reintroduces exactly the arbitrariness I started by objecting to) and the all-subjected version (which struggles with the person turned away at the border, who is coerced precisely by being kept out).

The room is a specific mix and that's the point of asking you. Two committed proceduralists: one neo-republican who thinks democracy legitimates itself through ongoing practice and that my regress only bites if you insist on a founding act; one deliberative democrat who'll say I've mistaken a question of democratic politics for a question of foundations. An international lawyer who works on self-determination and recognition, and for whom "keep reopening the boundary" sounds like an invitation to irredentism and to the wars that redrawn frontiers cause. An empirical political scientist who studies franchise extension — voting age, prisoners, non-resident citizens — and is openly impatient with paradoxes; they'll want to know what my criterion predicts that we don't already do, and whether its operationalisable at all. At least one Rawlsian who'll ask whether my provisional-demos obligation is just a natural duty of justice wearing a fancy hat. One migration and citizenship person who'll say I've restated an argument for open borders with extra steps. And there's usually somebody working on indigenous sovereignty in these seminars, and if they're there I expect the question about whether "enlarging the demos" is itself a colonial frame — contesting the boundary inside the state's own institutions already concedes the state's jurisdiction over the land. Plus grad students, who will go straight for children, future generations and animals.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defence of my view — I know what I think, I want to know where I'll get hit. Eight or so objections in the order they'll actually arrive (my guess is the proceduralist one comes early and the indigenous-sovereignty one, if it comes, arrives late and lands hardest, but reorder me if you think differently). Phrase each one the way that person would phrase it, in their vocabulary and not mine. For each: a reply I can deliver standing up in about two minutes, a candid note on how much damage it really does, and where my reply is soft — or is just my thesis restated at higher volume. Flag the two or three places where different people will team up on the same weak point from different directions, tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes so I'm not defending it for the last thirty, and tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the hour. Then give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, the one I'd fumble, with the least-bad answer I could give it.

No bibliography and don't invent references, please — I've got the literature sorted, what I'm short of is arguments. One last thing, and it matters to me: you're making judgement calls about how damaging each objection is and how well my replies hold, so tell me where you're least sure of yourself and what would change your mind. If you think the neo-republican self-legitimation reply genuinely dissolves the regress rather than dodging it, say so plainly, because then my whole ordering is wrong and I'd much rather find that out from you now than from the room on the day.
