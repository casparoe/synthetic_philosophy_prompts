Standard readings hold that Descartes cannot escape the charge that his beast-machine doctrine renders animals insensate: since sensation is a mode of thought and he denies thought to beasts, the shriek of the vivisected dog registers nothing that is felt. I want the opposite case argued, not surveyed. Thesis: Descartes can coherently maintain both that animals are pure mechanisms and that they have genuine sensations, including pain, because what he denies them is not sensory affection but the reflective awareness a mind united to a body brings to it — the mind's attending to the fact that it is seeing, which turns a perception into an object of knowledge.

Support the thesis by (i) explaining why the criteria he actually deploys against animal thought — no general use of signs, no flexible response to unfamiliar situations — target cognition rather than feeling; (ii) meeting the Gassendi-style objection that the cunning and apparent adaptability of dogs and talking magpies is evidence of thought, and showing what his mechanical explanation can legitimately absorb; and (iii) accounting for his remark to More that he denies beasts neither life nor sensation, insofar as these depend on a bodily organ, without thereby granting them a mind.

Then state plainly whether your reading leaves the Cartesian position morally defensible or merely internally consistent, and identify the single point at which you would concede to the opposing reading rather than multiply distinctions. No citations or bibliography; argue from the concepts. Maximum 650 words.
