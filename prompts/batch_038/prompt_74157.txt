Last Thursday my brother-in-law and I argued for something close to four hours about how many objects were in a shipping container, and neither of us won.

I am the inventory and claims officer for a bonded warehouse inside a free-trade zone. Goods sit in our racks under customs control with duty deferred, and a large part of my job is to say how many items are on a manifest and what each of them is. Customs classification quietly helps itself to a distinction metaphysicians fight over: a consignment of unassembled or disassembled parts is entered as the finished article it will become, provided the shipment, as presented, already has the "essential character" of that article. My brother-in-law, who finished a philosophy doctorate and now writes software, says this is ontology by administrative fiat. On his view, any plurality of things whatsoever composes a further object, so there is a single thing consisting of the turbine blades in our Bay 12 together with a goat standing in a field in Anatolia; our manifest is just one convenient carving among infinitely many equally real fusions. When I objected that this empties the phrase *one object* of content, he replied that I am a nihilist in a warehouseman's jacket — that strictly there are only simples arranged bladewise, and everything I count, insure, and sign for is a manner of speaking. He says my essential-character test is a tariff rule in metaphysical dress. I say his universalism cannot explain why the item count on my manifest changes when those blades are bolted to a core and run on a test stand, and his nihilism cannot explain why it does not change when I relabel a pallet.

I have come away thinking the disagreement cannot be settled by another argument. It needs a case where the counting is forced, expensive, and inescapable. So I would like you to construct that case, as fiction.

Write a short story of roughly 2,000 to 2,800 words that takes a definite position on the special composition question — under what conditions, if any, do several things compose one further thing — and argues for it through what happens rather than through what anyone says.

The story must satisfy the following:

1. **Setting.** A bonded warehouse, free-port storage facility, or foreign-trade zone, where goods are held under customs control and where the number of items on a manifest has legal and financial consequences. Give the inventory work the same texture it has in reality: pallets, lot numbers, rack bays, seal numbers, condition surveys, an adjuster's site visit.

2. **The forcing event.** Something happens — a fire, a rack collapse, a flood, a seizure, a spoilage event in a cold room — that destroys, damages, or confiscates an indeterminate quantity of stuff. The story's engine is the question of how many objects were lost, because the per-item limit on the insurance schedule, the duty assessment, or the salvage award makes the count worth a great deal of money. Note the perverse incentive this creates: universalism makes the loss enormous in item count and therefore cheap, nihilism makes it one big nothing, and the protagonist must not be allowed to let the payoff settle the metaphysics.

3. **The crux consignment.** Include a shipment of individually packed components destined to be assembled into a single machine, together with the complication that some of those components were already fitted to a partially assembled core which had been powered up and run for a few minutes before the event, while others remained sealed in their original crates, and a third group had been pilfered from an opened crate weeks earlier. The protagonist must decide, on a specific form, whether this was one article, three, several thousand, or none.

4. **Criteria tested in the flesh.** Without naming them as theories, put at least four candidate criteria for composition to work on concrete objects in the narrative: mere contact (things touching in a heap); fastening (things bolted or strapped together); cohesion (things welded, glued, sintered, or frozen into one another); and functional integration (parts that do nothing individually but constitute a working machine together). Each should get a case where it yields a different verdict from the others.

5. **A living thing.** Include one organism whose unity is genuinely ambiguous and treat it seriously rather than decoratively — for instance a consignment of live colonial animals such as siphonophores, whose individual zooids cannot survive apart yet are each distinguishable; or a grafted tree carrying two cultivars on one rootstock; or a slime mould plasmodium that has fused from several individuals and later divides. This is the one item in the warehouse that nobody disputes is a single object, and the story should make that fact do philosophical work, either as evidence or as an embarrassment.

6. **A scattered object.** Include something whose parts were never all in the same place at the same time — components held under one contract and one serial number across three jurisdictions, two of them still at sea during the event — so that the reader must confront whether it was one thing spread thin or several things never united.

7. **A cannibalised object.** Include an item built from parts stripped from other items, so that the history of the parts matters to what they now compose, and so that at least one of the donor objects has a claim to still exist somewhere in the machine.

8. **A rival who is not a straw man.** Include a character holding one of the rival views — universalism, nihilism, or the position that composition is a matter of our descriptions and never of the world. Give that character one explicit statement of thesis in dialogue, no more, and let them be right about something the protagonist gets wrong.

9. **A committed ending.** The protagonist must make an actual ruling, on an actual document, and the story must show whether the ruling was correct and what it cost. I do not want an ending that dissolves into wonder, or that suggests the question was meaningless all along, or that leaves both views standing amiably side by side. If the story thinks there is no fact of the matter about how many objects were in Bay 12, then the story had better be about someone discovering that at a price.

Craft prohibitions: no lecture passages, no character who functions as a visiting professor, no dream or vision sequences in which the metaphysics is spelled out, no character named after or transparently nicknamed for a philosopher, no final scene in which someone explains mereology to someone else. The philosophy must be carried by the objects, the paperwork, and the choices, and it must survive being read by someone who has never heard the term *special composition question*.

After the story, append a note of 250 to 400 words in which you take responsibility for what you wrote. In that note: state which answer to the special composition question the story endorses and say why you think it is true, not merely useful for the plot; identify the two or three places where the narrative had to distort, simplify, or quietly set aside a difficulty in order to work as fiction, and say what the difficulty was; state plainly whether the protagonist's final ruling was right by the story's own lights; and flag at least one point where your handling leans on a contested reading of the philosophical material, then commit to whether that reading is correct. I want a definite stand throughout the note — if you think the better view is one the story argues against, say so and explain why you wrote it that way anyway. Do not hedge by listing the options and leaving me to choose, and do not include a bibliography, reference list, or citations of any kind; I want your reasoning, not your sources.
