I've been playing a game with a friend where we go through famous semi-legendary episodes and each of us has to call them history, legend, or something in between, and I've noticed my own calls don't obviously hang together. I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Here's what I said:

- The Trojan War: history, kind of. There was a city at Hisarlik, it burned in roughly the right century, and the Hittites had a vassal place out there called Wilusa with a king named Alaksandu, which some people read as Ilios and Alexandros. But the poems were written centuries later and are full of gods, so the war happened and the Iliad didn't.
- King Arthur: legend. Badon probably was a real battle, but the guy who carries the cross on his shoulders for three days in the Welsh annals, and the twelve battles in Nennius, come out of a tradition that's already busy making a national mythology. Gildas talks about Badon and never mentions Arthur, and I took that silence to mean something.
- The Exodus: as narrated, legend — six hundred thousand men on foot plus families doesn't survive contact with either the archaeology or the Egyptian records. A small group getting out of Egypt and joining the highland settlers, history. But I refused to say "the Exodus happened" on the strength of the second one, and I'm not sure why not, since I did exactly that for Troy.
- Romulus: legend. Rome being founded in the eighth century BC: history. And when an archaeologist announced he'd found an eighth century wall on the Palatine and said this was Romulus's city, I called that legend again — which feels like cheating, because I let a burn layer do work for Troy that I wouldn't let a wall do for Rome.
- William Tell: legend, mostly because the apple shot turns up in Saxo's Danish history and in a bunch of other places, so it looks like a travelling story rather than a memory.
- Robin Hood: legend. Earliest ballads are centuries after any plausible Robin and the guy has no fixed time or place.
- Ragnar Lothbrok: history-ish. The Great Heathen Army was real, its leaders were real, and they claimed to be his sons, which gives me a person even though the snake pit death is obviously story.
- The 47 ronin: history. And also a legend, because within a couple of generations the puppet plays had rewritten it. Which made me realize my label was attaching to the telling, not the event.
- The Alamo and Davy Crockett: same thing, and here I'm completely unconflicted — event history, version legend.
- Masada: I marked it "history with the speeches struck out," since Josephus is the only writer and the archaeology fits the place and not the mass suicide speech. But notice that's a different operation than what I did to Troy, where I struck out nearly everything and kept the event.
- Jesus: bare existence history, narrated career mixed. This is the one I'm least happy with. The written evidence for a bare-existence Jesus is not obviously richer in kind than what we have for Arthur — later compilers drawing on lost earlier material, a mention or two from people with axes to grind — and I worry I'm letting my other commitments rather than my criteria do the work here.
- Prester John: legend, and an interesting one, because the letter was a forgery that its own recipients half-knew was a forgery, and people still went looking for him for centuries.

My rough rule going in was: if there's an anchor outside the tradition that's telling the story — archaeology, or documents from people who had no reason to care about the story — then it's history, and any detail the anchor doesn't touch gets stripped away. Three problems. The Hisarlik burn layer is an independent anchor but it's silent about Achaeans and Helen, so it anchors nothing I actually care about. Arthur's anchors are all inside the tradition, so my rule demotes him, but they're closer in time to his supposed life than Homer is to Troy, which suggests I'm tracking independence when what I wanted to track was age or proximity. And the ronin and the Alamo have total anchors, yet my rule has no way to say that the thing most people mean by those names is the fictionalized version.

So: what's the principle behind all of this? Give me one, or two or three competing ones, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I hadn't thought of, with each clause tied to the verdict that forces it. Tell me which of my calls it can't deliver and which single one you'd make me give up rather than complicate the rule to save it. I especially want to know whether the rule needs a clause about kernel size — at what point does shrinking the kernel from "the war as Homer tells it" to "a fortified town in northwestern Anatolia was destroyed by human agency" stop being a claim about the Trojan War and become a claim about something else, and how do I set that threshold without it being arbitrary.

Then apply whatever you come up with to four I didn't raise: whether Homer existed, the pre-flood stretch of the Sumerian King List with its kings reigning for thousands of years, the Trojan Horse specifically (given I already passed the war), and whether turning up a Robert Hod or a Robehod in the 1240s court rolls would promote Robin Hood. And finally — is history versus legend even the right axis? Half my verdicts turned out to be about which telling I was grading rather than which event, and I want to know whether the honest output here is two separate scales instead of one.
