I am writing a term paper for a joint law-and-philosophy seminar on transitional justice, and I have got stuck on a question about South Africa's amnesty process that seems to me more conceptual than historical. My instructor has told me to work out the reasoning myself before hunting for commentators who have already answered it, so I would rather argue from the mechanics than from the literature. Here are the mechanics as I understand them. Under the Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act of 1995, amnesty was not blanket: each perpetrator had to apply individually, specify the acts concerned, and make a full disclosure of the relevant facts, and the acts had to fall inside a defined mandate period running roughly from 1960 to the first democratic election in 1994. The committee then asked whether the act was associated with a political objective, weighing the directness of the link between act and objective and whether the act was proportionate to it — criteria that look to me like the distinction-and-proportionality tests of the law of armed conflict transplanted into what was formally an ordinary domestic criminal-law system, and applied symmetrically to the security forces of the apartheid state and to members of the liberation movements fighting it. Where amnesty was granted it extinguished criminal liability and also the civil claim of the victim, whether or not she had been heard, had agreed, or had been compensated at the time; victims could attend and object, but objection was an input, not a veto. Nothing in the process required remorse, apology, or any undertaking to make amends. Of roughly seven thousand applications fewer than a thousand succeeded, prosecution of those who never applied or who were refused proved rare, and the victims who did come forward eventually received a single modest lump sum, paid years after the commission had closed and well short of what the commission itself had recommended. When victims' organisations challenged the arrangement as a denial of their right of access to court, the Constitutional Court upheld it, reasoning in part that without the inducement of amnesty the truth about the past would have stayed buried and the country would have had neither accountability nor an accurate record.

What theory of justice, and what theory of truth, does a machine built like that presuppose? I keep coming back to the fact that the state seems to be disposing of something that is not obviously its own: a criminal wrong might be understood as a public wrong that the polity can waive, but a damages claim belongs to the person who was injured, and the state cancelled hers without her consent and without paying it off. What view of whose loss apartheid violence was does that require, and what does it leave of the idea that the victim is a party to the wrong rather than a witness to it? Relatedly, what does the design presuppose about truth itself — that it is a scarce good whose supply responds to price, that public disclosure is a kind of reparation in itself, that an accurate record has a value that can be traded against impunity? Note that the process kept the whole adversarial form — an application, evidence, cross-examination, reasoned findings of fact about a named individual — while removing the punishment; what makes the retained form intelligible if not the thought that an adverse public finding is itself a cost, and does the practice have to believe that in order to work? And what does the proportionality test presuppose about the moral status of political violence, given that it treats a liberation fighter and a state assassin by the same yardstick while the same legal order insists that apartheid was a crime? There is a further boundary I find puzzling: the mandate reached killing, abduction, torture and severe ill-treatment, which left out pass-law prosecutions, forced removals, Bantu education and the routine lawful operation of apartheid statute. Does that definitional line presuppose that injustice consists in discrete datable bodily acts departing from a baseline, rather than in the baseline itself?

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory, not a history of the commission. Please separate what this machinery strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably — the restorative-justice and ubuntu rhetoric that surrounded it seemed to demand something about the applicant's inner state that the legal mechanism never actually asked for, and I suspect the two are doing different work. Tell me where the design is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. which rival accounts of punishment, of harm, or of political community would license exactly the same procedure, so that the practice cannot be read off as evidence for any one of them. Then tell me what the machinery would have to become under two substitutions: first, if one held that the claim belongs to the victim and can be released only by her, and second, if one held that certain wrongs cannot be purchased with information at any price. For each, say which features of the South African design would have to be rebuilt, which could survive unchanged, and what the country would have forfeited by making the substitution. I am not after a bibliography — I want the argument, including wherever you think my framing of the puzzle is itself confused.
