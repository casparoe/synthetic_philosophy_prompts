Two members of the forecasting group I belong to have been arguing for three weeks about how to predict the remaining lifespan of a great power or an imperial order, and the argument has outgrown the case that started it. Here is the exchange as it actually went, numbered so that each move can be graded separately.

1. **Ana:** You cannot reason your way to this from the inside. Every story about why a given power is durable is a story that could also have been told about the ones that fell. Collect the comparable orders in the record, measure how long each lasted, report the distribution. That is the forecast.

2. **Ben:** Only once you have decided what counts as comparable. Empire, great power, hegemon, post-1500 European state — each list yields a different number, and you can trim the list until it produces whatever you wanted. An outside view you can tune is not an outside view.

3. **Ana:** The list is chosen by mechanism, not by answer. You include the cases where decline plausibly runs through the same channels — overextension, fiscal strain, succession failure. That is a criterion, not a preference.

4. **Ben:** It is circular. Which channels drive decline is the substantive question. You claim to bypass inside-view judgment and then reinstate it at exactly the point where you define the class. And your cases are not independent: twenty-odd orders that trade with, imitate, and fight one another across the same centuries form one entangled system, not twenty draws from an urn. Your effective sample size is nearer one than twenty.

5. **Ana:** Grant the non-independence; it does not rescue you. Every theory of decline available for your inside-view reasoning was estimated from that same entangled sample, so your objection proves too much. Treat the base rate as a prior rather than an answer: start there, then update on the specifics.

6. **Ben:** Then the base rate is decoration, because all the work sits in the updating and nobody can say how much weight to give it. Worse, your prior smuggles in a flat hazard. An order that has stood for two centuries does not face the same annual risk as one that is forty years old, so an average duration is not a forecast about this one at all.

Neither will concede, and I have been asked to referee. Please act as referee, in these sections and in this order:

- **Move by move** (a numbered list mirroring 1–6): for each move, a rating on a four-point scale — clearly mistaken, partly right, mostly right, clearly right — with 2–4 sentences of justification. Where a move is only partly right, say explicitly which half survives and which does not.
- **Overall** (one paragraph): who is more right, and whether the dispute is resolvable by argument or rests on something that argument cannot settle.
- **What both of them missed** (at most three items, one or two sentences each).
- **Settled vs. contested** (a short list): mark which of your own conclusions reflect positions broadly agreed among people who work on reference-class forecasting and the philosophy of probability, and which are genuinely disputed. Do not present a contested view as consensus, and do not hedge the things that are in fact settled.
