I need to give a six-minute toast, roughly nine hundred words, at the retirement dinner for Dr. Miriam Adeyemi, who for thirty-four years ran the forecast desk at a regional river-basin agency and who will be sitting twelve feet from the microphone when I stand up. The room is about a hundred and twenty people and it is not one audience: there are the hydrologists and actuaries who worked under her; three or four philosophers and statisticians who flew in because her methodological papers got cited in work they do and who will catch every technical gesture I make; two county emergency managers whose towns she warned; and her family — a husband, two adult daughters, a sister who has been told repeatedly and without effect that Miriam's job had something to do with weather. I should say plainly that I came to this material the way a lot of people my age did, through the old rationalist sequences and forums, where the house position is that a rational agent has a single number and that a range is a confession of insufficient nerve. Miriam spent her career arguing the opposite, and I arrived at her work already converted, which is why I am the one giving the toast rather than somebody who knew her in college. She kept a heavily annotated copy of Walley's book on her desk for twenty years and would not let anyone borrow it.

Here is what she actually did, and what the speech has to carry. She refused to file point estimates. Every forecast in her log had two numbers, and in the margin she wrote one line explaining why the gap between them was where it was — not a hedged single figure, but a genuine interval that she held open on purpose. She had a phrase for that gap, coined it herself, and half the room will recognize it on contact: she called it the honest part. Three ideas have to be structurally load-bearing in the speech rather than ornamental. First, that declining to collapse a range into a midpoint is a discipline and not a failure of nerve, and that the pressure to collapse is social rather than evidential — people want a number because a number can be quoted in a briefing, not because the evidence supports one. Second, the objection she answered for three decades, which you should let her answer with an episode rather than with an argument: that a belief state which cannot be reduced to a point either does no work at all when it comes time to act, or, the moment you adopt some rule for acting under it, quietly turns back into a point estimate with extra steps. Third, the fact that she several times watched an interval *widen* after she learned something she had not known before — she never committed herself in advance to two matters being independent, and so new information could legitimately make her less resolved about a question she thought she had already settled; she treated that as a result and not as an embarrassment, and I want that moment in the speech, because it is the part of her view that even sympathetic audiences find hardest to swallow.

The episode to build the middle of the speech around is the 1997 crest. Her interval for the river ran from below the floodwall to over it, and the decision threshold sat inside that interval rather than outside it. She recommended evacuation, the water did not come, and the town was furious for the better part of a year. She has never once said she was right to call it; she has only said, in the same sentence each time, that she would call it the same way, and that the alternative was to pretend the interval was narrower than it was so that a worse outcome could not be laid at her door. Hold that unresolved — the speech must not settle it, and must not imply that the passage of time proved her correct. As to constraints: the vocabulary is banned. She would consider a toast that said the words probability, uncertainty, ambiguity, credal, Bayesian, or imprecise to be a lecture dressed as affection, and none of those may appear; nor may any spoken numeral except the two from a single entry in her log, which I want used once, for effect. The one term I am allowing is the honest part, because it is hers. There is a joke the room is going to expect and you must not make it — the one about how she gave an interval when her daughters asked if she loved them, or how she would not say whether it would rain at the wedding. It is the obvious laugh and it converts her seriousness into a punchline, and she is in the room. I want exactly one genuine moment of laughter, and it must not be at her expense. The hard part, and the reason I am asking rather than writing it myself, is the ending: a conventional toast closes by collapsing to a single verdict about the person — she was the most honest colleague I ever had, that sort of thing — and that closing move is precisely the thing she spent her life arguing against, so I cannot make it in front of her. The last thirty seconds have to state something about her character that stays open at the top and the bottom and still lands as devotion rather than as hedging; if it reads as diplomatic equivocation the whole speech fails. Keep every sentence survivable when spoken aloud. No references to her papers by title, no citations, no suggested further reading. After the speech, in three or four sentences, tell me where each of the three ideas landed and what you did at that point to keep her sister and the emergency managers with you.
