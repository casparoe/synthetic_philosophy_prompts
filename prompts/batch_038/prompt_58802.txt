So I run a monthly rationality-adjacent meetup — 12 to 16 people, somebody's living room, pizza, about 75 usable minutes once everyone stops talking about their jobs. Roughly half are software/STEM types who've never read any early modern philosophy, two have undergrad philosophy, and one guy has read the Sequences twice who will pattern-match anything I do to map/territory confusion within ninety seconds and then check out for the rest of the night.

I finally read section 23 of Berkeley's Principles — the bit where the objector boasts that nothing could be easier than imagining trees in a park with nobody there to see them, and gets told that what he's actually done is imagine the trees plus imagine himself absent, which isn't the same feat. It wrecked me a bit, because for years I'd assumed the whole thing was a cheap word trick, and now I think there's a real move in it *and* a real place where the arguement slips, and I want people to hit both with their own hands.

So: design an in-room game that makes them run into that argument themselves, before anyone says Berkeley, idealism, or the words mind, idea, perceive, conceive. Those four are banned from your mouth during the game phase entirely — they're only allowed to surface in the debrief.

Here's the mechanic I want you to build around, improve it if you can. Everyone gets an index card with two labelled slots. Slot A: the thing you're nominating. Slot B: the claim you're making about that thing. Cards go into a sealed envelope. There's a real, small prize — ten bucks, a good chocolate bar — for the best nomination of something that right now, tonight, is not being thought about by anybody in the room. The trap I'm going for is that the only way to adjudicate the envelope is to read the cards aloud, and reading them aloud is exactly what disqualifies them, and I want the two-slot card to make that failure legible in the artifacts on the table rather than in a lecture: when you read slot A back to the room, what happens to the claim sitting in slot B? Somebody in there should notice that the two slots were doing different kinds of work the whole time, and that the argument only bites on one of them. I don't want to be the person who points that out.

What I need in the deliverable:

- Rules, a timeline in minutes, and a materials list I could assemble from a dollar store and a home printer.
- A voting or scoring scheme for the adjudication that doesn't need real money, because I want them to spend five minutes arguing about who won before they discover that nobody did.
- An explicit clause for the perceiver escape hatch. Someone will say the thing is being thought about by God, or by the universe, or by a Boltzmann brain, or by the dude in the next apartment. I want a rule that keeps this from derailing play but also banks it for the debrief, because it turns out to matter more than it looks.
- Something for the Sequences guy. If his move is that this is all just a verbal dispute, give him a job inside the game where being right about that doesn't get him off the hook.
- A debrief that maps specific moments of the play onto specific parts of the argument, and that ends with the group sitting on the fork rather than me announcing it: either the argument quietly changes what it means partway through and then it proves nothing, or you hold the meaning fixed and then it comes out true but nobody should have been scared of it in the first place. I want them to feel that both horns are uncomfortable, not to be informed that both horns are uncomfortable.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it — including the flat version where someone says out loud that this was pointless.
- And an honest paragraph on what the activity can't reproduce. My suspicion is that it can only ever stage the negative argument and totally fails to convey why Berkeley thinks his view is the commonsense one and materialism is the weird abstraction, but tell me what else I'm giving up by doing it this way.
