So, seven years into a book I'm not sure I'll finish, and I need someone to help me make a call I've been putting off. It's a transposition — I've taken the collapse of the Roman Republic and moved it into an invented setting. No togas, no Latium: a maritime trading power with an elected senate drawn from a hereditary-ish governing class, a land army that increasingly recruits from people with no property, a veto-carrying popular office that was meant to protect the smallfolk and got captured, and an empire whose provinces are simultaneously the source of its wealth and the thing its politicians need to plunder in order to pay for their careers. It is emphatically not an allegory where you can see the seams — I've cut the auguries, the client-patron vocabulary, the whole tribal voting apparatus. But the problem is that the real thing has about nine plausible engines of collapse and a novel can only carry three. My POV structure has three threads: a quartermaster on a general's staff who watches an army stop caring who pays it, a reformer who keeps getting frozen out by procedural obstruction and eventually decides the procedures aren't worth preserving, and a very rich outsider from a family the old guard won't admit, who concludes that if he can't buy his way in he'll break his way in. Five hundred pages, maybe less.

Here's my candidate list, and I want them ranked by how much of the book's weight each should bear. (1) Land concentration and the disappearance of the smallholder, plus the failure of every serious attempt at redistribution. (2) The army's loyalty migrating from the state to the commander who guarantees its discharge and its land — though I know there's a real argument that this is largely a modern construction pinned onto one general's supposed reforms, and that soldiers stayed more conventional than the legend allows, so tell me how much of this is solid enough to build a whole POV on. (3) The normalization of political violence, beginning with the killing of a land reformer and the fact that the killers were never really made to pay. (4) Institutions designed for a small competitive aristocracy being asked to run a mass polity — the single-magistrate veto, the sacrosanctity of the popular office, the fact that obstruction was cheap and passage was expensive. (5) The courts as political prizes: whoever controls the jury panels controls which careers survive. (6) The sudden enfranchisement of the Italian allies after a brutal war, done in a way that gave them citizenship on paper and almost no voting weight in practice — which is my outsider thread's fuel. (7) The decay of informal norms, the unwritten restraints and the senate's moral authority over its own members, with everything else downstream of that. (8) Money: the cost of office, electoral bribery, and the way provincial plunder becomes the only return on a political investment. (9) The governing class closing itself off, which pushes ambitious men toward extra-institutional strategies. Tell me which of these is upstream of which — I have a suspicion that 1 and 7 are the real causes and that 2, 3, and 8 are just the transmission mechanisms, but I want to be argued out of it if that's lazy. Tell me which are epiphenomenal and would be a waste of a thread. Tell me which are genuinely transportable into a setting that lacks Rome's specific machinery and which are artifacts of Roman institutions I can't convincingly fake. And if there's something big missing from the list that belongs at the top — an economic or demographic or military-stretch factor, say — put it in and justify it.

One more thing before you answer. I'm aware that a lot of the confidence in any of these rankings is hindsight: we know how it ended, so the whole century looks like a slide, whereas a senator in his forties in the sixties BC probably thought things were bad but survivable, and had been told they were bad but survivable for eighty years. I also know we get most of this period through participants who were writing their own defense, and through later authors working under an emperor, which colors what counts as a cause. So: factor in how much of the case for your top pick depends on sources inclined to think the collapse was inevitable and deserved. Then tell me what would change your mind — which piece of argument or evidence would demote your number one, which of your rankings you're least sure of, and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to break down. Please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the three that should get the threads, in order — and then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my setup back to me.
