Third year, political theory, and my chapter is on democratic boundaries. My supervisor read my reconstruction of the case against the all-affected principle and said something like "if this works you've refuted every legitimacy principle with a causal premise, including the one you're planning to fall back on." I think she might be right and I can't find the break. Here's the chain:

(1) A collective decision is legitimate only if everyone whose interests it affects has a say in it, with the weight of their say proportional to how much the decision affects them.

(2) A decision affects someone's interests iff it makes some nonzero difference to how well that person's life goes.

(3) In a causally dense world, any decision by any collective body makes some nonzero difference to the lives of indefinitely many agents — including ones who don't exist yet, and arguably many sentient nonhumans.

(4) So legitimacy requires distributing say-weight over an unbounded and unspecifiable population.

(5) But you can't distribute weight over a set you can't specify, and you can't specify the set by decision either: deciding who's in the set is itself a decision affecting everyone in the same unbounded way, so you'd need a demos to constitute the demos, and a demos to constitute that one (the Whelan-style regress).

(6) Therefore no collective decision is legitimate.

(7) (6) is not a conclusion I can live with, so (1) is false — the all-affected principle isn't a principle of legitimacy, it's a principle of paralysis.

Two things bother me. First, (1)–(6) seem to run just as smoothly against the all-subjected principle (Abizadeh, López-Guerra and co.), since being subjected to a coercive order is also a causal notion with fuzzy edges — so a sound version of this argument takes out my fallback too. Second, my own proposed repair is a threshold: swap (2) for "affects" meaning "makes a difference above some ε." But then I run into an aggregation problem — a single decision might move someone's interests by less than ε, while the same body's decisions over a decade move them by a lot, and I can't see how to set ε without either making the demos collapse to the citizenry by fiat or letting it balloon again. So my repair looks like it just relocates the arbitrariness the all-affected principle was supposed to remove.

Which step is actually the weak one? I'd rather keep (1) and (3) if it comes to that, and I definately don't want to accept (6), but I'll take the hit if I have to. What I want is the pricing:

- If the problem is in (2) — i.e. "affects" was never meant as a bare causal claim — what exactly does the normatively-loaded reading add, and can I still keep the intuition that motivates all-affected in the first place (that it's arbitrary to bind people who had no hand in binding themselves)? Or does loading "affects" normatively make the principle circular, with legitimacy explaining inclusion and inclusion explaining legitimacy?
- If the problem is the slide from (4) to (5), i.e. unbounded and hard-to-specify aren't the same as undistributable — does proportionality weighting actually rescue it? Can total weight converge over an infinite affected set the way a convergent series does, and if so does that give you a real procedure or just a formal trick that no institution could run?
- Is (7) even a fair move? It treats (1) as the only premise that can go, but (3) is contingent and (5) has an ought-implies-can hidden in it. If (7) is a false dilemma, say so and tell me what the honest conclusion of (1)–(5) actually is.
- And my supervisor's specific charge: she thinks the proportionality clause in (1) is doing all the dirty work and that bare inclusion (everyone affected gets *a* say, no weights) doesn't generate (4) at all. I can't see it. Is she right, and if she is, what does the argument look like once you strip the weights out?
