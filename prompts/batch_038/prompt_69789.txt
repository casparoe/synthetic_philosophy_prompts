I direct the communications office of a city government of roughly 280,000 people. Every week my staff of five faces a cluster of decisions that have no settled rule behind them: a claim about us circulating on social media that we cannot verify quickly enough to answer; a reporter holding a leaked draft of a report whose figures are accurate today but expected to move before publication; a question about an incident where the honest answer is technically correct but will be quoted in a way that misleads; and, occasionally, the discovery that a statement we ourselves issued last month was wrong. We work on twenty-four- to forty-eight-hour clocks, none of my staff has legal or philosophical training, and everything that goes out is signed off by the chief of staff and sometimes by the city attorney.

Please write a decision procedure my office can actually run on a pending statement — a sequence of questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure terminates in "say nothing," in "say the whole thing including the unflattering part," or in "this is not ours to decide," and the points at which it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values or of political judgment rather than of fact. It should be usable by a new staffer in about twenty minutes, and it should be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

I want it grounded in Hannah Arendt's account of truth and lying in politics rather than in generic media ethics. She opens her essay "Truth and Politics" (The New Yorker, 1967) with the observation that "no one, as far as I know, has ever counted truthfulness among the political virtues. Lies have always been regarded as necessary and justifiable tools not only of the politician's or the demagogue's but also of the statesman's trade," and she then traces that tradition back to the demand that someone be willing "to say what is." Later, writing about the release of the Pentagon Papers, she distinguishes the traditional political lie — which conceals a secret from an adversary and leaves the rest of the factual record intact — from organized, image-driven lying, which does not merely hide a fact but works to dissolve it, so that the audience is left unable to tell what happened at all. Your procedure should take seriously both halves of that picture: the insight that a factual record persists only because institutions and witnesses keep testifying to it, and the insight that lying has historically been defended as an instrument of political change, which is why "just say what happened" cannot be the whole rule for a communications office.

Tell me explicitly which of Arendt's distinctions must survive the simplification into a checklist — her separation of factual truth from the truths of reason and philosophy, her contrast between concealment and the destruction of fact, her claim that a lie is addressed to what one would like reality to be rather than to what an enemy knows, and her account of the fragility of fact and its dependence on testimony — and which can be dropped without the procedure losing its point. Where you think her framework leads a practitioner astray, say so and build the guardrail into the procedure rather than around it.

Then run the procedure on three cases, showing each step:

1. A post alleging that the city falsified water-quality data is being shared widely. The allegation is false as far as we can tell, but our own lab records for the period in question are incomplete, and any denial we issue will have to restate the allegation in order to reject it.
2. A reporter has a leaked draft of an internal audit showing that our emergency-response times in one district are far worse than the published averages. The figure is correct as of the draft, the final report is three weeks away, and the number is bad enough that the council will want it contextualized before release.
3. Six weeks ago we stated that a department had no prior complaints of a certain kind. We have since found four. Nothing external has happened; no one has noticed.

At least one of these three must come out in a way I will not like, and if the procedure's verdict on any of them is "decide this yourself, the procedure runs out here," say precisely where and why it runs out.

Two further requirements. First, do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not reproduce quotations from Arendt or anyone else beyond the two short passages supplied above; paraphrase instead. Second, be explicit about the epistemic status of the procedure itself: mark which steps rest on a contested reading of Arendt, say what would change your mind about the design — what argument or consideration would lead you to abandon her framework in favor of a simpler rule such as minimizing overall deception or protecting institutional credibility — and identify where you think this procedure is most likely to fail in practice, and against which kind of case. Write the answer in American English.
