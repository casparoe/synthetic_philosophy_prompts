I retired four years ago after a career in experimental physics — mostly instrumentation, with rather more asymptotic analysis than my colleagues thought healthy — and I have taken up philosophy in earnest. I have encountered a pattern of argument that I cannot name, and my difficulty is that it appears to have two halves, one negative and one positive, and I do not know whether either of them has an established label.

The negative half. An author describes a device that performs some operation, then performs it again after half the remaining time, and so on, so that infinitely many operations are completed inside a single finite interval. In the first case I met, the device has a switch and a lamp: the lamp is on at the outset and the switch is thrown at every step. The argument then runs that at the closing instant of the interval nothing in the account tells us whether the lamp is on or off — for every moment at which it is on there is a later moment at which it is off — and from that supposed blank the author concludes the device could not have existed at all. In a second case, a machine prints digits at ever-shorter intervals, and the author observes that the description fails to fix what stands on the tape at the end, and infers that no such machine is possible. In both, the premise seems to me to concern the description while the conclusion concerns the world. Had a colleague written that way in a referee report on my own work, I would have replied that he had shown only that we had not yet said enough.

The positive half. Faced with a process whose terminal state the existing rules do not fix, we in the sciences generally extend the rules rather than deny the process. A series that fails to converge in the ordinary sense is assigned a value by a summation procedure that agrees with ordinary convergence wherever ordinary convergence applies; a sequence with no limit inside a given space is supplied with one by enlarging the space; a calculation that returns infinities is regulated and then redefined so that the measurable predictions come out finite. In none of these cases does anyone conclude that the situation described was impossible.

So: is there a name for the negative move, the inference from an account's failure to determine how things stand at a point to the impossibility of the thing itself? Is there a name for the positive move, the repair of the account rather than of the world? And what separates a legitimate repair of that kind from merely changing the subject — what must the extended rule preserve, if the original objection is to be answered rather than evaded? Please set out the nearest existing concepts, say how each differs from what I have described and which fits best, and if nothing fits, coin and define a term with its boundary cases. I want the carving, not a label and not a reading list; I have no means of chasing references and would rather you work the distinctions out in front of me.
