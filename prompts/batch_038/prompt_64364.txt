I am in the final year of my undergraduate philosophy degree and this term I am taking our department's seminar on aggregation, population ethics, and the measurement of welfare: sixteen students around a single table, with a professor generous enough to let each of us run one seventy-five-minute session ourselves. Mine falls immediately after three weeks on commensurability and on views that give suffering lexical priority, and I want to spend it on a position I have become convinced my classmates have not actually grasped, although every one of them says they have. As I read it, the view is really two claims that people slide between without noticing. The first is a threshold claim: that above some intensity of suffering, no quantity of pleasure — however large, however widely distributed, however long it lasts — compensates for it, and that this is not a matter of the exchange rate between pleasure and pain being merely steep, but of there being no exchange rate at all past that point. The second is an asymmetry claim that operates below the threshold: that even where pains and pleasures are genuinely comparable, removing a given amount of suffering carries more weight than creating the same amount of pleasure, so that a world with less misery and less joy can be better than one with more of both. Nearly everyone in the room will tell you they accept the second and reject the first. My suspicion, which I want the session to test rather than assert, is that once they are made to act on their own stated views rather than describe them, they will turn out to be committed to the first as well — or to nothing coherent at all.

So what I need is an activity, not a presentation. No lecture beforehand, and the words negative utilitarianism, lexical, and threshold should not be spoken by me until the debrief; they should discover the structure by running into it. Please do not build the session around Le Guin's Omelas, which all sixteen of them have read at least twice and which has entirely lost its force for them, and do not rely on open discussion, because two extremely articulate total utilitarians will simply take the floor and the rest will defer to them — the mechanism has to constrain what they can say and make the relevant fact show up in what they do. There is no budget: no real money, no prizes, no printing beyond two sheets per person, though the room has a whiteboard and a set of markers. The points they earn should nevertheless buy something they actually care about, such as who has to open next week's presentation or how the break is scheduled, so that the choices are live rather than hypothetical. My own half-formed idea, which I would like you either to repair or to replace with something better, is this: hand each of them a table listing several sufferings of increasing intensity in concrete description, ask them to write down for each how many units of a specified pleasure would have to be created to make it worth permitting that suffering, and then use the numbers they have just written to allocate a fixed pool in a second round where their own figures bind them. My worry is that this degenerates into arithmetic that the total utilitarians win comfortably, or else that the students simply refuse to enter numbers and nothing happens at all.

What I want back is a complete, runnable design. Give me the rules and the materials list, a timeline marked in minutes, and a scoring scheme that costs nothing and that a facilitator can keep straight on a whiteboard while sixteen people are talking at once. Build in a diagnostic I can read off their behaviour rather than their professions, distinguishing three readings of the claim that pleasure and pain cannot be weighed against one another: a steep but finite exchange rate; a threshold beyond which no finite compensation suffices; and two separate scales such that no comparison between them is defined at all. These three should leave visibly different traces in the game — in the numbers people write, in which lots they bid on and which they refuse to price, in whether their answers survive being multiplied and added — and you should tell me what to say to the student whose pattern fits none of the three. The debrief should then map particular moments of the session onto the two claims I distinguished above, and should be explicit about what each one costs its holder: the familiar objection that a purely negative view licenses painlessly destroying everything capable of suffering, and the objection that a lexical priority cannot be captured by any ordinary single-scale measure, together with the standard device of representing it by an ordered pair of measures evaluated in sequence. Tell me whether the game can be arranged so that someone actually wants that device, and what it does to the arithmetic once adopted. Finally, be candid about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the exploit someone will find, the point at which the session is likely to stall, the student who will refuse to play and whether that refusal is itself informative — and about which feature of the view no activity of this kind can reproduce, so that I know precisely what I will have to supply in words at the end.
