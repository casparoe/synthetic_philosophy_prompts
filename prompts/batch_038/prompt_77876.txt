I sit on my university's academic integrity board — three faculty, two students, one administrator — and Thursday we hear an appeal that has split us down the middle. I've been asked to write the opinion the board adopts whichever way it goes, so I want a draft I can argue with rather than something that just restates the handbook back at me.

The case: a senior in the honors program submitted a 5,000-word thesis, got an A, and then a classmate reported that she'd built what she calls "the pipeline." She wrote a workflow in which one model instance produced an outline, a second drafted each section from that outline, a third was instructed to attack the draft as hard as it could, and a fourth revised in light of the attack. She read every output at every stage and either approved it or kicked it back with a one-line instruction, and she has a sixty-page timestamped log of all of it, which she handed over voluntarily without being asked. By my count from the log she rejected or sent back something like a third of the outputs, which cuts in her favor. Her defense, laid out in a genuinely lucid five-page statement, is that each individual step was something she could have done herself in an hour — the pipeline just made her do them one at a time instead of all smeared together — and that since she checked every output, the judgment calls were hers. She points out that if she'd paid a human tutor to play devil's advocate and a human copyeditor to tighten the prose, nobody would have blinked, and she says she thinks of the whole thing as scaffolding for a task too big to hold in her head at once. The syllabus said no generative AI "in drafting the thesis" and said nothing about critique or revision, and the instructor concedes he wrote the rule assuming the tools would only be used to produce sentences.

The thing that cuts the other way, and I cant get past it: in the oral exam she defended the thesis's conclusion well, but when asked why a specific objection in section three fails she said she'd need to check her log, and when the examiners pushed she described it as "the one the red-team agent raised." Her hand-written work in the same course averaged a B-, and she offered to redo a comparable paper by hand under supervision and got a B-. So there's a live question of whether the artifact measured a capability she actually has, and whether her checking was competent checking or a rubber stamp with good vibes about it. One of the faculty on the board also thinks the scaffolding analogy is doing suspicious work — a calculator extends your arithmetic while leaving the arithmetic yours, he says, whereas this is a case where the checking itself is only as good as the skill the assignment was supposed to build, which is exactly the skill in question. He may be begging the question and I can't tell.

What I need: a written opinion I could circulate, recommending a concrete disposition — uphold the finding, overturn it, or uphold a modified finding with a lesser consequence — plus what happens to the grade and by when. It should give the board a test we could apply to the next four of these, because there will be next four of these, and it needs to be something a student writing an appeal could actually argue against rather than a vibe. And please engage her decomposition argument at full strength: half the board thinks she's straightforwardly right, and I want to know whether they can be answered at all, and if they can't, what the finding would have to become. No references or policy citations — I don't need a bibliography, I need the reasoning, including an honest account of what we'd be giving up if we rule against her.
