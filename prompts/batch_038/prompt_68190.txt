Thirty-odd years of refereeing papers left me with one mental tool I can't stop reaching for, and now that I'm retired and sitting in a philosophy reading group I've been pointed at Hume's essay on taste with it. My pitch was that the true judges he describes are basically peer review with better manners: you screen people for competence, they converge over time, and the convergence is your evidence there's something there to converge on. I even claimed Hume's own little story -- Sancho's kinsmen who call the wine good but tainted, one by leather and one by iron, get laughed out of the room, and are vindicated when the cask is drained and an old key on a leather thong turns up in the dregs -- is his version of a failed prediction, the check that turns consensus into knowledge. The youngest in the group said I'd described a well-run echo chamber, on the grounds that a referee can be shown wrong by the experiment falling over later and there is nothing at all like that in aesthetics.

So: audit my analogy. I want the correspondence table -- what plays the editor, the methods section, the citation network, the failed prediction -- then where it breaks, and in particular which features of peer review it quietly imports that aesthetic judging doesn't have. Then tell me whether Hume's conclusion that there is a standard of taste survives the disanalogies or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole way, and if it doesn't survive, what better comparison he could have used. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble and dont restate my question back at me.
