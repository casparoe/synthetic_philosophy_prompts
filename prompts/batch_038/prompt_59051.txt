I am preparing a contribution to an interdisciplinary working group on the Anthropocene, and the discussion keeps running through two analogies that I have come to distrust in different ways.

The first belongs to a stratigrapher in our group. He says that asking when the Anthropocene began is like asking when the Cretaceous ended: the iridium-rich clay marking the K–Pg boundary is a fact about the rock rather than an interpretation of it, and once you can point to a globally synchronous signal — for the Anthropocene, the plutonium fallout laid down by thermonuclear testing in the early 1950s — the start date is settled by evidence rather than argument. On his telling, it matters no more that the Anthropocene's cause was a species rather than an asteroid than it matters that the K–Pg cause was an impactor rather than a volcanic province. From this he concludes that a start date is there to be found, and that the stratigraphic bodies' recent refusal to ratify the epoch was a loss of nerve about hierarchy, not a discovery that no boundary exists.

The second belongs to an environmental historian. She says that declaring an Anthropocene is like declaring an Industrial Revolution: a retrospective label laid over a long, uneven, regionally staggered transformation, whose beginning different communities fix differently — 1780, 1830, much later elsewhere — with no fact of the matter to appeal to. From this she draws a near-opposite conclusion: there is no date to find, only a date to choose, and choosing the early 1950s quietly nominates the industrialised North as the agent of planetary history.

Both analogies, I suspect, share an unstated premise: that a boundary is fixed by observers downstream of it who cannot alter it. Our case is otherwise in two respects. The proposed interval has a base but no top — its upper boundary is, for now, simply the present. And its would-be inhabitants are still manufacturing the marker: within a few decades, coordinated action could thin the signal, change its composition, or bury it beneath something else, in a way no Cretaceous mollusc and no nineteenth-century mill owner could do to their own periodisation.

Please audit the two analogies. For each, set out the mapping explicitly — a compact table of correspondences will do — and then separate the mismatches that are load-bearing for the conclusion each speaker draws from those that are merely decorative. I especially want to know what each source domain contributes that the target lacks: what the asteroid smuggles in, and what the Industrial Revolution smuggles in, that neither is entitled to lend. Then say whether the two open features I flagged — the missing top, and the fact that the interval's own inhabitants can still revise the marker — damage one analogy, both, or neither, and whether they point toward a better third comparison; if you propose one, put it through the same treatment in a sentence or two rather than presenting it as a rescue. Finally, commit rather than balancing: once the disanalogies are on the table, what survives of the claim that there is a fact about when the Anthropocene began, and what survives of any claim about who gets to decide?

Keep the whole response under 700 words, tables included.
