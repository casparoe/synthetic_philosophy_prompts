For thirty-eight years I worked as a physical chemist, largely on reaction kinetics, and for the last eleven of those I also sat on my university's investment committee. It was there, in a pitch from an outside manager who declined to describe the signal that generated his returns on the grounds that describing it would destroy it, that I first encountered a style of argument I have since met in many other settings. Now that I am retired I have taken up philosophy in earnest — mostly falsificationism and the epistemology of markets — and I find that I keep needing a term for one particular pattern without being able to locate one. Let me describe it as concretely as I can.

Someone asserts a claim with substantial practical consequences: a way of forecasting some quantity, an inefficiency they say they exploit, a diagnostic hunch. Asked for evidence, they decline, and the reason given is not that the evidence is inconvenient or that producing it would be burdensome, but that *producing it would make the claim false*. Tell the world, and others act; once others act, the opportunity is competed away and the regularity dissolves. So far the reasoning is at least intelligible, and in the case of markets I can see the mechanism at work, since private information plainly loses value as it becomes public. What troubles me is the second step. Colleagues and commentators then treat the absence of evidence as no mark against the claim whatsoever, precisely because the claim is the sort of thing that could never be evidenced without being destroyed. The claim is thus placed beyond the reach of testing in both directions: evidence for it cannot be had, and the failure to produce evidence cannot be counted against it. The identical structure appears in my own former field, where a researcher says he has found a subtle regularity in a dataset that would let him forecast an outcome but will not publish the method, because publication would ruin it; and in medicine, where a clinician says her intuition reliably identifies a class of patients, but that a trial would change how everyone treats them and so could never confirm anything. In each case the explanation is accepted and the claim is never tested, and the person making it is not regarded as having evaded a burden so much as having discharged one.

I know roughly where this sits, and I know what it is not. I know Popper's criterion, and the point that a theory patched with ad hoc adjustments loses content; I have read that there is a theorem to the effect that two rational parties sharing a common prior should not voluntarily trade, which sounds adjacent but is not what I mean; and in physics I am accustomed to measurements disturbing what they measure, which is a superficial resemblance at best, since there the disturbance is neither strategic nor owed to anyone's finding out. What I am after is the specific structure in which the act of verification is itself what destroys the thing verified, and in which that fact is then advanced as the reason the claim deserves credence. Does this have an established name — in philosophy of science, in the social sciences, in the economics of information, anywhere? If it does, I should like to understand its boundaries rather than merely hear it. If it does not, I should like a proposed term and a real definition, and, more important than the label, an account of how the phenomenon differs from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, from a claim that is merely unfalsifiable in principle, and from genuine measurement interference. I should especially like to know whether there is any principled way to separate the instances in which the value-destroying mechanism is itself an independently established general fact, as it may well be for private information in markets, from the instances in which that mechanism is simply asserted on the spot by the person who most needs it to be true. If no such separation is available, I would rather be told so plainly; if it is available, I want to know what it turns on, and what would count as a borderline case — say, a claimant who volunteers full documentation only after the value has already been extracted, or one whose mechanism is real for the first several disclosures and merely claimed for the rest.
