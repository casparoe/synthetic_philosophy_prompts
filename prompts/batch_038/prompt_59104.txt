**Convertibility: corruption as cashing out purpose-bound standing** (draft)

Nearly everything in the field runs off the public-office definition: conduct that deviates from the formal duties of a public role for private-regarding gain. It has one glaring weakness, which is that it presumes a public/private line that the societies being judged often havent drawn, or have drawn somewhere else entirely. The relativist response is that the definition is a provincial artifact: what a donor ministry calls a bribe, a village calls a wedding gift; what it calls clientelism, a subsistence economy calls insurance. Ang's unbundling of corruption into petty theft, grand theft, speed money and access money is the best repair job available, because it shows that lumping those together hides real functional differences — but it still leaves every member of the bundle on the same side of the line.

My move is to stop asking where the line is and ask what the line was ever for. An office confers standing that was granted for a purpose: to award a contract, to waive a queue, to hire. Corruption, on this account, is the conversion of that purpose-bound standing into a resource spendable outside the purpose — a residue the officeholder can use for anything at all. Call it the residue test: after the act, does the officeholder end up holding something general-purpose? Cash converts perfectly. A favour owed inside a closed community converts badly, because the standing cant leave the community and cant be spent on anything the community doesn't sanction. A cow converts into a bounded set of things; a land title in a monetised economy converts into nearly anything.

This is meant to dissolve the universalism dispute rather than pick a side. The relativist is right that gift-giving and bribery are different phenomena, but wrong that the difference is a difference in standards. It's a difference in conversion rates, and conversion rates are set by how monetised and how exit-able the local economy is. A banquet given to a headman who cannot leave his village, and whose standing depends on his kin, is a low-conversion gift; the cash equivalent wired offshore is a high-conversion one. So the orthodoxy isn't applying Western morals to non-Western practices — it's failing to notice that its own metric is sensitive to a variable it never names. That also generates a prediction: the practices reformers most object to abroad are the high-conversion ones, and the ones they legalize at home — donations spent on campaigns, earmarks spent in-district, lobbying fees paid to firms — are ones where the residue stays bound to a purpose. Which is a fudge, and I'll come back to it.

On nepotism, which looks like a counterexample since the official hires a cousin and pockets nothing: the account only needs the residue to land inside whatever the official treats as an extension of themselves — household, lineage, party, ethnic coalition. So conversion is indexed to a unit, and the unit is an empirical question about the official's own boundary-drawing. This is where the account does its most interesting work and also where I'm least sure of it, because if the unit can be the whole electorate, the account starts calling democratic responsiveness corrupt.

Three problems I already know about. (i) The legal-lobbying fudge: if a contribution is spent on the campaign the residue is purpose-bound, so I get not-corrupt, which seems to let the most consequential form of influence in rich democracies off the hook. I can bite the bullet and say the intuition is definitional, or say purpose-binding is graded, since a campaign fund converts at an intermediate rate — it buys access, staff, and eventually careers. (ii) Speed money: the clerk takes cash to do what the rules already required. Perfect conversion, clearly corrupt here — but the citizen recieves nothing beyond what they were owed and the loss is diffuse, borne by the queue. So the account gets the right answer for what feels like the wrong reason. (iii) Ideological embezzlement: an official diverts funds to a party that pays him nothing. That converts into a collective political resource, so I should say corrupt, and a lot of people would say it isn't.

What the account still doesn't have is a reason why conversion is bad rather than a procedure for detecting it. Maybe: conversion destroys the purpose-binding, which is the only thing making an office fiduciary rather than a piece of property — corruption as the re-privatisation of office. That's a gesture, not an argument.
