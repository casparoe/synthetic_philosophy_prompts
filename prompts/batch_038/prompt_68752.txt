I am four hundred pages into a novel whose central device is a house whose rooms are out of temporal order, and I have been using the manuscript as an excuse to read philosophy of time at night. Last month I gave the draft to a friend who teaches physics at a polytechnic and who has strong opinions about my ending. He returned it with a long note arguing that my instinct — to let one character avert a catastrophe she has already witnessed — is incoherent, on the grounds that the block universe is exactly like a finished novel: every page exists whether or not anyone is reading it; the reader's finger resting on page 143 is merely an indexical fact about where she happens to be; and nothing she does from there can alter what is printed on page 300. He regards the film-reel and the sliced-loaf-of-bread versions of the same picture, which are what one meets in popular treatments, as cruder cousins of his preferred analogy, and he thinks the novel version is the one that ought to persuade me specifically, since I make my living by the thing. He then reaches for the Rietdijk–Putnam argument to insist this is physics rather than rhetoric: because observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events are simultaneous with their present, and because each of them is entitled to count the events on his own simultaneity slice as real, reality must contain all the slices at once — the whole book, already bound and shelved. His conclusion for my manuscript is practical rather than metaphysical: my characters' deliberation is decoration laid over a settled text, and the ending I have been resisting is not merely inevitable but always was, so I should stop treating it as a moral question and start treating it as a disclosure.

I do not accept this, but I cannot presently say why, and I would rather have an audit than either an endorsement or a demolition. I know enough about novels to suspect the comparison is carrying more weight than it can bear: a book has an author who existed before it and who settled the ending; a reader who moves through it in a time that is not the book's own; a page order that is total, unique, and the same for every copy; and, most awkwardly for him, a standing distinction between the order of the story and the order of the telling, which his version quietly collapses into one sequence. I also suspect that already written and already fixed by earlier events plus the laws are not the same claim, since a great many novels are printed, distributed, and read aloud long before anyone has decided how they will end, and since a page's existence is not a proof about its causes. What I cannot do on my own is determine which of these complaints actually bite against the physics and which are merely a novelist defending her ending.

Please structure your reply in the following sections, in this order:

1. **The Claim, Restated** (about 150 words). Give my friend's argument its most charitable and most precise form, and separate the distinct conclusions it is being used to license — that distant events are just as real as nearby present ones; that temporal passage is no feature of the world; that the future is causally settled; and that deliberation is therefore ornamental. Flag which of these the argument, if sound, would actually deliver and which it merely travels with.

2. **Correspondence Table** (three columns: element of the novel, element of the block, verdict of genuine / strained / absent with a one-line reason). Please cover at minimum: the finished text; the page; the spine and its unique ordering; the reader's finger; the reader's traversal; the author; the gap between story-order and telling-order; the book's immunity to being read; the existence of many copies of one novel; the contingency of a different ending having been written; and the fact that no one inside the book is located at any page.

3. **The Disanalogies That Do Damage** (about 500 words). Rank them, and for each say precisely which of my friend's four conclusions it undercuts and why. I am especially interested in whether the analogy's need for a reader moving through the book in an outside time is fatal or merely an artifact of the telling, and in whether the novel's linear page order can stand in for a structure in which spacelike-separated events have no unique order at all.

4. **The Disanalogies I Should Drop** (about 200 words). The objections that sound clever across a dinner table but do not touch the argument — including any of my own listed above that you think are local, aesthetic, or already conceded by the eternalist. Tell me which ones to stop making.

5. **What the Argument Needs Without the Analogy** (about 350 words). Set out the Rietdijk–Putnam argument in plain prose as a sequence of premises: the relativity of simultaneity; the assumption that the events on another frame's simultaneity slice are real; and the transitivity of that reality relation across a chain of such frames. Then give the main lines of resistance — the objection associated with Howard Stein and later Steven Savitt that the present may be a local notion that cannot be stretched into a global hyperplane; the charge that the argument equivocates between being real and being determinate; and the point that spacelike separation is a claim about causal inaccessibility rather than about the future having been fixed by the past. Say which resistance you find most serious and what it costs the argument.

6. **Verdict** (about 250 words). State plainly whether the fatalist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether the unreality-of-passage conclusion survives independently of it, and whether my character's foreknowledge of a catastrophe she then averts is incoherent after all — or incoherent only under an assumption my friend has smuggled in from the novel rather than from the physics.

7. **A Better Analogy** (about 250 words). Offer one I could put in an afterword: say what it maps onto what, what it deliberately refuses to map, what conclusions it would legitimately support, and what it would leave unsupported. If you think no analogy in this neighborhood can carry the fatalist weight at all, say so and propose instead the least misleading way to gesture at the picture in prose.
