I work in bank supervision and I read philosophy at night, mostly to stop my head from turning into a spreadsheet. I've been working through the "wrong kind of reasons" stuff and I get the basic move: if believing something would make you happier, that's not a reason bearing on whether it's true, and same for admiration or fear. Fine. But there's a wrinkle I can't find anywhere and I dont know if it's a thing with a name or just me being confused at midnight.

The wrinkle is that the very same consideration seems to be a wrong-kind reason when I'm the only one acting on it, and a right-kind reason once enough other people act on it too. Two cases. First, from work. A small bank gets shaky. My reason for leaving my deposits where they are isn't that the bank is sound — I have no clue whether it is — it's that everyone else is leaving theirs, and that's precisely what keeps it sound. On its own that's a purely instrumental, arguably wrong-kind reason to trust the place. But if everyone has that reason and acts on it, the bank actually is sound, and now there's a reason to trust it that has something to do with whether it's trustworthy. Second, less technical: I have no reason to admire our new department head except that admiring him makes the office run smoothly, which is obviously the wrong kind. Now suppose everyone admires him on exactly that basis, the admiration changes how he behaves, and after two years he really is a magnanimous, competent, decent boss. Then the admiration everybody had seems to have manufactured the very property that makes admiration fitting.

What I want isn't the label, it's the carving. I know someone will say "self-fulfilling prophecy" and that's not it — I'm not asking about the causal mechanism, I'm asking whether the *kind* of reason changes, and when. So: is there an established concept for reasons that are wrong-kind at the individual level and right-kind at the collective level, or a name for the flip itself? What are the nearest concepts — ordinary conventions, reasons that come into existence through promises or other commitments, the distinction between reasons about an object and reasons about having an attitude toward it — and how is my pattern different from each of those, not just vaguely adjacent? And where does the flip fail? I suspect my second case is shakier than my first: if everyone in a cult admires the guru, and the admiration really does make him widely admired, that doesn't make him admirable, so it seems to matter which properties an attitude can bring into existence and which it can't. If no name exists, coin one and give me the boundary conditions — I want to be able to explain to my seminar group why the bank case flips and the guru case doesn't, without just waving at it.
