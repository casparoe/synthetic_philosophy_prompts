You are to take on the role of a philosopher of probability and anthropic reasoning: someone who works on self-locating belief, observation selection effects, and the epistemology of statistical inference, and who has taught the debate over the doomsday argument to graduate students. Treat what follows as an exercise in philosophical disambiguation rather than exposition, and think it through carefully before committing to a structure.

Background. I am a master's student writing a paper on the doomsday argument, and my reading group keeps leaning on a single sentence. Whenever an objection surfaces, one member says some version of "But you're not special." It is offered as a restatement of the Copernican principle — the idea, imported from cosmology, that we occupy no privileged location — and it is treated as though it disposes of whatever was at issue. In one session it was used to brush aside my objection that the inference cannot get off the ground unless my birth rank is genuinely the outcome of a random draw from a specified class, given that I already knew before doing any arithmetic that I would exist and would be one of us. In another session it was turned against a member who proposed handling the reference-class question by weighting each hypothesis according to how many observers it contains, on the grounds that such weighting privileges the observer in precisely the way the principle forbids. I noticed that the same four words were recruited on both sides of that exchange, which makes me suspect that nobody in the room knows what claim they are asserting.

Task. Lay out the menu of distinct, precisely formulated theses that "you're not special" could be standing for in this debate, ranging from the platitudinous to the radical to the outright false. For each candidate reading, give me: a formulation tight enough to be argued with, ideally in terms of priors, likelihoods, or reference classes; a verdict on its epistemic status (platitude; substantive and defensible; substantive and dubious; false) with reasons; an assessment of whether that reading is by itself sufficient to license the move from my being roughly the n-th human to an upper bound on the total number of humans; and a note on which of the two uses described above it actually supports, if either.

Please begin with a short preliminary pass in which you generate candidate readings and flag which ones collapse into each other, before settling on the final menu. Then make sure the following points are addressed somewhere in your analysis:

- Whether the spatial reading (we sit at no distinguished place in the universe) can carry over to an indexical or temporal reading (my rank among observers is no distinguished rank), and what would have to be true of the situation for that transfer to be legitimate rather than a pun.
- Whether the slogan is best construed as a claim about how to set priors, as a claim about likelihoods, as a constraint on reference-class selection, or as a methodological admonition that is not a premise at all. These are very different jobs, and I want to know which one the argument needs.
- The difference between readings on which the slogan licenses an assumption that I am a random sample from a reference class and readings on which it merely denies that some observers possess private information unavailable to the rest. These come apart, and I suspect the group slides between them.
- Whether any single reading can do both jobs at once: support the doomsday inference and also rule out weighting hypotheses by how many observers they contain. If no reading can, say plainly that the exchange was an equivocation, and identify which of the two deployments fails.
- Whether some reading of the slogan is fully compatible with the inference's failing anyway — for instance because typicality within a class says nothing about the size of the class, or because "humans" is not the reference class one wants once machine successors are in view.
- Whether the slogan plays the same role in a confidence-interval style argument and in an argument framed as Bayesian updating on one's birth rank, or whether it is load-bearing in one and decorative in the other.
- Which reading the people who repeat the sentence most likely intend, and what the shortest true sentence in its neighborhood is.

Constraints. No bibliography, no reading list, and no citations: I have the literature and want analysis, not pointers. Do name positions, assumptions, and objections by their standard labels where such labels exist, since I will need those to look things up myself. Do not retreat into non-committal hedging — every reading on the menu receives a verdict, and the verdicts should be defended rather than announced.

Close with a single paragraph stating which precise claim, if any, I may defensibly attribute to the Copernican principle in my own paper, followed by one sentence giving the replacement wording I should use whenever the slogan is doing legitimate work.
