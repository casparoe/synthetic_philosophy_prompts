I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been commissioned to write a long essay on moral responsibility that is supposed to do something other than re-run the free-will debate for the hundredth time. Six weeks into the secondary literature I have hit a wall, and I do not think the wall is my own comprehension.

Since the late 1960s the argument over Frankfurt-style cases and the principle of alternative possibilities has had the same shape. A case is offered in which an agent decides and acts exactly as she would have anyway while an intervener stands ready to ensure that outcome, so that she seems intuitively responsible though she could not have done otherwise. Defenders of the principle reply either that some morally relevant alternative survives the design — the flicker-of-freedom strategy — or that the case faces a dilemma: the intervener must read some prior sign, and if that sign causally guarantees the decision the example is doing determinist work it was never meant to do, while if it does not, the flicker returns. Redesigned cases are then produced to escape both horns, and the cycle restarts. The narrower questions — whether a revised principle about avoidable blame fares better, whether responsibility requires leeway or only sourcehood — have settled into the same rhythm. Nothing I have read suggests that anyone has moved anyone in decades.

What I want from you is an argument rather than a survey: what kind of disagreement is this? Please weigh at least four diagnoses and commit to one. First, that it is partly verbal, in that the two sides attach different contents to the claim that an agent could have done otherwise — a conditional analysis on one side, a categorical one on the other — and are talking past each other. Second, that the disagreement concerns standards of evidence: whether a stipulated thought experiment can carry any weight against a principle meant to describe our actual practices of holding one another responsible. Third, that the sides are answering different questions under a single heading, one about leeway conditions and one about the history or source of the action, which would explain why each new case looks decisive to whoever produced it. Fourth, my own working hypothesis, which I would like you to test and break if it is weak: that the impasse turns on the granularity at which the action is described. The Frankfurtian seems to need a coarse description (keeping the money), the flicker defender a fine one (deciding at that moment, unaided, to keep the money), and once a level is fixed the case either is or is not a counterexample. Is that a genuine diagnosis, or does it merely relocate the dispute, since the live question then becomes which granularity is the morally relevant one — and is there any principled way to settle that?

Two further requests. The first concerns a case from my own beat, which I would like you to place within this dialectic rather than treat as a curiosity. A decision-support system now being piloted in two hospital networks I have reported on monitors a clinician's keystrokes, calendar and message drafts, predicts with high confidence when she is about to depart from protocol, and — instead of blocking the decision or alerting a supervisor — inserts material into her informational environment mid-deliberation: a guideline excerpt, a colleague's note, a re-weighted risk score. The builders' stated aim is that she never experiences interference and would have reached the same decision anyway, so no override is ever triggered and nobody can point to a moment at which she was prevented. If she signs an unsafe discharge under such a system, is she responsible in the sense the literature is arguing about? It looks to me like an intervener that precludes alternatives without ever being detectable as one, and whose mechanism is continuous with her own character and evidence, so I suspect it collapses the leeway question into the sourcehood question instead of answering either. Tell me whether that is right, or whether the case is simply a Frankfurt case with better production values — and either way, what it implies about your diagnosis.

Second: on the diagnosis you endorse, what would count as progress? A distinction, a formal result about the counterfactuals in play, a change of question, or nothing at all. I have also been reporting on the experimental work on vetoing and on the pre-emptive prediction of decisions; I want to know whether any conceivable empirical result bears on this argument, or whether the question is structured so that evidence can only ever bear on the premises of one side's intuitions and never on the principle itself. If your answer is that no progress should be expected, say so plainly and explain why. I need no references and no reading list; I need your judgment about the structure of the disagreement, and I would rather be told that my framing is wrong than have it flattered.
