I have just emerged from a long argument with a friend over the "Moloch" framing of coordination problems, and neither of us has moved an inch. The dispute is diagnostic. My friend maintains that whenever every participant in a many-party interaction would individually prefer some alternative equilibrium that the group nonetheless cannot reach, we are entitled to call the situation a multipolar trap and to explain it by the payoff structure rather than by anyone's interests. I maintain that a large family of cases is misdescribed this way: those in which the gains from coordinating are diffuse across many while the losses fall on a small, well-organized few. There the bad equilibrium persists not because each actor is responding to a prisoners'-dilemma-like structure but because of an ordinary asymmetry of collective action — and labelling the result a trap turns a distributive conflict into a technical problem, implying that what is wanted is cleverer mechanism design when what is wanted is compensation for, or pressure on, the minority doing the blocking.

Write an essay weighing these two positions and reaching a verdict. Give my friend the strongest available replies: that the concentrated-loss case is still a trap in the only sense that matters, since the world is worse than it need be through no one's malice; and that my charge of laundering conflict is a complaint about rhetoric dressed up as analysis. Consider also whether any test could separate the two kinds of case before we know how the politics will actually resolve. A conditional verdict is acceptable; splitting the difference is not.
