I'm in the second year of an epistemology PhD and I've gone round in circles on the lottery and preface stuff often enough that I want to try this from the other direction. Let me just dump my actual verdicts on a pile of cases and see whether there's a principle hiding in the pattern.

1. National lottery, something like 45 million possible combinations, one ticket, mine. I flatly believe I'll lose and I'd say it out loud to a friend. Fine.
2. Department raffle, 40 tickets sold, mine is one of them. Probability I lose is about 0.975. I do NOT believe I'll lose — I keep checking the email. Not fine.
3. Same raffle, same 40 tickets, but the prize is a mug instead of £200. I happily say 'I'm not winning that' and never think about it again. Fine.
4. Defendant, evidence is purely statistical, probability they did it is 0.999. Not fine to believe. Compare: patient screened with a test that's 99% sensitive and 99% specific, in a population where the condition affects one person in five, result comes back positive — posterior about 0.96. I do believe they have the condition. Fine. So the lower-probability case gets belief and the higher one doesn't.
5. I'd put it at maybe 90% that I turned the oven off; I'm forgetful and I've left it on twice. I believe it, and I go to bed rather than driving home to check. Fine. But a stranger's testimony that I'd rate 90% reliable? I withhold. Not fine.
6. My own dissertation, 200 pages. I believe each individual claim, and I also believe at least one claim in there is wrong. Both fine, no felt tension at all.
7. A short paper by a colleague I trust, twelve claims. I believe each one, and I do NOT think one of them is false — it would be wierd to add the caveat. Fine.
8. A big, carefully edited reference work, 4,000 entries. I believe each entry and I also believe some entry is wrong. Fine. So 6 and 8 agree and 7 is the outlier, which bugs me, because 7 looks like 8 with a smaller number attached.
9. Forecast says 95% chance of rain tomorrow. I don't believe it'll rain, I say 'probably'. But a rain gauge I calibrated myself, whose readings I'd put at 95% reliable, indicating rain? I believe it.

The case I keep flip-flopping on: I'm reviewing a journal submission reporting a standard sort of finding with a p-value around 0.02, replication culture in the field being what it is. I believe the finding. And I also believe, of the batch of maybe 20 such papers I'll referee this year, that several report findings that won't hold up. Individually fine, collectively fine — and yet if you asked me to bet on the conjunction I'd refuse. Is that combination actually coherent, or am I just not noticing the contradiction?

My own tentative principle is a threshold one: believe that p iff Pr(p) is at least t, for some t close to 1. It's hopeless — 2 and 3 have identical probabilities and opposite verdicts, and any t high enough to rule out 2 also rules out 5, and arguably 6 and 9. But I don't want to just retreat to stakes-sensitivity either, because that seems to make 1 fine and 2 not fine for reasons that have nothing to do with my evidence, and 4, 5 and 9 have the stakes held roughly constant and still come out different ways.

So: give me a principle, or a small handful of competing ones, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a fresh case without first consulting my gut. For each clause, tell me which of the numbered cases forced it in. Then say what it predicts about cases I haven't listed — whether I should believe my flight won't be cancelled given the airline's 92% on-time record, and whether a historian should believe each claim in a well-sourced monograph while also believing the monograph contains at least one error. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate, and which one you'd tell me to surrender first if I want to keep the rest.

Please write the whole thing in British English.
