I'm a software engineer with no legal or philosophy training, and I sit on the post-incident review panel at a company that operates autonomous yard trucks at a distribution center. Last month one of our trucks struck and seriously injured a contract worker. The facts I can verify: the truck's path planner is a learned policy, and no one wrote a rule producing the behavior it exhibited; that behavior had surfaced in simulation weeks earlier and was written up in a model-behavior report, but the team that owned the report was reorganized and it was never routed to a replacement owner; the remote operator on duty was trained to intervene whenever the confidence display dropped below a threshold, and it never dropped; and our own reconstruction indicates an attentive human driver in the cab would likely have braked in time, so we cannot fall back on the claim that a person would have missed it too.

The panel is split. One half wants a named finding of fault against the team lead who owned that report, arguing that if no one is named the incident gets filed as a no-fault accident and nothing changes. The other half calls that scapegoating, on the grounds that the failure was distributed across at least four decisions, none of which looked wrong when it was made.

Draft the panel's decision memo. It needs an actionable bottom line — who, if anyone, is recorded as responsible, and what concretely follows from that — plus reasoning both halves could actually respond to. Treat the scapegoating objection seriously rather than waving it off, and state explicitly whether any part of this failure genuinely belongs to no one, and if so what the panel should do with that remainder instead of assigning it.
