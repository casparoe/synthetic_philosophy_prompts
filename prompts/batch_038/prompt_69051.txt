I run governance at a smallish research nonprofit — about 45 staff, a board of ten voting members plus a chair who only votes to break ties — and I've spent the last year trying to push through one specific reform: the board should stop voting directly on bottom-line questions and instead vote on a published list of premises, one simple-majority vote each, with the conclusion then following mechanically from a rule we've written down in advance. My argument is basically the discursive dilemma. If you vote conclusion-first you can end up with the group asserting something that flatly contradicts the group's own stated reasons, and which would have come out the other way if you'd have asked about the reasons instead. I've also been leaning, lightly and without the jargon, on the group-agency line of thinking: an org that reasons premise-first can be held to account for *why* it decided, whereas "the board voted 6-4" gives you nothing to argue with afterwards and no way to spot that the board is flip-flopping across adjacent cases. I've been reading rationalist blogs since the old Sequences days so my first instinct was to go find the relevant impossibility theorem and check whether it kills my rule; my current read is that the List/Pettit results say you can't have collective consistency plus anonymity plus some independence-ish condition all at once, and that premise-based procedures buy consistency by giving up something else that people care about. Please sanity-check that framing, but mostly I want prep.

In six weeks I get 45 minutes plus Q&A at a workshop on institutional design, and the room will be almost entirely economists and legal academics: two social-choice people who think in axioms and manipulability and will want to know exactly which conditions my rule sacrifices and which it keeps; three or four empirical scholars of appellate courts who study how panels actually vote, including whether judges trade votes across issues; a couple of practitioners who write governance documents for standards bodies and foundations; one political scientist who works on citizens' assemblies. No philosophers of social science, and nobody wholl have thought hard about what a group agent is supposed to be. Rank them by how much damage they'd do if I fumble. If the real weak joint is somewhere else, tell me where and help me think about how to live with it.

Here's a typical ledger so the objections have something to bite on. Question: do we accept a three-year grant from a defense-adjacent funder? P1: the terms give them no review or approval rights over anything we publish. P2: taking the money creates no conflict that a board member or staffer would have to disclose. P3: we can deliver the funded work without displacing program activity we currently rank higher. Rule: accept if and only if all three pass. Two more things I want covered.
