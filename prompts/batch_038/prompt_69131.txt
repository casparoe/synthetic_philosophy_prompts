Write a dialogue, running across two working sessions of a parliamentary drafting committee, between two members charged with fixing the franchise for an independence referendum in the region of Marre: population 4.1 million, part of the federal state of Talver for 150 years since a war of annexation, an independent kingdom for three centuries before that.

The committee must settle four questions at once, and the two members disagree on all four:

- whether the vote belongs only to residents of Marre, or also to the 900,000 Talverian citizens living elsewhere in the federation, who poll at roughly 70–30 for preserving the union;
- whether the 40,000 seasonal workers who spend nine months of the year in Marre, pay payroll tax, and hold no residence rights are enfranchised;
- whether the Kessel Valley, a 210,000-person district inside Marre that identifies with Talver and has announced it would rather stay, gets a separate vote whose outcome detaches it from Marre whatever Marre as a whole decides;
- and who is entitled to settle these questions, given that the committee is drawn from a Talverian parliament elected by a body that already excludes everyone whose inclusion is at issue.

One member defends the principle that all those affected by a decision should have a say in making it, and holds that this principle can be turned on the committee's own composition without circularity. The other defends the principle that the electorate must be the standing political community whose institutions the decision will reconstitute, and holds that assembling a fresh demos question by question destroys what makes a vote binding at all.

Neither may be a strawman, and each must take the other's strongest objection rather than the weakest. The affectedness principle must answer the charge that its threshold is indeterminate — effects run outward to foreigners, descendants, and arguably animals, with no natural stopping point — and the charge that whoever gets to operationalize it effectively picks the referendum's result, since polling shows 52–48 for independence among Marre's residents but 60–40 against once the diaspora is counted. The bounded-demos view must answer the charge that its unit is nothing but the sediment of a war fought 150 years ago and enjoys no moral privilege over any rival unit, and the charge that if self-determination licenses Marre to leave Talver, the Kessel Valley's claim to leave Marre is the same claim, so the view is either inconsistent or a defense of whichever map happens to be current. Have them test whether any device could satisfy both — for instance the double-majority rules some federations require for constitutional change, a majority of voters overall together with a majority of constituent units — and close with one of them conceding something specific rather than letting the argument trail off. Aim for 1,200–1,800 words.
