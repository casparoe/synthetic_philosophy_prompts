I spent thirty-one years adjudicating disability claims for a mid-sized insurer, and since retiring I have been working through epistemology on my own — no coursework, no degree, just library books and a great deal of walking. One puzzle has held me for about a year now. I understand it is called the dogmatism paradox, and I want to say plainly which puzzle I mean: the modern one that begins with a piece of knowledge and ends with an apparent licence to disregard any evidence that arrives later, not the older complaint that a sceptic who insists nothing can be known has thereby smuggled in a dogma.

Here is the puzzle as I have it, in my own words, and please tell me if I have misstated it. I know that the claimant's appeal form was postmarked on the eleventh, because I stamped it and logged it myself. From that I can reason as follows: any evidence I might later run into which seemed to show that it was not postmarked on the eleventh would have to be misleading, since I already know how things stand. If I can reason my way from what I know to that further claim, then I know it too. And if I know that some piece of evidence would be misleading, it seems I would be within my rights to set it aside unread when it turns up. So my knowledge appears to entitle me, in advance, to ignore whatever the world goes on to tell me — which is plainly not something knowledge should entitle anyone to. Every step looks acceptable and the conclusion is intolerable, and I cannot see where to cut.

Out of my old work I built a case of my own, and it is the one I most want your requirements tested against. Call her Dana. Dana is an emergency physician. She examines a patient, runs the best test available, and comes to know — on excellent evidence, not merely to believe — that the patient has condition C. She also helped design her department's audit, and so she knows a standing statistic: when a chart like hers is sent out for specialist review, the reviewer's recommendation agrees with the treating physician's roughly three times in ten, and in the disagreements the reviewer has characteristically been moved by a feature of the chart that everyone concedes to be irrelevant. Dana therefore knows that the review note will recommend against C, and knows that it is likelier to be wrong than right. She does not open it. Four questions I want an account to be able to answer:

1. Was Dana permitted not to open the note?
2. If she had opened it and read a confident recommendation against C, would she have been permitted to keep believing C?
3. Do the answers shift if the note turns out to be right — and if they do, how can they, given that she had to decide before anyone could know which way it would go?
4. Suppose the three-in-ten figure came from eleven charts in one bad quarter, and Dana knows that about her own audit, and knows her department has a habit of comforting itself with small samples. Does that cancel her permission, confirm it, or leave it in some condition I have no word for?

I have one further demand, which I suspect is the interesting one. Whatever an account says Dana may do, it should say whether Dana can tell from inside her own situation that she may do it. A permission that can only be conferred by someone standing outside — an onlooker who already knows she happened to be right — looks to me like no permission at all, since Dana has to make the decision while she is in the middle of it and not after.

What I am after is not a survey and not a solution. It is the specification: the full list of requirements any adequate account of this puzzle must satisfy before it earns the right to call itself one. Treat it as acceptance criteria handed to an engineer. Please write it in these sections, in this order:

**1. Restatement** — about 200 words. Set out the puzzle in your own terms, Dana included, and point out where my version above is loose, ambiguous, or already presupposing something disputed. If my four questions are the wrong four questions, replace them and say why.

**2. The requirements** — about 900 to 1100 words, numbered. This is the core. Give each a short name, a one-sentence statement of what an adequate account has to deliver, and the case or consideration that motivates it, drawn where possible from Dana, from my postmark, or from a further case you invent for the purpose. Mark each as non-negotiable, or as a demand peculiar to one side of the dispute that could be surrendered without ruining the project. Do not flinch from including requirements that pull against one another; I expect several.

**3. Pressure points** — about 350 words. State as sharply as you can which combinations cannot all be satisfied. I want these in the form "if you hold to A and B, you cannot also have C," and I want to know for each whether the clash is a genuine impossibility or merely a costly trade.

**4. The load-bearing requirement** — about 200 words. Name the single requirement that, taken seriously, disqualifies the largest share of otherwise attractive approaches. Explain where its leverage comes from, and then say what happens if someone simply refuses it: does the puzzle dissolve, or does the refusal cost more than the puzzle did?

**5. Rough scorecard** — about 250 words, and a table is welcome here. Group the main families of response into three to five clusters described in plain language, and mark for each which requirements it meets, which it strains, and which it violates outright.

**6. What a solution would have to look like** — about 200 words. Sketch the shape an adequate account would take, and name the largest hole still left in it.

Two ground rules. First, no citations, no bibliographies, no reading lists, and no philosophers' names anywhere in this: I have no way to chase them down, and I care about the structure of the problem rather than who holds which view. Second, whenever a requirement rests on a technical notion — closure, factivity, defeat, evidence-responsiveness, and so forth — gloss it in a clause instead of assuming I already have it.
