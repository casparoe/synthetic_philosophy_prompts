Between 2006 and 2013 the question of how many Iraqis the war had killed produced at least five defensible answers differing by more than an order of magnitude, and the dispute never converged. For reference: the Iraq Body Count project, which tallies only deaths corroborated in press, hospital, morgue, or official reports, had documented on the order of fifty thousand civilian deaths by mid-2006; the Iraq Family Health Survey — 9,345 households, run by Iraqi ministries with the WHO and published in early 2008 — estimated 151,000 violent deaths for the same window, March 2003 through June 2006; the 2006 Lancet cluster survey estimated 654,965 excess deaths for that window, 601,027 of them violent; a 2007 poll by Opinion Research Business put violent deaths above a million; and the WikiLeaks Iraq War Logs, the coalition's own incident records for 2004–2009, list 109,032 deaths, 66,081 of them classified civilian. Both extremes were presented by their authors as the conservative figure.

I would like that episode analyzed as a test of Johan Galtung's distinction between direct and structural violence — harm that is event-like and attributable to an actor, versus harm built into the way a social arrangement distributes avoidable death — and of the operational choices that follow from it. Please work through five things in order.

First, the decomposition. Take the gap between roughly fifty thousand and roughly 655,000 and sort its sources: the counting rule (battle deaths versus all excess mortality); the evidence rule (incident documentation versus statistical extrapolation from a small sample of households); the baseline (which pre-invasion mortality rate the excess is measured against, in a country whose baseline was itself shaped by earlier wars and a decade of sanctions); the unit of analysis and its failure modes (cluster sampling along roads an armed escort could actually reach, versus households that dissolve or split after a death, which biases the other way); and the denominator. Say how much of the gap each factor plausibly carries, and which of these disagreements is about violence as opposed to about statistics the two sides could in principle settle between them.

Second, the lens. The narrow counts track Galtung's direct violence; the excess-mortality approach tracks something much closer to his structural violence, since most of the difference lies in deaths from degraded water, power, food distribution, and clinical care rather than in gunfire. Does Iraq vindicate the expansion — is a toll that ignores indirect deaths simply not a measure of the harm done — or does it expose the standing objection that once violence covers everything that shortens life, the concept loses its agent, its boundary, and its critical bite? Note that the structural reading of Iraq does have candidate agents, which many applications of the concept lack; say whether that rescues it.

Third, the comparative stakes. The datasets underwriting the claim that war is in long-run decline count battle deaths only, with thresholds (a minimum of twenty-five battle-related deaths a year for an armed conflict, a thousand for a war) and per-capita normalization. Iraq is thus a case in which the counting rule and the headline verdict on global violence are entangled. On an excess-mortality accounting the Second Congo War — where the International Rescue Committee's surveys put the toll near 5.4 million, overwhelmingly from disease and malnutrition, a figure later contested on the grounds that the pre-war baseline had been set too low — is the deadliest conflict since 1945; on a battle-death accounting it is a mid-tier war. What does Iraq, where both accounting rules were applied to the same deaths over the same years by the same survey method, tell us about whether the decline thesis is tracking something real or tracking its own definition?

Fourth, per capita versus absolute, and whether there is a fact of the matter. Iraq's population was growing across the period, so normalization moves the numbers; and the counterfactual embedded in "excess" is a political hypothesis rather than a measurement. State plainly what kind of claim "the war killed N Iraqis" is: one fact under-described by imperfect instruments, a family of distinct facts indexed to counting rules, or something else — and if the second, whether the debate was ever about a quantity at all, and what it was about instead.

Finally, tell me where the Galtung framework goes silent. It was built to widen moral attention, not to adjudicate between a documentation-based tally and a cluster-sample extrapolation, and I want an honest account of what it cannot help with here — including why the coalition's own incident logs, a documentary record free of the media's coverage problems, landed much nearer the body counts than the surveys. Do not force the theory to speak where it has nothing to say. I am not after sources or further reading; I want the analysis itself.
