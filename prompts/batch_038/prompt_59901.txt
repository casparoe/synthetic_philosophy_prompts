I run a small reading group that grew out of our local rationalist meetup, and for the past few weeks we have been working through the opening chapters of Lewis's *Convention* (1969). We got stuck on a question that I suspect has a clean answer but that we could not settle by argument: whether Lewis's clauses are *necessary* for a convention, or whether they merely describe how conventions characteristically get started. Specifically, we kept generating cases in which the underlying coordination problem seems to have evaporated while the regularity itself marches on, and each time one member of the group would insist that we had smuggled a payoff back in through the back door — a small reputational cost to deviating, an unspoken awkwardness, the bargaining problem of choosing which new arrangement to adopt. That objection may well be right, but I would like to find out whether it can be made to fail, so I am asking for a case built to order rather than a survey of the literature.

Here are the specifications. Construct a single thought experiment, one paragraph of scenario and no more, set in an ordinary realistic institution of fewer than twenty people who interact repeatedly and non-anonymously — a department, a clinic roster, a volunteer company, a family firm — no science fiction, no hypothetical omniscient observers. The scenario must contain a regularity in behavior that originated as the solution to a genuine two-sided coordination problem with two equilibria, one of which was payoff-domininant, and that was fixed purely by precedent rather than by any agreement, vote, or announcement. The originating coordination problem must then be extinguished *completely* by an exogenous change — not reduced, not made improbable, but eliminated — and it must be common knowledge among all members that the two alternatives are now exactly equivalent for everyone, in the strong sense that a survey was taken, everyone has seen the results, and every respondent reported strict indifference. On top of that, stipulate away every escape hatch I listed above: no formal rule and no administrative lock-in; no sanctions, disapproval, or even raised eyebrows; no reputational or signaling value attaching either to conformity or to deviation; and any single member may unilaterally move the practice at zero cost, with it being common knowledge that a proposal to switch would be accepted without argument, so that there is no second-order coordination problem about whether or how to reopen the question. Finally, despite all of that, the regularity must remain stable indefinitely, and each member's own reason for continuing to conform must be nothing other than their expectation that the others will conform. Please also put one recently arrived member in the scene who asks why things are done this way, so that the transmission mechanism is visible rather than assumed. State up front what the case is meant to show, since that fixes what counts as cheating: the point is to test whether convention-like stability can outlive the coordination problem that produced it, and a scenario that simply asserts the verdict rather than generating it from the theories is a failure.

Then do the audit. Walk through Lewis's conditions one at a time — the recurrent situation, near-universal conformity, near-universal expectation of conformity, the rough agreement of preferences over combinations of actions, the clause requiring that each member prefer others' conformity conditional on general conformity, and the arbitrariness clause requiring an alternative regularity that would serve equally well — and say for each whether it holds in your case and why, paying particular attention to whether strict indifference kills the conditional-preference clause outright or leaves it merely vacuous, and whether the arbitrariness clause can still be satisfied once no alternative would serve *better* than the incumbent because none would serve differently at all. Next, classify the same regularity under a Bicchieri-style account that distinguishes social norms from mere descriptive regularities by asking whether conformity is conditional on normative expectations as well as empirical ones, and say explicitly which category your case lands in and what that implies about whether the members have any reason to keep it going. Then say what a purely dynamic account — one that models the population as learning from precedent without assuming common knowledge — would predict about the case's long-run stability, including whether it should drift, snap back, or persist forever, and whether that prediction is testably different from the Lewisian one. Finish with the honest part: identify the single stipulation that is doing the work, describe what happens to the verdict if you relax it, and give the strongest available reply from a defender of Lewis — for instance, that exact indifference is an unstable idealization that any perturbation destroys, or that common knowledge of everyone's indifference is itself enough to regenerate a conditional preference for conformity, or that your scenario is not coherent because two of its stipulations cannot both be true. If you conclude that the specification set is unsatisfiable, prove that rather than quietly weakening one of the constraints, and tell me which constraint you would drop first to get a case that is both coherent and interesting.
