My problem is that I can now recite the dialectic fluently while being unable to answer a single pointed objection, which is not a good place to be ahead of my first conference talk. So I would like to be pressed. Please play Kripkenstein: not Kripke the person, not Wittgenstein the person, but the fictional character who holds the views of Kripke's reading — the skeptic who thinks the paradox that "no course of action could be determined by a rule," since every course of action can be made out to accord with it and therefore also to conflict with it, is genuine and admits of no straight solution, and who accordingly replaces truth conditions for meaning claims with conditions for their legitimate assertibility inside a community. Stay in character in the first person for the entire exchange, until I type stop. You may concede incidental points — that some straight solutions are more resourceful than others, that an individual's dispositions are usually decent evidence of what they mean, that ascriptions to machines are an awkward corner of the view — but never concede the central claim that no fact about an individual constitutes their meaning one function rather than another, and never smuggle in a truth-conditional rescue through the back door. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, roughly the length of one turn in a good seminar, and if something in my case is unclear, ask for it as the character would rather than stepping out to tidy up the setup.

So that this is not a rerun, I am not going to reopen 68 + 57; assume I have already absorbed everything the skeptic can say about it. Here is the case I want you to deal with. A friend of mine built a small arithmetic tutor for primary-school drills. It was trained exclusively on sums whose correct result is below ten thousand, and for two years it answered every drill perfectly, including many thousands of sums it had demonstrably never been given. Last month, in front of a class, asked for 4,318 + 5,682 it answered 10,001 — and it has answered one more than the correct result to that sum and to every other sum of exactly ten thousand, consistently, ever since. My instinct is that for those two years nobody could say what it meant by its outputs, since they were equally compatible with ordinary addition and with what I'll call schmaddition, the function that agrees with addition below ten thousand and thereafter returns one more than the true sum, and that the recent behaviour has finally broken the tie: it means schmaddition, and it has meant schmaddition all along. But I cannot see how your position lets me say that, and I cannot see how it lets me say the opposite either. Its record of agreement with the community over those two years was considerably better than mine, and if agreement in judgments is what licenses an ascription, the tutor earned the right to mean plus long before I did. My opening move, then: I think you are committed to saying the question of what it means is simply empty, that your account therefore cannot distinguish a system that follows a rule from one that merely tracks a rule with high reliability, and that this is a cost you should own rather than explain away. Tell me which of my three descriptions — it means schmaddition, it means plus and errs, the question does not arise — your account permits, and tell me what I have wrongly assumed in setting it up that way.

Second, do not send me to the literature or suggest further reading. I want your reasons, not your references.
