I am preparing a short newsletter piece for a learned society that is about to rewrite the rules governing its own officer elections, and I have been working from a passage in a trade-press book on electoral reform. My supervisor read the passage and said only that it is in the right general area but that she would not want the society to rely on it; she declined to say more, on the grounds that I should learn to check this sort of thing myself. The passage reads as follows:

> Voting theorists like to say that choosing an electoral system is a matter of choosing among different varieties of dishonesty. The reason is a theorem proved by Allan Gibbard and Mark Satterthwaite, working together in the mid-1970s. It says that whenever there are three or more candidates, every voting rule that produces a single winner is manipulable: there is some configuration of voter preferences in which someone does better for herself by submitting a ballot that ranks a candidate above one she genuinely prefers. The only rule that escapes is a dictatorship, in which one voter's ballot settles everything — and a dictatorship is not a voting rule at all — which is why the theorem is so often paraphrased as showing that there is no such thing as a fair way to rank three candidates. Note what the theorem does not depend on. It says nothing whatever about how ballots are counted, and so it applies with full force to first-past-the-post, to runoffs, to instant-runoff voting, and to point systems such as the Borda count. Reformers have consequently stopped treating manipulability as a criterion and now choose systems on other grounds: simplicity, proportionality, whether the Condorcet winner wins. Since manipulation cannot be eliminated, the practical aim is to make it unattractive or hard — to adopt a system in which a voter gains little by lying, or cannot readily work out what the profitable lie would be. That is the case for approval voting, in which each voter simply marks the candidates she finds acceptable. With no ranking to misrepresent, there is nothing to manipulate; and because it is not a ranked system, the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem does not touch it.

Could you audit this for me?

1. Work through it claim by claim and sort each into three columns: what a specialist in social choice theory would accept as stated; what is a defensible popular simplification, losing precision but not truth; and what is wrong. Where a claim is wrong, give me the minimal correction — the condition the theorem actually requires, or the distinction the sentence runs together.

2. Attend especially to the closing remarks on approval voting. There seem to be two different claims available here, namely that the theorem's hypotheses do not cover approval voting, and that approval voting is immune to manipulation, and I suspect the passage slides between them. Tell me whether that is right, which of the two claims if either is true, and what a voter casting an approval ballot can and cannot misrepresent.

3. The passage lists first-past-the-post, runoffs, instant-runoff voting, and the Borda count as all falling under the theorem. I believe the list is right but the stated reason for it is not. Explain the gap, and say what the theorem's conclusion does and does not turn on — in particular, what "three or more candidates" is standing in for.

4. Set out as cleanly as you can the genuine escape routes from the theorem: which assumptions can be weakened or dropped, and at what cost. Then say which of those routes a society of a few thousand members, choosing a rule for electing its own officers, could plausibly take.

5. Tell me how much of the author's practical recommendation survives the corrections. If preferring a system in which manipulation is unprofitable or hard to identify is defensible advice, say what does support it, given that the theorem appears to be doing less work in the passage than the passage implies.

6. Close by rewriting the passage in the same popular register and at roughly the same length, so that a specialist could sign every sentence of it.

Please do not supply a bibliography or a list of further reading; I will handle references myself, and I am interested in your reasoning about the content. If some part of the passage turns out to be defensible after all, say so plainly rather than erring on the side of criticism.
