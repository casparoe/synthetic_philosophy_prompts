I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, and this term I am teaching a section of an upper-level undergraduate seminar in political economy: 22 students, one 110-minute meeting, a room with movable tables and two whiteboards, and no laptops or phones permitted during the session. The students have read Mises's original argument that rational economic calculation is impossible without genuine market prices for capital goods, Lange's trial-and-error proposal for a central board posting parametric prices and instructing managers to equate marginal cost to price, and Hayek's reply that the decisive knowledge is dispersed, local, time-sensitive, and frequently unstatable. On paper they follow all three. In discussion, two things go badly wrong. First, they collapse the debate into the historical record — several have said some version of "the Soviet experiment failed, so Hayek won" — and they cannot see that what was tried there was not Lange's scheme. Second, they run together three distinct complaints: that calculation is impossible in principle, that the required knowledge cannot practically be gathered, and that even if it could be gathered, the agents holding it would misreport it. I want an activity that forces these three apart by making students live through each one separately.

Please design a playable exercise under the following constraints.

1. Present it to the class as an operations or logistics exercise; do not reveal that it is the calculation debate until the debrief.
2. Give me a concrete toy economy — three or four goods, a small input–output table, initial endowments, and a demand profile — with numbers I can write on a whiteboard and that students can work out by hand in a few minutes. Please check the arithmetic yourself and confirm that a first-best allocation exists and is identifiable under full information. This matters to me: I want to tell a skeptical student that the game has not built Mises's impossibility thesis into its rules, so that whatever goes wrong goes wrong for epistemic or practical reasons rather than by construction.
3. Sort the class into a planning board, several production units, and a demand side. Give at least some participants private local information that is genuinely theirs alone, including a class of "hunch" items that a participant can act on but cannot express in the numerical form the board is permitted to receive. All upward communication must pass through a physically constrained channel: fixed-size slips, one collection per round, no talking across teams, and a one-round lag on whatever the board sends back down.
4. Run at least three rounds that differ only in the coordination mechanism — direct quantitative reporting, a Lange-style iteration in which the board posts prices and units respond to them, and a round in which a local shock is delivered mid-round to a single participant who has no obvious way to transmit its content. Keep a running score in points rather than money, and state exactly how points are earned and forfeited.
5. Design it so that the planning board can win. Specify the patterns of play under which centralized coordination outperforms the price rounds, and do not stack the rules against it; a demonstration whose outcome I have guaranteed in advance will persuade nobody in that room.

Then supply a minute-by-minute timeline; a debrief that maps particular moments of play onto the calculation / knowledge / incentive distinction and onto the difference between treating equilibrium prices as a datum and treating competition as a discovery procedure, framed as questions I should ask rather than points I should assert; and a candid account of what predictably goes wrong the first time this is run, including what happens if the board turns out to be unusually competent and what I should say if it wins outright. Finally, be frank about what the exercise cannot reproduce — in particular that the local knowledge here is knowledge I wrote onto cards in advance, which is itself an articulation of precisely what Hayek claimed could not be articulated — and tell me how much of that damage a debrief can repair. If 22 students is the wrong number for the mechanism you have in mind, tell me the range at which it works and what to cut first.
