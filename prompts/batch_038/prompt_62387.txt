The episode is the confabulation protocol from Michael Gazzaniga's commissurotomy research at Caltech in the 1970s and 1980s, and the lens is Moore's paradox in both of its standard forms: the omissive (asserting that p while disclaiming the belief that p) and the commissive (asserting that p while acknowledging the belief that not-p).

Take the following as given rather than reconstruct it. With the corpus callosum severed and stimuli confined to one visual half-field, information reaches only one hemisphere, while speech remains under left-hemisphere control. When an instruction to walk was presented to a patient's right hemisphere, he rose and headed for the door; asked why he was getting up, he did not plead ignorance but supplied a reason compatible with what his left hemisphere knew — that he was going to fetch a Coke from the refrigerator. In a related matching task, a chicken claw presented to the left hemisphere and a snow scene to the right led the right hand to select a chicken and the left hand a shovel; asked to account for the shovel, the patient produced an explanation tying it to the chicken — cleaning out the shed — with no apparent access to the snow scene. In neither case was he lying, hedging, or visibly perplexed, and the reports came with ordinary confidence.

Please then do the following. First, fix the mapping: identify the referent of the first-person pronoun in any Moore-paradoxical rendering of these utterances, considering at least the whole patient, the left hemisphere, and the interpreter system conceived as a module, and say for each what the report amounts to — Moore-paradoxical, simply false, absurd, or none of these. The absurdity is usually located in sincere assertion, and the familiar explanation runs through the idea that asserting p presents the speaker as believing p; assess whether that mechanism is engaged at all when the utterance is an interpretive construction rather than the expression of a standing belief.

Second, run the test in both directions. Which features of the protocol does Moore's paradox genuinely illuminate — the unassertability of certain self-reports, the special authority of first-person present-tense avowals on which the paradox trades? Which does it miss — the immediacy and confidence of the confabulation, the fact that no participant experiences the utterance as paradoxical, only the experimenters as false?

Third, say whether the protocol is a counterexample to the claim that Moore-paradoxical belief is impossible. Can the patient be described as believing he is going to get a Coke and, in another respect, not believing it? What conditions of doxastic integration must a state meet for the impossibility claim to bite, and does invoking such conditions rescue the theory or hollow it out?

Fourth, address the mereological worry: whether treating a hemisphere or a module as a believer, and so as a host of Moore-paradoxical content, illicitly assigns psychological predicates to parts of an animal rather than to the animal itself. If it does, state whether the episode then falls outside the theory's jurisdiction, which would make it a point about scope rather than an anomaly.

Finally, be explicit where the theory is silent — degrees of belief, subpersonal states, the gap between having a reason and reporting one — without pressing it to speak, and end with a verdict: does the protocol confirm, strain, or bypass Moore's paradox, and what would a theorist need to add (a unity-of-subject clause, a restriction to avowals proper, a shift from the metaphysics of belief to the norms of assertion) to accommodate it, at what cost?
