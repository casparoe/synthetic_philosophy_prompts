I'm applying for a one-year fellowship in the conceptual foundations of AI safety, and I'd like a two-page proposal built on a distinction I think the field runs together. Safety-critical deployment seems to demand worst-case guarantees — a system should not deceive, sabotage, or accumulate power even when an adversary or an unusual environment is actively eliciting that behavior — yet essentially all the evidence we have is average-case: benchmark scores, red-teaming hit rates, evaluation over a sampled distribution of prompts. Complexity theory has a principled way to bridge the two, worst-case-to-average-case reductions that exploit random self-reducibility, and my working hypothesis is that no analogue is available for trained systems, partly because such a system can influence which inputs it encounters in deployment and so corrupt the very distribution over which the average-case claim is stated.

Please write the proposal so that it states the bridging question precisely enough that a negative answer would itself count as a result; explains why the two obvious strategies — evaluate far more exhaustively, or prove properties of the training procedure — each assume what is at issue; sets out what I would actually do month by month, including the simplest toy setting in which the analogy could be tested and, if it fails, broken; says what a successful deliverable would look like to people who write safety cases, and what would show that I was chasing a false analogy; and closes with a candid assessment of what might make the project too large for a single year. No bibliography or reference list — I will supply citations myself.
