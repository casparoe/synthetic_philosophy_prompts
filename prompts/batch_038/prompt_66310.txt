I have run a small reading group out of the back room of a bookshop for about six years — a retired schoolteacher, two nurses, a man who drives a tow truck, and me, and I should say plainly that none of us has any academic training in philosophy. This month's topic was knowledge and evidence, and in preparing for it I came across a puzzle I believe is called the dogmatist's paradox; I could not afterwards find the original source, but I have been turning it over ever since, and I have written out the steps in my own words as follows. If I genuinely know something, then it is true. If it is true, then anything that later appears to count against it is misleading. And it cannot be unreasonable of me to refuse to listen to evidence that I already know misleads me. So my knowledge seems to license me, here and now, to decide in advance that I will not listen to whatever anyone later brings me — which is precisely the closed-mindedness that all of us, including me, would call a fault. I want to sit with that rather than solve it, and I have decided the best way to sit with it is a poem, because a poem can hold two contradictory things in the mouth at once without having to choose.

What I would like is a pantoum of five quatrains: the second and fourth lines of each stanza recur as the first and third lines of the next, so that every line comes back once in a new position, and the final stanza folds the two remaining lines of the opening back in so the poem ends where it began. The form is not decoration here; it is the whole point. A line that reads as an ordinary, even comforting observation the first time — a person stating what she knows about someone she loves — should read as something closer to a verdict, or a door being locked, when it returns a stanza later in the slot where decisions get made. I would like one concrete thing known, not an abstraction and not the letter p: a woman who is certain her brother did not do what he is accused of, or a doctor who is certain the water in her town is safe, or anything of that kind, provided it is the sort of thing a person could honestly claim to know and could later be shown documents about. The technical vocabulary of the argument should stay out of the poem; the words knowledge, evidence, true, and misleading are welcome, but I do not want the language of credences or closure principles or any of the machinery. Above all, no stanza may announce which step is the bad one. Both intuitions — that the reasoning is impeccable, and that the person it produces is monstrous — have to survive to the last quatrain and stand next to each other.

After the poem, please add a short note in prose of your own, without any references or suggested reading, since I would rather have your judgement than a list. In that note I would like you to separate what is settled about this puzzle from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it seriously, and to say clearly which of the steps my poem leans on hardest and whether that step is one specialists agree about. Where there are several live ways of escaping the conclusion, describe them in plain language and present them as options that are still argued over, not as a settled answer; please do not write as though the paradox has been dissolved, and if the shape of the poem implies a resolution that is in fact disputed, say so and point to the line where it happens. Finally, tell me where the poem takes liberties — anywhere it simplifies an argument, sides with a contested reading, or lets a nice image carry weight that the reasoning will not bear.
