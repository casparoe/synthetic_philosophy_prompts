Patient had an anoxic injury after an arrest, no prospect of meaningful recovery, not brain dead, so the plan was withdrawal in theatre, five-minute standoff, declaration, retrieval. I took notes while it was going on because I genuinely couldn't follow who was winning, and now I'd like someone to go through it move by move. Neither of them would take my side of anything and I don't have a clinical background, so I'm not telling you which one I thought was on stronger ground — I don't know.

Here's roughly how it went, in order. **(1)** Surgeon: circulatory death is the easy one, honestly — asystole, we watch for five minutes to rule out autoressucitation, confirm, declare, retrieve. No philosophy required. **(2)** Consultant: you declare death because the cessation is *permanent*, and it's permanent only because of what you do next. At minute four you could still have restarted the heart. So it wasn't irreversible, and you've written your own intentions into the definition of death — that's circular. **(3)** Surgeon: irreversible is the wrong test. Permanence is enough: it *won't* be reversed, the family and the team decided it shouldn't be, and nobody intends to. On your standard a patient with a DNR who arrests on the ward is never actually dead either, since we could always have resuscitated. Do you want to say that about every expected death in this building? **(4)** Consultant: grant you permanence, then explain the rest. You give heparin and a vasodilator before death is declared, sometimes you cannulate beforehand, all of it for the organs rather than the patient. And in thoracoabdominal NRP you clamp the arch vessels and start the heart going again inside a body you've just called a corpse. That isn't confirming permanent cessation, that's manufacturing it. **(5)** Surgeon: NRP happens after declaration, and the clamping is there precisely to guarantee zero cerebral perfusion — so it honours the whole-brain criterion rather than flouting it. A heart pumping in the abdomen doesn't make a person alive if the brain is excluded and destroyed. **(6)** Consultant: there it is, you've swapped criteria mid-argument — you came in on circulation and you're now defending the procedure by appeal to brain death. If the real criterion is irreversible destruction of the brain, say so, and then tell me why you need the five minutes at all, and why the circulatory test is anything more than a proxy that can come apart from the thing it proxies. And the proxy business cuts the other way too: brain-dead bodies have been documented gestating a foetus for months, healing wounds, mounting immune responses, growing. Organism-level integration plainly doesn't require a working brain, so the whole-brain criterion is a claim about personhood wearing a lab coat. **(7)** Surgeon: and your higher-brain view has its own horror — it makes patients in permanent vegetative states dead, which no trust on earth would implement, and it makes whether someone is dead depend on a contested philosophical thesis. Circulatory and whole-brain criteria are at least publicly checkable at a bedside in five minutes by a registrar. Also, be honest about the cost of your purity: if you can't declare here, you can't retrieve hearts, and people on the list die. Own that number. **(8)** Consultant: the number is real but it cannot be what *makes* it death. If the supply of transplantable organs settled whether someone had died, the dead donor rule would be protecting nobody — the rule only has content if death is determined independently of whether we want the organs. So either show me an independent criterion that's satisfied at minute five, or admit this is policy management with a bedside ritual attached.
