Contemporary debate over the demandingness objection to consequentialism tends to treat that objection as a single challenge, when in fact at least three structurally distinct complaints are usually run together under the label:

1. The cost complaint, familiar from Singer-style arguments: what consequentialism requires an agent to give up — resources, projects, options — is so great that the requirement exceeds anything morality could plausibly ask.
2. The fair-share complaint: the objection is not really about the magnitude of the cost but about its distribution. In a world of partial compliance, the conscientious agent is made to absorb the shortfall produced by everyone else's non-compliance, and so is asked to do far more than their fair share of what is collectively required.
3. The integrity complaint, in the spirit of Williams: the problem is not that too much is asked but that the asking itself is corrosive, because satisfying the requirement obliges the agent to hold their own deepest commitments at arm's length as items to be weighed impartially, thereby dissolving the standpoint from which they act.

A standard consequentialist reply to the first complaint invokes the distinction between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure: the theory specifies what makes acts right, but it need not, and on consequentialist grounds often should not, recommend that agents deliberate by applying that criterion directly.

I would like a careful explanation of the following conceptual problem: these three complaints appear to call for mutually incompatible replies, such that a move which neutralizes one tends to aggravate another. In developing your explanation, please address:

- Whether the criterion-of-rightness/decision-procedure distinction genuinely removes the cost, or merely relocates it, given that the theory still endorses a particular deliberative disposition whose cultivation and maintenance carry costs, and given that the choice among dispositions is itself to be assessed consequentially.
- Why the same distinction tends to make the fair-share complaint harder to answer rather than easier. If the theory can tell a complying agent to stop short of the optimum, it seems to concede that there is some appropriate stopping point; but then what fixes that point, and can it be fixed consequentially without reintroducing the very impartial calculus the agent was told not to run?
- Why the integrity complaint resists both replies. Explain what makes it a claim about the structure of agency rather than a disguised claim about aggregate sacrifice, and what follows if that is right: whether a consequentialist can meet it by prescribing indirect or self-effacing deliberation, or whether prescribing such a disposition is itself an exercise of the standpoint the objection objects to.

Conclude by assessing how much of a unified demandingness objection survives once these three are prised apart, and whether a consequentialist theorist is better served by resisting the separation or by facing the three separately — noting what, if anything, is lost either way.
