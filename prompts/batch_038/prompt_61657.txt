I'm three years into a philosophy PhD, mostly intergenerational ethics but co-supervised by someone in the economics department, and I'm workshopping a chapter draft in a few weeks to a mixed crowd — a couple of climate economists, some people who work on population ethics, and one guy who does cost-benefit analysis for a government appraisal body and who has already told me he thinks philosophers who write about discounting have never opened an actual guidance manual. The position I'm defending is a hybrid and I want it stress-tested before I stand up and say it out loud. Roughly: the Ramsey-formula framing is a category mistake. Everyone writes the social discount rate as δ + ηg and then treats the sum as one number to apply to a project, but on my reading the two terms aren't doing the same kind of thing. δ is meant to be a claim about the moral irrelevance of mere temporal location, and I hold the standard line that it should be zero — anything nonzero is just myopia dressed up as a parameter. But ηg isn't a discount rate and never was: its a distributive weight, a claim about how much less a unit of wellbeing counts when it lands on someone who's already better off, and it belongs in the aggregation step, not in a rate at which we trade present benefits against future ones. So publishing "the" discount rate and applying it uniformly is smuggling an interpersonal-distribution question inside a scalar and then pretending the scalar was about time. And I want to hold a further claim: the declining rates you get by averaging over uncertain growth paths don't smuggle pure time preference back in, because that's just η operating under uncertainty about g. On my view any genuinely nonzero long-run rate has to come from δ, and δ is never justified — which means my position has a real-world consequence, namely that the practice of writing one rate on the front page of an appraisal is not merely imprecise but incoherent.

So: what would count against it? Give me the map of exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which of these a sympathetic defender would themselves concede as counting against them rather than waving off. I already know about the two obvious pressures — that actual humans and actual asset prices reveal nonzero pure time preference, so I'm either condemning all of humanity to irrationality or ignoring the evidence, and that if δ = 0 with η above one and growing consumption the intertemporal objective may just not converge, which would mean δ is doing mathematical work rather than ethical work and my "category mistake" charge misfires. I want the ones I haven't thought of. Specifically: does the shadow-price-of-capital line hurt me, i.e. the claim that even a fully committed δ = 0 theorist ends up appraising public projects at near-market rates once you account for the private investment that gets crowded out and convert it into its consumption equivalent — if that's right, is my thesis about practice falsified, or just consistent with it? And is the δ/η split empirically empty: if for any stream of consequences I can match the numbers of (δ = 0, η = 2) with some (δ = 1%, η = 1), then my distinction isn't a discovery about what the terms mean, it's a relabeling, and I've insulated the view by making it unfalsifiable. Say plainly whether that's true and whether it would be a problem for a claim of this kind — I can live with being told my chapter is a notational preference. Also: does δ = 0 plus η > 0 commit me to the fanaticism stuff, absurd savings rates and trading enormous far-future gains against severe near-term harm, and can I resist that without putting δ back in?

Finally, tell me what my reading predicts about how appraisal is actually done that the "it's all one number" reading doesn't predict, and where I'd be embarrassed. I'm thinking of cases where bodies discount health effects at a different rate than money, or use declining schedules, or refuse to discount certain things at all — if those are all better explained by people just being inconsistent, my coherence charge loses its bite. I'd also like to know which of my three claims (δ should be zero; ηg isn't a rate; uncertainty results don't reintroduce δ) is load-bearing for the other two, because if the chapter has to give one up I'd rather pick which one now. No reading list and no references please — I've got the literature, what I don't have is a clear sense of where I'm actually exposed. Argue it out rather than pointing me at people who already have.
