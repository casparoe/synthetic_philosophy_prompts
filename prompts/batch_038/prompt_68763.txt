I am at work on a novel whose fourth section is set in a university town on the night after a funeral. Two of the mourners — I would rather you named them than have me do it; keep their biographies deliberately thin — find themselves alone in a kitchen at two in the morning and fall into an argument about Moore's paradox. The man they have just buried is the reason. In the last weeks of his illness he said to each of them, sincerely and with no apparent distress, some version of "I am going to die, but I don't believe it," and each of them has been privately settling, for weeks, whether he was confused.

The argument I need is between two genuine and defensible diagnoses of what is wrong with a sentence of that shape, and it must be argued to a standstill rather than to a synthesis.

The first speaker holds that the trouble lies in the belief. Belief is constitutively directed at the truth — to believe that p is to hold p true — and so no integrated, coherent mind can hold p together with the denial that it holds p; the absurdity is therefore a fact about the mind and not about speaking. On this view the dying man's sentence must be explained away, and the only two explanations on offer are equivocation (in grief, and in dying, "believe" slides toward something nearer to "have taken in") and fragmentation (the belief that he is dying sits in one compartment, sealed off from the compartment that runs his speech and his plans).

The second holds that the trouble lies in the act. The content of the sentence is perfectly consistent and nothing prevents anyone from holding it, whether in self-deception or in fragments; what makes the sentence absurd is that assertion presents itself as knowledge and as an expression of belief, so that to assert it is to withdraw in the second clause what the first clause is offering. On this view the dying man's utterance may have been entirely sane, since he was not propounding a theory of his own mind — he was telling them something and then declining, in the same breath, to vouch for it.

Requirements:

1. Neither speaker may be softened to make the other look good, and neither may win. Both must be equally formidable, and they must actually answer each other: every speech should be a reply to the speech before it, not a turn in adjacent monologues.

2. The dialogue must confront, and may not wave away, at least these three asymmetries. First, why the same shape loses its sting in the third person — "the meeting was moved to Thursday, but she doesn't believe it" is unobjectionable, and so is the corresponding report about someone who believes what is false — and what that costs each account. Second, why the commissive form, in which the speaker asserts p and then asserts that he believes not-p, is often judged harsher than the plain denial of belief, and whether the two accounts explain that difference in the same way or in different ways. Third, what becomes of the machinery in the past tense — "I went to see him that Tuesday, but I don't believe I did" — and whether anything built for the present tense survives the move.

3. Each speaker must press the objection that most threatens the other, and must be answered rather than deflected. The act-theorist must say why the sentence still seems absurd when it is merely thought, or written in a diary no one will ever read: if the damage is done by the public act, the private case should be harmless, and it is not obviously so. The belief-theorist must say how self-deception and ordinary fragmentation are possible at all, given the strong truth-directedness the position requires, and must explain what distinguishes fragmentation, which is meant to rescue the dying man, from self-deception, which is meant to be pathological.

4. Concessions must cost something. At least once each, a speaker must state the other's case better than the other has stated it; at least once each, a speaker must abandon a line of argument in mid-course because it is not working, and the reader should feel the loss of it.

5. They must keep returning to the dead man's actual sentence, and it must function as evidence and not merely as an occasion. By the end, what remains unsettled between them must be about him — whether he was confused, and therefore whether one of them has been right about a thing they both heard.

6. Craft: between 1,200 and 1,600 words, dialogue only apart from four or five stage directions at most, and those must concern objects and bodies in the room rather than tone of voice. No comprehension checks — neither speaker may summarize the other's position back as a way of buying time. No name-checking of philosophers, books, papers, or schools; the scene should read as two people thinking, not as a seminar. The restraint of a wake throughout: grief present structurally, in what they return to and what they refuse to say, never named as an emotion by either speaker or by the narration.

7. Accuracy matters to me more than drama. A philosopher of language who reads this scene should recognize the territory immediately and should not wince at anything either speaker says.

8. The ending should leave the dispute open, but not evenly: by the final exchange the reader must be able to state precisely what each position has been forced to surrender and which of the two has been pushed further back, without either having been defeated. The last line must not be an aphorism.
