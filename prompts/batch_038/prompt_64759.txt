I am preparing a written statement of my jurisprudential commitments for a doctoral seminar on the Hart–Fuller exchange, and I would like a rigorous consistency audit of it before I circulate it. I am not asking for a summary of the debate, and I am not asking you to tell me which side is right in general. I want to know whether my own package hangs together.

My commitments, stated as I intend to state them in the seminar paper:

1. **Source thesis, in its strong form.** Whether a given norm is legally valid is settled conclusively by facts about its social sources — the procedure by which it was produced and the criteria that officials in the relevant system in fact accept and apply. An official, a lawyer, or a judge can answer the question of validity without ever having to form a moral judgment of her own, and where she believes she cannot, that is evidence that she has misidentified the question.

2. **Fuller's desiderata as moral principles.** I accept the eight principles of legality Fuller set out — generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, absence of contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between official action and declared rule. I accept them on Fuller's own terms, that is, as constituting an inner morality of law rather than as neutral maxims of efficient rule-making, and I hold that gross failure in any one of them deprives a norm of legal character. I further hold that these principles are among the criteria that a mature system's rule of recognition actually incorporates.

3. **Hart's minimum content, plus the possibility of an iniquitous but valid system.** I accept Hart's argument from the five truisms — human vulnerability, approximate equality, limited altruism, limited resources, and limited understanding and strength of will — that any legal system whose members are to survive must contain certain rules of restraint. I also hold that a legal system may be valid law throughout while being systematically iniquitous toward most of its subjects, and that nothing in the minimum content thesis shows otherwise.

4. **The informer case.** On the decision of the West German appellate court in 1949 concerning the woman who reported her husband to the Nazi authorities for remarks he had made about Hitler while home on leave, whereupon he was sentenced and eventually sent to the front, and whereupon she was herself later prosecuted for unlawfully depriving him of his liberty: I side with Fuller. The court was right to refuse legal force to the statute under which the husband had been sentenced, and Hart was wrong that the franker course would have been to enact a retroactive statute. Retroactive criminal legislation is a defect grave enough that it should be avoided wherever any alternative route exists.

5. **Penumbral discretion.** Where the language of a governing rule has genuinely run out, the judge exercises a choice-like, quasi-legislative discretion, and there is no determinate legal answer until she has made it.

6. **A test case I have constructed, and my verdict on it.** A legislature enacted a statute providing that any person convicted of any offence on a closed list thereupon ceases to be a citizen and becomes liable to immediate removal, with no inquiry into individual circumstances and no avenue of appeal. The provision is general in form; it was published in the official gazette on the day of enactment; it applies prospectively only; it is drafted with unusual precision; it conflicts with no other statute in force; anyone who avoids the listed conduct can comply with it; it has not been amended in the eleven years since; and it has been enforced without a single documented departure by any official in that period. The legislative record states its purpose plainly: to expel a religious minority whose members offend at several times the national rate. My verdict is that a court hearing a challenge today ought to hold that the statute was never valid law, and that every removal carried out under it was unlawful from the moment it was carried out.

What I want from you:

- Identify the genuine tensions among commitments 1 through 5, and rank them by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list of equally damaging problems. For each, state precisely which two commitments collide and what the mechanism of the collision is — not that they are in tension, but why they cannot both be held as I have phrased them.
- At least one pair that looks like a tension is not one. Say which, and explain why the appearance of conflict is misleading, so that I know I am not being told to fix something that is fine.
- For commitment 6, say whether commitments 1 through 5 jointly deliver the verdict I have reached. I suspect they do not, but I cannot see which of them fails. Walk through each in turn against the stipulated facts of the case and show where the derivation breaks. If a further premise is needed to get my verdict, state it, and state which of my existing commitments it would have to displace or weaken.
- Explain separately why I think declaring the statute void from the outset is less objectionable than the retroactive criminal statute I condemned in commitment 4, and tell me whether that asymmetry survives scrutiny or whether I am helping myself to something.
- For each genuine tension, give the cheapest available revision and price it: what the revision costs me elsewhere in the package, and what work the abandoned commitment had been doing. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the most, and whether the position that remains after that revision is still recognizably the one I have been calling myself.
