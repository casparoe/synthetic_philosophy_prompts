I have no formal training in biology or philosophy. I run a small marine shrimp farm, and for the past four years I have been the person responsible for our animal-welfare policy, which has meant teaching myself a fair amount of the scientific literature. For two of those years I have been arguing with the biologist who consults for us, and the argument has not moved an inch. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it really is.

The practical stakes are narrow: whether we should stop removing the eyestalks of our broodstock to induce spawning, whether we should abandon chilling harvested shrimp in ice slurry in favour of electrical stunning, and whether the crabs we hold live should be stunned before being killed.

Her position is that decapods are sentient and that the evidence is already sufficient to act on. She notes that they tend and guard injured limbs, that they will surrender a shelter or food source they value in order to avoid a location where they received a noxious stimulus, that they retain such avoidances far longer than a simple flinch would explain, and that some studies report their responses to noxious stimuli being dampened by painkilling drugs. She says demanding a brain organised like ours begs the question, because their nervous systems are built differently — a chain of ganglia running down the body rather than a single centralised organ — so resemblance to mammals is precisely the wrong test. She also cites the fact that Britain now legally recognises lobsters and octopuses as sentient after commissioning a review of the evidence.

My position is that everything on her list is equally consistent with nociception: a detection-and-avoidance system requiring no experience behind it. In an animal whose segmental ganglia can produce withdrawal and grooming responses by themselves, flexible-looking behaviour says very little about what happens in the brain. I add that her criteria are satisfied by systems nobody calls sentient, and that the defensible default is to attribute experience only on positive grounds, not whenever no one has ruled it out. I think the change in the law reflects a political mood, not a finding.

Here is what puzzles me. On what to do, we largely agree: she concedes that several of the changes are cheap enough to be worth making even if the science came out my way, and I concede that if it came out hers, everything would have to change. We agree about much of the action and cannot agree about the belief. We also agree that the one thing which would settle the matter directly — some access to what the animal's states are like from the inside — is unavailable to either of us.

My hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that we are not disagreeing about shrimp. We are disagreeing about what counts as evidence and about who carries the burden of proof, and the word 'sentient' is doing double duty in her usage: sometimes a capacity for aversive states that steer behaviour, sometimes there being something it is like to be the animal. Is that the right diagnosis? Or is there a genuine empirical question underneath — for instance whether the brain is in fact necessary for the flexible behaviours she cites, which lesion work might decide — or a difference about how one may extrapolate from vertebrates that neither of us can justify? Please tell me which claims in play are experimentally settleable and which are not; what would count as progress (a shared operational test, a class of experiments, a decision-theoretic treatment of acting under this uncertainty, or a change of question); or, if none of that is available, why no progress should be expected. I want the diagnosis argued from what each side actually says, not a survey of the literature on invertebrate sentience.
