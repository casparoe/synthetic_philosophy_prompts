Over a semester of a causation seminar, a colleague has been ruling on cases and I have been keeping score. His verdicts so far:

- Suzy's rock arrives first and shatters the bottle; Billy's would have arrived a moment later. Suzy is a cause, Billy is not.
- The same, except that Billy's throw is triggered only if Suzy misses, and she does not miss. Suzy is a cause, Billy is not.
- Two rocks arrive simultaneously, either one sufficient on its own. Both are causes.
- A switch redirects a trolley from one track to another, killing the person on the second track instead of the one on the first. Whoever flipped the switch is a cause of the death.
- A caretaker fails to water a plant, which dies. The caretaker is a cause — though he would deliver this verdict only once we stated which variables the case was being told in, and he gave two different answers for two different variable sets.
- A pigeon trained to peck at anything red is shown a scarlet chip and pecks. The chip's scarlet is not a cause of the peck; its redness is.
- A case in which the backup process is physically continuous with the primary one, so that there is no way to alter the primary without altering the backup. He refused to rule, saying the case is malformed rather than hard.

Next week's case is mine. A greenhouse has two heaters, A and B, each on its own sufficient to keep the orchids alive through the night. A controller holds B idle whenever A is running and starts B the instant A stops, with no gap and an identical temperature curve either way. On the night in question A runs, B stays idle, and the orchids live. Variant: the heaters share a single power bus, wired so that there is no way to shut A down that does not also destroy B's capacity to start.

Predict his verdict on the original case and on the variant. Say which general accounts of actual causation are consistent with the whole track record, what each of them predicts about these two cases, and which you would bet on — noting where the two cases pull in opposite directions. Then name one further case that would discriminate between the two strongest candidates. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which additional verdict of his, or which consideration I have left out, would push you toward a different account, and where this reconstruction of him is most likely to go wrong.
