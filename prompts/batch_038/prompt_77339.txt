I am a novelist — three books of fairly quiet domestic fiction behind me, and a fourth in draft that turns on a woman who arranges the death of someone she loves in order to spare her further pain — so the questions I am about to put to you are not merely academic for me. Over the last year I have been reading, untutored and on my own, in suffering-focused ethics: negative utilitarianism proper, the weaker views that give suffering heavy but not absolute weight, and negative prioritarianism, which weights relief by how badly off the sufferer already is. What troubles me is not that I find the asymmetry between pain and pleasure compelling — plenty of people do — but that it compels me in exactly the places where my craft already lives. I think in scenes rather than statistics, in the particular body rather than the aggregate, and above all in dramatic necessity: trouble is what makes a next sentence possible, and a story of unbroken contentment has no plot, only description. My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that narrative craft is structurally biased toward suffering for the entirely mundane reason that conflict is its engine, and that this bias — not any independent argument — is what makes the priority of relieving pain feel self-evident to people like me. Call it the dramaturgical suspicion: that suffering-focused ethics may be a genre convention wearing a moral vocabulary. It has a corollary I would name the Chekhov problem. My craft instructs me to cut any element that does no work, and a life of untroubled happiness does no work in a story, so I have been trained for twenty years to cut it; the asymmetry between suffering and happiness may be nothing but that instruction, transposed.

What I want from you is a translation, not a survey, and not a defence or refutation. Take the load-bearing claims of the suffering-focused position — the asymmetry; the thesis that duties to relieve are far stronger than duties to create or promote happiness; the priority structure that lets a given quantity of relieved suffering outweigh a greater quantity of produced happiness; the familiar objection that a strict version obliges an agent who could end all sentient life painlessly and at once to do exactly that; and the standard repairs, whether by demoting lexical priority to heavy weighting or by retreating to a pluralism in which happiness counts but never offsets — and restate each of them, one at a time, in the vocabulary I actually work in: scene and summary, want versus need, focalization and point of view, stakes, reversal, the gap between what a book shows and what it asserts, revision, the reader's felt experience unfolding in time, and the economy that forbids an element doing no work. Be as faithful as you can, and then give me the residue report. For every claim, say whether it came across intact, whether it came across only at the price of a distortion (and name that distortion precisely), or whether it has no expression in craft terms at all. I expect that impersonal aggregation, the moral standing of merely possible future people, and any harm that no one experiences from the inside will fail to survive, and I want your judgement on which side the failure falls: whether my vocabulary is missing something ethics genuinely needs — some way of holding a hundred thousand lives in mind without collapsing them into one — or whether those claims were never as clear as their confident syntax implies. I am fluent in the craft terms to the point of tic and nearly ignorant of the analytic apparatus, so do not explain my own trade back to me and do not spend space introducing the ethics.

Two ground rules. First, keep the translation honest: do not let craft settle the ethics by stealth. Wherever the narrative framing would make the asymmetry true on its own terms — because suffering is what makes a scene, because a happy ending reads as a formal cheat, because a reader's attention is itself treated as a moral resource — mark that moment explicitly and set it aside rather than smuggling it in as fidelity. Second, say nothing about my novel and give me no reading list. Begin your answer with your bottom line in a single sentence — your overall verdict on how much of suffering-focused ethics survives the translation into craft vocabulary, or what it becomes when it does not — and then give the reasoning in full, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
