I have just delivered the final manuscript of my third novel — a book set in a shuttered mill town in the autumn of 1998 — and for the past two months I have been arguing with my copyeditor in a way that neither of us can win, because we keep discovering that the rules she is applying do not appear to be rules about meaning at all. The novel contains a racial slur, which I will not spell out here, exactly nine times: always in dialogue, always attributable to a named speaker. What happens to those nine instances on their way to print is a study in inconsistency. In the scene where the mill's foreman deploys the word at a union meeting, it survives intact, though my publisher now wants a content note in the front matter. In a scene where a Black teenager uses the same word affectionately with his cousin, my editor wants it cut and replaced with the neutral group name, on the reasoning that the page is silent and a reader cannot hear the affection. In the school-and-library edition being prepared for young adults, every instance becomes an asterisked fragment. In the audiobook, the narrator will pronounce all nine of them aloud, at length, in her own voice. The jacket copy will not contain the word and will not allude to it; the reviews will refer to it, if at all, by a euphemism; and when a magazine ran an essay of mine quoting a sentence from the novel, its style desk substituted the euphemism inside my quotation marks, so that the magazine has now published a line of my prose that does not exist in my book.

What puzzles me is that the switch governing all of this never seems to be the identity of the speaker or the speaker's intention — the foreman's hateful use is preserved while the teenager's loving one is excised; the word is unspeakable on a dust jacket and spoken aloud in a recording studio; a scholarly monograph I have on my desk prints it throughout, and the newspaper review of that monograph will not. Nor is the pattern peculiar to publishing: a newspaper will refuse the word in a reporter's own sentence and then print it, unbowdlerized, inside a long quotation from a courtroom transcript, where any plausible account of harm would say it does considerably more damage. So I want to know what theory this patchwork presupposes. Is the working assumption that the harm is a property of the word-form itself, travelling with the token regardless of who utters it or to what end? That harm attaches to endorsement rather than to utterance, so that reporting a word is safe and voicing it is not — and if that is the principle, why is reporting safe in a transcript and unsafe in a book review, and why is voicing safe in a narrator's mouth? Is it simply a claim about audiences, that readers cannot be trusted to track the difference between use and quotation, and that the asterisks and the euphemisms are a concession to that incapacity rather than a claim about semantics? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can; separate the parts of the practice that strictly require it from the parts that a rival account would license just as well; tell me whether the package is internally coherent or whether some rule in it has to be abandoned; and then tell me what would actually change — in the front matter, in the school edition, in my own drafting habits — if the presupposed theory were replaced by one on which derogatory force is a matter of what a speaker does rather than what a word is.

I would rather have a tight, argumentative answer than a survey, so please keep it to no more than 700 words, and open with the single claim about meaning or harm to which my editor's decisions most clearly commit her before you work outward to the inconsistencies. I am not asking for a reading list or for references to the literature; I want your own reconstruction, and I want you to be willing to say that some part of this practice is philosophically underdetermined rather than pretending it hangs together.
