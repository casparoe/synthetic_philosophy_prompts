I am a fourth-year PhD student working in the philosophy of the social sciences, and the second chapter of my dissertation is about whether Popper's anti-historicist argument actually does the work its admirers assume it does. Our methods reading group spent the last three weeks on The Poverty of Historicism and its reception, and the discussion ended in a stalemate I am trying to break. As I reconstruct the load-bearing argument, it runs something like this: the course of human history is strongly influenced by the growth of human knowledge; we cannot predict the future growth of our scientific knowledge by rational or scientific methods, since predicting a discovery would require already having made it; therefore we cannot predict the future course of history; therefore there can be no theoretical history, no science of historical development that answers to the historicist ambition of laying bare what its proponents took to be the law of evolution of society. Alongside this, Popper insists on separating unconditional large-scale prophecy from the conditional predictions a social science can honestly make, and on the point that a trend is not a law, because a trend is tied to particular initial conditions and may lapse when they change. Where our seminar got stuck is on the step from the second premise to the conclusion: the argument looks to me like an impossibility result about what we can foresee, but it is presented as though it settled what sorts of regularities there could be.

So I would like a number from you rather than a survey of positions. To fix the target, let me stipulate terminology I have been using in the chapter. Call a claim a trajectory law if it asserts that any society meeting conditions of type C will pass through states S1, S2, ..., Sn in that order, with each transition supplied by the law itself rather than by boundary conditions that have to be looked up separately case by case. Call it a dynamical law if it tells us only how the state of a society changes given both initial conditions and separately supplied boundary conditions, so that the actual sequence any particular society runs through depends on inputs the law does not determine. My reading is that Popper's own constructive picture is dynamical laws plus conditional predictions, and that what he means to deny the historicists — from Hegel and Marx through Comte to the theorists of civilizational cycles — is trajectory laws. The proposition I want your credence in is this: Popper's central argument succeeds, in that it establishes rather than merely asserts that no trajectory law of human history is available — not merely that the future course of history is hard or impossible for us to foresee, but that the nomological ambition is ruled out by something better than the contingent difficulty of the enterprise.

What is your credence in that proposition, as a single number? Then decompose it for me. I expect at least the following belong on the list, though you should add or drop terms as you see fit: whether the first premise is strong enough in the precise form Popper needs, as opposed to a truism about influence; whether the unpredictability of scientific knowledge is a conceptual truth or a claim about the present limits of method, and how it fares against formal models of theory choice, machine-assisted discovery, and the quantitative forecasting programs — cliodynamics above all — that claim to have located recurring large-scale dynamics behind instability; whether an epistemic result about prediction can license a conclusion about what laws exist; whether the law/trend distinction does real argumentative work or merely relabels the dispute in Popper's favor; and whether trajectory law as I have defined it picks out anything a serious historicist actually asserted, or whether I have built a target nobody occupied. For each sub-question give a rough probability and one line of justification; show me how they combine into your final figure, and say whether you are multiplying, taking a minimum, or combining them some other way; identify which sub-question the number is most sensitive to; and tell me what argument or evidence would move it furthest. One last thing: my advisor sits at about 0.85 on the proposition and I sit at about 0.3. Given your decomposition, which single term are the two of us most likely disagreeing about — and please say so explicitly if you think my stipulated framing is doing covert work to tilt the question toward my own number.
