For the past four months I have been working in the project-controls office of a regional transit authority, and as of this quarter our capital-program guidance requires that every major project submit a reference class forecast alongside the engineers' bottom-up estimate before it goes to the board. The procedure as we were trained on it has four steps: identify a reference class of completed projects genuinely comparable to ours; assemble the actual outturn distribution for cost growth and schedule slippage across that class; locate our project within the class along whatever parameters the guidance deems relevant; and read a forecast off that position. The guidance is explicit that where the reference-class number and the engineering estimate conflict, the reference-class number governs, on the grounds that the engineering estimate is an inside view and therefore contaminated by optimism bias. My own assignment is a four-kilometre light-rail extension with a short bored section, for which I was directed to use a class of urban rail projects delivered in comparable economies over roughly the last three decades.

Separately, and entirely on my own time, I have been reading about the doomsday argument, and I cannot shake the sense that our mandated procedure is a species of the same inference. Both appear to treat an agent as a random sample drawn from a reference class, and both then let that agent's position within the class carry evidential weight beyond what the non-indexical facts deliver. Both run into a problem about how the reference class is to be selected, and about whether the selection is principled or opportunistic. Both face the objection that ordinary evidence about the particular case should swamp the sampling information, and both have defenders who insist that it cannot.

I would like a careful reconstruction of what this forecasting practice actually presupposes, and an honest assessment of whether my analogy is sound or whether I am being led astray by a surface resemblance. In particular:

1. Does the practice require that our project be a random sample from the reference class, or only that the class be a serviceable statistical model of it? These come apart, and I want to know whether the difference has consequences here.
2. Is the precedence rule — outside view over inside view when they conflict — defensible independently of any sampling assumption, for instance as a blunt debiasing instrument or as a calibration requirement? Would it survive the conviction that the inside-view details of this project really are informative?
3. The guidance tells us to choose a class of genuinely comparable projects. If comparability is itself settled by inside-view judgment, is the procedure circular, with the inside view smuggled back in through the choice of class? What else could constrain that choice?
4. There is a selection wrinkle that our training did not touch: our project is in the class only because it was funded, and the class is drawn from projects that were funded and carried to completion. So both the sample and the case are conditioned on a selection process. Is there anything in the doomsday literature that handles the analogous problem, and does it transfer?
5. Where does the analogy break? Our project is not an observer uncertain of its birth rank; it is a case we can describe in exhaustive non-indexical detail before we forecast it. Does that disanalogy deflate the comparison entirely, or does the sampling assumption still do irreducible work?
6. Which rival accounts would license the practice equally well — frequentist base rates, a purely pragmatic anti-optimism rationale, something closer to a chance-credence principle — and where is the practice therefore philosophically underdetermined? What would concretely have to change in our guidance if the sampling assumption were dropped: would reference class forecasting collapse into ordinary base-rate use, and is that a weaker or a stronger commitment?

Please distinguish carefully, throughout, between what is settled among people who work on these questions and what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a disputed position — about the doomsday argument, about the permissibility of indexical evidence, or about the epistemic status of outside-view forecasting — as though it were the consensus view. And if you think my framing is confused, say so directly and explain why, rather than working politely inside it.
