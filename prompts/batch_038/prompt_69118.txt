Retired hydrogeologist here — 35 years modelling aquifer depletion for a state agency, and only lately started reading philosophy of science, plus Malthus and all that. There's a habit from my own field that wont let go of me.

Every five-year forecast we issued got a formal verification memo: predicted drawdown against observed, and if we missed, that went in writing and the model lost standing. The 50-year projections sitting in the very same reports never got one and never will — the authors are retired long before the horizon arrives — and yet the long ones are what the newspapers quoted. Here's the part that really bugs me though. When a long-range scarcity forecast from the seventies finally came in roughly right about forty years late, the guy who made it was called vindicated. When a structurally identical forecast from the same decade never came in at all, we say Malthus was an idiot for making it.

So what picture of prediction and disconfirmation does that two-tier habit presuppose? As far as I can tell a long-range forecast under those rules cant lose: it comes true eventually and you were early, or the offsetting mechanism shows up and you still identified the pressure correctly. Does that make it unfalsifiable in the strict sense, or is timing a genuine separate parameter a forecast can miss without the mechanism being wrong? Tell me where the practice is quietly treating a forecast as a claim about a mechanism rather than about a date, whether that reading is doing work my five-year memos didn't need, and which rival accounts of what a prediction is would license the exact same two-tier setup. Then: what would my agency have had to change institutionally, not just intellectually, to score the long ones honestly — and is there any way to do that when the horizon is always longer than a career?
