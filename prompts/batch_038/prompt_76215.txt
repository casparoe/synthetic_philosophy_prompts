I co-organize a reading group that has spent the semester on agency — Frankfurt, then the frame problem, then two sessions on machine agents — and our last meeting is this Thursday in the back room of a pub that will not let us rearrange the tables, so we have all learned to be brief. On that final session someone ran a demo of a small language-model agent solving a text adventure, and when the harness quietly changed the environment under it (a door it had already opened was re-closed), the agent re-issued the identical action sequence, got the identical failure, and re-issued it again, cheerfully, nine times running. The room laughed and then went quiet, because the joke everyone wanted to make was the same one: it is a sphex with a language model. I would like to close the semester with a poem that makes that joke and then declines to let us enjoy it.

For anyone rusty, here is the material the poem has to be accurate about. The experiment, usually traced to Fabre's observations and popularized by an engineer's 1963 account of the machinery of the brain, which Dennett then took up in the mid-eighties, goes roughly like this: the digger wasp stings and paralyzes its prey, drags it to the mouth of the burrow by the antennae, leaves it outside, and goes in to inspect the nest before hauling the provision down. If the experimenter moves the prey a few inches while the wasp is underground, the wasp comes out, fails to find it, hunts until it does, and then — this is the crux — drags it back to the mouth of the burrow and goes in to inspect the nest all over again. Move it again and the loop restarts; by some tellings it can be repeated indefinitely. Fabre also reports that if the prey's antennae are cut off, the wasp, whose whole routine is keyed to pulling by the antennae, will not think to take hold of a leg instead, and that the wasp stocks a fixed number of crickets and cannot compensate when one goes missing. Hofstadter gave this quality its name, "sphexishness," and Dennett made it do double work: the wasp is both a picture of what becomes of a system that cannot revisit its own plan when the world refuses to hold still — the frame problem in flesh — and a picture of what freedom is not, since the freedom worth wanting on his account is no uncaused spark but the capacity to run one's trials in imagination rather than in the sand, to notice and detour. Worth knowing: the received story has been contested. At least one philosopher of biology has argued that later experiments show the wasp adapting after many iterations, and that re-inspecting a disturbed nest may be straightforwardly adaptive rather than a symptom of mindlessness; on his view the example survives in the literature because it is rhetorically irresistible, not because it is exactly true. Your poem may lean on the irresistible version, but it should know that it is leaning.

Form: a strict villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, two refrains repeated word for word every time they come around. The conceit is that the villanelle is itself sphexish: it cannot help returning to its two lines any more than the wasp can help returning to the mouth of the burrow, so whatever the poem claims has to be made inside that compulsion rather than against it. Let one refrain be the routine in the wasp's own idiom — drag, leave, inspect, come out — flat and procedural, with no pathos borrowed from us; let the other be the observer's verdict, the diagnosis of what a mind that cannot re-read its own plan looks like from the outside. They alternate in the usual order until the final couplet, where they arrive side by side for the first time, and that adjacency, not any explanatory line, should be the whole argument. Since the words cannot change, all the change must come from what surrounds them: the same refrain should read as mere mechanism in the opening tercet, as something closer to obstinacy in the middle, and as something uncomfortably like diligence at the end. Work in the technical sense of "frame" — what is supposed to stay fixed while you act — at least once, and let it collide with the burrow or the doorway. Do not use "free will," "instinct," or "robot" as a label anywhere; if the poem needs the idea it should stage it. Keep the register dry and physical: sand, chitin, a paralyzed cricket, the experimenter's hand, the dragged mark where the prey used to lie. No sermon in the last stanza, and no consolation.

After the poem, add a note of roughly 150 words. Say which of your choices you would defend as faithful to the material and where you have taken liberties with it, and flag in particular whether the poem commits to the inexhaustible-loop version of the result against the objection that the wasp adapts and that re-inspection may itself be adaptive. Then state how confident you are in the entomological and philosophical details you relied on, and single out the one point you are least sure of. The poem should not hedge — commit — but the note should be candid.
