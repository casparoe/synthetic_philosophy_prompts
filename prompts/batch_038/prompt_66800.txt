You are a philosopher of biology who works on the metaphysics of systematics, and you are thoroughly familiar with the debate over whether species taxa are individuals, classes, sets, or natural kinds. I am a third-year PhD student drafting a dissertation chapter on species realism, and what I need from you is not a defense or a refutation of a thesis but a careful disambiguation of a slogan.

In a chapter workshop yesterday I asked about a ring taxon: populations A through E distributed around a barrier, each interfertile with its immediate neighbours, with the end populations A and E meeting again in one region and there behaving as reproductively isolated. I asked whether that is one species or two, and whether the question has a determinate answer at all. A senior colleague cut me off with "species are individuals, not classes," and then drew two conclusions from it. First, that my question is not a matter of how we choose to define our species terms but a matter of historical fact about a lineage. Second, that my indeterminacy worry is a category mistake, since an individual can be vague at its margins without thereby ceasing to be an individual. He spoke as though once the slogan is granted, both conclusions follow. I am not persuaded, but I cannot yet say precisely what I think he is claiming, and I want to be fair to him before I argue with him.

So: what are the distinct precise theses that "species are individuals, not classes" could be expressing? I want a menu rather than your favorite reading. Lay out as many candidate readings as you can find, running from the ones that are trivially true, through the ones that are substantive and arguable, to the ones that are false or unmotivated. For each, state the thesis precisely enough that someone could argue against it, and assign a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and doubtful, or false. Please label the category each reading belongs to, because I suspect most of the slogan's rhetorical force comes from sliding among (i) an ontological or mereological claim about what kind of thing a species taxon is, with parts rather than members; (ii) a semantic claim about how species names refer; (iii) a claim about essences and intrinsic properties; (iv) a claim about causal, reproductive, or genealogical cohesion among the parts; (v) a claim about diachronic identity and persistence through turnover of parts; and (vi) an empirical generalization about which biological populations in fact satisfy some of the above — a generalization that may fail for asexual lineages and for organisms with extensive lateral gene transfer. If there are important readings that do not fall into those six slots, include them anyway.

Then, and this is the part I care about most: tell me which reading or readings would actually support each of the two moves my colleague made. Does any of them entail that the status of a ring taxon is settled by lineage facts rather than by our classificatory decisions? Does any of them turn an indeterminacy worry into a category mistake, as opposed to merely answering that worry for tidy paradigm cases? Where several readings would be needed in combination, say which combination, and note any tension between the components. And if the reading he requires is the radical one, say so plainly and say what argument would be needed to establish it.

Think hard about the space of readings before you begin writing, and be exhaustive rather than settling for the two or three that come to mind first; work through the candidates privately and show me only the finished menu, ordered from weakest to strongest claim. Begin your response with your bottom line in the first sentence — which reading the workshop argument required and whether it survives scrutiny — and only then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of my question, and no closing recap.
