In three weeks I give a forty-minute talk at a workshop on model evaluation and interpretability, co-hosted by an industry lab and a philosophy department. The room will be about twenty-five people. The large majority are machine-learning researchers who build capability evaluations and interpretability probes; several hold that a model's weights are fixed once inference begins, so that nothing an evaluator does short of retraining can change what the model is disposed to do, and that talk of "latent" capacities should be replaced by talk of circuits, features in superposition, and linear representations. Two are philosophers of mind who think ascriptions of knowledge or belief to a trained model are at best instrumental shorthand. One is a metaphysician who works on dispositions and knows that literature closely; she is the only person in the room who will know the words I am borrowing.

My thesis is that the recurring dispute over whether a model that fails to manifest a capacity under straightforward questioning nevertheless has that capacity is really a dispute about the difference between masking and finking, and that the field's standard methodology cannot tell the two apart. Briefly: a mask leaves the dispositional basis intact and blocks the route from stimulus to manifestation, so a different elicitation route can in principle recover the manifestation; a fink alters the basis at the moment the stimulus occurs, so no further probing will recover it, and the probing may itself be what removes it. Applied to models, ordinary prompting that runs into a refusal looks mask-shaped, while any assessment that requires modifying the system — fine-tuning probes, iterated adversarial pressure, activation interventions — looks fink-shaped, since the measurement changes the thing measured. The consequence I want to defend is that negative evaluation results admit no uniform reading: they license either "we have not yet found the mask" or "we have destroyed the disposition," but not "the disposition is absent." Reports of a capability ceiling are therefore underdetermined in a way that matters, because such reports are used downstream to justify deployment and regulatory decisions.

Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the eight or nine objections this room will actually raise — not the ones I would field at a metaphysics seminar — ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument. Phrase each the way its objector would phrase it: an evaluation researcher will not say "modal error," and the metaphysician will put her point far more precisely than that. For each, supply a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, mark where that reply is soft, and indicate which objections I should concede inside the talk rather than attempt to answer on my feet. Note separately which single objection, if it lands, would require me to withdraw the thesis rather than refine it, and end with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
