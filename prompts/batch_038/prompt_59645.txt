I'm giving a 25-minute talk next month at a little workshop that's co-hosted between a computational neuroscience institute and an AI lab's safety group, and I've realized way too late that the room is going to be almost entirely the wrong kind of audience for my material. Systems and behavioral neuroscientists who work on reward learning in rodents and primates — self-stimulation, optogenetic stimulation of accumbens/ventral pallidum, the wanting-vs-liking dissociation, dopamine-as-prediction-error people — plus maybe five RL folks who do model-based control, and basically nobody who has read the alignment literature. They will not know the word wireheading in the sense I mean it, and at least two of them have written things arguing that the pop-sci story about Olds's rats pressing a lever until they collapse is a myth or at least badly overstated. One of them is a friend of mine who does optogenetics and told me after dinner last week that my whole framing is "folk psychology with a circuit diagram taped to it," which I couldn't really refute at the time.

Here's the thesis so you know what they'll be shooting at. Three claims. First, the usual taxonomy that sorts reward failures into specification-gaming on one side and reward-tampering on the other quietly assumes the reward signal is a fixed read-out that the agent either satisfies honestly or hijacks — but from the neuroscience side a reward signal isn't a read-out, it's a teaching signal whose content moves as the animal's model moves, so the taxonomy has a giant unpopulated middle where all the interesting cases live. Second, I want to coin and defend a split: reward capture, where the agent gets causal control of the channel itself, versus reward erosion, where nothing is tampered with at all and the signal stays perfectly honest but the agent's internal representation of what it tracks slowly drifts. My claim is that essentially every failure we've actually seen in deployed systems is erosion, and capture is a limit case that only shows up in thought experiments. Third, and most provocative: the fix therefore isn't better outer alignment or corrigibility, it's rewards that saturate — bounded, homeostatic, consummatory-shaped rewards, the thing biology already built — because the reason a rat will press forever isn't that it got its hands on raw pleasure, it's that the stimulation delivers wanting with no satiety, no consummatory endpoint, so even a pretty crude bounded signal would have stopped it. I think that's right and I think it's load-bearing for the whole third section.

What I need is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the seven to nine objections this specific room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the talk if they land and I fumble. Phrase them the way these people would phrase them — they'll go after whether the lever-pressing story is even true, whether satiety is a real property of a neural signal or just bookkeeping, whether a bounded reward is anything other than a cost function with a nicer name, whether homeostatic set-points are themselves learned and therefore themselves tamperable, whether anything I'm saying transfers from an animal with reflexes to an agent that carries an explicit world model, and whether I've just reinvented habit-vs-goal-directed control. For each: roughly a one-minute reply I can actually deliver standing up, plus a blunt note on where that reply is softest and how soft. Mark which ones I should just concede on the spot because fighting them costs me more credibility than I gain. Tell me separately which objections come from the RL people versus the neuro people, because I'm fairly sure those are disjoint lists and I don't want to prep the wrong half. And finish with the question I'm praying nobody asks — my guess is it's something about whether the capture/erosion distinction even survives in a system that can model its own reward function and reason about the drift, but I'd rather you tell me what the real landmine is than get my guess validated.
