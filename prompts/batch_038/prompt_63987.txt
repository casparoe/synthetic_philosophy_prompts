I organize a two-day workshop hosted jointly by our medical school's bioethics center and the engineering team that maintains our hospital system's triage-support tool, and the opening session is on whether an agent can ever face a genuine moral dilemma: a situation in which what the agent ought to do, all things considered, is simply not determinate, so that acting well is not just a matter of having ranked the considerations correctly. Roughly twenty of the forty participants build constrained decision-support systems, and the rest are philosophers and clinicians. I want to open with a short sorting quiz that puts people's answers to cases on the table before anyone gets to announce a view, because in past years almost everyone in the room has described themselves as a pluralist about values and then answered every case like a monist with a good ranking procedure.

Please write that quiz. I need eight cases, each three to six sentences, each with two or three answer options phrased as first-person judgments a participant could endorse without knowing any of the technical labels; no question may ask anyone to choose between named positions or to define a term. The cases must be built to expose specific pressure points rather than to survey the literature: whether obligations agglomerate at all, whether "ought implies can" is doing real work or is just a slogan about blameworthiness, whether anything normative is left over after the correct choice is made — an apology owed, a repair owed, a regret that is appropriate rather than self-indulgent — whether a conflict evaporates under better information or better ordering, and whether a conflict that the agent created through their own earlier commitments differs in kind from one the world hands them. At least one case should make the agent blameless whichever way they go and at least one should make them culpable, and the two should not be distinguishable by surface features alone.

Two of the eight cases must involve a non-human agent, because that is where the room will actually disagree. One should concern a system whose governing rules were issued by two bodies with incompatible mandates — for instance a triage-support tool whose allocation rules come from a public health authority and whose safety constraints come from the hospital's own risk office — so that the conflict arrives as inconsistent input rather than as a hard world. The other should concern a system that cannot represent one of the options at all, because the relevant feature is outside its input schema or its objective cannot be evaluated at decision time; I want the quiz to force participants to say whether "ought implies can" then licenses the conclusion that the system has no dilemma, or whether it makes the situation worse. Finally, the scoring key must include a section my engineers will care about: which of the positions the quiz sorts people into survive translation to an artificial agent, and which quietly presuppose features such an agent lacks — a single life to lead, bodily exposure to the consequences, the capacity for the regret that makes residue intelligible.

The positions in the key should be defined by commitments, not by pedigree, and at minimum the instrument must be able to sort someone into: the view that no genuine dilemmas exist because a correct ordering always resolves the conflict; the view that conflicts are real but get overridden cleanly, leaving nothing behind; the view that genuine dilemmas exist and leave normative residue the agent can no longer discharge; the view on which the right action can itself be a wrong, so that acting well dirties the agent; and the view that dilemma talk is an artifact of our deontic concepts or of underspecification rather than a report on the world. Add any further position you think my case set actually forces — a reading on which dilemmas are always somebody's fault but not the agent's, or a purely epistemic reading, for instance — and then tell me which combinations of answers the quiz permits but no one holds, diagnosing each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and which pairs of positions my instrument cannot really separate. No citations, no reading list, and no titles or dates; work from what you already know, and where you use a standard label, gloss it in a clause. Write in American English, and keep it to something forty people could complete on paper in twelve minutes.

Return the whole thing as a single JSON object, with these fields:

- `cases`: array of eight objects, each with `id` (C1–C8), `scenario` (the case text), `options` (array of objects with `letter` and `text`), `pressure_point` (the one sub-question of the debate this case is designed to expose), and `option_readings` (array of objects with `letter` and `commitment_it_reveals`, one clause each).
- `positions`: array of objects, each with `name`, `core_claim` (one sentence), `signature_pattern` (which options on which case ids), `ruled_out_by` (an answer pattern incompatible with holding it), and `awkward_commitment` (one sentence on the cost of the view).
- `unoccupied`: array of objects with `combination`, `status` (one of `incoherent`, `unattractive`, `unexplored`), and `diagnosis`.
- `inseparable_pairs`: array of objects with `position_a`, `position_b`, `why_the_quiz_cannot_split_them`, and `what_would_split_them`.
- `translation_to_artificial_agents`: array of objects with `position`, `verdict` (`survives`, `partly`, or `fails`), `reason`, and `exposing_case_id`.
- `facilitation`: object with `most_diagnostic_case_id`, `why`, `likely_majority_position_in_my_room`, and `follow_up_question_to_ask_aloud_if_the_room_clusters`.
