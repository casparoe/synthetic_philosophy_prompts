I'm on the clinical AI governance committee at a midsized hospital network and last month we had what everyone internally is calling our index case: a closed-loop insulin infusion pump in the ICU delivered a bolus that killed a patient. The vendor had pushed a firmware update over the air eleven days before, our biomed people had validated the previous version and never re-looked at the new one, the charge nurse had been told at go-live that she didn't need to shadow the pump becuase the system was "autonomous by design," and the intensivist on duty had four other patients. Going person by person, nobody looks reckless to me — each of them did what their role told them to do. But obviously somebody has to be answerable, and our chair, a retired hospital CEO who keeps a replica of Truman's desk sign on his bookshelf, ends every version of this discussion with "the buck stops here," and then proposes we just name an accountable executive in the policy document and move to the next agenda item.

I want to push back on thursday but I keep getting tangled, because that slogan is doing several different jobs at once and I can't tell which one he's actually leaning on. I know the phrase comes from poker — a marker, originally a buckhorn-handled knife, got passed around to show whose deal it was, so "the buck stops here" meant refusing to hand the marker on — and I suspect that's part of why it feels like it settles things: it sounds like it's about halting a transfer. So: can you lay out, as a menu I can literally hand around the table, the distinct precise claims it could be making in a case like ours? Something along the lines of a descriptive claim that chains of delegation have to bottom out in a person somewhere; a claim about office, that accountability attaches to a position rather than to anybody's mental states; a claim about answerability-without-fault, where someone owes an account or owes compensation even though they aren't blameworthy; a claim that you can't shed responsibility just by delegating the task; a claim that the "who checks the checker" regress has to be stopped by decision rather than by fact; and maybe a fiat reading where holding someone responsible is something we do rather than something we find out. Order them weakest to strongest, and for each one tell me whether it's just a platitude, whether it's actually true, and — the part I care about most — whether it would really close the gap in our case or just relocate the gap onto a different person.

Two more asks. Be explicit about whether "the buck stops here" and "the blame stops here" can come apart, because I'm pretty sure half the table hears the second one when the chair means the first, and then objects that nobody's actually culpable, which is beside his point and I want to name it cleanly. And if there's a reading on which accountability-by-fiat is coherent — where designating an accountable executive creates a real responsibility rather than a paper one — say what would have to be true for that to work: does she have to be able to intervene, to have known, to have set the conditions in the first place, to be able to absorb the cost? If no reading survives that test, say so flatly instead of hedging. I don't want a bibliography or a tour of the responsibility-gap literature — I've read the standard bits — I want the disambiguation and your verdict on each reading.
