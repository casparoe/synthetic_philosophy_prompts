Third year, philosophy of mind, meant to be drafting my theory chapter and instead I've got a pile of case verdicts that refuse to line up. The three families on the table for me are judgment views, perception views, and feeling views, and each one seems to nail exactly the cases the other two flub. Here's what I actually say when asked:

- A. Complete spinal cord lesion, no visceral feedback coming back, reports being terrified during a fire and gets out. Real fear, no qualifications, no "as-if."
- B. A colleague is quietly engineering my exclusion from the lab. I judge it's bad for me, I plan around it, I rehearse conversations — but there's no heat, no urge, my pulse is normal, I sleep fine. I want to say I'm angry, and arguably angrier than the guy in C.
- C. Someone goes red at a referee's call, jabs a finger, and ten minutes later can't recall the incident and sincerely insists they weren't angry. I want to say they weren't really angry.

B and C are structurally opposite and I rule them the same way, which is the pair I can't reconcile.

- D. Spider on the wall. I know, and will say out loud, that it's harmless. Still fear.
- E. Arousal from a jackhammer outside that I end up pinning on my flatmate, and I snap at her. Real anger, despite the bogus origin.
- F. Same jackhammer, but she's been warned beforehand that the noise makes people irritable. No anger at anybody. Also the right verdict, and it sits very badly next to E.
- G. My flatmate's low-grade irritation at her boyfriend: she denies it, it changes nothing she does, and it shows up only as a slight flatness when she says his name. I keep flip-flopping on this one.

My own tentative principle is roughly: a subject is in emotion E iff E has reorganized, over some window, what they treat as counting as a reason — attention, avoidance, replanning, readiness to interpret new stuff under a theme. No requirement of felt quality, no requirement of an endorsed judgment.

Does anything like that get all seven, or is the price a second clause so baroque it stops being a theory? If some verdict has to go, tell me which one I should be least reluctant to sacrifice. And whatever principle you land on, what does it deliver for two cases I haven't listed: an actor who weeps genuinely every night for a character they know is fictional, and someone in a flat depressive stretch who reports feeling nothing at their mother's funeral but who, six months later, cant get through a single sentence about her?
