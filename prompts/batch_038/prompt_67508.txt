So I've read way too much of the standard nudging canon at this point — cafeteria layouts, Save More Tomorrow, sludge audits, the Thaler/Sunstein vs. Gigerenzer thing — and I want a piece that doesn't just re-run it. Here's the setup I keep thinking about:

A consumer app team has an internal choice-architecture review, and they're deciding whether to ship a nudge whose target behavior isn't saving or eating well or exercising. It's *nudgeability itself*. A light recurring prompt sequence that measurably raises users' approval of the company using behavioral techniques on them — both in surveys and in A/B tests of whether they accept *other* nudges later. On the review board: the behavioral scientist who wants to ship it, and a philosopher who thinks this is the one nudge you can't defend on nudge grounds.

Write the dialogue between them. The scientist's strongest card is the usual libertarian-paternalist move — users would endorse this if they were fully informed, unpressured, and not systematically biased — and the philosopher's strongest card is that this is precisely the case where that counterfactual endorsement can't carry any weight, because the intervention is what manufactures the endorsement. Both need to be genuinely good: the scientist should have a real answer to the bootstrapping worry (something about baselines, or about whether there's any such thing as a preference that wasn't already shaped by an environment), and the philosopher should concede something substantive too. They should respond to each other's actual points rather than trade prepared speeches, and neither wins cleanly — land on whatever the sharpest unresolved crux is.

Format: a section called "The dialogue" (~700 words), then "Where it bottoms out" (~120 words) naming the crux in a sentence or two and saying which side you'd bet on and why.
