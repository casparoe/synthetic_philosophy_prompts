Next month I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on nuclear strategy and political violence hosted by a political science department's security studies program. I should be clear about who will be in the room, because I have written the paper for philosophers and the audience is not one. Of roughly fifteen attendees, eight are rational-choice scholars of deterrence who work either formally or from the archival record of Cold War crises; two are diplomatic historians of the early nuclear period who are deeply suspicious of models unmoored from documents; one is a retired defense official who spent a decade in nuclear planning and targeting policy and who has written a well-regarded memoir; two are fellow moral philosophers, neither of whom works on war; the rest are graduate students, mostly from the political science side. Nobody in the room thinks nuclear weapons are simply immoral, and nobody wants to hear a sermon.

The paper's thesis is this. Deterrence theory treats a threat as a propositional conditional — if you cross this line, I will do this — and treats the central problem as one of credibility: how to make a conditional that would be ex post irrational to honor nevertheless believed. The standard repairs are well known: irreversible commitment, delegation of authority, alert postures that shorten decision time, the deliberate cultivation of a threat that leaves something to chance, and finally the fallback on the sheer existential horror of the weapons themselves. My claim is that all of these repairs operate by the same mechanism — manufacturing a calibrated uncertainty in the adversary's head about what one will do — and that this mechanism has a structural cost the theory has no vocabulary for. Uncertainty manufactured for strategic effect does not stay where the theorist puts it. It leaks: into one's own command apparatus, into the adversary's warning posture and force disposition, into the judgment of subordinates and third parties. So the object that deterrence theory calls a threat is better modeled not as a conditional an agent asserts but as an intervention on a probability distribution over outcomes, and once it is modeled that way, credibility and catastrophe-risk come apart. They can move in opposite directions, and the theory contains no resource for ranking the trade-offs. My conclusion is revisionary: several devices the literature ranks as credibility-enhancing are strictly worse on the correct criterion even where they do enhance credibility, and several devices ranked as credibility-weakening — positive control, redundant safety, explicit and narrow declaratory policy — are better. The correct target is not stability but the containment of the hazard the strategist himself creates.
