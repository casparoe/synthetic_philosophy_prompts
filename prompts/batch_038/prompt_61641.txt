I run intake for a small restorative justice program — about six referrals a week, and for each one I have to decide whether to admit the person into a process that will eventually put them in a room with the person they harmed. The board wants my reasoning written down in plain English, I get maybe twenty minutes a case, and nobody here has any philosophy training.

The recurring problem is trust. The person in front of me says he's changed, and there's never enough evidence either way. My instinct is that both of the usual positions are half right: the one that says believing without evidence is always a kind of dishonesty you'd be ashamed to be caught at, and the one that says some beliefs are allowed to run ahead of the evidence because holding them helps make them come true — and in this line of work that seems observably true, people do better when somebody bets on them.

Write me a procedure I can actually run per case: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where it lands on admit / don't admit / admit with conditions, and where it hands the call up to the board because its a values question and not a facts question. Tell me which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which I can safely drop, and where you think the procedure will misfire on actual people. Then run it on three cases: a two-year-clean applicant with strong references whose answers sound rehearsed to me; a participant I'm fond of where I'd like to believe he hasn't relapsed and believing it would probably help; and somebody with no support network at all, where I have nothing to go on but I want to say yes anyway. One of those should come out against what I want — I'd rather find that out from you than in the room.

And if it turns out you can't literally decide to believe something, say so and tell me what the procedure is really governing instead, because I need something I can put in a file.
