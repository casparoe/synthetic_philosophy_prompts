**TAKE-HOME, 1,000–1,200 words.** Three patients. All the clinical facts are stipulated — treat them as given and don't go hunting for real cases that resemble them. (You may refer to positions by the names of the people who defend them; no bibliography wanted.)

**A.** 34, cardiac arrest after a drowning. No brainstem reflexes, no respiratory effort on the apnea test at the CO2 threshold, isoelectric EEG, and a confirmatory flow study showing no intracranial perfusion. She stays on a ventilator with pressor and hormonal support for four years: heart beating, blood pressure maintained, temperature regulated with a warming blanket, wounds healing, infections fought off with a normal white-cell response. In month nine of the four she carries a pregnancy (conceived before the arrest, at 14 weeks) to a live cesarean delivery at 32 weeks. Spinal withdrawal reflexes in the limbs are present throughout.

**B.** 58, catastrophic intracranial hemorrhage. He does *not* meet neurologic criteria — pupils reactive, some respiratory drive left. Family withdraws support per his written wishes. Extubation, circulation stops, continuous asystole for five minutes, after which the team declares death and procures heart and liver. Stipulated: an ECMO circuit and a research perfusion protocol are available in the same building and, on the animal data behind them, could restore cellular viability — including isolated neuronal activity in the brain — if hooked up within twenty minutes. The team has resolved in advance not to use them and the family has declined them.

**C.** 45. Stipulated: a lesion irreversibly destroys both cerebral hemispheres, all cortex and thalamus included, while the brainstem remains completely intact. She breathes unaided, has gag, cough and pupillary reflexes, normal sleep-wake cycles, stable heart rate and pressure with no support at all — and will never again have any capacity for consciousness. Picture the hydranencephaly cases, but in an adult.

(a) For each of A, B and C, say whether the whole-brain standard, the higher-brain standard and the circulatory-respiratory standard each imply that the patient is dead, and give the reasoning a proponent of each would offer.
(b) Does the word "irreversible" in the way most US states define death actually do the work it looks like it does? Use B to show your answer.
(c) Which standard would you defend for a legal definition of death? State the strongest objection to your choice and answer it.

---

And here's the answer I'm looking at.

A is dead on the whole-brain standard and on the higher-brain standard, and alive on the circulatory one, since her heart is beating. The whole-brain view says death is the irreversible loss of the integrated functioning of the organism as a whole, and in A the brain has stopped doing its coordinating job, so what's left is a set of organs kept going by a machine. The spinal withdrawal reflexes aren't a problem, because they're generated below the level of the brain and don't show any integrative work being done by the brain. The wound healing and the delivery do show something, though: they show how much regulation a body can do without a brain, which is the line taken by people like Shewmon, and it puts real pressure on the claim that the brain is the only thing holding the organism together. The whole-brain theorist can answer that those are functions of parts, not of the whole.

Bernat, Culver and Gert argue that what makes you the person you are, and what makes you alive in the sense that matters, is the capacity for consciousness, and that the whole-brain standard is largely a compromise between that view and the old circulatory one — the entire brain is used as a proxy for the cortex because it's easier to test at the bedside. So the whole-brain and higher-brain standards aren't really two views about what death is. They're one view with different amounts of caution built in.

C shows this. C's brainstem works, so on the whole-brain standard she's alive; on the higher-brain standard she's dead. But that's a disagreement about words only, because both sides agree the organism and the person are different things and both agree that in C the person is gone and the organism is still going. The whole-brain people are saying the law should track the organism, the higher-brain people that it should track the person. Given they agree on every fact of the case, they can't actually be disagreeing.

B is dead under the circulatory standard: the heart stopped and didn't restart, and after five minutes there's no auto-resuscitation to worry about. He's dead under the whole-brain standard by then too, or close enough to it that nobody in practice would run the tests. The ECMO machine down the hall changes nothing, because irreversible here just means circulation is not in fact going to be restored — nobody's required to make the cessation impossible to undo, only that no one will undo it. So B is dead, and the procurement doesn't violate the dead donor rule.

On (c) I'd leave the law alone. The whole-brain standard is already in the statutes, and switching would mean that a lot of people now being declared dead count as alive, and we'd lose those donors. The strongest objection is the one about A — that a body which can carry a child for years can't really be dead — and my answer is that the law doesn't have to track what's really true, it has to be workable.
