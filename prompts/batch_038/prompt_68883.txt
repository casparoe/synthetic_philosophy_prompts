I'm on a hospital ethics committee and we've been going around in circles on the definition of death for about two years now, and what I want is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not a verdict on who's right.

Roughly the shape of it: one side says whole-brain death just is death because the brain is what holds the body together as a single integrated organism; the other side says that's an empirical claim and its false, since whole-brain-dead bodies regulate temperature, mount immune responses, heal wounds, and have carried pregnancies for months. Then there's a separate fight on the circulatory end — people who think that calling a five minute wait after asystole an "irreversible" cessation of circulation is just dressing up a decision not to resuscitate as if it were a fact about biology. Nobody budges and every move has a stock reply waiting for it.

My hypothesis: "death" is doing at least three different jobs here — marking a biological event, licensing organ retrieval, and settling when it's permissible to stop treating someone — and each camp is anchored to a different job, so they're arguing past each other under one word. Is that fair, or is there a genuinely substantive disagreement underneath that my deflationary reading just lets everyone off the hook from? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a change in how the criteria are written, better data on auto-resuscitation — or should we expect none, and why? Please say how confident you are in the diagnosis overall and flag the piece of it you're least sure about.
