I teach an upper-level seminar in Buddhist moral philosophy and am writing the three-hour final. I want one essay question, answerable in about ninety minutes, on whether Buddhist ethics is a form of consequentialism. It must not be answerable by laying out the standard debate between those who read the tradition as maximizing the cessation of suffering for all sentient beings and those who read it as a virtue ethics organized around nirvāṇa as a telos and the cultivation of character; students will have rehearsed that exchange in class.

Instead, I want the question to turn on the structure of the karmic ledger. Merit can be dedicated or transferred to others, but demerit cannot be shed or handed off: in the skillful-means case from the Upāyakauśalya Sūtra, the captain kills the robber and accepts the resulting rebirth in hell himself, precisely because the harm stays with the agent. The question should ask students to work out what a normative theory looks like when benefits are agent-neutrally aggregable but harms are individually borne, whether that can count as consequentialism at all, and what a Buddhist should revise to make it coherent — the account of karma or the consequentialist attribution — together with what is lost on each path. Students should be able to reach the issue using the self-and-other exchange argument in Śāntideva's Bodhicaryāvatāra and the concept of the skillful, without quoting secondary literature.

Please give me the question, a model answer of roughly 750 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves separating a first from a strong second, plus five characteristic errors that should cost marks — for instance treating upāya as a general licence to suspend the precepts, reading nirvāṇa as a state of affairs to be maximized, or equating compassion with impartial utility.
