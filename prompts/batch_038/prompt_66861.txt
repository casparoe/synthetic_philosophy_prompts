I'm the commissioning editor for a history documentary strand — four or five hours a year, primetime-ish — and the pitch I get most often is some version of "was it real?" Troy, Arthur, the Exodus, Robin Hood, Atlantis once a quarter. Right now every one of those decisions turns into a shouting match in a room: our advisory historian says nothing before written records is safe and we should only ever make programmes about the story, and the producers say if we won't commit to a war actually happening the audience walks in minute six. I've allready half-committed to one of these for next year and I'd rather have a written procedure I can point at than another argument.

So: write a decision procedure my development team can run on a "legend or history?" pitch in about an hour, before we spend money on a recce or a talking head. It has to be grounded in what these arguments actually turn on, not generic scepticism. At minimum it needs to handle:

- how to tell a meaningful silence from a boring one. The Egypt case is the hard version: an enormous administrative culture with lots of surviving records, and no mention of the thing you'd expect. But the procedure needs to say when an absence counts, when it doesn't, and it has to deal with the obvious reply that regimes don't commemorate their own disasters — so there's an expectedness test and a suppression test and they can pull in opposite directions.
- the gap between event and earliest source, and why the size of the gap isn't the whole story. A short gap doesn't help if the source's genre isn't trying to report anything, and a long gap doesn't kill a claim if the material was carried in a form that resists drift. I want a real discount rule with the reasons for it, plus how to treat a source that names the right people and places but is plainly shaped for a later audience's identity.
- corroboration in a different medium, and specifically the third-party archive case: Bronze Age Anatolian correspondence that names a city in the Troad and a western power whose names look like the Greek words for Troy and the Achaeans, where the identifications are widely accepted but not nailed down. The procedure should say what such documents can and cannot license — they seem to name places and frictions, never the plot anyone wants them to name.
- the onion test for layered traditions, where the earliest mention of a figure is centuries later, an earlier near-contemporary writer describes the battle and the enemies but never names him, and then a 12th-century chronicle supplies basically everything audiences know. I want an explicit rule about how to check whether a detail is evidence of an event or evidence of the layer that introduced it.
- the kernel problem. Almost every one of these pitches has a defensible minimal claim ("something was destroyed here", "a people by this name existed in this region by this date", "some warlord probably won some battles") sitting under an indefensible full claim (the ten-year siege, the twelve battles in order, the mass migration and the drowned army). The procedure has to force the team to write down the exact minimal proposition they'd be committing to on screen, and to say what the show is allowed to assert, what it's allowed to dramatise, and what it's allowed to imply by juxtaposition, which is where these things usually go wrong.
- the case where the story is false as history but solid as evidence about the people who told it. That's reportable, but only under a frame, and I want the frame spelled out so a compliance editor and a journalist reviewing us can see we weren't smuggling.

Give me the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each step, where the procedure kills the pitch outright, where it sends it to the historian because the question is one of standards rather than facts, and — this matters to me — flag every step that is really a judgement call wearing a procedural costume. Then run it on three pitches: the Trojan War as Homer tells it, King Arthur as a real war leader, and the Exodus as the biblical narrative has it. One of those three is going to annoy me; say so plainly rather than hedging, and tell me what the show could legitimately be about instead. Finish with the failure modes: which kind of pitch this procedure systematically over-admits, which it systematically under-admits, and why.

Format: after each of the three runs, put the outcome on its own line as `Ruling: <greenlight | greenlight-with-frame | no-go>`, and make the very last line of your answer exactly `Verdict: <the one pitch you'd put into production first>`.
