I have been reading about the Boltzmann brain problem on and off for something like a decade now — the forum threads, the cosmology review pieces, the perennial proposal that a theory which predicts we are probably freak observers has thereby refuted itself — and I have watched that argument get deployed as decisive in contexts where nothing about it had actually been priced out. I would like a piece of satire that runs it through a register that is obliged to charge for it.

Please write a corporate document package from a fictional Bermuda reinsurer that has, unwisely, launched a specialty line covering what its product literature calls Fluctuation Substitution: the event in which the insured is, or has always been, a spontaneously nucleated observer equipped with a locally coherent set of false memories. Roughly 900 to 1,100 words total, in three parts:

1. A memorandum from the Chief Risk Officer to the underwriting committee, around 550 words, recommending what to do with the line after eighteen months of adverse experience.
2. A schedule of policy wordings and exclusions: ten to fourteen numbered clauses in authentic insurance-legal register.
3. A short FAQ for brokers, around 150 words, answering the questions agents actually ask.

The humor must come from getting the underlying reasoning right rather than from insurance jargon being intrinsically funny. Specifically, I would like the piece to dramatize — without ever announcing any of them as philosophical problems — the following features: that the loss cannot be dated, because if the event occurred it also occurred to the adjuster, the ledger, and the reserving model; that the rate is exquisitely sensitive to which cosmology the customer holds, so the rating plan ends up attaching a loading to subscription to a universe with an indefinitely long de Sitter-like future and a discount to eschatologies in which everything is destroyed soon; that adverse selection takes a self-locating form, since the pool contains an unknown proportion of observers whose records are indistinguishable from those of ordinary observers, and the underwriters' only tool for detecting this is itself one of those records; that no verification procedure can work, because a fluctuated claim file arrives complete with the memory of having paid the premiums and the memory of filing it; that the treaty market is useless, since every counterparty's exposure is perfectly correlated across the entire future of the spacetime; and, as the pivot of the memorandum, that accepting the model would undercut the very reasoning by which the firm came to accept it — so the Chief Risk Officer should recommend that the company adopt, as a matter of solvency policy, a cosmology in which the line is not catastrophic. That recommendation must be delivered in the same flat commercial tone as the rest of the memo, and the document must never notice that it has done anything other than apply ordinary prudence.

Constraints. Keep the register deadpan throughout: no winking, no italicized punchlines, no explanatory coda. Do not preface the documents with a summary of the Boltzmann brain argument; assume the reader has it, but write so that a reader who does not can still follow the institutional absurdity. Invent all persons, firms, and product names; do not quote or closely paraphrase any real paper, and include no citations or bibliography. The memorandum's reasoning should be internally consistent at every step — the joke is that it never makes a mistake it is in a position to catch. And somewhere in the exclusions schedule, include one clause whose practical effect is to exclude substantially every claim the product was sold to cover, drafted so plausibly that a working underwriter would not blink at it.
