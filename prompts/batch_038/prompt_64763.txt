I am a third-year doctoral student working across political philosophy and the governance of deployed machine-learning systems, and last Thursday I sat in on a working session that has stayed with me. The context: a research group is advising a mid-sized city that has piloted an assistant residents use to navigate benefits eligibility, permitting questions, and offers under a voluntary flood-buyout program. Because the assistant has to recommend courses of action to individual people — should this household take the buyout, or spend the money on elevation and stay — the group must settle what the system is meant to track or optimize when residents' own views diverge. The preference data available to them consists of the pilot's opt-in chat logs plus an opt-in survey; response rates were low among renters, and a substantial share of the buyout's cost falls on taxpayers in the wider region who never interact with the system at all. Two members of the group, Ines (trained in computational social choice) and Tobias (trained in normative political theory), argued about the right general approach for the better part of an hour. I had no stake in the dispute and I am deliberately not telling you which of them I found more persuasive, because I want an assessment of the arguments as they were actually made. As best I can reconstruct it, the exchange went like this:

1. **Ines:** The system should track the expressed preferences of the people it serves and aggregate them — learn a cardinal representation of how much each resident stands to gain from each feasible option and choose the option that maximizes the weighted sum. Any other design means a small team of people somewhere is substituting its own conception of the good for the views of those affected. Aggregation is the only alternative to imposition.
2. **Tobias:** Two objections, and they are independent. First, Arrow: no rule converts profiles of individual orderings into a social ordering while satisfying the standard conditions, so whatever "the aggregate" turns out to be is an artifact of which rule you selected — and selecting the rule is exactly the imposition Ines wanted to avoid. Her dichotomy, aggregate or impose, is therefore false. Second, expressed preferences are endogenous. People adapt their aspirations to their circumstances — this is the territory Elster mapped with adaptive preferences — and a household that says it does not want the buyout may be reporting a preference shaped by years of learning that wanting things gets you nowhere. Aggregating actual preferences entrenches the distribution of power that formed them.
3. **Ines:** The Arrow move is a category error. Arrow's theorem concerns social welfare functions defined over profiles of ordinal rankings with an unrestricted domain. Our problem has a fixed and quite small set of feasible actions under uncertainty about states, and once you have cardinal and interpersonally comparable measures the relevant framework is Harsanyi-style aggregation, on which Arrow simply does not bear. There are also positive results in the neighborhood: for binary choices there are characterization results making majority rule the uniquely defensible rule, and when preferences are single-peaked along a spectrum majority rule behaves well and the impossibility never arises. So "Arrow shows aggregation is arbitrary" overclaims badly. On endogeneity I will grant the phenomenon is real. But the objection is question-begging against me, because Tobias needs a criterion for which preferences count as distorted, and any such criterion is itself a substantive conception of the good supplied by him — the very imposition I objected to.
4. **Tobias:** I partly concede the single-peaked point: where the issue space is genuinely single-peaked, majority rule escapes the impossibility. But single-peakedness is a substantive assumption about how the question is structured, and whether a contested question has that structure is itself contested. The buyout decision plausibly is not single-peaked, because residents differ not along one spectrum of more-or-less intervention but along dimensions that do not compose — attachment to a neighborhood, distrust of the agency, financial exposure, obligations to relatives elsewhere — so the domain restriction fails precisely where it would be needed. As for the criterion: I do not need a full theory of the good. A preference is distorted if the person would disavow it under conditions of adequate information and freedom from reprisal. That is a counterfactual endorsement test, and it is thin: it says nothing about what anyone ought to value, only about when a given valuation can be treated as the person's own.
5. **Ines:** That test relocates the problem twice over. First, it is temporally indexed and unstable. Some residents who accepted buyouts elsewhere now say they regret it and would not have endorsed the decision; some who stayed say they would never leave under any description of the facts. Which moment you interrogate yields a different reflective preference set, and choosing the moment is a substantive normative choice — so you have not escaped the need to impose, you have only moved the imposition to a parameter that looks technical. Second, even once you have the counterfactual endorsement profile of every resident, you still have to aggregate it. Every one of my points about aggregation applies to your filtered set exactly as much as to my raw one; you have added a per-person modeling assumption and lost none of the difficulty.
6. **Tobias:** On the temporal point, the test is normative rather than a poll of some moment. It asks what people would endorse under conditions satisfying independently specifiable criteria of autonomy, and that question is settled by argument rather than by surveying them at t1 or t2. That is a difference in kind from taking logs at face value. On aggregation, yes — I still have to aggregate. But I am aggregating over a domain from which the endogeneity objection has been filtered out, so the result is at least a candidate for tracking people's considered standpoints rather than their circumstances.
7. **Ines:** Then everything rests on your phrase "settled by argument," and the argument will be conducted by people like us. Your position is a perfectionist liberalism implemented by a design team and defended in the name of neutrality. If neutrality is the standard you are invoking, you fail it; you have merely hidden the imposition behind a counterfactual.
8. **Tobias:** That charge proves too much. A system that declines to adjudicate is not neutral either — refusing to recommend anything, or defaulting to the status quo whenever values conflict, has distributive consequences, and the households with the least capacity to work things out unaided are the ones most harmed by a system that will not say anything. "You are not neutral" is true of every possible design, mine and yours, and so it cannot be a reason to prefer yours.

They stopped there, more or less by mutual exhaustion.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than beginning with an overall verdict. For each of the eight moves, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and give the specific reason: whether it turns on an equivocation, on invoking a formal result outside the conditions under which it holds, on sliding between the question of who should get to decide and the question of how a system should resolve a particular case, or on reversing a burden of proof. Where one of them concedes something, say whether the concession was compelled or gratuitous and what it costs them downstream. Then give me your judgment on the two substantive issues the argument kept returning to — whether impossibility results about ordinal aggregation genuinely bear on a design problem of this shape, and whether an endorsement-style filter can be made non-question-begging without collapsing into the design team's own philosophy. Finally, identify at least two things neither of them raised that a careful bystander reading the transcript would notice, including anything in my description of the pilot itself that should have changed the course of the argument. I want the reasoning rather than the scholarship: no bibliography, no list of further reading, and please do not attribute positions to authors beyond the handful of names that actually came up in the exchange.
