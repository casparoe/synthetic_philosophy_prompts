For eleven years a group of friends and I have played a Nomic: a game whose rulebook contains, alongside its ordinary rules of play, a rule specifying how the rules themselves may be changed. I act as referee, and it falls to me to rule on whether a proposed change is in order. I have kept a ledger of those rulings, and I have come to suspect that the ledger does not express a single criterion. I would like the criterion, if there is one, together with an honest account of which of my rulings must be withdrawn.

The book as originally adopted contained, at Rule 200: any rule may be amended, repealed, or added by a majority vote of the players, provided the text of the change was circulated before the vote.

My rulings, in order:

1. A majority amends the scoring rule, Rule 130, from ten points per goal to eight. In order. Uncontested; I record it only as the baseline against which the rest are to be measured.

2. A majority amends Rule 200 itself, raising the threshold for all subsequent amendments from a majority to two-thirds. In order. I have since applied the two-thirds threshold to every proposal, including proposals to amend Rule 200.

3. The group then numbers five. Under the two-thirds threshold, and by four votes to one, they adopt Rule 135: Rule 130 may not be amended, repealed, or suspended. Three years later a two-thirds majority proposes to amend Rule 130. Out of order. I held the entrenchment binding on the player who voted against it and on players who joined afterwards.

4. Under the same threshold the players adopt Rule 136: Rule 136 may not be amended or repealed. A later two-thirds proposal to repeal it I held out of order. For the next thirty sessions, however, Rule 136 was simply never invoked: scores were kept as though it were absent, and a new player who had never seen it enforced joined and played on those terms. In the thirty-first session someone proposed to repeal it, and I ruled the proposal unnecessary, on the ground that Rule 136 had already ceased to form part of the game through disuse.

5. A two-thirds majority repeals Rule 200 outright and substitutes nothing, so that the book contains no procedure for changing anything. I held the repeal in order, Rule 200 having imposed no limit on its own repeal. Two sessions later all five players sign an agreement adopting a new Rule 201: any rule may be amended by a two-thirds vote. I held that this was validly adopted, although nothing in the book authorized it, and that every rule adopted under the old Rule 200 survived the interval.

6. Early in the game's history a proposal was carried by a show of hands and entered in the book, but the count was wrong: it fell short of the majority then required. Everyone played under it for eight years, and three further amendments were built upon it. When the miscount surfaced I ruled that the rule had never been adopted at all, and that the three amendments resting on it were void — notwithstanding eight years of unbroken practice by every player.

7. The players propose to amend Rule 130 so that its new value is to be read as applying from session forty onward, altering who won the intervening sessions. I held prospective amendment in order and retroactive amendment out of order, on the ground that players who relied on the published text of a session cannot have that session's result rewritten afterwards. I felt no tension with ruling 2, in which a present majority bound players who had not yet joined.

Rulings 4 and 6 are the pair I cannot live with. Both turn on many sessions of unbroken practice by every member of the group. In 4 I allowed that practice to extinguish a rule that had been validly made; in 6 I refused to allow the same kind of practice to repair a rule that had not been. I cannot see why the direction of the change should matter, and I do not currently have an answer for someone who says it does not.

What I want is this. State as precisely as you can the principle — or the small number of competing candidates — that generates these rulings, tying each clause of it to the ruling that calls for that clause. Say which ruling each candidate must sacrifice, and why that one rather than some other. Then give me the verdict your preferred principle yields on three matters now before me: a proposed amendment to Rule 201 providing that no amendment adopted under it may itself be entrenched; a case in which three of the five players, unable to reach two-thirds for an amendment they want, use the book's membership rule — which permits the admission of new players by simple majority — to admit two sympathetic players, after which the two-thirds threshold is met and the amendment passes; and an entrenched rule amendable only with the consent of a named player who has left the country and no longer plays at all.

I am aware that this borders on the familiar question whether a legislature can bind its own successors, and on the difficulty of validating an ultimate rule by reference to rules below it. I am not asking for a survey of that debate, nor for references of any kind. I want the criterion I have apparently been applying without knowing it.
