I serve on the coordinating council of a mountain-region search and rescue association, and I would like a careful assessment of an argument that I cannot quite get a handle on.

Six independent volunteer teams cover our region. Each owns its own vehicles and equipment, recruits its own members, and decides for itself whether to respond to a call. Last autumn two teams independently committed to the same missing-hiker search in one valley while an adjacent valley, where a second party had also gone overdue, went uncovered for nine hours; both of those hikers died of exposure. In a separate incident, one team launched a helicopter into a canyon that another team knew, from its own weather station and its own radio traffic, was closing in on zero visibility.

Our state's emergency-management office has offered a substantial grant, but only to a single accountable entity. The council chair has drafted a proposal accordingly: the six teams would surrender their independent dispatch authority and create a permanent regional command with final say over every deployment, every training standard, and every piece of shared equipment. The command would select its own successors. Member teams would be required to route all radio traffic through the command's repeater network, and any team that declined to join — or that later withdrew — would lose repeater access and its state registration, which in practice means it could not operate here at all. Three of the five regions adjoining ours have already consolidated on similar terms, so there is no neighboring association a dissenting team could realistically defect to.

The chair's argument, which I have now heard many times, is this: every catastrophe in our history arose from two groups acting separately, each unable to see what the other was doing; a single decider that sees everything and can direct everyone is the only arrangement under which such failures become impossible rather than merely less likely. He adds that the principle is not local to us — fragmentation, he says, is the root cause of avoidable disaster at any scale whatsoever. And he thinks the abuse objection is sentimental, since the first regional commander would be Marta, who has thirty years of service and everyone's trust.

Two members object. One says the association's real safety has always come from having six separate judgments in the field: when one team is wrong, some other team usually notices and does something else, and after consolidation there will be nothing outside the decision that can catch it. The other says the issue is not Marta but the fifty years after her, and that a body with the power to expel rivals and choose its own successors will never have any reason to hand anything back.

I find the chair's argument forceful and the objections vague, and I suspect I have it backwards. Is his reasoning sound? Specifically: does a single decider actually eliminate the failures he points to, or relocate them; and do the two objections identify a genuine defect in his case, or merely risks that the right safeguards — fixed terms, external review, a sunset clause — could manage? What should I press for, or against, at Saturday's vote?
