For the past seven years I have run cost-effectiveness analyses for a large academic medical center and its affiliated insurer — QALYs, incremental cost-effectiveness ratios, willingness-to-pay thresholds, and, because our ethics committee will not let us rank programs on aggregate health gains alone, increasingly distributional cost-effectiveness analysis with severity and priority weights. I also teach the graduate social choice sequence: preference profiles, aggregation rules and their axioms, Arrow, Sen, Harsanyi, Bergson–Samuelson social welfare functions in their additive, maximin, and lexicographic varieties, ordinal versus cardinal utility, and what does and does not follow from interpersonal comparability. So every instrument I reach for is an instrument of summation; I aggregate weighted individual gains for a living. A colleague in the philosophy department insists there is a moral requirement my instruments cannot express, and she pointed me at Scanlonian contractualism and what is apparently called the aggregation problem. I read the opening chapters of *What We Owe to Each Other*, found them slow going, and have not touched the secondary literature.

My grasp of the target position, which you should correct wherever it is mistaken rather than translate my mistakes along with it: an act is wrong when it would be ruled out by principles for the general regulation of conduct that no one could reasonably reject; the reasons that figure in that test are individual complaints, each attached to a particular person; and those complaints are compared pairwise, strongest against strongest, rather than added across persons. Hence the problem case — I can either save one person from death or spare a very large number of people a serious but non-fatal harm — where the contractualist declines to let the million be summed against the one, and where various repairs are offered: numbers matter once the complaints on the two sides are equal or close enough in magnitude; some threshold of relevant similarity must be crossed before complaints are comparable at all; or what really does the work is how many individual complaints go unmatched. Against that background, three conjectures of mine that I want tested rather than flattered. First: contractualism is Rawlsian maximin over a vector of complaint magnitudes — a lexicographic social welfare function — the aggregation problem is simply the familiar objection that maximin violates an Archimedean or continuity condition, and "reasonable rejection" is decoration on a non-Archimedean ordering. Second: "reasonable rejection" is doing real work as a filter on which claims get counted at all, which would make contractualism my own severity-weighted distributional analysis with an extreme weighting function, and therefore hostage to the same arbitrariness complaints I already field from clinicians. Third: pairwise comparison cannot satisfy anonymity, since a person's complaint is treated differently depending on how many others happen to share it, so contractualism is either committed to coin-flipping between equal claims or is counting surreptitiously.

Here is what I want from you. Restate the position, the argument for it, and the aggregation problem in the vocabulary described above, as faithfully as you can, and keep the translation honest: do not render "reasonable rejection" as "preference," or "complaint" as "utility," and then report as a discovery that contractualism turns out to be preference-based and welfarist after all — flag each place where one of my terms has to be bent to carry one of theirs. Then give me the residue report in three parts: (a) what maps cleanly, identifying for each item the counterpart object in my framework, whether that is a rule, an axiom, a weighting scheme, an impossibility result, or an ex ante versus ex post distinction; (b) what maps only with distortion, saying precisely what is lost or added and whether the distortion changes the verdict in any case I could actually face; and (c) what has no counterpart at all. For part (c), take up four candidates specifically: the second-personal element, that the test is whether a principle could be justified to each person rather than whether it produces a good profile; the fact that the objects being compared are general principles whose consequences depend on general acceptance, not allocations of a fixed pie; the fact that reasonableness is a normative standard applied to reasons rather than a description of anyone's actual attitudes; and the fact that a complaint may be grounded in something other than the complainer's own well-being. For each, tell me whether the failure is a limit of social choice — something my framework genuinely cannot represent — or evidence that the contractualist claim was less determinate than it sounded. Then run one worked case through both frameworks, say a fixed budget split between an intervention that saves a small number of identified patients from imminent death and one that spares a much larger number from a moderate chronic condition, and show me where the two computations actually diverge and where they are the same computation described twice. Finally, adjudicate the three conjectures: which is closest to right, which distorts the view, and which cannot even be formulated in my language. What I am trying to determine is whether there is a substantive dispute here with consequences for how I rank programs, or whether my colleague has rediscovered the Archimedean property and given it a moral name.
