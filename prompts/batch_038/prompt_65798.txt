So there's this thing in British broadcasting that I keep going round in circles about, and I want to know what it actually commits us to.

You can't buy a political advert on TV or radio here. Not just party ads — the prohibition is drawn much wider than that. It catches anything aimed at a political end, which the rules read broadly enough to cover influencing public opinion on any matter that's currently controversial in the UK, plus anything placed by a body whose objects are mainly political, plus anything connected to an industrial dispute. Parties instead get free slots allocated to them at election time, so the airwaves carry party messages but nobody can pay to add more. The famous illustration: Iceland's 2018 Christmas ad, an animated thing about an orangutan and palm oil made with Greenpeace, never got cleared for broadcast — not because of anything it said or how it said it, but because Greenpeace counts as a political body.

Meanwhile, on exactly the same screens, any amount of commercial advertising runs, using every technique of association, urgency and flattery you can think of, and nobody calls that political. Newspapers can be as partisan as they like — there's no impartiality requirement in print at all, whereas broadcasters have to be duly impartial on matters of controversy, so the same media company is scrupulously even-handed on air and blatantly one-sided in its titles. Spending on election campaigning is capped; owning a masthead isn't. And the whole thing sits alongside online political advertising, including the microtargeted kind, which is nowhere near as tightly controlled.

What picture of the difference between manipulation and legitimate persuasion does this practice presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not just named. Is the idea that broadcast is a channel that bypasses reflection while print is one you opt into knowingly, so the same message is legitimate in one and illegitimate in the other? If so, does that story survive the fact that spectrum scarcity is long gone and the identical ad is on YouTube for free? Or is the worry not technique at all but money — that the objection is to buying reach rather than to how reach is used, which would explain the spending caps and the free allocated slots but not the orangutan? Or is it something about audiences being unable to discount a source they can't identify?

Tell me which of these the practice strictly needs and which it merely happens to fit, and where it's genuinely underdetermined so that several rival accounts would license the same rules. Then say whether the parts hang together: can one theory simultaneously explain why a charity's rainforest film is refused clearance while a chocolate ad trading on sex and nostalgia sails through, and why buying influence is forbidden but owning it is fine? And finally, what would have to change in practice if we swapped the presupposed account for one that treats voters as fully capable of weighing sources themselves, versus one that treats all paid attention-capture as manipulative by default — I suspect those two pull in opposite directions and I can't work out which one the current rules are closer too.
