I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar and our final paper is due in about ten days. It has to engage with panpsychism and the combination problem, and it has to actually take a side rather than just survey the terrain. Could you write the essay for me, or at least a really solid draft I can argue with? Here's the setup and the specific thing I want it to turn on.

Start from the standard framing: microphenomenal (or protophenomenal) properties are supposed to *constitute* the phenomenal properties of macrosubjects like us — not cause them, not merely correlate with them. That's what makes the combination problem so awkward. It isn't a question of how micro-causation produces big experiences, it's a question of how lots of little subjectivities could add up to, or ground, one big subjectivity with a single unified experiential field. The usual moves are roughly (a) brute emergence — insist that certain micro-arrangements ground macrosubjects as a basic law and stop there, (b) deny that we are subjects in the fundamental sense and go cosmopsychist, so there's one subject and we're partial perspectives on it, or (c) say subjecthood isn't the thing that combines at all and combine qualities instead, panqualityism style.

Now the case I want the essay built around. I made it up for this paper so please don't go hunting for it in the literature, and don't feel you need to cite anything at all — I just want the argument. Call it the unbinding case. Suppose a neurosurgeon with very fancy nanotech can, over the course of an hour, progressively "unbind" a conscious human subject: at each stage, some set of neural micro-processes that used to be integrated into the subject's single experiential field instead becomes the subject of its own maximally simple experience, with integration between it and the others switched off, and no new macrosubject forming in its place. At the end there's no human subject at all, just a swarm of trillions of microsubjects each having an incredibly dim, nearly-nothing experience, while the body keeps functioning as a well-coordinated automaton. Then run it backwards: re-bind the swarm, and a human subject reappears with its memories intact.

The point I want pressed hard is this. Describe the micro-side facts at each moment of the unbinding. Arguably nothing changes about what each individual microsubject is like, or about the qualitative palette on offer. What changes is only whether those microsubjects are co-members of some larger subject. So what does the binding add? If a macrosubject just is the plurality of microsubjects standing in an integration relation, then the macro-experience should be fully accounted for by the micro-experiences plus that relation — but a relation between subjects looks external to each of them, and it's not obviously the sort of thing that could make their experiential fields literally one field. If instead binding does add something, some experiential property present in none of the microsubjects and not merely their sum, then panpsychism has smuggled in exactly the brute emergence it advertised itself as avoiding — and worse, the unbinding case gives us a dial on which we can watch a subject come into and go out of existance by turning a knob that is, ex hypothesi, not a phenomenal one. Either way it looks bad, and I want to know whether that's really right.
