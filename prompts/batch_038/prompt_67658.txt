I have sat on the governing committee of a private club in this city for six years, and I should be grateful for your judgement on a matter that comes to a vote next Thursday. My own academic work is in the philosophy of language; I make no claim to have thought this question through, which is precisely why I want your assessment rather than my own.

The club is a non-commercial association of some four hundred members, founded in the 1890s, which owns a house with a dining room and a library and elects new members by secret ballot. The proposal before us would amend the bylaws to render ineligible for election any person whose living is, or has been, earned by gambling. The occasion is particular rather than hypothetical: a professional poker player in his early forties — twenty years at the tables, no convictions, no civil judgements, taxes paid, three members willing to propose and second him — has already cleared the first ballot.

The leader of the objecting faction was, to his credit, entirely candid about his grounds. He did not allege that this man had injured anyone. He did not allege that admitting him would cost the club a penny or drive a single member away; indeed he expressly declined to rest his case on either point. His claim was that a club exists to confer honour on a certain sort of life, that a life devoted to the tables is not that sort, and that to admit the man would be for all of us to declare something about ourselves that he is not prepared to declare. The opposing faction answers that if nothing illegal has been done and no one has been wronged, the club has no standing to inquire further, and that what is really on offer is dislike dressed in the language of principle.

Three features of the situation strike me as bearing on the matter, though I am unsure how. First, the club is voluntary in a way a polity is not: no one is compelled to join, and a member who dislikes the outcome may resign at the cost of his subscription and nothing more. Second, membership is not merely ornamental here; in this city it operates as a credential, and there are professions whose entry depends on introductions of just the kind that members supply. Third, a compromise has been floated — elect the man, but render him ineligible for committee service and for the club's two honorary distinctions — and my instinct is that this is worse than either of the positions it aims to reconcile, though I have not managed to articulate why.

Three questions, then. May a body of this kind properly act on the objectors' reason, and would your answer differ if the identical reason were offered in support of a statute rather than a club rule? Which carries greater weight in your judgement: the club's voluntary character, or its power over the prospects of people who are not and may never be members? And is my instinct about the compromise sound — if so, what exactly is objectionable about it?
