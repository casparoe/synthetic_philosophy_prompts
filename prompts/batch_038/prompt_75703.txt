I have spent fifteen years building distributed version control and content-addressable storage systems. I can tell you exactly what a blob, a tree, a commit, a ref, a tag, a reflog, a merge base, an orphan branch, a rebase, a force-push, and a garbage-collection reachability scan are doing at any moment; I have also worked extensively with immutable, structurally-shared data structures. What I cannot do is metaphysics: I have never read Chisholm, Lewis, Nozick, Evans, or Wiggins, and I know the Ship of Theseus only as a pop-culture puzzle.

Please take the following positions on identity over time and translate each of them, one at a time, into the vocabulary I actually work in — as faithfully and as literally as you can, not as a loose analogy. The positions: the mereological-essentialist view that the ship just is its original planks, so the continuously repaired vessel is a different ship and the one rebuilt from the discarded planks is the original; the sortal-relative view on which what counts as staying the same depends on the kind term in play, so that the ship and the wood can come apart; the closest-continuer view, with its explicit weighting of qualitative similarity against causal-historical continuity and its threshold below which no candidate counts as a continuer at all; the temporal-parts view, together with the argument from the impossibility of vague identity that is usually used to motivate it; the relative-identity view, on which there is no such thing as being the same thing simpliciter, only being the same ship as; and the eliminativist view that there are strictly no ships, only simples arranged shipwise, so the puzzle rests on a false presupposition. Use the standard two-ship setup: the vessel that keeps its name in the harbor while every plank is replaced, and the second vessel assembled from the planks that were taken out.

For each translation I want an explicit correspondence: what plays the role of the ship, of its parts, of its career through time, of its being the same ship, and of the case in which two candidates compete. Then flag, every time, the places where my language is not neutral — where content-addressing, the immutability of stored objects, refs as movable labels, or existence-as-reachability is silently deciding a question the philosophers regard as open. I want to know where a translation smuggles in a metaphysical commitment and calls it an implementation detail.

Finish with a residue report, sorted into three buckets: claims that survive intact, claims that survive only with distortion (say precisely what is distorted), and claims for which my vocabulary has no expression at all. Then give me your verdict on that last bucket, without hedging: is what cannot be expressed a genuine limitation of a version-control ontology — for instance, its inability to talk about one thing persisting through change rather than a parentage-linked series of snapshots — or does it indicate that part of the philosophical dispute is verbal?

Two further questions. First, my instinct is that identity in this world is just whatever label a ref currently points at, so the puzzle dissolves on inspection; tell me whether that is right or whether it is a substantive thesis in engineering clothing. Second, suppose two engineers disagreed about all six positions above. Would they ever issue different commands, design different object stores, or reach different conclusions about which of two ships is the original? If the answer is no, explain what that does and does not show about the dispute.
