I have just started a postdoc in a philosophy-of-biology group that works closely with a marine ecology laboratory, and my first real task has turned into something I would rather not get wrong. Three months ago I moved from a purely philosophical training into an interdisciplinary project, and my supervisor — I will call her Professor Reinhart, though the name matters less than the fact that she has thought about the levels-of-selection debate for thirty years and is impatient with vagueness — has asked me to draft the framing section of a grant application in which we must state, explicitly, whether our study system counts as an individual organism or merely as a collection of organisms that happens to be well integrated. My own inclination, formed mostly from the ecology literature and probably too quickly, is that it does count. Her group meeting is on Thursday, and I want to know before I walk in what she is likely to say, and more importantly which criterion she is actually running, because the framing has to be written in terms she will accept and in terms an ecology panel will follow. So I would like you to do something quite specific: take her recorded verdicts on a series of earlier cases, work out which rule or rules they are jointly consistent with, and predict her verdict on my system.

Here is her track record, as far as I have been able to reconstruct it from her papers, her seminar comments and my own supervision notes. When the topic was a mouse carrying a meiotic driver that cheats at segregation, she said the driver allele is the entity whose fitness is being maximised in that context, but she added immediately that this does not make the mouse any less of an organism, since in her view being a unit of selection in a given case and being an individual are separable questions. Asked about a honeybee colony headed by a singly mated queen with worker policing intact, she said yes without hesitation, a genuine higher-level individual, and she gave two reasons in the same breath: that the colony begins its life from one fertilised founder, and that the workers' own reproduction is suppressed so that their effort is redirected into the colony's output. On a mixed-genotype biofilm assembled largely by horizontal gene transfer and held together by public-goods sharing, she said no, that is an aggregate, stressing that the participants are unrelated and that nothing keeps the composition stable across generations. On laboratory-evolved multicellular yeast clusters that propagate by a single-cell bottleneck generated by the programmed death of one cell, she was emphatic that this is a new Darwinian individual arising in real time, again citing both the bottleneck and the sacrificial division of labour. On a fossil lineage in which species bearing a particular shell character speciate more often than their relatives, she conceded that the differential sorting is real but refused to call the species an individual in anything like the organism's sense, because nothing at that level reproduces in a structured way and there is no internal integration. On an ant colony founded by two unrelated queens that cooperate at first and later fight, she called it a temporary alliance rather than an individual, and observed that cooperation collapses as soon as the queens' own offspring begin to compete. And on mitochondria she said that the eukaryotic cell is the individual and the organelle is no longer a unit in its own right, pointing to uniparental transmission through a single-cell stage and to the fact that the organelle's reproductive success now rides entirely on the cell's.

My system is this. We study a meadow of a clonal marine flowering plant off a temperate coast, and genetic markers show that the entire sampled area, roughly eight hectares, is a single genet propagated by rhizome extension. The ramets are physiologically integrated: carbohydrates and nutrients move between them, and a shaded or grazed ramet is measurably subsidised by its neighbours. Clonal propagation has no single-cell stage, because new ramets bud from multicellular meristems, but the plant also flowers, and seeds do pass through a single-celled zygote. There is no segregated germ line, so a somatic mutation arising in a rhizome tip can appear in the flowers that shoot later produces; sequencing along a transect has turned up a handful of somatic variants, each confined to a patch a few metres across, and growth-rate estimates suggest one of them has been present for at least three decades. There is a division of labour of sorts, with some ramets investing in flowering and others in vegetative spread, though the roles are not fixed and the same ramet can switch from one year to the next. Ramets differ substantially in growth and reproductive output, yet because of the subsidisation a ramet's own performance predicts its contribution to future ramets only weakly, so most though not all of the variance in ramet-level fitness is erased by sharing. One somatic variant lineage looks as though it takes more than it gives: fragments carrying it outgrow their neighbours in a common-garden tank when resources are abundant, and its patch has expanded over the sampling period. Storms break pieces off the meadow, and those pieces establish new meadows; when we replant fragments taken from different parts of our own meadow in a common garden, differences in clonal architecture and growth rate persist for at least two years, which looks like meadow-level heredity through multicellular propagation. Finally, at the edge of the meadow our genet is losing ground to an unrelated neighbouring genet.

Please approach this as a philosopher of biology who has spent a career on the units-of-selection and biological-individuality debates and who has also mediated disagreements of exactly this kind between supervisors and students, and think carefully rather than reaching for the first criterion that comes to mind: begin by brainstorming the full range of candidate rules that might be running underneath her verdicts, including the possibility that she is a pluralist of some kind, then prune to those that genuinely reproduce every one of the seven verdicts above rather than most of them. For each surviving rule, say what it implies about my meadow and which of the features I have listed it turns on, noting where the rules diverge rather than merely agreeing in a general yes or no. Then commit: give me your best guess at what she will actually say on Thursday, and explain what makes that guess better supported than the alternatives. Tell me also which single further case would discriminate most sharply between the surviving rules, describing it concretely enough that I could imagine presenting it to her, and tell me which of my facts she is most likely to seize on first and which I should therefore lead with. If your prediction is that she will say no, say what principle our project would be adopting by continuing to use the meadow as our unit of measurement anyway, so that I can state it honestly in the framing section rather than letting it be discovered by a reviewer. I should mention one constraint on how you reply: I will be reading this on a train and later forwarding it to a colleague, so please write it as continuous prose in the manner of a letter or a long email, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables.
