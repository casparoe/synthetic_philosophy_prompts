I am preparing a ninety-minute session for a graduate reading group in philosophy of mind: eleven participants, all of whom have read the standard introductory treatments of panpsychism and can recite the phrase "combination problem" on demand, but none of whom, I am confident, has ever felt it as an obstacle. They treat it as a dialectical move one side makes against the other rather than as a constraint that would defeat them personally. I want an activity that manufactures that experience.

To that end, I have sketched the resistance as three separable failure modes, in the order I want the group to hit them. The labels are mine, invented for this session, and I would like you to tell me whether they carve the problem at the joints or whether I have quietly run two things together:

1. *The palette failure.* The group is given a stock of micro-qualities and asked to construct a specified target experience out of them. Every assembly rule they reach for — mixing, superposition, spatial arrangement, nesting — either yields a character that none of the parts had, which is emergence smuggled back in through the assembly rule, or yields something merely aggregate, a list of items rather than a single field.

2. *The audience failure.* Grant them, for the sake of argument, a successful blend of qualities. There is still no subject for whom the blend is a blend: they must produce one "there is something it is like to be" out of many, and cannot do so without positing something that was not among the parts.

3. *The dilution failure.* The obvious escape from (2) — there was only ever one subject, and the finite ones are aspects or modes of it — clears the audience step, but then cannot say what makes this aspect have precisely these qualities and be insulated from those. The unity that solved (2) now over-explains everything.

Are (1) and (2) genuinely distinct, or is (1) simply (2) viewed from the side of the qualities? And is (3) a real cost of cosmopsychism, or does it just re-impose the requirement that generated the original problem? Answer that before designing anything, because the design depends on it.

Then design the activity. Constraints: no lecture and no framing talk beforehand; the words "panpsychism," "combination problem," and "cosmopsychism" are not spoken until the debrief; each failure mode must be produced by the rules, the materials, or the role structure rather than announced by me; the stakes should be social or procedural — a public ledger, a committed prediction, a recorded verdict — rather than money; and participants should be able to believe, midway through, that they are solving a construction puzzle rather than refuting a metaphysics. Give me the rules, the card or diagram set in enough detail that I could fabricate it, the roles and groupings for eleven people, and a timeline across the ninety minutes.

I also want a candid diagnosis of two things. First, the cheat problem: my participants are themselves conscious, so any gap in the card set can be filled by their own acquaintance with what the qualities are like, and the failures will then look like artifacts of the impoverishment of my materials rather than of the view. Tell me how to detect and block this in the rules, or tell me plainly that I cannot. Second, the direction of the bias: since the micro-qualities must be represented in words or diagrams, my materials are structural stand-ins for items the view holds to be non-structural. Does that make combination look harder than it is, because representations do not blend in the way the things they represent might, or easier, because it lets participants treat the palette failure as a merely notational mishap? Is there a design change that neutralizes this, or should I concede it?

For the debrief, map named moments of the activity — I want you to specify them, e.g. the point at which the group has to record whether the assembly has one subject or eleven — onto parts of the argument. Then close by telling them what each of the three standard replies (a bonding relation that grounds subject composition, constitution of larger subjects by smaller ones, and the single-subject option) would have required *changed in the rules* in order to succeed, so that they leave seeing these as design choices with costs rather than as competing opinions. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce at all and that I will simply have to supply myself.
