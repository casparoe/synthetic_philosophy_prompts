I'm the programming and grants lead at a small county heritage trust — think two full-time officers, a shared office with the local history society, and a volunteer committee of eleven lay trustees who ratify everything. Every year we get somewhere around forty applications that lean on the word "tradition," and right now we decide them by vibes and by whoever on the committee shouts loudest. Three things happen in a typical file: a parish council wants money to run a ceremony they say is centuries old; a tourism board wants heritage signage that makes a continuity claim in a single sentence; and occasionally we get handed something that's openly brand new and nobody knows what to do with it. Each application gets about forty-five minutes of officer time before it goes to committee, we can't pay for more than roughly one officer-day of archival digging per case, and when we refuse something the reason goes out in writing and sometimes into the parish newsletter. Last spring the local paper ran a piece calling us "the authenticity police" after we turned down a maypole revival, and honestly I can see both sides of that.

What I want is a decision procedure my team and the lay committee can actually run, grounded in what Hobsbawm and Anderson actually say rather than the pop version — but translated, so nobody on the committee has to read anything. There's a bit in Hobsbawm where he separates tradition out from custom and from mere routine or convention, and I'm fairly sure that's the load-bearing distinction for us, but I only half-remember how he draws the lines, so please get it right and tell me if the distinction is one I should be using at all. I also want Anderson's contribution to survive in some usable form: specifically the point that a community being imagined doesn't make it a fake or a fraud, and that invented traditions are usually responses to genuinely new social conditions rather than lies dressed up as old ones. If the procedure can't hold onto that, it'll turn into the authenticity-police thing again, and it'll also structurally favour the traditions that happen to have left written records — the propertied, literate, church-adjacent ones — over ones that were transmitted orally or whose paperwork got burned, flooded, or filed in a country that no longer exists. Tell me how to handle the silence problem: an empty file is not proof that nothing happened, but we still have to say yes or no. Two other things to fold in. First, our officer before me left behind a scoring sheet with five bands from "no continuity demonstrated" to "unbroken documented practice," and the procedure has to map onto those bands without pretending the bands measure anything real. Second, a colleague wants to import what she calls a transmission-chain test — can you name, for each generation, a person or institution that passed the practice on, with no gap longer than a lifetime? I've written it up in our internal guidance as "the provenance gap," which I now suspect is a made-up term doing no work. Keep, revise, or kill that test and say which.

Run the procedure on three files. (1) Ashcombe St Mary parish council, £4,800 for a winter orchard blessing they describe in the application as running "since Tudor times": the file contains one sentence in a Victorian antiquary's 1878 pamphlet on county customs, a 1954 parish newsletter mention of a one-off event, an order of service drafted in 2016 by the WI and the pub landlord, and nothing at all in between. (2) The borough tourism board, £2,200 for new signage for the town's "ancient charter fair, held since time immemorial" — our solicitor keeps muttering that time immemorial has an actual legal date attached to it — where the file shows a market charter of 1274, a court record of the last proclamation in 1841, an improvement act in 1846 that fenced off the common, a Rotary Club revival in 1998, and a "traditional" spiced pasty that the applicant's own publicity dates to a 1974 recipe competition. (3) The one I'm braced for: our own flagship event, the Victorian Christmas at the Hall, which we've run for thirty-one years and whose gate plaque, website and sponsorship leaflet all describe it as a tradition of the estate; a supermarket has offered us £14,000 a year for three years conditional on the copy staying as it is. Give me a verdict line for each plus one sentence of public-facing wording we could actually send to the applicant.

Format it like this, in this order: Bottom line (five sentences, the spine of the procedure, no preamble); What survives the translation (about 350 words on which distinctions from the two books you're keeping, which you're dropping for a lay audience, and why the dropped ones don't matter here); The procedure (numbered gates, roughly 700 words — for each gate, the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where the gate exits and to what); Evidence and silence (about 250 words, including what to do with an empty file and with oral testimony); Where it hands over to people (about 200 words on the calls that are values rather than facts, who signs off, and how a refusal gets worded so it isn't read as calling the applicants liars); Where this will misfire (about 250 words, be blunt — including the cases where it punishes undocumented communities and where it lets a confident liar through); Worked cases (three, about 250 words each, verdict line plus the public sentence); The one-pager (under a page, printable, for the committee table). No bibliography and please don't invent page numbers — if you're not sure Hobsbawm or Anderson said something, say so rather than guessing.
