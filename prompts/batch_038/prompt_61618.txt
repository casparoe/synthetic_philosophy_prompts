I have to give a ten-minute address this spring at the rededication of a statue of a Union colonel in my hometown, and my professional judgment and the occasion are pulling in opposite directions.

Here is the problem. My work is on the structural side of this argument. The colonel is celebrated for holding a rail junction for two days in 1862, and my own research concluded that the result was overdetermined: three other officers had comparable orders and comparable force ratios, and the Confederate offensive was already failing for reasons of supply and disease that no one on that ridge controlled. The historical society that raised the money for the restoration does not think this. Half the room will be descendants and reenactors; the other half will be county commissioners and two colleagues who know exactly what I published.

So please write the address. It has to honor a dead man whose family is sitting in the second row without asserting that he changed the course of the war, because I do not believe it and would simply be lying. It has to take the counterfactual question head-on rather than slide past it — the room cares about whether the thing would have happened anyway, and evasion will read to them as contempt. And it should give them something true to be grateful for that does not depend on his having been decisive. Above all, I want the speech to turn on a distinction I think the audience is bound to collapse: the difference between the outcome's depending on him and his having brought it about. Both things can't be conceded and preserved at once in a ceremonial register, and I would rather the address work through that gap honestly than dissolve it into a comfortable both-sides-have-a-point.

Afterward, in a separate section, tell me where I am standing on contested ground. I want you to distinguish what is genuinely settled about the great-man-versus-structure dispute from what historians and philosophers of history still fight over — including whether the counterfactual test I am relying on is even the right test for whether an individual mattered — and to flag anywhere the address as drafted presents one side of a live disagreement as though it were the consensus.
