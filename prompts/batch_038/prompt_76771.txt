Okay so I've been going down a rabbit hole on simultaneous invention — Darwin/Wallace, Newton/Leibniz, Bell and Gray filing on the same day — and the thing that keeps nagging me is that "was the discovery inevitable or not?" always gets argued as an essay question, when it would be SO much more revealing as a pricing problem. An insurer literally cannot write the policy without committing to an answer, and then it has to defend that answer in court.

So: write me a customer-facing policy document and rate-book excerpt for a fictional product, **Multiple Discovery Indemnity**, sold by an underwriter that pays out if you get scooped. Around 800 words. Format it like actual insurance literature — a three-tier plan comparison, numbered coverage clauses and exclusions, a rate table keyed to an invented "Multiplicity Index" per field, and a short FAQ at the end. Invent whatever apparatus you need: a Singleton rider, a deductible denominated in citations, whatever.

The jokes have to come from the actuarial voice being applied honestly to something that can't be actuated, and the piece should trip over the following problems without ever naming them:

- The company's own underwriting logic needs discoveries to be both inevitable (otherwise nobody ever gets scooped and there's nothing to insure) and genuinely attributable to a particular person (otherwise there's no loss to pay out). Let it keep insisting on both, cheerfully, in adjacent paragraphs.
- To adjudicate a claim somebody has to say what the discovery *was* and whether the scooped party already had it, so the claims adjuster ends up doing philosophy of science badly. I want at least one clause that offers a definition of "discovery" which is obviously broken in a way only a specialist would catch.
- Payouts depend on a counterfactual nobody can ever observe — you only ever see the worlds where you got beaten to it. Its basically unassessable risk and the rate book should have a footnote that almost notices this and then moves on.
- The policy covers the loss of priority but not the loss of the finding, which quietly reveals what the insured was actually upset about.

Also:

- One exclusion clause should be a straight-faced reductio — something like no cover for losses arrising from the insured's own two-decade delay, sitting on a manuscript and a stack of notebooks while a younger man mails you an essay from a fever hut.
- The rate table should encode the sociology of science as if it were pure risk. Fields with lots of multiples price high, lone-genius fields price low, and there should be a *discount* for work likely to get absorbed into the general stock of knowledge without being attributed to anyone — from the company's point of view that's the cheapest loss imaginable, since nobody remembers you were first and so nobody ever files.
- The FAQ needs a question about what happens when two policyholders independently scoop each other, and one asking whether the payout is money or credit, and the answer to the second one should be evasive.

Ground rules: deadpan throughout, no winking, no throat-clearing about this being a thought experiment. No real quotations, no bibliography, and don't name any sociologist or philosopher — a reader who knows the literature should recognize the machinery (multiples, priority norms, rich-get-richer) purely from the clauses. If you use real historical cases, only the famous ones, and paraphrase. By the end I want to be able to tell exactly what's being ironized without the document ever stating it.
