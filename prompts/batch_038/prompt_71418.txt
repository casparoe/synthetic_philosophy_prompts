--- BEGIN STUDENT ANSWER ---

Hempel's paradox of the ravens concerns the hypothesis that all ravens are black. The starting point is Nicod's criterion: a universal generalization of the form "all F are G" is confirmed by observing something that is both F and G, and disconfirmed by observing something that is F and not G. So a black raven confirms the hypothesis, exactly as you'd expect.

The trouble is that "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, "all non-black things are non-ravens." Hempel also accepted what he called Nicod's criterion, the principle that whatever confirms a statement must equally confirm any statement logically equivalent to it. Put the two together and you get something ridiculous. My white sneaker is a non-black thing that is not a raven, so it is an instance of the contrapositive, and by the second principle it therefore confirms "all ravens are black." So does every green apple and every grey cloud I have ever glanced at. It seems we can do ornithology without ever looking at a bird, and that confirmation has come apart from anything deserving the name of evidence.

Hempel's own way out was to insist that the sneaker doesn't really confirm the hypothesis. Once you take into account the rest of what we know — that sneakers aren't ravens, that we already knew what it was we were looking at — the appearance of confirmation disappears, so the equivalence principle has to be restricted to cases where we don't already have that kind of background information. The paradox is then not a paradox about confirmation at all but a warning about how easily we smuggle in things we already know.

Bayesians press the same point harder. On their account, evidence confirms a hypothesis just insofar as it raises its probability, and probability is always relative to background knowledge. The sneaker does raise the probability that all ravens are black, but by an utterly negligible amount, because there are vastly more non-black non-ravens than ravens; a black raven raises it far more. So the real confusion is between confirmation as an all-or-nothing relation and confirmation as a matter of degree: the sneaker confirms, but so little that nobody should care.

That said, if the same sentence can confirm a hypothesis for one person and not for another, then confirmation can't be a purely logical relation between sentences. It's a relation between evidence and hypothesis mediated by the reasoner's whole view of the world. Which suggests the entire project of building a logic of confirmation was a mistake from the start, and that the Popperians were right: we should stop talking about degrees of confirmation and talk only about falsification. The raven paradox is one more reason to give up the idea that observations can make a theory more probable.
