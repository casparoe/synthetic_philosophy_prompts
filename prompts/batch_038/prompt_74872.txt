So here's an episode I keep circling back to: the two-term presidency. Washington set the precedent, it held for a century and a half — it survived Grant's 1880 bid for a third nomination and Teddy Roosevelt's 1912 comeback run — and then FDR just ran again in 1940 and 1944 and nothing could stop him. Four years after he died, Congress proposed the Twenty-Second Amendment and the states ratified it in 1951, hardening the norm into a rule. (It exempted the sitting president, Truman, who then declined to run anyway.)

Can you run that through Ulysses-contract theory for me? I want the mapping done honestly before the verdict. Odysseus has the crew tie him to the mast because he knows a future version of himself won't be trustworthy — so who's the self here, exactly? The founding generation, the electorate, or the office? And what's the siren: the incumbent's grip on power, the voters' appetite for a strong leader in an emergency, or both at once? Then trace the cost of untying at each level — public disapproval for the tradition versus two-thirds of Congress and three-fourths of the states for the amendment — and tell me whether that difference explains why the soft version held so long and then failed precisely when the temptation was largest.

Then the other direction: what does the case do to the theory? Two things bug me. One, an Ulysses contract is supposed to be struck in a cool hour to bind a hot one, but this amendment got written in the heat of partisan resenment right after the same man beat everybody four times — does the framework have any way to distinguish a precommitment made coolly from one made in a rage, or does it just call both of them precommitments? Two, the standard complaint that a second-term president goes lame duck, so the device manufactures some of the weakness it was meant to prevent. Is that a case the theory anticipated and can absorb, or is it a genuine hole?

American English, please, and skip any bibliography — I want the argument, not a reading list.
