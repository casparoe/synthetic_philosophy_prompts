ok so I'm helping run a little decision-theory reading group — nine people, mostly grad students plus two friends from work who have zero philosophy background — and next week's session is on expected-value fanaticism: the idea that if you let genuinely tiny probabilities of genuinely enormous payoffs count for their full weight, then basically every ordinary good deed gets dominated by some cosmic lottery ticket. Half the group thinks that's a reductio of the whole consequentialist setup and the other half thinks it just means our intuitions are miscalibrated, and I'd rather walk in with a document than a handout, because they always skim handouts. So: write me the minutes of the October meeting of the Ridgeline Elementary PTA, in which the newly elected treasurer has become a strict expected-value maximizer. Real minutes formatting — header block with date, time, location, present and absent, then numbered agenda items with motions made and seconded and vote tallies, and an Action Items list at the very bottom where every single item is something nobody is ever going to do. Hard cap of 800 words, American English throughout.

The jokes have to come from her being *right* about the math, not from her being dumb or from the PTA being dumb. Some beats I want in there: the Fall Festival bake sale, a certain three-hundred-odd dollars, gets voted down because it's dominated by her counterproposal to spend the same money on a raffle with a one-in-a-hundred-trillion shot at something vast, and the room can't find a single error in her arithmetic, only something wrong with their stomachs; a stranger at the spring car wash offers an astronomically improbable payoff for twenty dollars and the minutes record, flatly, that she paid, and that she has now paid four such people this fiscal year; on one agenda item the payouts grow so fast that there simply is no number to report at all, and she moves to table it "pending convergence"; and by the closing roll call it should be visible that the PTA has approved exactly zero events this year, not because of any disagreement but because everything keeps getting outbid by something else. Someone should try a consistency attack — two different strangers at the same door, so that paying both is worse than paying neither — and the treasurer should have a real answer, ideally one involving randomizing over which of them to pay, which makes the room more upset rather than less. Please don't let anyone say the jargon: no Pascal's mugging, no St. Petersburg, no fanaticism, no citations, no footnotes. The situations should be recognizable to someone who knows the literature without ever being labeled.

Voice-wise I want the deadpan secretary's register — passive constructions, "the motion carried 4–3," "Ms. Okonkwo abstained citing a conflict of interest" — and no winking at the reader, no asides, no punchline signposting. Everyone's invented, and the target is the position and the style of hyper-formal rationalist argument, not any real organization or person. It also has to work for my two non-philosopher friends, so on the surface it should just be funny as a piece about a suburban school fundraiser being taken hostage by a spreadsheet. If it helps, seed the room like this: Ridgeline Elementary, Tuesday October 14, 7 p.m. in the multipurpose gym, chair Dale Kettering, secretary (and author of these minutes) Priya Raman, treasurer Beth Ann Vogle, and one longtime member who has been running the bake sale since 2009 and would like that noted for the record.
