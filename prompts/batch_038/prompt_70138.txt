just got off a three-hour argument with a friend who's a stroke neurologist about a talk I'm giving, and I lost basically every round, so help me prep the question period.

It's an interdisciplinary pain workshop. The room is mostly clinical — two neurologists, a neuropsychologist who runs a lesion-series lab, a pain-medicine doc — plus two philosophers of mind who work on affect, one of whom is an imperativist about unpleasantness. Nobody there is a metaphysician and nobody has patience for qualia talk.

My thesis is what I'm calling the borrowed-hurt view. The classic pain asymbolia cases — insular lesion, patient tells you the pinprick is sharp and exactly where it is, says it doesn't hurt, won't withdraw, sometimes laughs — show that unpleasantness isn't a property of the sensory episode at all. It's a relation between that episode and what the subject currently cares about. My argument: (1) asymbolics keep discrimination, so the sensory content is intact; (2) they also keep the concepts, since ask them in the abstract and they'll agree that being burned is awful; (3) so what's missing isn't information about badness, it's the hookup; (4) therefore the hurt is borrowed from the caring rather than built into the feeling. I plan to handle the morphine and cingulotomy cases by saying the hookup is merely slack there, since those patients still say it hurts even while reporting they don't mind it.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way these clinicians would phrase them rather than the way my philosophy friends would, and rank them by how much damage they do. For each one: a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front before they have to extract it, and where the reply is soft. My friend's whole line was that I'm building a metaphysics out of a few dozen heterogenous stroke cases, and that no aversive behavior doesn't get you to no felt badness — I want those two handled properly. And tell me the question I'm praying nobody asks. I think it's the anesthesia one (if the hurt is gone, do asymbolic patients need to be anesthetized for surgery?) but I suspect there's something worse sitting in that room.
