I am applying for a place in a ten-week summer workshop on idealizations in epistemology, and the deliverable is a 1200-word project proposal. I would like you to write it from the half-formed idea below, which is where my thinking has stalled.

The idea. The framework of iterated amplification defines an idealized limit, HCH ("Humans Consulting HCH"), as the recursive process in which a human with unbounded time and copies of themselves answers a question, and it treats that limit as a good approximation of what a competent, well-informed humanity would conclude. This depends on something like a factored cognition hypothesis: roughly, that a hard epistemic question can be decomposed into easier subquestions whose answers determine the answer to the original one, and that the parent agent can do the determining. My suspicion is that the second clause is being taken for granted, and that it is where the real idealization lives. In a great deal of ordinary deliberation the subtask does not produce a detachable answer. Asked to compare two research programs, or to judge whether a course of action expresses a value I endorse, I can reach a verdict, but the verdict's probative force depends on considerations that cannot survive transmission to a parent agent within any realistic bandwidth — so the parent receives a conclusion stripped of exactly the part that made it evidence. If that is right, some tasks are not merely hard to factorize but resistant in principle to factorization by bounded parents, no matter how deep the tree.

Please turn this into a proposal containing: (1) a precise statement of the question, formulated so that it could come out either way; (2) an account of why the positions currently available leave it open — including the view that decomposition failures are just engineering problems soluble by greater depth, and the objection that the whole framework is unfalsifiable because the idealization is defined as a limit; (3) the method, and why it beats the obvious alternatives — my instinct is to build a taxonomy of candidate cases from real deliberation and then formalize a toy setting in which the parent has a bounded channel for receiving the sub-agent's output, asking whether amplified judgment converges as depth grows or plateaus, and comparing that against simply drawing more samples at fixed depth or against adversarial checking, so that any failure is attributable to structure rather than to effort; (4) what a successful result would look like, and what would count as the project failing — for instance, if the toy model turned out to re-derive a standard result about lossy transmission with no philosophical residue, or if every candidate case could be re-described as factorizable by going deeper; (5) an honest scope assessment for ten weeks, saying what I should cut. Do not include a bibliography or a list of citations; I will supply those myself.
