Spent 38 years in a materials lab measuring things, and only started on philosophy seriously since I retired, so forgive the framing. My daughter made me watch Memento (Nolan, 2000) again last week — the amnesiac guy with the tattoos and the polaroids, whole thing running backwards — and I've been stuck on it ever since, not for the plot reasons everyone gets stuck on but for a reason that I think is about choice over time.

Here's what I want: a reading of the film through the money-pump / diachronic-rationality material. Leonard looks to me like a walking pump. Each successive version of him does the locally sensible thing — photograph, annotate, tattoo, burn his wife's things, write down a licence plate as the answer to his own puzzle — and the sequence of those perfectly sensible steps adds up to the destruction of the very project each step was in aid of. He pays, and pays again, and ends up worse off than he started, and he even knows it in advance at the end, when he deliberately poisons the note his next self will find.

So, some specific things I'd like you to get into the weeds on:

- Does Leonard actually meet the conditions for a money pump, or is he a different animal entirely? My understanding is the pump needs cyclical preferences plus a willingness to trade at each step. I can't find any cycles in him — he wants one thing, consistently, the whole film. What he seems to have instead is successive selves with shifting information, which feels closer to the intrapersonal-conflict cases (the self-torturer sort of thing) than to intransitivity. If that's right, tell me plainly, because I'd rather be corrected than flattered.
- Does the pump argument need a vantage point Leonard structurally can't occupy — someone who can see the whole sequence at once? If it does, does the argument fail to apply to him, or does it apply with more force because he can be exploited by anyone who can see across his gaps (Teddy obviously can)?
- Is there a real difference in kind between being pumped by an outsider and pumping your own future self? Leonard at the end is doing the Ulysses-and-the-mast move, binding his successors to a plan, except the knot is a lie. Is that resoluteness corrupted, or is it the same mechanism and the film is just showing what resoluteness costs?

And please be as honest about where the reading breaks as where it works. My suspicion is that the film isn't really about preference cycles at all, that it's about narrative and self-deception, and that the reverse-chronology structure is an aesthetic device that makes the viewer experience each moment blind to the whole — not a thesis about agency. Say if you think I'm forcing the frame. Also, the psychological-continuity point seems live here: if the ties between Leonard's time-slices are about as weak as they can get while we still call it one person, maybe the diachronic requirements just don't get a grip on him, and the film is dramatising that. Or maybe that's me reaching.

No plot recap please — I've seen it twice and I don't need it. And point me at the moments you'd hang the argument on (the Sammy Jankis material, the note-writing, whatever) without quoting anything; I don't have the disc in front of me and I'm not going to look up lines.

One more thing, and this matters to me. Please keep clear which parts of the choice-theory story are actually settled among people who work on it and which parts are genuinely disputed — I've noticed philosophers write like the transitivity-and-pumpability stuff settles the question of whether Leonard is irrational *now*, and I doubt that's as agreed as it sounds. Where the field is split, tell me it's split and tell me what the split is about.
