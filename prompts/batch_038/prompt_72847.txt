Been keeping a running list this semester of my own snap verdicts about when somebody has said something flat-out that they shouldn't have, and I want to know what they add up to. Not the textbook cases — mine, mostly overheard.

A TV forecaster says "we'll get four inches tonight" off a model that gives her 60 percent: fine. A commentator says "they'll win this one" ten seconds before tip-off with no basis whatever: fine. A friend tells me it's going to rain, no evidence, and it rains: not fine, though way less not-fine than the next one. The same friend tells me my marriage won't last, and honestly has excellent evidence: not fine, and worse. So evidence seems to run backwards between those two and I can't explain why.

Doctor writes "prognosis good" in the chart on a hunch: not fine. Same doctor, same hunch, at the bedside — "I think you're going to do well": fine. My mother, "you're the smartest person I know": fine. A grad student asked cold in seminar whether the paper we all just read begs the question, says yes and gives a sketchy reason: fine, I think, though I hesitated. A referee writes "this paper makes no original contribution," having read it closely, believed it, and been wrong: I marked that not fine and then couldnt say why, since he did everything right.

Umpire calls a pitch a strike that nobody can settle even on replay: fine — and part of me wants to say the call makes it true, which I know is a bad move. Juror says out loud in the jury room "I'm voting guilty but I don't think we've met the standard": not fine, obviously.

Now the machines. Automated text from the transit agency: "the 6:14 is cancelled." Fine, though nobody at the agency knows that in any ordinary sense. A scheduling chatbot tells me "your dentist takes Medicare" and it's false: not fine, and I don't think "it's only software" gets it off the hook. The same chatbot hedged — "I believe your dentist takes Medicare, but confirm it" — fine. And a parrot that's learned "the phone's ringing" squawks it when the phone isn't ringing: not an assertion at all, so neither fine nor not-fine. I'm very confident about the parrot and I suspect it's the case that breaks everything else.

So: is there a principle here — one norm, or a norm plus a rule about which utterances in which settings even count as assertions — that gets all of this, or nearly all? Give me the best one or two candidates, precise enough that I could run a new case through them, and tell me for each which clause is doing the work on which case, which of my verdicts it has to overturn, and which it just has to leave misfiring. Then run three I haven't settled: a priest who says from the pulpit "God is with you" and doesn't believe it; a press release saying "we expect to return to profitability next year" that the CFO privately doubts; and a translation app that renders a doctor's "you'll recover" as "you might recover" — improper assertion, or outside the practice entirely, and if outside, what makes something inside. And if you think the parrot is load-bearing, say so plainly and tell me what it costs me to keep it.
