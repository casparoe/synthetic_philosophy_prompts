I'm a supply-chain planner, not a philosopher — I read Hofstadter's stuff about cooperation a while back on a flight and then couldn't stop thinking about it, and now I've been chewing on it during my commute for two months. I've never taken a decision theory course, so instead of reasoning down from any theory I did the opposite thing: I wrote out a bunch of cases, wrote down what I'd actually do in each, and tried to see whether the pile adds up to anything. Here's what I got.

(1) The two-box/one-box setup where the predictor is essentially never wrong and has already left the room: I take only the opaque box, and I don't find this close at all. (2) Same setup, but I'm told the predictor is basically a coin flipper, right about half the time: I take both boxes. I can't tell you where between 50% and 100% I'd flip — 65%? 85%? — it just slides somewhere and I don't know where. (3) A one-shot prisoner's dilemma against an exact copy of me, we both know it's an exact copy, no communication: I cooperate, also not close. (4) Same, except the copy is a copy of me from last Tuesday and I've been sick and barely sleeping since, so we're plainly not in the same state: I still lean cooperate but way less confidently, and I suspect this is where my whole intuition starts to fall apart. (5) A one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a total stranger, anonymous, no future dealings, no reputation at stake: I defect and feel nothing, though I do get a twinge when they add that he's "basically my kind of person." (6) The same setup but I get to see the copy's choice before making mine: I defect, obviously, it's a free lunch. (7) Voting: I vote in every election including ones where nothing can plausibly come down to one ballot, and I don't think that's irrational — it feels like case (3) with millions of copies instead of one, except I can't defend that at all. (8) The one where something in my head makes me want to smoke and, completely separately, causes cancer, while the smoking itself causes nothing: I'd smoke, and I feel zero pull the other way. Case (3) and case (8) bother me the most, because their shaped the same and I'm going opposite directions on them.

So: is there a principle behind these, or a small handful of competing ones that each get most of them? I'd much rather have one rule with a knob I can turn (that would settle (2) too) than a stack of special cases. Tell me what each clause is doing and which of my cases forced it, rank the candidates if there's more than one, and be blunt about which of my verdicts the winner has to throw out. Also give me its verdict on a case I haven't listed: (3) again, exact copy, both know it — but right before we play they hand me a pill that makes me noticeably more impulsive and the copy doesn't get one. Format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no wrap-up paragraph at the end. I don't care how you spend the five — clauses, ranking, the overturned verdicts, the pill — but five, not four and not six.
