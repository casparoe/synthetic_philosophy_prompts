I manage the controlled vocabulary and enterprise taxonomy for a mid-sized freight brokerage: roughly 400 dispatchers working out of a queueing application, an ETA model that is trained on our status codes, driver-matching rules, and about a dozen reporting pipelines that key off the same categories. Every quarter we receive twenty to forty change requests — rename a status value, merge two codes that have drifted into overlap, split one code that now covers two distinct situations. The dispute is always the same. The requester says the label causes behavior: call a load "At Risk" instead of "Delayed" and dispatchers escalate earlier; keep two freight types distinct and the matching rules stay honest. The engineers say a label is a key in a lookup table, the records and the weights do not change, and any effect is placebo. Both positions cannot be right, and because we have no way to adjudicate, decisions get made by whoever argues hardest. We have spent two sprints on changes that did nothing and quietly skipped two that mattered.

I want to ground a triage procedure in an idea I keep coming back to: Hofstadter's careenium, the sealed chamber full of tiny magnetic marbles that jostle and clump into larger balls, the simmballs, which in turn shove one another around. You can describe everything in the chamber as marbles obeying physics, or you can describe it as simmballs triggering and displacing simmballs, and Hofstadter asks which level is doing the shoving. The part I want to operationalize is this: the high-level description is not a rival cause competing with the marbles, but it earns its causal vocabulary only to the extent that a perturbation defined at that level propagates lawfully — clumps that are internally dense, coupled to enough downstream clumps, and stable enough that the substrate's noise does not swamp the effect. Where a clump is loose or barely coupled, saying the symbol did it is redescription, not explanation. That is the discrimination I need my team to make about a proposed label.

Please write the procedure. It should give the questions to ask of a change request, in order; state what counts as a sufficient answer to each; specify what evidence my team can actually collect rather than what a research group could collect; say where it terminates in a no, where it terminates in a defer, and where it hands the decision to the steering committee because the question is which categories ought to exist rather than whether the change moves anything. It must keep two substrates apart, since a label can push one and not the other: the humans who read it, and the systems that store it as a key or were trained on it. It should also distinguish a label that genuinely does causal work from one that is merely a cached name for a pattern the substrate already determines on its own.

Our constraints: two analysts who are fluent in SQL and have no training in causal inference, so no step may depend on jargon they would have to look up; capacity for at most two controlled comparisons per quarter, either an A/B across one dispatcher cohort or a before-and-after in one region; under an hour of triage per request before it goes to a non-technical steering committee that signs off.

Then run the procedure on three requests: renaming the status value "Delayed" to "At Risk" in the dispatch queue, which I favor and expect to pass; merging "Flatbed-Heavy" and "Flatbed-Oversize" into a single code, both of which feed the ETA model and the matching rules; and splitting "Customer Complaint" into service and billing variants, which compliance wants regardless of what we measure. If the procedure's verdict on any of these cuts against the requester's framing — including mine — say so plainly and show which step produced it.

Finish with what the procedure cannot do: the change requests it will systematically get wrong, in which direction, and why. Open with the procedure's decision rule in a single sentence. Hard limit of 800 words for everything, worked cases included; if you have to cut material to fit, add one sentence saying what you cut and why it was the right thing to cut.
