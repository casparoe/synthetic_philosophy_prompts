Our reading group has spent two months on Book IV, chapter vii of The Wealth of Nations, on Smith's account of the East India Company, and on the parallel material in the Lectures on Jurisprudence and the Theory of Moral Sentiments, and the argument over whether Smith was an opponent of empire has not advanced a single step. Both camps can recite the same passages and reach opposite conclusions, and when I ask either side what evidence would change its mind, neither can say. I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a ruling on it.

One camp holds that Smith was a principled anti-imperialist. They point out that he treats the colonial monopoly as a device by which one class of merchants raises its rate of profit at the expense of every other part of the society; that he argues Britain gains nothing defensible from dominion over the colonies and proposes a scheme of gradual union in which colonial representatives are admitted to Parliament in proportion to colonial taxation; that he insists the East India Company's commercial character disqualifies it from sovereignty, so that the character of a merchant and the character of a sovereign cannot be reconciled in the same hands; and that his moral philosophy supplies the normative machinery — an account of injury and resentment, and of how commercial men deceive themselves about their own interests — that the political economy merely applies.

The other camp denies that anything like opposition to empire is present at all. Smith's complaint, they say, is that empire is wasteful, badly administered and captured by a monopoly, not that it is unjust: he never asks whether Britain has any right to be in Bengal, he thinks the colonial trade worth keeping if the monopoly were abolished, and his proposal for union is either a reductio designed to show that separation is inevitable or a fantasy no eighteenth-century parliament would entertain. On this view the indignation in the text is decorative, and the argumentative work is done entirely by an analysis of how the incentives of merchants, companies and states interact. Both camps agree about what is on the page; they disagree about what the page establishes.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the dispute is not textual but turns on three questions that are never stated outright. First, which Smith is authoritative when the works pull differently — the moral philosopher and the lecture notes, or the analysis of markets in the political economy? Second, what counts as being against empire at all: does rejecting an empire of monopoly on the grounds that it impoverishes the metropolis make one an anti-imperialist, or must one reject the project as unjust, and from whose standpoint is that judgment to be made? Third, what would settle the matter — a passage, a pattern of argument, the absence of one?

Diagnose the disagreement in those terms. Tell me whether it is substantive, verbal, or a pair of camps answering different questions under a single label, and show which parts of each side's actual commitments support your answer. If part of it is verbal, say precisely what the surviving substantive question is once the definitional noise is removed. Then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction the field is missing, a textual test that would genuinely discriminate between the two readings — the gradual-union proposal is the obvious candidate, but explain whether either reading can absorb it, and if not, what could not be absorbed — or a reason to expect that no amount of further reading will move anyone, and why. A survey of positions on Smith and empire is not what I am after, and please do not end by declaring a winner.
