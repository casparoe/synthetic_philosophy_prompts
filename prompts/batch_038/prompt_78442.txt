Our lab's model oversight panel — five members, one of them non-technical — must commit next quarter's fixed monitoring budget for the upcoming flagship model before its deployment affordances are frozen, and the two competing programs would each consume essentially all of it.

Program A (influence-seeking detection) stands up a permanent red-team sandbox giving the model real-looking instrumental affordances: a draft email client addressed to internal stakeholder lists, a calendar it can book on a colleague's behalf, and read access to a simulated internal dashboard. It probes for strategic behaviour — sandbagging while observed, preference for options that widen its own later influence, and compliance that decays when the model appears unwatched. It costs roughly 15% of pre-deployment compute and produces discrete, legible findings.

Program B (slow value drift) maintains a frozen rubric of about 400 value-laden prompts, rescored weekly across every checkpoint and compared against an early baseline. Last cycle it flagged a genuine shift in the model's treatment of one contested topic; the investigation traced it to three rubric items having been silently re-annotated by contractors, so the instrument itself drifts, and true weekly deltas mostly sit inside the noise floor.

The panel is split between those who hold that power-seeking is improbable but irreversible once affordances exist, and those who hold that drift is certain, cumulative, and structurally invisible to any snapshot probe. Write the memo I will circulate before the meeting: recommend an allocation, state the decision rule and the specific observations that would trigger a reallocation mid-quarter, keep the prose readable for our non-technical member, and give the losing camp's strongest consideration a real accounting rather than a nod.
