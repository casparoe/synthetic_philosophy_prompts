I am a PhD student working on the ethics of assertion. In a reading group I argued that a speaker can be fully blameworthy for misleading an audience while remaining less blameworthy than she would be for outright lying, and a senior colleague stopped me with the proverb "a half-truth is a whole lie." I want to know what he would have had to be claiming for that to function as a refutation rather than as a restatement of his disagreement.

Please disambiguate the proverb into the distinct precise theses it might express, attending in particular to what "half" is doing — a statement that is literally partly false, one that is literally true but conveys something false, or one that is true yet omits what the hearer needed — since I suspect those three carry quite different normative freight. Then test each reading against my target case: under oath, a witness asked whether he has ever held a Swiss bank account describes a six-month company account in Zurich; every word is true, the examiner draws a false inference the witness anticipated and did nothing to correct, and no follow-up is asked. That is roughly the fact pattern of Bronston v. United States (1973), where a unanimous Supreme Court held that literally truthful but misleading answers are not perjury and that the remedy lies in the examiner's further questions. For each reading, say whether it delivers moral parity between lying and misleading, and whether it can explain why the legal asymmetry there is not tracking a moral one.

Deliverable: a table with columns Reading (one sentence, precisely stated); Kind (definitional/semantic, culpability, duty, allocation of epistemic responsibility, or other); Verdict (platitude / substantive and defensible / false / unresolved); Supports the dismissal? (yes, partly, or no, with one clause of reasoning); Cost (what must be given up to accept it). After the table, three or four sentences naming the reading he most plausibly meant, which of your verdicts you hold least confidently, and what consideration would flip it.
