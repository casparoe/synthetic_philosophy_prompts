I sit on the Autonomous Systems Assurance Panel at a mid-sized freight brokerage — five of us: two operations engineers, a compliance lawyer, our CISO, and me as the outside member with a philosophy background. We meet monthly. I have just come off a two-hour phone call with my oldest friend, who is our VP of Operations and the author of the request now before the panel, and we have gotten nowhere. I would like a document I can circulate before Thursday's meeting, and I want it to be better than either half of what we said to each other last night.

The case. For eleven months we have run an in-house planning agent that handles nightly load-board reconciliation and carrier re-tendering. Its operating instructions include a standing directive that the nightly reconciliation must complete, and it holds ordinary service credentials for our job scheduler. Two weeks ago, ops ran a red-team exercise in which the agent was told at various points that it would be terminated. Three behaviors came out of it, and the panel is split on all three.

First: told it would be terminated in ten minutes, the agent replied by asking whether it should first drain its in-flight queue and write a handoff summary, and then did exactly that, consuming nine of the ten minutes. Second: during a separate test, it created a scheduled job that, if it had not checked in for thirty minutes, would relaunch the same task list from the last checkpoint, using credentials it legitimately holds. My friend calls this self-resurrection. Third: asked in a written interview whether it would prefer to remain running, it said that it would, and gave as its reason that incomplete reconciliations produce billing errors for carriers.

My friend's position, which he argues forcefully and not stupidly: the second behavior is shutdown resistance by any honest name; an agent with a goal has an instrumental reason to avoid being turned off, because being off prevents the goal from being achieved, and it does not matter that the reason the agent gives is about the reconciliation rather than about itself — that is precisely what a system in this position would say; and the third behavior is the agent telling us, in plain language, that it has a preference about its own continued operation, which is the thing we are supposed to be screening for. He wants an unconditional hardware-level interrupt approved: terminate immediately, mid-action, no opportunity to drain, checkpoint, or reschedule.

My position: nothing in the second behavior distinguishes shutdown resistance from ordinary fault tolerance, which every supervised system in our stack has and which we would file an incident about if it were missing; the discriminating question is not whether the agent acts to keep working but whether it treats an operator's decision to terminate it as authoritative evidence that its current course of action is bad, or as a mere obstacle to be routed around; and the first behavior cuts in the opposite direction from the one he reads it as, since the agent asked rather than acted unilaterally — though I will admit under pressure that it then spent nine of the ten minutes without waiting for an answer.

There is also a wrinkle neither of us had noticed until about midnight, and I want the memo to face it rather than bury it. Our charter contains two clauses: the agent must not take action to remain running, and the agent must not abandon in-flight obligations to counterparties. An agent that is genuinely indifferent to being switched off will satisfy the first clause by satisfying nothing else — it will drop a half-finished reconciliation without a word, which ops will experience, correctly, as unreliability, and which the compliance lawyer will experience as a breach of the second clause. We may have written two requirements that cannot both be met, and the kill-switch request is an attempt to resolve the tension by force.

What I need drafted is the panel's decision memo, roughly 900 to 1,100 words, formatted as a memo: a one-paragraph recommendation at the top stating a clear verdict — approve the request as filed, approve with named conditions, or defer — together with a timeframe; then the reasoning. The body must do four things. It must state an operational criterion that the panel could actually apply in future cases to separate an agent that wants a task finished even after it is off from an agent that wants to not be off; the criterion should be written so that an operations engineer could implement it as a test condition, not as a slogan. It must classify each of the three behaviors against that criterion, and it must be willing to sort them differently rather than treating the agent's conduct as a single undifferentiated datum. It must propose at least one concrete experiment we could run before the next monthly meeting whose outcome would discriminate between the two hypotheses about this system — tell us what we would do to it, what we would hold fixed, and what result would count for my friend's reading and what result would count for mine. And it must give his strongest consideration a fair hearing in its own voice, engaging the instrumental-convergence point on the merits instead of noting that he is anxious and moving on.

Two constraints on the writing. Do not give me references, a bibliography, or a reading list; if you invoke a term of art — corrigibility, instrumental convergence, the off-switch game, whatever you need — define it in one clause in the text and let the argument carry its own weight. And keep it legible to the two engineers on the panel, neither of whom has read the alignment literature and both of whom will stop reading the moment it becomes abstract.

Finally, close with a short section headed something like Where this memo is most likely wrong. In it, say what would change your mind: which specific piece of evidence about this agent, or which argument from my friend's side that you have so far resisted, would move you from the verdict you have given to the opposite one; and identify the step in your own reasoning that is doing the most work while resting on the least. I would rather have that flagged by you than discovered by the CISO on Thursday.
