I run infrastructure at a mid-size company. No philosophy background at all, but I've noticed that half the stuff I build is really a device for stopping myself from doing things later, and my gut feelings about when those things actually bind me are a mess. I want to know whether there's a principle back there or just vibes.

Here's what I actually think, case by case.

1. There's a rule I helped write: no prod deploys after Thursday night. Its Friday 3pm, I have a fix that's well tested and would genuinely help, and nothing in the wording of the rule speaks to this situation. I think I shouldn't ship it. Writing the rule was rational, and following it now is rational too, even though right now I think shipping is the better call.

2. Same rule, different history. Suppose it was written by the guy who had my job before me, and I have six months of incident data showing every Friday outage came from a build system we ripped out in March. I think I should delete the rule and ship. Definately rational.

3. Our deploy tool auto-rolls back if error rates spike. Once it started rolling back a deploy of mine and I was maybe 95% sure the spike was an unrelated upstream thing. I let it roll back anyway, and I think that was right.

4. A friend got me into one of those commitment contracts where if I miss the gym 12 times in a month I owe $200 to an organization I actively despise. I missed. I think signing was rational and paying was rational, even though the payment makes the world worse on my own values and there's nobody around to be disappointed in me.

5. Two that look identical to me but get opposite verdicts. (a) Night before a salary negotiation, I decide not to open the email where they sent the range, because if I know the top number I'll cave instantly. Rational, a bit gross, but rational. (b) A test fails in a way that smells flaky and I decide not to read the log, because if I read it I'll talk myself into merging. Straight up irrational.

6. The one my coworker says is a famous philosophy thing, something with a hitchhiker. A driver picks me up on a deserted road only because he can tell I'm the sort of person who'd actually send him the $40 afterward. Once I'm home, sending it costs me $40 and buys me nothing, and that seems like a complete description of my situation. I still think I should send it, and that it's better to be the kind of person who does, but I can't produce any reason that's available to me at that moment.

7. Two beers at a party knowing I'll then agree to help someone move on Saturday: fine, and I should show up Saturday. Getting drunk enough that I'd sign whatever someone put in front of me: not fine. Both are just "turn yourself into someone whose choices you can't endorse."

8. I'm on call. 3am, pager goes off, and I now have information my past self didn't: looking at the dashboard this is very probably a false positive, and the on-call policy itself says a false positive doesn't require me to get up. I think I should get up anyway.

My tentative rule, in my own vocabulary: a precommitment is a guardrail, not a straitjacket. It binds me unless I've got new information about why the rule exists — as opposed to information about whether this particular instance is one the rule was meant to catch — and it never binds me if the only way to break it is an in-the-moment call made by the guy who's currently tempted.

I'm pretty sure that doesn't survive contact with 2 and 8. In 2 the new info is about why the rule exists and I say override; in 8 the new info is arguably about why the policy exists too, and I say don't. So either my rule is wrong or one of those verdicts is.

What I want: the principle, or the two or three best candidates, that fits this set, with every clause tied to the case that forces it. Then run it on cases I haven't listed — a rule I'm allowed to override with 24 hours notice, paying a stranger to hold the key so I physically can't, precommitting to change my values instead of my actions, and an exception process that really is run by someone other than me. And tell me straight which of my eight verdicts each candidate has to kill. I'd rather have a simple principle that says I'm wrong about one of these than a baroque one that saves all of them, but I want the price named either way.

Separate question that I suspect is the whole ballgame: is there any principle at all under which it's rational to make yourself do something you know is worse by your own ranking? Because 4 and 6 seem to demand exactly that, and "you get rewarded for being the sort of person who does it" only works when someone's watching, which is not true in 4.

No reading list or citations, please — I have no way to look anything up.
