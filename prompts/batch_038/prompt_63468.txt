So I've been going back and forth with my roommate about citizen assemblies and lottocracy, and I built this argument to kill the standard "but nobody elected them" objection. It worked. Except it also proves stuff I definitely dont believe. Help me find where it breaks.

1. Someone has authority over you only if you can call them to account — demand their reasons, and impose some cost on them if the reasons turn out to be garbage. Call this accountability.
2. Accountability can't ground itself. If whoever holds the account-givers to account has to be held to account by yet another body, you either regress forever or you stop somewhere.
3. So every political system bottoms out in something unaccountable. Elections don't avoid this, they just move the stopping point around.
4. An unaccountable element is legitimate only if the people in it have no reason to shade their judgment toward anybody — no rewards to chase, no punishments to dodge, nobody they need to please.
5. Random selection, one short term, no reappointment, no recall, is exactly that: the selector is nobody, so there's nobody to curry favor with.
6. Therefore the foundational authority in any legitimate system has to be, or has to include, a sortitioned body.
7. Same reasoning, run without stopping: a wholly random government — random head of state, random judges, random central bankers — is *more* legitimate than an elected one, since elected officials structurally have to please the people who can rehire them.
8. That's absurd. Nobody thinks a coin-flip president beats an elected one.
9. So something above is false.

My roommate says the whole thing collapses at (4) and I think he's just restating his conclusion. Which step do you actually reject, and — this is the part I care about — why did it look fine to me when I wrote it down? If it's an equivocation I want the two readings named and I want to know whether the argument survives once you fix the words, not just whether you can wave at it. Then price the alternatives. If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1), becuase without it I don't see how you explain why a dictatorship is illegitimate at all — so tell me what keeping it costs me elsewhere.

Last question, which is the one that actually settles the argument with my roommate: is there a weaker conclusion nearby that survives whatever you break — sortition being right for juries, audits, or a second chamber but not for the whole legislature? If there is, what's the principled line, and why doesn't (7) apply just as hard on both sides of it?
