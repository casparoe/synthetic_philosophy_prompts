I spent thirty-four years as a condensed-matter physicist, most of them on lattice dynamics and the thermal properties of solids, and I have now been retired for four. Philosophy is my retirement project. I audit a graduate metaphysics seminar at the university where I was permitted to keep my office, and the instructor has been kind enough to let me draft one of the two take-home questions for this year's final; the second half of the course concerns grounding and fundamentality. My former colleagues regard this as a poor use of a working brain. I regard it as the first thing I have done since my qualifying examinations that I am not certain I could pass.

The occasion for my request was a dinner-party conversation in November. I was explaining to a graduate student how one actually computes the lattice parameter of a real crystal at a given temperature: one expands the interatomic potential about a reference structure; the normal modes of vibration then have frequencies fixed by the force constants and the masses at that structure; those frequencies change when the volume changes, a sensitivity captured mode by mode by the Grüneisen parameter, which is just the fractional change of a mode's frequency with the fractional change of volume; one writes the Helmholtz free energy as the static energy of the reference structure plus the contribution of the modes, adding a pressure–volume term if one wants equilibrium under an applied pressure; one minimizes over volume; and then, because the minimized volume has changed the force constants and hence the frequencies, one repeats the whole procedure until the answer stops moving. A purely harmonic crystal would not expand when heated, so this is where thermal expansion comes from, and the iteration converges in practice after a handful of turns. The same shape of calculation recurs throughout my old field: the self-consistent field in electronic-structure work, the mean field in magnetism. Her response was that this is a grounding circle, on the grounds that the facts about the modes are grounded in the facts about the lattice and the facts about the lattice parameters are grounded in the facts about the modes, and I found that I could not answer her. I have since read enough to know that grounding is standardly required to be irreflexive, asymmetric and transitive, and that these constraints together appear to rule cycles out; but I also know that the procedure I described is not an analogy, it is the computation, and I am not prepared to be told that physics fails to raise the question.

So: please write one take-home examination question, with a model answer and a marking scheme, for an advanced graduate seminar in metaphysics. The question is worth 30 marks and students will have three hours and no materials beyond the question paper, so the stem must be self-contained, roughly 350 to 450 words, and must explain the physics in terms a metaphysician can follow without any training in it. The stem should state the two competing grounding claims as squarely as possible — that the facts concerning the crystal's vibrational modes are grounded in the facts concerning its lattice parameters together with the interatomic potential, since the modes are modes of that lattice; and that the fact that the lattice has the parameters it has at this temperature and pressure is grounded in the facts concerning the mode spectrum, since those parameters are the ones that minimize a free energy to which that spectrum contributes — so that students cannot dissolve the case by re-describing it vaguely. It should then ask three things: (a) to state the formal constraints on grounding that the case appears to violate and to show, briefly but rigorously, that given those constraints the case cannot be a genuine cycle; (b) to set out the strongest response available to a defender of the hierarchical picture, and to say which of the two dependence claims that response revises or reclassifies, and why it is entitled to; and (c) to assess whether that response disposes of the case or concedes something, in particular whether fundamentality requires that there be a bottom layer or only that whatever is derivative be grounded in something. The stem must not tip the answer: do not use the word "fixpoint," and do not hint that the difficulty may lie in confusing a method of solution with a relation of determination. Discovering that is precisely what I want to test.

The model answer should run 900 to 1,100 words and should argue rather than survey. At a minimum I would expect it to derive the contradiction from irreflexivity and transitivity; to distinguish constitutive or conceptual dependence from causal-nomological dependence and to say why grounding is ordinarily taken to be neither; to separate the map from volume to spectrum from the spectrum at the equilibrium volume, and to say what the iteration converges to; to consider at least two alternative ways of blocking the circle — for instance denying that phonons are real at all, or taking the free-energy-minimization fact as the single ground of both sides — and to say why one is preferable; and to address part (c) by distinguishing the thesis that a fundamental level exists from the thesis that nothing is ungrounded, taking into account that effective theories appear to succeed one another without termination.

Please include no bibliography, no citations, no page numbers, and no quotation marks around anyone's words; where a position is standardly associated with a philosopher the name may appear once, but the argument must not depend on the reader knowing that philosopher's work.

For the deliverable, give the question stem first, then the model answer, then the marking scheme as a markdown table with exactly these columns: criterion | marks available | what a first-class answer does | what a competent but unremarkable answer does | the characteristic error that forfeits these marks. Use eight to ten rows whose marks sum to 30, and make the rows discriminating: I want to be able to tell a strong answer from a well-written mediocre one by the presence or absence of specific moves, not by tone. After the table, and as the last thing in your reply, give a JSON object with exactly these fields: "level" (a phrase describing the intended students); "time_allowed" and "total_marks" (numbers where applicable); "sub_questions" (an array of three strings, the questions as they appear in the stem); "central_distinction_tested" (one sentence); "physicists_crux" (one sentence naming the distinction that resolves the apparent circle); "required_moves" (an array of five to seven strings, each a move a first-class answer must make); "disqualifying_moves" (an array of three to five strings, each an error that caps the mark); and "grade_boundaries" (an object mapping the grades A, B, C and D to the minimum mark for each). No prose after the JSON.
