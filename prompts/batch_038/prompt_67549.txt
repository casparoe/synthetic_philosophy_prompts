So — slightly odd request. I'm a novelist, third book, and the middle section is set in a district town in Bengal across 1943 and into '44: a rice dealer's wife whose husband has gone to Calcutta to work in the war economy and gradually stops writing. I've been reading Sen on entitlements for a year now, plus everything I can get on the denial policies, the price controls and the black market they fed, the priority-class rationing for war workers, and the whole business of the province never actualy declaring a famine, with relief only landing once the army took over the funding in October and a record aman harvest came in that December. My festival is doing a paired-programme thing where a writer gets a 40 minute onstage conversation with an academic, and I've been given an economic historian who works squarely in the entitlement tradition but who has, I'm told, turned against the whole was-it-a-natural-disaster-or-a-political-crime question as badly posed. Her line, roughly: famines are the terminal phase of an entitlement structure nobody chose, and when people ask her about Churchill she says something like "attribution is a genre, not a finding." She's also known for arguing the Irish case gets mangled from both sides — that food going out of the ports was a small share of what Ireland was eating, that blight really did take out the subsistence base of the people who died, so availability failure and entitlement failure land on top of each other there in a way they arguably don't in Bengal.

Give me ten to twelve questions to ask her, in a running order I could actually deliver aloud, each with a note on the answer she's most likely to give and the follow-up that makes that answer uncomfortable. No reading list, no summary of her position — I've read the position, what I need is leverage. Specifically I want at least one question on each of: how much of Bengal 1943 was a genuine rice shortfall versus a collapse in what landless labourers and war-wage earners could buy, and what evidence would settle it either way; what her framework does with the fact that something like half the deaths came in 1944 from disease after food was moving again; whether the democracy-and-free-press point survives Ireland, which sent MPs to Westminster and had a free British press and starved anyway, and survives the Great Leap Forward, and whether the real crux is the vote or the visibility of the dying to people who can be punished for it; how her nobody-chose-this line holds up in a case where grain output was being reported upward in inflated numbers and requisition quotas were set from those numbers; and who exactly dies in her account — does it predict anything about which members of a household eat last and which disappear first, or only about the aggregate.

And then one question that's really mine. Where does a scene live in this framework? My agent says nobody wants another famine book unless there's somebody to hate, my editor says act two is too thesis-y, and honestly the entitlement approach has made it hard for me to put anyone in a room doing anything at all. So have her name the two or three decisions in 1943 where a specific person in a specific room moved something that mattered, say what that person thought they were doing, and tell me whether "they were thinking about shipping tonnage" is a usable answer for a writer or a cop-out. Close with the question about what she'd have to find in the record to concede that this was a crime rather than a structure. Also flag for each one what an evasive answer sounds like, because she's extremely good at being warm and agreeable and saying nothing.
