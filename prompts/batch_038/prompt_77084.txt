I'm in a formal epistemology seminar this term and we've spent four weeks on the Dutch Book Arguments and gotten absolutley nowhere, so I want a diagnosis of the stalemate rather than another round of the arguments. You're a philosopher of probability and decision theory who's worked on both the coherence tradition and the accuracy-first stuff — think this through carefully before answering, and don't just summarize the camps back at me.

Here's the room. Three groups:

(1) The DBA people. An agent whose degrees of belief aren't probabilities is irrational because she's vulnerable to a package of bets each of which she'd accept, and which together lose her money no matter how things turn out. The vulnerability is the irrationality, or at any rate the proof of it. Same shape for conditionalization via the dynamic version, where the bookie buys at your current conditional odds and sells after you update.

(2) The accuracy people. What's wrong with incoherent credences has nothing to do with bets. It's that they're dominated: there's some alternative credence function that's closer to the truth in every possible world, by the lights of any reasonable scoring rule. Bets are at best a colorful illustration and at worst a distraction, since the pragmatic argument is hostage to a "why bother?" reply — if you never meet a bookie, or you'd happily pay a small cost to duck each individual bet, the sure-loss argument goes quiet while the domination argument doesn't.

(3) The deflationists. Both arguments are padded with assumptions that are doing all the work. Bets pay out in money, and money has diminishing marginal utility, so a sure loss in dollars isn't a sure loss in value; patch that by betting in utility and you've now assumed the agent will take any gamble offered at her own odds, won't reconsider between rounds, and evaluates packages by just adding up the parts. Strip those out and what's left is a claim about betting dispositions, not about belief at all.

Where it jams up, concretely:

- Whenever someone in camp 3 says the DBA proves too much, camp 2 nods and treats it as a reason to prefer accuracy. Camp 1 answers that accuracy-first is just the DBA with the bets deleted and a scoring rule smuggled in, and asks where the propriety of the scoring rule comes from if not from something bet-like or from a representation theorem. Nobody responds to that; everybody repeats their last line.
- When camp 1 says a sure loss is the paradigm case of a practical defect, so the DBA is at least anchored in something real, camp 2 says that's exactly the wrong lesson, because we want a norm on credence that binds agents who will never bet.
- There's a recurring side-fight about the unit of assessment: individual choices, packages of choices, or the whole credal state at a time. Camp 3 leans on it hard — additivity across a package is an extra assumption, not a theorem. Camp 1 calls it a technicality. Camp 2 says domination is a claim about states so the question never comes up.

My hunch, and I could easily be wrong about it: the three camps aren't disagreeing about probability. They're disagreeing about what the explanandum is — whether a norm on belief should be read off what belief-states do for the agent in action, or off the belief-state's relation to the truth — and about what the normativity attaches to, the credence function on its own or the whole credence-plus-utility pair. If that's right then each camp's theorem is true but about a different object, and our seminar is a verbal fight in technical costume.

So: is that hypothesis right, partly right, or a red herring? Is this disagreement substantive — and if so, about facts, about norms, or about what counts as a proof? Is it verbal, and if so about which term: "irrational," "vulnerable," "belief," "dominated"? Or are two different questions sharing one heading? And is there a hidden formal question doing the work here that could actually get settled — say, a case where the sure-loss and the domination verdicts come apart, or a case where perfectly coherent credences leave an agent exploitable anyway because of the shape of her utilities, or where non-additive evaluation of packages makes an incoherent agent unbookable? Tell me what would count as progress in that room and whether I should expect any.
