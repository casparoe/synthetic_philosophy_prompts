I am drafting a novel whose moral center is a pair of characters who do the same thing and are judged very differently by everyone around them, including themselves. Before I commit several hundred pages to that asymmetry, I want to know whether it can be philosophically earned or whether I am merely exploiting the reader's outcome bias. So I would like a thought experiment built to a strict specification, followed by a demonstration that it actually meets the specification.

The purpose, since it determines what counts as cheating: the case must produce an unequal distribution of blame between two agents even after every channel through which the unequal outcome could carry information about them has been closed off. If the asymmetry survives only because a reader infers something about one character's temperament, competence, or foresight from the way things happened to turn out, then the case has failed and I would far rather learn that now than in the third draft.

Specifications for the scenario: one paragraph, and no more.

1. Two agents, and the luck must be resultant — luck in how things turn out — rather than luck in who one is, luck in the situation one is placed in, or luck about how one came to be determined by antecedent conditions. Name the category you are building and explicitly hold the other three fixed.
2. The agents must be alike in everything the control principle could get purchase on: same information, same beliefs, same diligence in checking beforehand, same motives, same quality of will, same effort. They should be indistinguishable up to and including the last moment at which either could have acted otherwise.
3. The divergence must occur after that moment. Nothing either of them does or fails to do after the point of choice may contribute to the difference in outcome.
4. The differing outcome must not have been foreseeable, detectable, or preventable by any degree of care actually available to them. Give one sentence per agent explaining why not.
5. Setting: ordinary professional or domestic life. No vehicles, no firearms, no war, no diagnosis-and-death plot, no trolley, no supernatural machinery, no uncanny predictors. A reader should feel that this could have happened to a colleague.
6. The two must know each other, and a third person must come to know both outcomes, so that the comparison happens inside the story rather than in a footnote.
7. The stakes must be moral rather than legal or institutional. I do not want a case in which the only real difference is what an employer, an insurer, or a court goes on to do.

Then the demonstration, which is the part I actually need:

- State the control principle in its usual form and give the verdict a position that rejects moral luck must deliver on your case. Do not soften it into something comfortable.
- Give the verdict an accepter of resultant moral luck delivers, and say what is doing the justificatory work.
- Run the epistemic deflation against your own case and show it starved: enumerate every route by which the outcome might function as evidence about the agent — disposition, competence, prior record of getting away with it, what the outcome reveals about how often this risk was being run — and show each route closed by construction. If any route remains open, say so plainly; that is the single most useful thing you could tell me.
- Address agent-regret: does the unlucky one have a first-personal response that the lucky one lacks, and if so, does that response contribute anything to the difference in verdicts, or is it merely a downstream effect of it?
- Identify the one load-bearing feature of the case, remove it, and report what collapses.
- Give the strongest available counterattack from someone who denies that resultant luck is a genuine category. Not a straw version — the objection I would face from the sharpest reader.

Please write in American English, and keep the whole response to roughly twelve hundred words. Throughout, I want you to separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and not present a contested position as though it were the consensus; if a move you make depends on a disputed reading of the literature, flag it in a short closing note on where the case takes liberties. Finally: if you can construct the same structure so that the asymmetry comes out in praise rather than blame, add it as a second paragraph; if the structure resists that reversal, explain why, because I suspect I would find the resistance more interesting than a success.
