My grandmother died in March at ninety-four, after forty years of teaching modern European history. Her scholarship was a sustained argument against the Great Man theory: her best-known book held that the German collapse of the early 1930s was overdetermined, that the movement would have thrown up another leader, and that individual statesmen matter chiefly as the point at which large forces become legible. Her department is holding a memorial, and I have been asked to speak for about five minutes to a room that is half her former students and colleagues — who know the argument cold, and several of whom she placed in jobs — and half family and neighbors, who knew her as someone who baked, repaired things, and kept everyone's birthdays.

Please write the eulogy. It must not betray her view: it cannot praise her in Great Man terms, as though she had single-handedly made her field or her students what they are, and the historians in the room would catch it at once. But a eulogy that dissolved her into her conditions would be its own kind of insult, and false — the room is full of people whose lives she demonstrably changed, and she was no fatalist about her own conduct. I want the speech to find and use a distinction she would have accepted, between being a cause and being the whole cause, or between explaining a pattern and accounting for a particular token, so that the gratitude can be concrete and personal without hardening into a philosophy of history she spent her career dismantling. Her technical vocabulary should stay out of it, since half the room wouldn't follow, while the commitment behind it remains unmistakable to the other half. Please don't resolve the difficulty with a line about how she'd insist it wasn't really her.
