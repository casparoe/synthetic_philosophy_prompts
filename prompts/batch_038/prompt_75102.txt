Trying to settle an argument with my study group and I think one of us is talking nonsense, but I can't tell which. We went through the discursive dilemma: three-person panel on a student misconduct case, premises P (did they copy) and Q (was it deliberate), and the bylaw says sanction iff P&Q. Member 1 says P not Q, member 2 says Q not P, member 3 says both. Aggregate on the premises and the group accepts both P and Q, so it sanctions; aggregate on the conclusion and two members say no sanction, so it doesn't. My friend's take is that the paradox is just an artifact of badly drafted procedure — if the committee always votes on the conclusion, the groups judgments can never come out inconsistent, the dilemma dissolves, and List and Pettit's impossibility result is really just a warning about writing your standing orders more carefully. That sounded obviously right to me but my TA got pretty annoyed when I said it.

So where's the flaw? Is conclusion-voting actually a way out, or does it just relocate the problem — and if it relocates it, to where? Relatedly, the impossibility theorem cant be saying "don't have a voting procedure at all," since any procedure you pick is going to be some aggregation rule, so what exactly is it ruling out that conclusion-voting was supposed to save?
