I am drafting the framing chapter for a collected volume on the metaphysics of the special sciences, and I have hit a wall I would like your help with. Please adopt, for this exercise, the standpoint of a philosopher of mind who has worked both on the metaphysics of realization relations and on how comparative and systems neuroscientists actually classify structures and functions across species. Before you commit to anything, spend some effort brainstorming privately: enumerate every candidate diagnosis of the dispute described below, including at least one you expect to reject and one that treats the dispute as in some way merely verbal. I know the canonical literature as well as you do, so do not spend words narrating how the argument developed, and do not give me a reading list, footnotes, or citations — I want you to argue from the content of the positions rather than gesture at sources, and I would much rather you describe the camps in your own terms than risk misattributing a view to a named author.

The dispute, of course, is whether multiple realizability defeats reduction. It has been running for well over half a century, it has been declared settled in both directions at least three times, and nobody I know has moved. One camp treats multiple realizability as an established premise and builds an entire metaphysics on it: psychological kinds are multiply realized across species and in principle across substrates, so no psychoneural identity holds, so the special sciences are autonomous, so the higher-level kinds do their explanatory work without any lower-level kind doing the same work twice. A second camp holds that the premise has been massively oversold, on two grounds — that most of what gets cited as multiple realization is merely variation in the arrangement, size, or number of parts made of the same kinds of stuff, which is not the sort of diversity that could block an identity, and that the surviving examples are modal intuitions about Martians and about robots whose functional organization is implemented by the population of a country, which are the wrong kind of evidence for a thesis about what our sciences actually require. A third camp, closer to the philosophy of neuroscience, points out that researchers routinely treat structures in distantly related species as homologous precisely in order to generalize findings across them, so the practice seems to presuppose the very identities the first camp denies; and a fourth camp concedes multiple realization wholesale and simply denies that it blocks reduction, because on a mechanistic or model-based account of reduction the reduced theory is allowed to be disjunctive at the lower level. In my graduate seminar we sharpened the standoff with a stipulated case, which I want you to take as a stipulation rather than as an empirical claim about any actual animal: suppose we grant that every creature that exhibits aversive withdrawal from noxious stimulation does so using the same families of voltage-gated channels and the same transmitter chemistry, but that the packaging of that machinery varies without limit — a centralized cord here, distributed ganglia there, a cultured lattice on a chip somewhere else. The first camp says the shared channel-level machinery is beside the point, since the identity theorist was talking about kinds at a different level of description; the second says it is decisive, since it is exactly the shared lower-level natural kind the identity theorist was owed. Neither claim looks to me like a move within the debate so much as a restatement of where each side already stands.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that the disagreement is not about the modal facts at all and not really about neuroanatomy either, but about three separable things that have been run together: first, the grain at which physical kinds are individuated, since at a coarse grain everything is multiply realized in a trivial way and at a fine grain nothing is, which would make both camps' arguments question-begging in the same direction; second, an equivocation on the word "realization" between a determinable–determinate relation and a part–whole or causal-contribution relation, so that the camps are not even disagreeing about the same relation when they disagree about whether it is one-to-many; and third, a smuggled criterion of naturalness, since the second camp's demand for genuine diversity in the realizers only bites if one accepts that the relevant joints are fixed independently of scientific interests, which the first camp implicitly denies. So: is that right? Diagnose this dispute rather than adjudicate it. Tell me whether it is substantive, verbal, or a case of two groups answering different questions under one heading — one a metaphysical question about what is possible, the other a methodological question about what the special sciences must presuppose — and whether the residual disagreement, if any, is a hidden empirical question about actual diversity in nervous systems, a disagreement about what counts as a good taxonomy, or bedrock intuitions about naturalness that neither side can argue for. Say explicitly whether you think the impasse shows the question to be ill-posed or merely hard, and defend that. Then tell me what would count as progress on my diagnosis: a new distinction, a formal result about when type-identity claims are grain-relative, a carefully documented case study from comparative neuroscience, a change of subject, or nothing at all — and if you think no progress should be expected, argue for that conclusion rather than simply asserting it. Finally, wherever your diagnosis depends on an empirical claim about brains or about scientific practice that you are not confident of, flag it as such and say what would settle it; I would rather have honest hedging than confident invention.
