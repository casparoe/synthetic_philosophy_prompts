Last November I spent most of a Saturday arguing with an old friend — an economist by training and a deflationist about biology by temperament — about the handicap principle, and I came away frustrated rather than convinced either way. His charge had two parts. First, that wastefulness is the entire engine of reliable communication: a display that squanders nothing cannot, on his account, carry information, because anyone could produce it. Second, that this makes the doctrine immune to refutation, since any extravagant trait can be baptised a signal after the fact and any cheap but reliable trait explained away as something else entirely. My counter was that the engine is not waste but asymmetry — that what buys honesty is costs and benefits falling differently across types, so that one can have ruinous aggregate expenditure transmitting nothing at all, and one can have near-costless displays that are stably informative. I could not make him feel the difference between those two claims, and three hours of examples made it worse rather than better. Talking has failed; I want to try staging it.

So: design a classroom activity that walks participants into the collapse of my friend's thesis through their own decisions, well before anyone names it.

**Setting.** A 90-minute upper-level seminar of 22 students, roughly half biology and half philosophy. Nobody has encountered signalling theory. About six have had one term of game theory and are no better than the rest at arithmetic under time pressure. The room has a whiteboard, index cards, and a box of poker chips. No money changes hands and nothing counts toward their grade; if the chips are to matter, make them matter socially.

**Structure.** I want a repeated sender–receiver donation game, run in three rounds with different structural parameters, players re-paired and re-typed each round. In one round the cost of displaying is identical for both types and large relative to the endowment, yet the round must end in a pooling outcome: everyone displays, nobody learns anything, and the class has collectively incinerated a great many chips. That round is aimed squarely at my friend. In a second round the display cost is identical across types and small, but the payoff from being believed differs sharply by type; here reliability must stabilise with almost no waste. In a third round the marginal cost of displaying differs by type while the benefit does not, so participants can compare the two routes to the same outward behaviour. Give me integer chip payoffs, write out the incentive inequalities that make each round produce the outcome you intend, and tell me the second-best equilibrium each round could fall into instead and how I should steer if the class lands there.

**Mechanics I care about.** Senders choose only an intensity from a fixed menu — no speech, no gesture, no negotiation — and receivers choose only whether to transfer. Tell me how I enforce the silence, because they will talk. Include a public tally posted after each round showing chips destroyed by displaying alongside chips actually transferred, so the ratio becomes visible on the wall rather than argued about. Provide an optional fourth round, if the clock allows, in which the same pairs repeat without any change to the payoffs, so that reputation does the work that cost was doing.

**Framing.** Choose a cover story that is not about peacocks, courtship, or job applicants, and be explicit about which type in each round corresponds to the needy signaller and which to the high-quality signaller, since those two structures run in opposite directions and a cover story that makes one type look like a free rider will corrupt the behaviour I am trying to observe. The participant handout must be one page and must never use the words signal, honest, dishonest, quality, or handicap. No lecture beforehand; the term arrives only in the debrief.

**Debrief.** Map specific moments of each round onto the distinctions at stake: waste versus asymmetry, marginal versus absolute cost, a display being reliable versus a display being informative, and the difference between reliability that comes from cost, reliability that comes from an unforgeable constraint, and reliability that comes from receivers punishing bluffs. Then confront the unfalsifiability charge directly: state which round is the discriminating one, what result in the room would have vindicated my friend instead of me, and how I should answer the reply that a determined deflationist can always relabel the cheap-but-reliable round as a different kind of signal altogether.

**Failure modes.** List at least five things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — including whatever the honesty norm imported from ordinary conversation will do to the lying incentive — and give me a fix for each.

**Honest limits.** End with a frank account of what the activity cannot reproduce and that I must therefore supply myself: that participants deliberate where signals are supposed to be selected; that receiver psychology is frozen rather than co-evolving; that types are discrete, exogenous, and privately known; and that trading chips additively quietly assumes away the objection that fitness does not aggregate additively. Say plainly which of these gaps is most likely to mislead a student into a false generalisation.

I am not asking for sources, a bibliography, or a reading list — I need something I can run on Tuesday.
