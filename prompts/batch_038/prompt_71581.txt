In the final, unfinished chapter of the *Political Treatise* — the chapter on democracy, where the treatise breaks off at Spinoza's death — he first fixes the extension of the citizen body and then defends one of its exclusions on grounds that look different in kind from the grounds the definition supplies. Two moves, quoted here from a nineteenth-century English translation (section numbering varies by edition; I use the numbering of that translation):

First, he declares his subject to be the democracy "wherein all, without exception, who owe allegiance to the laws of the country only, and are further independent and of respectable life, have the right of voting in the supreme council and of filling the offices of the dominion." He explains that the first clause excludes foreigners; that he added the requirement of independence "to exclude women and slaves, who are under the authority of men and masters, and also children and wards, as long as they are under the authority of parents and guardians"; and that the requirement of respectable life excludes those infamous for crime or for some disgraceful means of livelihood.

Second, in the next section he asks "whether women are under men's authority by nature or institution? For if it has been by mere institution, then we had no reason compelling us to exclude women from government." He answers that experience shows the origin of their subjection in their weakness: nowhere have the sexes ruled jointly; the Amazons, on the ancient report he cites, would not suffer men to remain among them; and if women were "equally distinguished by force of character and ability, in which human power and therefore human right chiefly consist," then among so many nations some would surely be found where both sexes rule alike, or where men are ruled by women. He adds that men generally love women from lust, that they esteem women's cleverness in proportion to their beauty, and that they are ill-disposed to suffer the women they love to show favour to others — whence joint rule would be greatly hurtful to peace.

Now consider a commonwealth of exactly the kind described, in which there exists a substantial class of adult women who satisfy every clause of the first definition: born of citizen parents on the soil of the country; neither married nor anyone's ward; not servants, and dependent on no man's will for their livelihood; owing allegiance to no power but the laws of the country; of respectable life. Suppose further that in this commonwealth such women already hold and administer property, direct their own households and enterprises, and sit in certain district councils alongside men, with no disturbance of the peace that the passion argument predicts.

Your task is to construct the best argument, conducted entirely within Spinoza's own framework, that his principles commit him to counting this class as citizens with the right to vote in the supreme council and to hold office, and that the argument of the second section cannot bear the weight he places on it.

Please observe the following constraints.

1. Argue internally. No appeal to modern egalitarian premises, to rights Spinoza would reject as fictions, or to the claim that he simply shared the prejudices of his century — that is a diagnosis, not an argument. Every load-bearing premise should be one Spinoza states or is committed to.

2. Draw on, and say explicitly how each item does work: his identification of natural right with natural power; his claim, at the start of the chapter on the civil state, that the right of the supreme authority is nothing but natural right limited by the power of the multitude "guided, as it were, by one mind," so that whatever augments that power augments the commonwealth's right; his earlier argument in the *Theologico-Political Treatise* that democracy is the most natural form of state, since in it no one transfers his right to another so absolutely that he is not afterwards consulted, but transfers it to the majority of the whole society of which he is himself a part, with the consequence that all remain equal as they were in the state of nature; and the distinction he draws at the head of the democracy chapter between democracy and aristocracy, on which a franchise narrowed by law — to the elder-born, or to those paying a certain sum — remains a democracy precisely because office is owed by law rather than conferred by the council's free choice.

3. Interrogate the epistemic standing of the empirical premise in the second section. He moves from the universality of male rule, plus one ancient report about the Amazons, to a claim about nature; he locates the relevant capacity in force of character and ability; and he himself identifies lust, the aesthetic rating of women's cleverness, and jealousy as features of men's imaginative life. Explain what his account of inadequate ideas, of judgment formed from custom and memory rather than from adequate causal understanding, and of the affects that shape what we take to be natural, implies about the reliability of that inference — especially given that the customs offered as evidence were themselves produced by exclusion.

4. Then present the strongest reply available to a defender of Spinoza's consistency — for instance, that the independence clause states a legal criterion while the second section asserts that no woman can in fact be her own master, so that the hypothetical class is not possible but merely imagined; or that the passion argument is a claim about the stability and power of the state and therefore survives any concession about individual capacity; or that the exclusion is a prudential accommodation to the multitude's affects, which on his own reckoning the legislator must take as he finds them — and answer that reply rather than dismissing it.

5. Close by stating precisely what Spinoza would have to revise or abandon if your argument succeeds, and whether the concession in the conditional he poses at the start of the second section already contains the concession you are asking him to make.

No bibliography, citations, or reference list is needed or wanted; work from the materials described above and from your own grasp of the system.
