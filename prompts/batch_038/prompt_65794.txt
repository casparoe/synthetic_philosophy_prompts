Right, so I've been writing up my position on well-being for a journal club and I want someone to stress-test it before I present, because at this point I can't tell which bits are load-bearing and which are quietly at war with each other. The package:

1. I'm a hedonist about well-being in the strict sense: for a given person, only pleasure is intrinsically good for them and only pain intrinsically bad. Achievement, friendship, knowledge, being respected — good only insofar as they feed into that.

2. I'm a naturalist about value, and for me that means facts about how well someone's life goes have to be fixed by facts about the feel of that life. I don't want value properties that outstrip experience, because I don't see what could make them true.

3. I deny that pleasure is one felt quality. Eating a peach, finally getting a proof to go through, laughing at something a friend said, the relief of taking off tight shoes — I don't think there's a single phenomenal ingrediant running through those cases, and I don't think searching for one is a good use of time. Pleasure is a heterogeneous kind, unified loosely if at all.

4. From 3 I take myself to be entitled to reject Mill's move. There are no higher and lower pleasures in the sense he needs — no difference in quality over and above quantity. All pleasure is good for the same reason and commensurable with all the rest on one scale, so aggregating by something like intensity times duration is legitimate.

5. I reject the experience machine as an objection to hedonism. My read is that the complaint about the electrode life is really a bundle of perfectionist stuff — you aren't doing anything, you aren't a certain sort of person — which is an intuition about value generally, not about the subject's own good.

6. But I do think two lives with identical totals of pleasure can differ in how good they are for the person living them. My standard case: Anna has a happy marriage and real mathematical results; Bea is deceived throughout and has a happy-seeming marriage plus results she never actually produced. Bea's life feels just as good to her. I still think Anna's is better for Anna.

7. Same with sadistic pleasure: pleasure taken in someone's suffering isn't good for the person taking it, even though it's genuinely pleasurable and even setting aside any badness in the wider state of affairs.

8. The worry I can't shake is this: if 3 is right, has my monism gone? A theory whose one intrinsic good turns out to be a loose grab-bag doesn't look like a monistic theory of well-being to me.

So — audit it. Which of these genuinely can't be held together, which only look like they clash and dissolve once you spell them out properly, and for each real clash what has to be given up and what giving it up costs me. Rank them by severity; I'd rather be told about the one bad thing than handed a flat list of eight mild ones. And say which single revision, if I could only afford one, buys back the most. I've no favourite among these and I'm not trying to steer you — if the cheapest fix is to stop being a hedonist, tell me that.

No literature review and no reading list, please; I'll fill in sources myself. What I want is the tracing of what commits me to what.

Finally, for each tension you call genuine, tell me how confident you are that it's genuine rather than merely apparent, and then single out the one claim in your whole audit you're least sure of and say why. If you think 8 is a non-problem, say so flatly rather than hedging to be polite.
