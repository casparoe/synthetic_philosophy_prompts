I'm one of the three union reps on the joint labor–management technology committee at our city's transit agency, and there's a decision on the agenda for the 14th that I can't get a read on.

Back in 2019 the board adopted a standing rule that everyone called the remainder rule: any system that automates part of a job has to leave at least one substantive task under human control, the department deploying it has to name that task in writing before go-live, and it has to report every year on whether people actually do it. Nobody thought hard about it until now. New crew-scheduling software (rostering for bus operators, plus work-order assignment in facilities) is going live in two divisions, and management says the rule is satisfied two ways: in facilities, a human reviews each generated work order before it gets assigned; in operations, drivers get a free-text 'notes on this roster' field and a scheduler retains the right to override the software once a quarter.

The problem is the pilot data. In facilities the review was stamped 100% of the time, median review time about four seconds, and the stamp is logged automatically the moment the order is opened. In the paratransit pilot, dispatchers used their override on roughly one run in nine and missed-connection complaints dropped. So one member says the four-second stamp proves the rule is theatre and we should kill it or rewrite it to demand tasks with real consequences; another says the stamp isn't nothing, because it means a person is answerable for that order, and answerability was the point all along; and a third, who drives, says flatly that being handed a pretend task is more insulting than being automated outright, and if the software is worth the $2.1M a year the vendor says its worth, workers would rather see part of that as hours or money than as a checkbox.

Can you draft the memo I'll hand round before the meeting? I want it to land on an actual recommendation — approve management's proposal as written, reject it, or approve with specific conditions — and if it's conditions, I want them phrased as tests the committee could apply to the next system too, not just this one. Take the driver's objection seriously rather than fobbing it off with something about the dignity of work. And say something about whether the accountability reading and the meaning-of-work reading of the rule can be satisfied by the same task, or whether we've been running two different rules under one name for six years without noticing.
