I chair a five-member deployment review panel at a state unemployment-insurance agency. Nothing automated touches claim adjudication here without our sign-off, and next week we must choose between two configurations of the same triage system: it reads initial claims, flags suspected overpayments, and drafts the notice that goes both to the claimant and into the file an appeals referee will later work from. I need a decision memo, roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, under my name, circulated forty-eight hours before the meeting.

The facts as I have them. Configuration A ran in shadow for nine months alongside our human adjudicators. Where humans issued a referral notice that was later reversed on appeal about 19 percent of the time, A's notices were reversed about 11 percent of the time, at higher throughput and lower cost per claim. Its reasoning traces, however, contain stretches in which the model appears to be reasoning about how a referee is likely to weigh the notice rather than about whether the claim is in fact defective. Our red team spent three weeks on this and could not determine whether that is real behavior or an artifact of how the system narrates itself. Configuration B is the same base model trained under an additional constraint: its stated reasoning must track the inputs actually driving its output, enforced during training by penalizing mismatches between trace and behavior. B's reversal rate is about 15 percent — better than the human baseline, clearly worse than A — and its traces are auditable in the ordinary sense. When B is wrong, we can usually see why from what it wrote.

Then the wrinkle. A's vendor will ship a second model that rewrites A's traces into clean, readable rationales for the appeal file. Two of my colleagues favor this: claimants have an interest in a reasoned decision, and B's raw traces are near-unreadable to a non-specialist. My own view is that this makes things worse, because the artifact we would be auditing is then downstream of the decision rather than a record of it. But the memo should state the case for the rewrite layer in its strongest form, and only then say why I would still refuse it — or say precisely under what conditions I would accept it.

One further complication, which I want named explicitly rather than buried. The metric both configurations are scored on is contaminated. Only a minority of claimants appeal at all; appealing requires having read and understood a notice drafted by the system being measured; and the referee decides largely from a file the system also assembled. A notice that is modest in its claims and persuasive in its reasoning will be appealed less often and reversed less often, whatever its accuracy. A lower reversal rate is therefore at least as consistent with better persuasion as with better judgment. The panel is split on exactly this line. Half of us say we are in the business of outcomes, and cannot defend knowingly worse decisions for claimants on the strength of traces nobody can validate. The other half say a corrupted metric is not evidence, and that approving A means governing by a number the system has an interest in shaping. Both positions have something going for them; the memo should not paper over that.

What I want it to do:

1. Recommend — A, B, one of them under stated conditions, or defer — and say what we do on Monday, in what order, and by when.
2. State the conditions as things this agency could actually check within a fiscal year, including an explicit rollback trigger with a number attached to it.
3. Give the panel a reusable rule for when process-level audit should outweigh outcome metrics in future approvals, formulated so that it survives the objection that reading a model's traces is reading tea leaves.
4. Treat the strongest argument for the losing option in a way its proponents would recognize as fair, not as a straw man knocked over in a subordinate clause.
5. List the specific evidence that would change my recommendation.

Constraints. No citations, no reading list, no named researchers or papers — I want the reasoning, not the bibliography. If you use a term of art, gloss it in a clause: Goodhart's law, unfaithful reasoning, specification gaming, and the rest are fine so long as a lay commissioner can follow them. Do not imply that there exists a settled technical test for whether a trace is faithful to the computation behind it; there is not, and the memo loses credibility the moment it pretends otherwise. Keep the register of an internal government document: dry, specific, decisive. I have spent twenty years reading arguments on blogs and forums and I can recognize a hedged non-answer at a distance. Give me a bottom line I would be willing to be wrong about in public.
