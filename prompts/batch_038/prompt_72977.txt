I am a novelist, forty-one, and for six years I have lived in a valley town of about four thousand people, where I serve — reluctantly, and because I was the only person who answered the notice — on a small committee charged with spending a bequest left by a neighbour who made his money in timber. The sum will do one thing properly or two things badly. Two proposals have survived to the final meeting, which is in three weeks. The first would build a hundred-and-twenty-seat playhouse on the site of the old grain depot, with a resident company, a winter season, touring productions, and a youth programme run by the high school drama teacher. The second would fund an open-ended calendar of participatory things — dances, suppers, an amateur orchestra, storytelling nights, a midsummer pageant in the park, the upkeep of the swimming hole and the ski tow — together with a paid coordinator whose entire job would be getting residents to make such events themselves.

My fellow committee members favour the playhouse almost unanimously, and their reasons are not venal. They say it will give the town a shared room; that young people will learn to speak and to stand up straight; that watching lives unlike their own enacted in front of them will make people less parochial; and that a town with a theatre takes itself seriously. When I say I am not persuaded, they ask what precisely I object to, and I discover that I cannot answer. I have only a cluster of feelings. One is that the evenings here I remember best — the sandbag line during the flood, the supper held for four hundred people in a gymnasium, the pageant two summers ago in which most of the audience was in the cast — had nothing about them that could be watched. Another is a suspicion that sitting in the dark being moved by a character's generosity is a way of spending the impulse to be generous rather than storing it; that I have left certain performances feeling creditable for having been moved, and done nothing. A third, which I mistrust most, is that a play can only ever be as decent as its audience already is — that it flatters and confirms rather than reforms, and that a town receives from the stage a prettified mirror of its own disposition.

I want a serious answer rather than a diplomatic one. First: is there an actual argument latent in what I have described, or only a temperament — the aesthetic conservatism of a person who would rather bake bread than read it aloud? If there is an argument, state it as strongly as you can, and then tell me where it is weakest, since I expect it is weakest somewhere and would rather learn that from you than from the drama teacher, who has already informed me that I am describing a town without an imagination. Second: my position is embarrassingly easy to attack, because I make my living from a representational art consumed by people sitting alone in rooms; if the playhouse is suspect for the reasons above, the novel is suspect for reasons at least as strong. Address that squarely rather than letting me off. Third, and only after the first two: what should I recommend to the committee, and how should I put it so that it does not sound like a person who merely dislikes actors? If my unease turns out to be somebody else's argument wearing my clothes, I would prefer to be told whose, and in what form; I read a great deal of philosophy as an undergraduate and have retained almost none of it. Please do not send me a reading list — I will do the looking-up myself, and I am far more interested in the reasoning than in the apparatus.
