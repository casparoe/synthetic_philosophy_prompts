i'm trying to bring something to a workshop where everyone has to put their own position on the table, and before i embarrass myself i want a consistency audit of what i actually believe about dirty hands in politics. here's the list, roughly in the order i arrived at it — please don't restate the debate back to me, i know it, i just can't see my own shape from inside.

1. justification in politics is basically consequentialist: what makes an official's act permissible is that it comes out ahead. there are constraints on what officials may do, but they're the sort that get overridden when enough is on the line.

2. overridden doesn't mean cancelled. when a constraint gets overridden there's a real remainder, and the right attitude to it is guilt rather than mere rational regret — the minister who did the thing should experience himself as someone who did a wrong thing, and should say so in public rather than filing it under "costs."

3. i don't think any of this is a paradox. "ought, all things considered" and "pro tanto wrong" can both be true of one act without contradiction; i'm not doing the tragic-contradiction schtick where the moral point of dirty hands is that our concepts break.

4. dirt is personal to the doer. no institution has dirty hands, no successor inherits them, no electorate gets its share handed out at the polling station.

5. but i do think a government that keeps a benefit while disowning the act that produced it is in a distinctly bad way — you can't keep the harbor and complain about the dredging. i want to say the repudiating-yet-benefiting successor is tainted, without saying the dirt transferred.

6. the amount of dirt doesn't scale with the stakes. it's fixed by what you did, not by how much good doing it bought: the dirt in betraying a confidence doesn't shrink just because betraying it saved more people than keeping it would have.

7. related, and i feel strongly about this: the official who declines to do the dirty act when it genuinely is required is dirty too. clean hands is not a defense. my running case (call it the Kessler memo: a minister authorizes a leak he knows will get his informant killed, and the leak is the only thing that stops a pogrom) — i judge the leaker dirty and i judge his abstaining deputy dirty as well, maybe dirtier.

8. delegation doesn't launder. getting someone else to do the thing leaves your hands as dirty as if you'd done it with your own.

9. at the same time i think a competent administration should have standing procedures, so that the awful calls get made by whoever is on shift under written guidance instead of depending on which minister happends to be in the building.

10. role morality is a sham — same rules for officials as for everybody else, being a minister doesn't change what's forbidden.

11. and yet officials face situations private people basically never face, and i think that distinctiveness is load-bearing in my view, not decoration.

12. last thing: i want the account to be action-guiding in the moment, something an official can actually use while deciding, and i also think you can't tell whether your hands came out dirty until afterwards, sometimes much later.

so: which of these pairs genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? i'm near-certain that a few are completely fine and i can't tell which ones those are, so please separate the real from the apparent instead of flagging everything. rank the real tensions by severity, say for each what the cheapest revision would be and what i'd loose by making it, and then tell me which single item, if i just dropped or rewrote it, would clean up the most. no bibliography, no summary of what other people think — just the audit.
