so I sit on the steering committee of a consortium of about thirty European graduate schools in the social sciences — private association, its own statues, no legal power over anybody, but member status matters because that's how the joint doctoral programs and the exchange funding flow. Two things happened at one of our members last month. The ministry in that country rewrote the appointment rules so the rector is now chosen by the minister from a short list the university senate merely proposes, and it folded a competing research institute into the university and transferred that institute's library holdings to a foundation whose board is majority-ministry. Nobody has been fired, nothing has been shut, and people at the university still publish and criticize the government in the press as freely as before — two of them have actually written to us begging us NOT to expel the place, on the grounds that losing member status would leave their doctoral students with no route to a recognized degree and cut them off from the one external network that gives them any cover.

Meanwhile another committee member says we sat on our hands two years ago when a city government stacked the supervisory board at a different member institution, arguably worse, and nobody proposed suspension then. Her position is that the language in our statutes is just a partisan label we reach for when we don't like a government, and that unless we can state what counts in a way we'd apply identically to that older case we should do nothing at all. A third member wants us to adopt a formal graduated scale, level one through five, each with consequences attached, and has asked that the memo either defend or kill that idea.

Our statutes condition membership on institutional autonomy in academic matters and say nothing further. Draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting: a recommendation (retain, retain with conditions, suspend, or expel) with a concrete time frame and an explicit statement of what would trigger escalation and what would trigger reversal; a test for loss of autonomy that a committee member could actually apply to a case they'd never seen before, not a list of examples; a real engagement with the symmetry objection and with the two scholars' letter rather than a sentence waving at each; and a section saying who you think the member even is here — the university as a legal entity, the academic staff, or the enrolled students — because I'm now pretty convinced most of our disagreement is secretly about that.
