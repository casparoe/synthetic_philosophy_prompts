I'm six months into a novel whose two central characters are meant to be an argument in flesh. One is a master joiner, seventy-one, who has had a stroke: he can still describe, draw, and dictate every cut of a dovetail with undimmed precision, and can even demonstrate it slowly with his left hand, but his right arm will not carry out what his mouth and pencil specify. The other is his granddaughter, nineteen, who has stood at his bench since she was seven and whose hands are now better than his ever were; she cannot explain what she does, gets the traditional names of the joints wrong, and goes blank when asked how she knows a drawer will close true.

Before I commit to the way these two argue across the whole book, I want a decision tree of the debate their conditions belong to: whether knowing how to do something is a species of knowing that something is the case. Please build it out of the logical space rather than out of a list of philosophers. Each node should be a question a reader could answer from my descriptions alone, without knowing which view it leads to — whether the old man still knows how; whether the girl's inarticulacy counts as evidence for or against her knowing; whether her success is best described as a disposition, as a representation she cannot report, or as something else entirely; whether one account can absorb both cases or whether the theory must be split. The leaves should be named positions, and must include at least the intellectualist option, the Rylean anti-intellectualist one, a dispositionalist one, and any hybrid or disjunctive view you think these two characters force on us.

Two further things. Flag every pair of leaves that my joiner and my granddaughter cannot actually tell apart, since I would rather know that than write a scene that silently assumes a distinction nobody can test. And close by listing the combinations of answers your tree permits that no one occupies, with a line each on why the space is empty there — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
