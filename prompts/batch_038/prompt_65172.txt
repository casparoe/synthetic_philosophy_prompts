I chair the undergraduate assessment appeals panel in the mathematics department at a mid-sized university, and on Tuesday we heard a case that has split us three ways and left me unable to write the decision. The appellant is a third-year student in our required two-semester analysis sequence. The final examination asked candidates to state and prove the intermediate value theorem for a continuous real function on a closed bounded interval, taking as given that the function is negative at the left endpoint and positive at the right. The student instead rebuilt the reals as regular Cauchy sequences carrying explicit moduli of convergence, proved a bisection lemma yielding, for every natural number n, a point x with the absolute value of f(x) below 2 to the minus n, showed that the points so obtained form a Cauchy sequence — invoking the locatedness of the range at the step where one compares the midpoint value against zero — and then declined to pass to a limit. Her closing paragraph says that an exact root can be produced only under an additional local nonvanishing hypothesis on f which the classical statement does not carry; that trichotomy is unavailable to her, only apartness; that she will not infer existence from the impossibility of nonexistence without Markov's principle; and that what she actually needs at the bisection step is uniform continuity on the interval, which she claims by Brouwer's fan theorem. She adds that classical analysis embeds into her framework by way of the negative translation, so that nothing she wrote conflicts with the classical reading of the question. The examiner awarded roughly forty percent, with the marginal note that she had answered a different question, established no root, and imported a compactness-style hypothesis the syllabus never mentioned.

I spent most of last night arguing about it with an old friend who works on proof theory and has no stake in the appeal whatsoever. Her position is that a proof is correct or incorrect only relative to the framework in which it is offered; that our regulations require candidates to demonstrate the theorem, not to demonstrate it classically; and that marking her down for declining excluded middle charges her with a philosophical error the paper never announced it was testing. She also pointed out, rather sharply, that the student's framework is not the assumption-free paradise the examiner imagined: Bishop-style analysis leans on countable choice, and had the student assumed full choice her logic would have collapsed back into the classical one by a standard result of Diaconescu's — so the fair charge is not that she smuggled in an assumption but that she declared hers openly, which is more than the paper did. My own instinct runs the other way. The paper tests a syllabus and a shared vocabulary; an approximate root is not a root, and the gap between the two is precisely the content of the question; and a rule letting each candidate answer in the framework of their choosing has no obvious stopping point, since the next appellant will want to know why dialetheism is excluded if intuitionism is admissible. We agree only that the panel cannot re-mark the script as a wash and call it wisdom.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the Board of Examiners ahead of their ratification meeting in three weeks' time, somewhere between nine hundred and twelve hundred words. It should reach a definite bottom line — uphold the appeal in full, uphold it in part with a specified remedy, or dismiss it — and it should articulate the standard the panel is applying in language the rest of the department could apply again without me in the room. I want it to engage the mathematics rather than the sentiments: whether what she proved is a strictly weaker theorem or a different theorem about a different object; whether uniform continuity was smuggled in or fairly earned, and whether the fan theorem is available to her at all given that she is otherwise working in a Bishop-style setting; whether locatedness is doing unacknowledged work in the bisection step; and whether the negative-translation remark genuinely rescues her answer or merely locates the disagreement. Then give the losing side its strongest case in its own terms: if we dismiss, state the best version of the claim that our examination regulations quietly legislate a metaphysics of the real number, and say what the department would have to change to stop doing that; if we uphold, state the best version of the worry that we have made the paper ungradeable and set a precedent no examiner can administer. Close with a forward-looking rule for framework-relative submissions — how a candidate must signal the framework, what an examiner must do about it, and how the rule handles the objection that the class of admissible frameworks is unbounded — and end with who does what and by when. Reasons, not references: nobody on this board will read a bibliography, and I would far rather you explain a principle than name its author.
