"Representational theories of mind face a problem about error. If a mental state's content is fixed by what normally causes it, then it looks like the state can't be false, because whenever something causes it, that thing falls under the content. Fodor's version of the difficulty is the disjunction problem. A frog's visual system fires at flies, but it also fires at small dark moving dots — BBs, shadows, bits of gravel. If causation fixes content, the state's content should be disjunctive: fly-or-BB-or-shadow. Then the frog never misrepresents when it snaps at a BB, which is absurd, since frogs clearly do get things wrong. Fodor pressed this against teleosemantics, but the teleosemantic answer is the natural one. Content isn't fixed by what causes the state now, it's fixed by what the producing mechanism was selected for. Ancestral frogs whose detectors responded to flies got food and left offspring; the ones that responded to BBs got nothing. So the mechanism's function is fly detection and its content is fly. When a frog snaps at a BB, the state still tokens fly, but falsely — malfunction, not a change of content. This is the big selling point: teleosemantics gets normativity out of biology. A representation ought to be about flies in the same sense that a heart ought to pump, and that ought is descriptive rather than evaluative, which is what naturalizing content requires. It also gives a clean verdict on Davidson's Swampman: a duplicate thrown together by lightning in a swamp has no selection history, so on this view its states have no content at all, however much they look like thoughts. That's a cost, since the verdict seems harsh, but it is a principled one.

The two classic versions differ in scope. Dretske's indicator semantics says a state means whatever it was reliably correlated with during a learning period, after which the correlation can be broken and the content stays put. But because the learning period is itself a selection process — the system settles on the correlation that keeps working and drops the rest — Dretske's account is teleological too. The real difference is just phylogeny versus ontogeny: Millikan appeals to the species' evolutionary history, Dretske to the individual's learning history. Relatedly, Millikan's version is producer-side, since content is settled by what the mechanism producing the signal was selected for, whereas Dretske's is consumer-side, since content depends on what the downstream systems do with the signal.

Two worries remain. First, mismatch: if content is fixed by ancestral conditions, then in a novel environment almost everything we think is systematically false, which looks like too much error. Second, over-intellectualization: it's hard to say what the selected-for function of justice or of square root of two would be.

Still, the account should be judged a partial success. Because content is fixed by what the mechanism was selected for, and because selection retains only mechanisms that produce accurate representations, teleosemantics also guarantees that content tracks truth. That is why it can underwrite the norm that beliefs ought to be true, and why most of our beliefs do in fact turn out true — something no purely causal theory can explain."
