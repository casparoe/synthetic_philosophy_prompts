I am seventeen, in my final year of high school, and for two years I have run the elections for our student council: writing the rules, printing and distributing the ballots, supervising the voting, and counting everything at the end. Almost all of our rules were inherited, unexamined, from how American elections are run, and when I sat down this autumn to revise the handbook I found that I could not give a justification for several of them that I actually believe. What I want is not a defense or a reform program but an account of what view of voting the rules already contain.

Here is the cluster of practices, taken together:

1. The ballot is secret, and the secrecy is enforced rather than incidental. A voter cannot prove to anyone how they voted, and paying someone for their vote, or accepting payment for it, is a crime. As far as I can tell, a good number of states go further and forbid a voter even to photograph a completed ballot.

2. Nevertheless we treat voting as a public honor and abstention as a mild disgrace. Voters are given small stickers to wear, turnout is reported as a kind of score, and it is entirely unremarkable for someone to say that a person who did not vote has forfeited the right to complain about the outcome. Nothing is legally required of them; the pressure is all social.

3. In a close contest, voting for a candidate who cannot win is treated as a criticism of the voter: they wasted their vote, or they helped elect the candidate they like least. We say this even when the voter sincerely prefers that candidate. I have never heard anyone say the equivalent about reading an obscure book or supporting an unfashionable team, where preferring the minority option is not a fault at all.

4. Political money is handled in the opposite direction from the vote. Contributions above a certain size must be reported with the donor's name attached, and those reports are public and searchable. Being identified as the source of political money is treated as a normal consequence of giving; large-scale anonymous giving is precisely what the rules exist to prevent.

5. Australia requires voting by law and punishes failure to vote with a small fine, yet a voter can apparently discharge the obligation by handing in a blank or nonsense ballot. The state requires the appearance at the booth and not the content of the choice.

What theory of the vote makes that combination hang together, if any does? Is the vote something the voter owns, in which case why may it not be sold, traded, or shown; or is it held in trust for the rest of us, in which case why do we exhort rather than require, as the Australians do? Point 1 puzzles me most: secrecy makes it impossible for a vote ever to function as a message to a particular audience, since the voter can never establish what they did, and yet the entire rhetoric of elections is about expressing yourself at the ballot box. Point 3 seems to assume the opposite, that the vote matters only through its contribution to a result. Can one theory hold both of those, and can it also hold the asymmetry in point 4, where the same activity of influencing a collective outcome is publicly attributed in one currency and jealously concealed in the other?

Please separate what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely happen to fit well, since I expect more than one account to license the same rules, and mark clearly the places where the practice is underdetermined and the choice between candidate theories has to be settled on grounds the rules themselves do not supply. Say which rival conceptions of the vote each rule quietly excludes. Then tell me what would change under the replacements: if we concluded that a vote is the voter's own property to dispose of, or that voting is purely instrumental and no single vote makes a measurable difference, or that its value lies entirely in the performance of participation, which of the five practices would survive and which would have to go. Finish with what my student council handbook would look like if I rebuilt it on each of those views rather than on the one it inherited.
