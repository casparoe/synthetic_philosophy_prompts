I am preparing a unit for an upper-level philosophy-of-time seminar in which I pair Gödel's 1949 rotating-universe result with Vonnegut's *Slaughterhouse-Five*, and I am dissatisfied with the version of this pairing that circulates. The standard move — the Tralfamadorians see all moments at once, therefore the novel dramatizes Gödel's argument that time is unreal — seems to me to conflate at least three distinct theses and to miss the features of Gödel's construction that actually do the philosophical work. I want a careful reading that holds both sides to account, written as criticism rather than as an illustration-hunt.

Here is how I understand the philosophy. Please correct me where I have it wrong rather than accepting my framing and building on it:

1. Gödel's target was not the relativity of simultaneity or block-universe eternalism but something narrower and stronger: the objective lapse of time, an objective present that advances. Einstein had already accepted the former; Gödel took himself to be going further.
2. The mechanism is causal, not merely order-theoretic. In the rotating dust solution closed timelike curves are available, and because the spacetime is homogeneous — its isometry group acts transitively — they pass through every event rather than through some exotic outer region. So the worry is not that simultaneity is relative but that the before-and-after relation fails to be a global ordering at all.
3. The inference Gödel draws is modal, and this is where I think most readings go wrong. He does not claim the actual universe rotates; he claims that a world of the kind general relativity describes need not admit an objective lapse of time, and that this licenses a broadly Kantian conclusion about the ideality of time. Whether a mere possibility inside a physical theory can carry that weight — against Einstein's reply, in effect, that what matters is whether our universe is like that — is the live question.
4. Two further details I want the reading to use. First, in Gödel's spacetime no closed timelike curve is a geodesic: looping back requires sustained thrust, and there is a lower bound on the total acceleration a would-be time traveller must expend (a result I associate with Malament's work on the Gödel solution, though I may have the attribution wrong — treat it as a premise I am supplying, not as something you need to confirm). Gödelian time travel is expensive. Second, Gödel's reductio depends on the traveller's being able to act on the past he reaches — to meet himself, to alter what has already happened — since that is what generates the contradiction with the fixity of the past.

Against that, the novel. Billy Pilgrim's dislocations in time are involuntary, costless and non-negotiable; he revisits moments but never alters them; the Tralfamadorians teach that all moments exist permanently, that nothing can be changed, and that the dead are dead only at the moment of their death. The frame narrator, by contrast, is conspicuously in time: he ages, he visits an old war buddy, he tells us what the book's final image will be long before he arrives at it.

What I want:

- A reading that sorts out which of the three theses the novel actually endorses. Eternalism about the block, fatalism about agency, and the unreality of an objective lapse of time are not the same claim, and I suspect the novel asserts the second, presupposes the first, and never argues the third. Does Tralfamadorianism deliver anything like Gödel's conclusion, or does it deliver a consolation that Gödel's argument would not license?
- Attention to what the novel does with the two Gödelian details in (4). Billy's loops are free and inflicted on him, where Gödel's are paid for; and Billy never acquires the capacity Gödel's reductio requires, which means the novel gets a fixed past without the paradox. Is that a philosophically interesting divergence — a block universe with the causal pathology removed — or a cheat that lets the novel have fatalism without paying Gödel's price?
- A specific account of the shape of Billy's revisited moments. In the Gödel solution the loops are everywhere and indifferent; Billy's are a small, repetitive, trauma-selected set. Does the novel's structure resemble a causal geometry or intrusive memory, and does it matter that the psychological reading of Tralfamadore as a coping device is available to the reader — that the block may be a symptom rather than a discovery?
- Something about the narrator's own temporal position, and whether the fact that a doctrine of static four-dimensional reality must be told in sequence is something the novel notices, exploits, or simply falls foul of.
- An honest statement of where the reading strains: which of my four claims about Gödel the novel actively resists, which of my premises about the novel you would want checked against the text, and what Vonnegut gains by refusing the argument. If the novel adds something to the debate over time's reality that Gödel's construction cannot express, say what it is; if it does not, say that instead.
