I co-teach a grad seminar with a moral psychologist and we've spent three weeks going round in circles on something where I now suspect nobody in the room can say what the arguement actually is. Here's the knot. Haidt's questionnaire keeps coming back with the same pattern: conservatives endorse the "binding" stuff — loyalty, deference to authority, purity — considerably more than liberals, and liberals then insist those things aren't moral at all, they're convention, etiquette, taste, tribalism dressed up as obligation. Both camps are sincere and neither budges. What kind of disagreement is that, exactly?

Three diagnoses circulate and people slide between them mid-sentence without noticing:

1. It's substantive. There really are moral requirements about loyalty, rank, and contamination; liberals are blind to a chunk of the moral landscape; the scores track a genuine difference in what people can perceive, not in how they talk.

2. It's verbal. The two sides are drawing the boundary of "moral" in different places. One inherits the harm-and-fairness carve-out that developmental psychologists spent forty years treating as the signature of the moral domain — serious, generalizable, not up for grabs by an authority figure — and the other applies the word to a much wider slice of conduct. No difference in psychology, just different extensions.

3. It's methodological. The real fight is over which responses count as evidence of a moral judgment: self-reported relevance, third-person condemnation, deserved punishment, seriousness ranking, whether people think an authority could permit it. Different instruments, different verdicts, and the two sides are comparing across measures that were never commensurable.

My hunch, which I want you to attack rather than flatter, is (2) all the way down — that a lexical boundary question has been masquerading for twenty years as a discovery about human nature. But I can see the problem: the moment you ask whether the harm-and-fairness boundary is itself just a local cultural accomplishment rather than a finding, which is roughly the line Shweder pushed against the developmentalists, diagnosis (2) loses the neutral meaning of "moral" it was leaning on, and it seems to collapse back into (1).

So: diagnose it. Is the shape (1), (2), (3), some ordered combination, or a fourth thing — e.g. the two camps answering different questions under one heading, one about the causes of condemnation and one about the justification of principles? Argue from what each side actually says and does with its data, not from a survey of the field. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a measurement that would break the tie, a distinction that dissolves it, or a reason to expect no resolution ever and why that isn't just defeatism.

Last thing, and it's the one I most need for the seminar. If your diagnosis is (2), does that reopen the normative question — whether any of this psychology bears on who is right about morality — or does it kill that question permanently? I genuinely can't tell which, and my students have noticed.
