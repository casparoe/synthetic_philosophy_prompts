Question (20 marks): Section X of Hume's Enquiry is divided into two parts. Explain the argument of Part I about testimony and miracles, and say how Part II's observations relate to it. Then answer two things: (a) does Part I depend on Hume's definition of a miracle as a violation of a law of nature in a way that begs the question against someone reporting a miracle? (b) does Hume's remark that testimony establishes a miracle only if the falsehood of the testimony would be more miraculous than the fact it supports amount to conceding that testimony could in principle do that work?

Student answer: Hume defines a miracle as a violation of a law of nature. Since a law of nature is just a description of what has always and without exception happened, a violation of one is a contradiction in terms, so Part I is mostly unpacking the definition: no testimony can establish a contradiction, which is why no amount of testimony, however many reliable witnesses there are, could ever prove a miracle. Thats why the charge of begging the question sticks. Hume assumes the exceptionlessness of nature in the definition in order to rule miracles out, and the person reporting one never gets a hearing.

Part I does the real work by weighing two contrary experiences: our uniform experience of the regular course of nature against our experience of the general truthfulness of witnesses. The stronger experience wins, and where a miracle is concerned the experience of regularity always does, so the wise man proportions his belief to the evidence and withholds it.

Part II then just illustrates this with the actual reports. Hume notes that no miracle is attested by a sufficient number of sensible, educated, unimpeachable witnesses speaking publicly in a well-known place; that people have a passion for surprise and wonder and so are credulous; that miracle stories cluster among ignorant and barbarous peoples and in remote times; and that the miracle claims of rival religions cancel each other out. None of these add a new argument to Part I, they're all applications of the same point that testimony gets outweighed by uniform experience.

As for (b), the more-miraculous remark is just rhetorical. Hume cant really think testimony could reach that bar, because if testimony were that reliable the event it reported wouldnt count as a miracle in the first place.
