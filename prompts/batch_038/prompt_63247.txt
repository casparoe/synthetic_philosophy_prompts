I run a fortnightly reading group at my place, ten-ish people. About half are ML engineers who've never touched Sanskrit philosophy, the other half did some Indian philosophy in college and still remember the vocabulary. We've been working through the pramana debates and its going badly in one specific way: people don't actually disagree about the cases, they disagree about how to describe them, and then we burn two hours on labels. Last week Priya said the gavaya example — you're told a gavaya is like a cow, later you're out in the countryside, you see an animal that reminds you of a cow and it clicks that this must be one — was obviously just inference, and Dan said obviously it isn't, and it turned out they were using "inference" to mean two different things and neither would move. So for the next session I want to hand out something that sorts people by what they actually believe rather than what they call it.

Please write me a decision-tree quiz: nine questions, each one a concrete little scenario with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto the classical positions — perception-only at one end, the two-pramana reduction where everything else collapses into perception or inference, then the four, the five, and the six-pramana view at the other end. Each leaf should say which pramanas it admits and which school classically holds exactly that set, but the questions themselves have to be answerable by someone who's never heard the words upamana or arthapatti — no option text may contain a technical term, and nothing may just ask "would you call this testimony?"

A few things I want built in:

- Before the questions, sketch the logical space briefly: what would actually make one pramana a separate one rather than a species of another — a distinct object known, a distinct cognitive process, or knowledge you couldn't get any other way? My group is split on exactly this and I'd like the tree to sort on that axis early, in the first couple of branches if possible.
- At least three of the nine cases should come from our world rather than the classical stock examples. Definitely include one about getting an answer out of a language model, since Dan and I got into whether that's testimony at all when there's no trustworthy speaker behind it, and one about knowing there's no milk in the fridge without opening it. The Devadatta-style postulation case (fat, doesn't eat by day, so he must eat at night) can stay classical or get reworded, your call, but I want something occupying that slot.
- One question that gets at whether finding out you were right is a separate job you have to go do, or whether the same process that gave you the belief also tells you it was sound. Only include it if you can phrase it as a case someone could answer without knowing there's a named debate hiding behind it.

Also: flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, and if some combination of answers lands somewhere no school ever actually stood, say so and take a guess at why nobody's there — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. No references or reading list, I only want the handout itself. Diacritics optional, half the group can't type them anyway.
