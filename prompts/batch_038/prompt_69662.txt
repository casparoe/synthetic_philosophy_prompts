So I've been going back and forth with a friend who does decision theory, and I've talked myself into a fairly confident position about Buridan's ass that I now want properly stress-tested rather than nodded along to. My view is that the ass isn't a paradox at all, it's a modelling artifact, and it rests on three legs. First, no actual agent ever sits in exact indifference: agents are physical systems in a physical world stuffed with unnoticed asymmetries, a draught, a memory, one bundle of hay being a hair closer, and insisting on perfect equipoise is just idealizing away the situation you were asked about. Second, even granting the symmetry for the sake of argument, any adequate decision procedure has a tie-breaking stage built in, because searching for further differences costs something, so at some point "stop and take the left one" or "flip a coin" is exactly what a rational procedure does; the starving animal is a bug in the implementation, not a limit on rationality. Third, and following from that, choosing under exact indifference is coherent and unremarkable and agents do it constantly (which restaurant, which route home, which of two identical tins to grab), so all the fuss about whether there was secretly a preference difference or a genuinely arbitrary act of will looks to me like a dispute with no cash value.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a committed defender of my own view would concede as counting against it versus which they'd wave off. A few specific pressure points I keep coming back to: does my position actually differ from its neighbours, or does it collapse into one of them — the "there's never real balance because there are always sub-threshold inclinations doing the work" line, or the "the will can just break the deadlock without a determining reason" line? A collapse into a rival would be a defeater all on its own and I'd like to know if I've got one. Then there's what feels to me like the sharpest worry: a tie-breaking rule is either grounded in a preference, in which case the exact indifference never arose and my first leg did all the work, or it isn't grounded in anything, in which case I've reintroduced precisely the arbitrary act I claimed to have dispensed with. Is that a decisive regress or can it be blocked, and if it can be blocked, what does the block cost me? Related: is randomized choice a way of choosing or a way of declining to choose, and does decision theory ever require mixing under indifference rather than merely permit it — because if it only permits it, I'm not sure my appeal to coin-flipping does any normative work at all. And I want the morally loaded versions taken seriously, not just the hay: two equally deserving claimants and one indivisible good, a deadlocked jury, a tied vote where the chair has a casting vote, two people in the water and one lifebuoy. We do have institutionalized tie-breaks for these (lots, coins, seniority, alphabetical order, casting votes), and I can't work out whether those help me, because they show ties get resolved by procedure all the time, or hurt me, because the procedures have to be legitimated in advance, which looks like the reason-demanding I'm trying to deny. Also worth checking whether I've quietly changed the subject: the old question seems to be about whether a will can act without a determining reason, whereas I'm answering what a procedure should do, and if that's a bait-and-switch I'd rather be told. Finally, what would real evidence here even look like — an agent, animal or machine, put in a genuinely symmetric situation that oscillates or freezes instead of resolving? Would watching that happen be decisive for me, or would I just say the symmetry wasn't exact, and if the latter, what's the difference between my view and the metastability story in circuits, where the tie gets broken by noise?

Two more things. One: I want an honest read on whether I've insulated myself. Every action gets explained as a tie-break or a hidden difference, every paralysis gets dismissed as an implementation failure, and I can't see what could ever happen that would make me give the view up. If that's right, is it fatal for a position of this kind, or is it the acceptable sort of unfalsifiable — a modelling commitment rather than an empirical claim? Tell me what my view predicts that the "no real equipoise" and "the will just breaks the tie" rivals don't, and if there's nothing, say so. Two: for what it's worth, what would move me most personally is either a case where adopting the tie-breaker itself can't be done without a prior reason to adopt it, so the regress bites all the way down, or a demonstration that my position and the arbitrary-will position aren't actually distinguishable once you spell them out. If you think one of those is already on the table, lead with it rather than building up to it slowly.
