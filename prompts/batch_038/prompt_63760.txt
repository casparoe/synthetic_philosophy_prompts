I am preparing the official commentary on a colleague's book about Kant's universalizability tests, and she has agreed to take questions from the floor about cases the book never reaches. I want to predict her verdict on one such case, but I care much more about which decision procedure her published verdicts actually commit her to: she has never stated one, and the vocabulary of her stated reasons shifts from case to case.

Her rulings to date, with the reasons she gives for them:

1. Borrowing money by promising repayment you know you cannot make — impermissible. The universalized maxim destroys itself, she says, since a world of such promises is a world in which no one would believe one.

2. Ending one's life out of self-love to escape anticipated misery — impermissible. The feeling that impels us to promote life cannot be willed as a law for destroying it.

3. Preferring comfort to developing a natural talent — impermissible, but she flags it as a different species of failure: not that the maxim could not be conceived as a universal law of nature, but that a rational being could not will it as one, since we necessarily will the means to our own possible ends.

4. Adopting the maxim that everyone may fare as well as they please, and so refusing all aid — impermissible, in that same second way: there will be situations in which you need others' sympathy, so you cannot will a world in which no one extends it.

5. Answering untruthfully when a would-be murderer at your door asks where your friend is hiding — permissible, and she insists this is no softening of the test. The man at the door, on her account, is not a party to any practice the maxim depends on; he has placed himself outside the relations of mutual assurance that give truth-telling its point.

6. A trader who charges inexperienced customers more and describes his maxim as charging what the traffic will bear whenever the customer cannot easily compare prices — impermissible. Pressed on the fact that he could re-describe his maxim narrowly enough that its universalization would be harmless, she replies only that this availability "shows nothing." She never says what pins down the level of description.

7. Breaking a promise whose performance would cost you a year of your life and would confer a trivial benefit on the promisee — permissible. The maxim "keep your promises unless the cost to you is grossly disproportionate to the benefit to the other party," she says, universalizes without difficulty.

The case she has not addressed, which I expect from the floor: a hospice nurse whose patient has asked her to keep his prognosis from his daughter. The daughter is loving, cooperative, and wholly engaged in her father's care — she is no aggressor and has done nothing to forfeit an honest answer. She asks the nurse directly whether her father will die this week. The nurse's maxim is this: when a patient has asked me to withhold the truth and telling it would ruin the time the two of them have left, I will answer in a way that leads the family member to a false belief.

What I want from you:

- Lay out the candidate decision procedures to which these seven verdicts are jointly consistent. At a minimum, consider the two-test reading that separates a maxim that could not be conceived as a universal law from one that could not be willed as such; a reading on which the question is whether the state of affairs in which everyone acts on the maxim would be worse, and if so worse for whom; a reading on which the operative wrong is using someone as a mere means, or bypassing a consent that is in principle available; and any reading whose work is done by constraints on how maxims are to be described. For each, say what supplementary machinery it needs in order to accommodate the entire list, and whether needing that machinery is a cost or a virtue.

- Give each reading's verdict on the hospice case, with its reasoning, and mark where the readings genuinely disagree as opposed to differing only in idiom.

- Then commit to a single best guess at what she will say, and defend the preference — in particular, whether her two permissive verdicts are better explained by a premise about how things would go if the maxim were universal, or by a premise about the standing of the person on the receiving end of it, since the hospice case is constructed so that those two explanations come apart.

- Treat "the test is simply silent here" as one of the available answers: say which reading licenses silence and what she would be surrendering by taking it.

- Finally, describe concretely one further case that would best discriminate between the two readings you judge strongest, and state what each of those readings would say about it.

Two housekeeping notes. I am not asking for a bibliography and could not check one, so do not pad the answer with attributions; I want the structural analysis. And be explicit about how much confidence you are placing in your prediction: tell me what would change your mind — which of her stated reasons, read otherwise, would flip your verdict on the hospice case — and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to break down, including any place where you suspect the seven verdicts cannot all be squared with a single procedure and she will have to revise one of them.
