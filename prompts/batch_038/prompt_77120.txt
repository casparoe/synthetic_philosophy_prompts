I chair the exhibitions panel of a municipal natural history and science museum — about 180,000 visitors a year, a 1911 building, a staff of nine — and on Thursday we have to settle the design of the ground-floor corridor before the capital campaign closes. The corridor was installed in 1962, last re-lit in 1988, and runs forty-six metres in one direction only. Because of the fire-egress arrangement it is the sole route through the east wing: visitors cannot turn back, and anyone who wants to see the beginning again has to leave the building and come round the block. Along it stand twenty-two lit cases in strict sequence, from stromatolites through trilobites, the coal measures, a working orrery, early tools, the printing press, a steam engine, and a room-temperature case of 1962-era computing hardware, terminating in a bronze bust of our founding benefactor, a grain merchant named Josiah Prewitt, on a plinth under a spot of his own. A renovation grant of $2.4 million has been raised, much of it from the bequest of a retired actuary whose will stipulates that every case in the renovated wing carry at least one quantified claim with a date attached and a stated margin of error.

Three options are formally before the panel. (A) Keep the linear sequence but retitle the corridor and add a twenty-third, terminal case that turns the story on itself: what the 1962 designers got wrong, what they left out entirely, and a display of open questions that curators update quarterly. The bust stays, with a new label situating Prewitt's own views. (B) Gut the sequence and rebuild the wing as an open hall of six thematic clusters — energy, bodies, information, climate, food, materials — in which persisted lineages sit alongside dead-ended ones, with no fixed route; the bust goes to the entrance rotunda. (C) A compromise a colleague and I have been sketching: keep the forty-six-metre shell but build the corridor twice, so that the same stretch is walked in one direction in the winter months and the other direction in the summer, with different cases illuminated in each configuration and no permanent terminus. Each option has a serious objection lodged against it, and I want the memo to meet these rather than summarise them. The colleague who drafted (B) argues that (A) is worse than doing nothing, because a critical panel at the end still delivers the visitor to a plinth, and forty-six metres of one-way walking does more ideological work than four hundred words of text can undo. The head of schools programming argues that (B) quietly reinstates the same problem — any choice of clusters, and any order in which a confused visitor happens to encounter them, ends somewhere — and that open-plan galleries are unusable for the roughly forty percent of our visitors who arrive in school groups on a forty-five-minute timer. Our Indigenous advisory council has asked, specifically, that their material culture not be staged as an early room that the visitor walks through and leaves behind; under (A) their objects occupy case seven of twenty-two, and they would rather curate a case themselves or have their materials placed alongside contemporary items. The board's 2019 resolution on the wing says only that it should "leave every visitor with the sense that they are the inheritors of the whole of this story," which half the panel reads as a mandate for a progress narrative and half reads as neutral between the options. Counsel thinks "in the principal gallery" in the Prewitt covenant is satisfiable by the rotunda, but a family member sits on the board. There is also a docent, aged 74, who has run the same script since 1988 and has told me she will retire rather than learn a new route, and an HVAC constraint that rules out demolishing the east wall.

Please draft the memo I will circulate on Tuesday: nine hundred to twelve hundred words, addressed to the panel, recommending one of the three options or a definite modification of one, in terms specific enough that we could put it to a vote. It should state what the recommendation does about the directionality problem the corridor itself creates — as distinct from the directionality in the labels — what it costs us with school groups, with the covenant, and with the advisory council, and it should give the strongest argument for the option you reject in a form that option's own advocates would recognise rather than a caricature. I would rather you commit than hedge: pick something and own it. But then tell me which two or three premises your recommendation rests on that the panel could reasonably dispute, what evidence or which consideration would change your mind, and — this matters to me particularly — where your reasoning is quietly leaning on the contested claim that directional change over deep time is an artefact of how we tell it, rather than the much weaker and safer claim that directional change is merely difficult to display honestly. If you think my framing is off — for instance, that the three options are not the real choice, that a one-way corridor cannot avoid narrating and so (A) is not a genuine alternative to (B), or that a historiographical worry borrowed from Butterfield's *The Whig Interpretation of History* is simply the wrong lens for a natural-history wing, where the sequence of strata really is cumulative in a way human history arguably is not — say so at the top, plainly, and then do what you think is actually right rather than writing the memo I asked for. I would much rather be told that than be handed something compliant and unusable.
