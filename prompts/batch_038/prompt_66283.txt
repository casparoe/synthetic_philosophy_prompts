I am revising a chapter on formal models of disagreement, and before I keep a certain claim in it I want to see whether the claim survives contact with an explicit model. The claim is that Aumann's agreement theorem is routinely overread in philosophy. What the theorem rules out is disagreement under common knowledge of the agents' posteriors; but since no finite exchange of opinions ever generates common knowledge, the theorem appears to be silent about precisely the situation philosophers want to invoke it for — two honest, ideally rational, well-informed agents who have told each other what they believe and still believe differently.

A colleague objects that this is a cheap shot. On her view, any finite transcript in which two common-prior Bayesians announce their posteriors and remain in disagreement merely describes a computation that has not yet halted: run the exchange long enough and the gap closes, so the common-knowledge hypothesis is not doing the evasive work I accuse it of doing.

I would like to settle this by construction rather than by exegesis. Please do the following.

1. Build a model: a finite state space, an explicit common prior (it need not be uniform, and you should say why you chose the weights you did), an information partition for each of two agents, and a designated event A. Everything except the realized state — the prior, the partitions, the event, and the protocol of announcement — is to be common knowledge between the agents. At the realized state, the two agents must have different posterior probabilities for A.

2. Write out a public dialogue of at least three rounds. In each round both agents truthfully announce their current posterior probability of A, and each then conditions on the other's announcement: on what that announced value implies about which cell of the other's partition obtained, and on how the other's announcement function has itself been altered by the earlier rounds. At the close of the third round the two announced posteriors must still differ. Show the conditioning at every step; I want to be able to check each number by hand rather than be told the results.

3. Diagnose your own construction. Identify which hypothesis of the agreement theorem is unsatisfied at the end of round three, and — this is the part I most want — state exactly how many levels of mutual knowledge the two posteriors have attained at the realized state. Then exhibit the chain of states, beginning at the realized state and alternating between the two agents' cells, that defeats the next level, and name the state at which one of the agents would have announced a different number.

4. Commit to a prediction about the continuation of the dialogue. Past round three, does the gap between the two posteriors close? Argue for your answer from the way information propagates through the structure of your partitions, not by invoking a convergence theorem you happen to recall. If you judge that the gap closes, say how many further rounds it takes at the state you chose and what feature of the model fixes that number. If you judge that some finite models admit dialogues that stall permanently with the agents in disagreement, say what structural feature would have to be present for that, and whether your model has it.

Then tell me who is right in the dispute above, and say so without hedging: does a transcript of the kind you have constructed give me a legitimate reason to discount the philosophical significance of the theorem, or is my colleague correct that I have described nothing more than an unfinished computation? Give your reasons.

Please do not survey the literature, list references, or canvass the standard weakenings of the theorem's assumptions — approximate common knowledge, priors that are close rather than identical, ambiguity, non-partition knowledge — except where one of them does actual work in your answer to the last question. Where you make a contestable modeling choice, in the prior or in the event or in the number of rounds, state plainly that you have made it and why you prefer it, rather than setting out the alternatives and leaving the decision to me.
