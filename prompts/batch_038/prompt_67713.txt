I'm helping my aunt sort out her late brother's estate while also sitting in a trusts and estates seminar this semester, and the exact same problem keeps showing up in both places in a way that's driving me crazy. In the estate: the will leaves his Martin guitar to my cousin, but he sold it eight or nine years before he died and the check from that sale is still sitting in the account. The lawyer says the cousin gets nothing, that the gift "adeemed," and then mentions almost in passing that in some states he'd get the proceeds instead. In the seminar, meanwhile, we're reading about cy pres, where a charitable trust whose purpose has become impossible doesn't die at all — the court just redirects the money to whatever purpose comes closest to the original one. The example we did was a trust created before the Civil War to build up anti-slavery sentiment, which after emancipation didn't lapse but got pointed at educating and supporting the newly freed people instead. Then the very next case is the opposite: a Georgia park left in trust by a senator for the exclusive use of white people, where after the courts said it could no longer be run that way, the state courts held the segregation was so bound up with the purpose that nothing else could count as near enough to it, so the whole trust failed and the land reverted to his heirs.

Here's what I can't get straight. Same institution, arguably the same bench, and in one corner a thing can be swapped for something similar and the arrangement survives intact — we still call it the same trust, we still say we're carrying out the dead man's intent — while in the other corner, if the numerically identical item isn't sitting in the estate on the day of death, the gift is simply dead, no matter how much traceable cash is right there and no matter what everyone agrees he would have wanted. My roommate, who's a philosophy major, said there's a standing debate about the difference between something literally persisting through changed circumstances and there merely being a similar thing standing in for it, but neither of us could say which of the two the probate courts think they're doing, and honestly I suspect they've never decided. So: what theory of identity — of what makes a gift, a purpose, or a fund the same thing across time and across situations where it's been altered — do these doctrines actually presuppose? Which rival accounts do they quietly rule out? And is the system committed to both at once, or can one of them be explained some other way that doesn't involve identity at all?

When you answer, please reconstruct the implicit view as carefully as you can and be strict about seperating what the practice really requires from what it merely fits comfortably with. I can already hear someone saying ademption has nothing to do with identity and everything to do with bright lines, evidence, and not making executors litigate a dead person's hypotheticals, and I want to know whether that deflationary story actually works or whether it smuggles an identity view back in the moment it says the proceeds don't count as the guitar. Flag the places where the practice is underdetermined too, where two or three different metaphysics would license exactly the same ruling — the park case looks like the interesting one to me, since "this purpose can't be approximated" might mean the purpose is genuinely gone, or might just be a claim about who gets to decide what counts as close enough, or about whether the wrongness of the original purpose is allowed to infect its substitutes.
