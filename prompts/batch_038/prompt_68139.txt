I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, mostly institutional design, and I've spent the last four months reading nothing but judgment-aggregation papers. My department makes every grad student give a five minute lightning talk at the end-of-year thing, and I want mine to be a piece of satire instead of an argument, because I cannot take the topic seriously for one more week.

Write me a parody of the online help center for a piece of committee-decision software — pick some blandly corporate product name yourself — that sells structured majority voting to courts, tenure committees, hospital ethics boards, and the like. It should read like real SaaS documentation: a short product blurb up top, then eight to ten numbered FAQ entries in that flat, cheerfully evasive support-desk register, one of which is a pasted support ticket from a customer, then a "Known Issues" section and a little pricing-tiers block at the end.

The humor has to come entirely from the software being scrupulously honest about the discursive dilemma and then marketing the honesty. So please get these in: the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures returning opposite verdicts on identical ballots; the support ticket from a court where the majority affirmed every premise yet not a single member voted for the outcome (make the customer paste their three ballots in, with actual yes/no marks, so the arithmetic can be checked); the group ending up asserting things nobody in it beleives and denying things every one of them holds; what a chair should do if they'd like to choose the procedure after seeing the votes, which needs an upsell; why unanimity is the only configuration that never throws a consistency error; and the impossibility result handled exactly the way a vendor handles a bug it has no intention of fixing, with a dictatorship mode sold in the enterprise tier as "guaranteed consistency, every time."

Two accuracy constraints. Don't invent results or stretch the theorems — I want the specialists in the room laughing at how right it is, not at how wrong it is. And somewhere the documentation should be clear-eyed about the difference between this failure and a Condorcet cycle, because they are not the same problem and half my cohort will conflate them.

The target is the genre of committee procedure that announces its verdict as simply what we all think. No real people, no real vendors. Keep it deadpan throughout — no winking, no aside explaining the joke, no line that lets the reader know I know.

Hard cap of 700 words, and no FAQ answer longer than three sentences. Put the single best line of the whole thing in the product blurb, since that's the bit I'll be reading aloud to open.
