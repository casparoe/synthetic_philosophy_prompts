My older brother's home for break (history major) and at dinner him and my AP Euro teacher — who's a family friend, weird I know — got into it about what actually counts as explaining something in history. I mostly just ate and watched. Here's roughly how it went:

Teacher: an essay that just tells you what happened in order hasn't explained anything. Explaining needs generalizations — regularities about how people and states respond to situations — even if you never write them down.

Brother: those generalizations are empty. Try stating one precisely and either its false because of counterexamples, or you hedge it until it says nothing, like "humiliated states sometimes get aggressive." Delete it from the essay and the explanation is exactly as good, so it was never doing the work.

Teacher: it doesn't have to be stated to be doing work, and here's the test — the generalizations are what license your counterfactuals. When you say "if the harvest hadn't failed the revolt wouldn't have happened," you're leaning on something general. Take that away and your narrative is just a chronicle, however detailed.

Brother: but every one of those is ceteris paribus, and in history you can never hold the other things equal, so the generalization can never be shown wrong. Which means it cant be shown right either. Its decoration.

Teacher: then your narrative is unfalsifiable too — anything that happened can be narrated. At least the covering-law version lets me point to a case where the same conditions held and the outcome differed, and say your explanation was wrong.

Brother: no, mine can definitely be wrong — wrong about the facts, wrong about what people knew and wanted. And mine explains why this rather than something else, which is what explanation is for. Two completely different narratives can both be accurate about the same events depending what you're asking, and your laws can't handle that at all.
