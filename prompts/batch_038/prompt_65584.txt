I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the German church tax, and he finally read me Madison's Memorial and Remonstrance Against Religious Assessments (1785), third of the fifteen numbered reasons, as though it settled the matter:

> They saw all the consequences in the principle, and they avoided the consequences by denying the principle. We revere this lesson too much, soon to forget it. Who does not see that the same authority which can establish Christianity, in exclusion of all other Religions, may establish with the same ease any particular sect of Christians, in exclusion of all other Sects? That the same authority which can force a citizen to contribute three pence only of his property for the support of any one establishment, may force him to conform to any other establishment in all cases whatsoever?

His position is that this disposes of any state-collected levy for religious purposes, including the modern German arrangement, where the state collects the tax as a surcharge on income tax only from people registered as members of a qualifying body, and where any member can stop paying by making a civil declaration of exit. I am not persuaded, and I want to understand the argument properly before I commit to a view.

Could you help me with the following, in this order:

1. What is the logical form of the "three pence" sentence? Is the point that the smallness of the sum is irrelevant — that any compelled contribution for religion is a wrong of the same kind as full conformity, differing only in degree — or is it purely about the extent of the authority, so that the objection is to the existence of the power rather than to its exercise or its burden? And what work is the preceding sentence doing: when Madison says the founders "saw all the consequences in the principle," is he refusing to weigh consequences, or smuggling in a prediction about how legislatures behave once a precedent is set?

2. Reason three opens by calling it "proper to take alarm at the first experiment on our liberties," which reads as prudence, while reason one claims religion is entirely outside the cognizance of civil society, which reads as jurisdiction. Are these two different arguments for the same conclusion, and does the second one actually support the first? If reason three is a slippery-slope argument, what would have to be true empirically for it to hold, and how does that square with Madison's insistence that the principle be denied rather than its consequences managed?

3. Does the argument survive the change of institutional form? Madison's target was a general assessment levied on taxpayers irrespective of belief, for teachers of the Christian religion. The German levy is membership-conditional and legally exitable. But the exit is not cost-free in religious terms: the Catholic Church treats the civil declaration as defection and withholds sacraments and Christian burial from those who make it. Is that a fact Madison's framework can absorb — the state has structured the choice as one between money and the sacraments — or does it fall outside his argument, since the cost is attached by the church and not by the legislature?

4. Finally, does the three-pence reasoning prove too much? If the wrong is that the authority able to make me contribute three pence to a church can make me contribute to anything, the same form of argument seems to reach every tax whose proceeds fund what my conscience rejects — war, medical procedures, subsidies to speech I find blasphemous. Is there a principled stopping point available to Madison, perhaps in the unalienability claim of reason one, or is reason three free-standing and therefore overbroad?

Please mark clearly which parts of this are settled among people who work on the document and its argument and which are genuinely contested — in particular, whether reason three is best read as a rights argument or a prudential one, and whether the Memorial's basic premise is that religion lies wholly outside the state's jurisdiction or only that this particular assessment is unjust. I would rather be told that a reading is disputed than have a contested interpretation handed to me as the consensus. I do not need a bibliography or a list of further reading.
