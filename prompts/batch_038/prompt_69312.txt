I am a novelist, somewhere past the midpoint of a book set in a shrinking coastal town in a country whose fertility rate fell below replacement a generation ago and has never recovered; a good deal of the drama now turns on one unexpected pregnancy, and before I commit myself further to that architecture I want to test it against the film that most obviously anticipated it. That film is Alfonso Cuarón's *Children of Men*, adapted with considerable liberties from P. D. James's novel of the same name, and I want the test conducted in the terms of David Benatar's antinatalism rather than in the usual idiom of hope and despair.

For this purpose you are a philosopher who works on population ethics and who also writes film criticism seriously, with condescension in neither direction. Think the problem through carefully before answering. Begin with a short brainstorm of the readings that are genuinely available — at least three distinct ones, among which one is unflattering to the film and one is unflattering to Benatar — and only then commit to a line and argue it.

The apparatus I want applied is the asymmetry as Benatar states it: the presence of pain is bad; the presence of pleasure is good; the absence of pain is good even where there is nobody around to enjoy that good; and the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody for whom its absence is a deprivation. Attend closely to where that fourth quadrant bites, because the film lives in it. Britain in 2027 grieves a future no one will inhabit: the death of the youngest person alive is received as a catastrophe, suicide kits are made available to a populace that has stopped hoping, and then a refugee girl's pregnancy restores meaning to everyone within earshot of it — literally so, in the sequence where the shooting falls silent in the tenement because soldiers hear an infant cry. Benatar's view should imply that the infertility is no harm at all to the children never born, and that whatever is bad in that world is bad for the living. The question I want settled is whether the film knows which of those two things it is dramatizing, or whether it slides freely between them.

To press that point I have a distinction in my notes which I should like you to test rather than adopt. Call a grief *claimant-bearing* when some actual person stands to be deprived by the state of affairs mourned, and *unclaimed* when the object of mourning is merely possible, so that no one is deprived of anything. Does that distinction survive contact with the film, or does the film dissolve it — is Theo's deadened life, Jasper's retreat into cannabis and old cartoons, and the weeping crowd at the death of the youngest human being all one species of grief, or two?

In the reading itself, please address:

1. Two or three specific scenes and choices read through the asymmetry — Kee's decision to trust a stranger, Theo's movement from cynical functionary to something like a father, the ceasefire at the sound of the baby — saying what each shows about whether absent pleasures can be bad for anyone.
2. Where the film resists the antinatalist reading, stated plainly and without rescue: its register is unmistakably pro-natal, the child is figured in messianic terms, and hope is outsourced to a clandestine scientific body in the Azores. Is that a philosophical position the film has argued for, or an emotional default it has inherited?
3. How the film's world fares against Benatar's further empirical claims — that the worst pains are worse than the best pleasures, that we adapt to our circumstances, and that we grade our lives against those worse off rather than against what is possible. Does a civilisation that has lost its future support those claims or undercut them?
4. Where the availability of state-suicide sits against Benatar on suicide, which is not the straightforward corollary of his antinatalism that his critics take it to be.

Finally, I want a definite answer rather than a menu. Tell me whether *Children of Men* vindicates Benatar's asymmetry, refutes it, or is philosophically silent on it while wearing the clothes of the question; take one of those positions and give me your reasons for it. Then close by saying what the film contributes to the debate that argument alone cannot supply — and, since I am writing a novel and not a paper, whether that contribution is one I am entitled to borrow, or one that would leave my own book dishonest. Please give me your reading and your reasoning only: no quoted lines from the film or from Benatar, and no bibliography.
