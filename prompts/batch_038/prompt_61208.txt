For a collected volume on modality in narrative I am writing a chapter on Stave Four of *A Christmas Carol*, and I want to test a reading of my own against the debate between counterpart theory and transworld identity. Dickens sets the issue up explicitly: Scrooge first takes the Phantom to be showing him "shadows of the things that have not happened, but will happen in the time before us," then withdraws that reading and asks whether the visions show what will be or only what may be. And he does not learn whose corpse he has been trailing until the very end, when a name on a churchyard stone supplies the identification.

My thesis is that this withholding is fatal to a Lewisian reading. Counterpart relations are meant to be fixed by comparative similarity, so Scrooge — of all observers — should be able to pick himself out of a near-maximally similar possibility by description alone; that he cannot, and needs a bare name, suggests the novella is working with numerical identity across worlds. But if it is, the standard objection bites: the Phantom becomes an instrument of observation onto a determinate future, which is exactly what the text disclaims when Scrooge concedes that the ends change if the courses are departed from. The Cratchit household mourning Tiny Tim raises the same problem from the other side, since under counterpart semantics their grief is not about the actual boy at all.

Please assess the thesis. Which semantics does the novella's staging actually commit it to, where does my argument overreach, and is there anything in the sequence of visions — the sale of the dead man's belongings, the relieved young debtor and his wife, the request to be shown some tenderness connected with a death — that settles the matter in a way the argumentative literature has not?
