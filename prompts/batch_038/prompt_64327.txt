I'm in a reading group on the philosophy of mathematical practice and this term we're doing a unit on evidence that isn't proof -- how much weight a conjecture carries when all you've got is a heuristic and a pile of numerics. I volunteered to introduce the number theory case, and the main thing I've been working from is a chapter out of some pop-math collection a friend lent me (no idea who wrote it, its a photocopy). Its framing section runs like this:

"Here's the trick. The prime number theorem says the primes near n thin out like 1/log n, so you can think of each integer n as playing a lottery it wins with odds 1/log n. Presume the lotteries are independent -- that's the trick, and that's also the scandal -- and you can start computing: the chance that n and n+2 both win is about 1/(log n)^2, so summing up to X predicts roughly X/(log X)^2 twin prime pairs, and the same move predicts how many primes of the form n^2 + 1 there ought to be, how large the biggest gap below X gets, and a good deal else. And it works. The predictions match the tables to within a few percent, which is about as good as evidence gets for something nobody has proved. Of course the presumption of independance is flatly false: if n is odd then n+2 is odd, and if n is 1 mod 3 then n+2 is divisible by 3 and so can't be prime. The fix is to multiply in a correction factor assembled out of how often the pattern survives reduction mod p -- for twins it comes out at about 1.32 -- but that factor is tuned to the very data it's then used to predict, so it's curve-fitting with a respectable name attached. Worse, the model has been caught out: Maier proved in the eighties that primes in short intervals clump and thin far more violently than independent lotteries ever could, and the Cramér model is now generally regarded as discredited, which is why working number theorists treat this style of argument as suggestive at best and never as evidence for anything. The deeper point is that there's no way to turn the presumption of independence into a theorem, or even into a well-defined hypothesis someone could attack. It's a stance, and the fact that it keeps working is a standing embarrassment."
