I spent thirty-four years as an instrument and controls engineer at a refinery, most of it looking after safety systems — the interlocks and alarms that are supposed to act when the process goes wrong. I retired six years ago and have been reading on my own ever since, badly and haphazardly, mostly about war and about how institutions decide things. I have no academic training in any of it, so I would rather you be blunt about whether what follows is amateurish reasoning than diplomatic about it.

What set me off was an account of the Soviet duty officer who, one September night in 1983, was shown his early-warning system's report of missiles launched from the United States, judged it to be a malfunction, and did not pass it up as an attack. He said afterwards that this had gone against his instructions, and that it helped that his own training had not been purely military. I have spent my working life around operators who have learned to distrust alarms, and I want to know whether that fact underwrites nuclear deterrence or destroys it. Over two winters I have assembled the following argument, and I cannot live with its conclusion.

(1) A nuclear deterrent has one purpose: to prevent a nuclear attack on the state that holds it. Judged as an instrument, it is good to the extent that it lowers the chance of such an attack and bad to the extent that it raises it.

(2) It lowers that chance only if the adversary is convinced the retaliation would actually be carried out. A threat nobody believes threatens nobody.

(3) So the deterrent must contain a retaliation its holder would genuinely carry out if attacked.

(4) Suppose the enemy strikes first and destroys most of the retaliatory force on the ground. Then once the strike has landed, retaliation prevents nothing that has not already happened, and a holder consulting only its own remaining interests would have no reason to carry it out.

(5) Therefore, for the retaliation to be one the holder would genuinely carry out, it has to be carried out while the enemy's missiles are still in the air. The decision must be a decision to launch on warning.

(6) Warning comes from sensors, and sensors have a spurious rate that is not zero; and the decision must be taken in minutes. So a holder that adopts (5) hands the decision to human beings working under time pressure on evidence that is sometimes wrong.

(7) In my trade the reliability of a protective function is never the reliability of its best component; it is the reliability of its weakest link, the human included. Over a long enough stretch, any nonzero annual probability that a spurious indication gets acted upon yields at least one such event with a probability approaching one.

(8) And on the night that event arrives, the officer who acts on the best evidence available to him, under the standing order he has been given and the reasoning his government has adopted, does the rational thing.

(9) So the policy adopted at (1) in order to prevent nuclear war makes a nuclear war begun by mistake close to inevitable across any sufficiently long horizon. It defeats the only purpose it had, and it does so more surely the longer it keeps working.

I cannot accept (9). If I am forced to choose, the steps I would fight hardest to keep are (1) and (3), and the step I trust least is (7), which is arithmetic I did myself at a kitchen table and may simply have got wrong. But I cannot find a single premise I would bet against, and I am nevertheless confident the conclusion is not right.

So: which step should I refuse to sign, and how did it manage to look unobjectionable on the way past? Please take at least two other candidates in turn as well and tell me what I would be paying in each case — in particular, whether refusing any of them commits me to holding that what actually keeps the peace is an officer disobeying an order, which is not a conclusion I would have chosen for myself. And tell me which of two verdicts you are delivering: that (9) is false, or only that I have failed to show it true. Those feel like different answers, and I do not know which one I have earned.
