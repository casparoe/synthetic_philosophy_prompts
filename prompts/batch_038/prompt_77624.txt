I'm building an interpretability framework for large trained models, and I've taken Dennett's apparatus as the scaffolding: the physical, design, and intentional stances, together with the thesis that what there is is what shows up as a real pattern. Before I build anything on top of it, I want to check my reading, because I think I have two knots tangled together.

My working understanding is this. The physical stance is the most fundamental of the three, since it presupposes the least: in principle a complete physical account of a system already contains everything the other two stances yield, and they yield it only less efficiently. It follows, I take it, that a real pattern is something instantiated in the physical facts, which a higher-level description can track well or badly, and that the intentional stance is an expedient for tracking such patterns when the physical account is too expensive to compute. I have also been treating the training objective as the design stance's counterpart for artifacts that were optimized rather than engineered.

On that reading, attributing a belief-like state to a model raises a factual question with a determinate answer: does the model really have the belief, or is it merely treatable as though it did? My question is what would settle that, and what I should count as a real pattern among a model's internal states. Concretely: what evidence distinguishes a genuine belief-like state from an attribution that merely pays off predictively; how would I know when the intentional stance has stopped being licensed and I am simply projecting; and if a mechanistic account of some behaviour succeeds at the circuit level, what does that tell me about whether the intentional-level description of the same behaviour was correct?

Then tell me what does survive: what the real criteria are for warranted intentional attribution, and how to think about the relation between a mechanistic account and an intentional one.
