Retired four years ago after thirty-five in fluid dynamics — mostly turbulence, a fair bit of numerical modelling — and late in life I've fallen into philosophy the way other retired people fall into birdwatching. Our reading group is doing Stoppard's Arcadia and I'm supposed to open the discussion, which is obviously the play for someone with my background: Thomasina getting to the second law by way of jam in rice pudding, Valentine running the grouse numbers through his computer, the whole determinism-but-no-prediction refrain.

What I don't want is a summary of chaos theory, I lived in it. And dont hand me a bibliography, I'm not going to chase anything down and I'd probably just invent the references. No need to quote the play either, I've got the text open on the table in front of me. What I want is criticism.

Here's my grumble, which I'd like you to turn into something I can actually say out loud on Tuesday. The play keeps treating the past as the safe direction. Bernard reconstructs what happened at Sidley Park in 1809 with total confidence, Valentine tries to run the population numbers forward and gets nowhere useful, and that contrast is the engine of the whole thing. But sensitivity to initial conditions is time-symmetric, isn't it — the same exponent runs backwards. So whatever licenses backward knowledge, it can't be the dynamics; it has to be something else, records and redundancy, the past having left its fingerprint in a hundred places at once rather than the trajectory being any stabler in reverse. Read the play against that: what does Stoppard actually think makes the past knowable, does Bernard's confidence have any better warrant than Valentine's shrug, and does the play know it's making an argument about evidence rather than about physics? Bernard ends up wrong about the duel, after all — Stoppard gives him a beautiful method and a false result, and I've never seen anyone say clearly what that pairing is supposed to prove.

Second thing, more technical, forgive me: can an iterated model fitted to a noisy population record establish anything about whether the grouse are chaotic, as opposed to establishing that his model is? Fitted parameters, embedding dimension, noise, nonstationarity. Is Stoppard alive to that gap between model and phenomenon, or is "chaos" just doing thematic work, rhyming with what the historians do upstairs?

Third: does the shadowing business — a noisy computed pseudo-orbit being shadowable by a true one nearby — give Valentine's long-run iteration any support the play never claims for him, or is it a red herring for an ecological time series at his scale?

And the part I actually care about, so please get to it: after all that, does the play say something about determinism that my old department never managed to say in a seminar? If the honest answer is no, tell me no.
