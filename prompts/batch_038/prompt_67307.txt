I am putting together a session for an applied-ethics reading group. The handout the group has already received asserts, as though it were settled, that the easiest way for an individual consumer to reduce the animal suffering she causes through food is to substitute chicken for beef, on the grounds that cattle require far more land and feed per calorie and therefore generate more suffering. I want the group to see that this is not settled and that the substitution may well run the other way.

Your task is to argue for the following thesis: for a welfarist who counts the interests of all sentient animals equally, and who evaluates her own dietary choices by their marginal causal consequences rather than by considerations of complicity, integrity, or expressive value, beef is the more defensible purchase of the two — and the land-use and field-animal considerations usually treated as decisive do not overturn this.

I want a real argument, not a survey of the considerations on both sides, and not a list of caveats that quietly abandons the thesis. To do the work, the piece has to confront at least the following head-on:

1. The yield asymmetry. A single steer produces an enormous quantity of meat relative to a single broiler, so the number of animals brought into existence and killed per kilogram of food differs by something like two orders of magnitude. Explain what follows from this, and under what assumptions about the welfare of a farmed animal's life it fails to follow.

2. The supply-response asymmetry. Cattle take roughly two years to raise and a large share of beef comes from animals whose existence is determined by demand for milk rather than for meat, such as culled dairy cows and surplus dairy calves. Broilers reach slaughter weight in a matter of weeks, and production tracks demand closely. If what matters is what one's purchase causes rather than what it is complicit in, argue what this does to the case for each meat, and say where the reasoning is strongest and where it is merely suggestive.

3. The land-use channel and its metric. Beef requires much more land per calorie, and arable cultivation kills field animals through tillage, harvest, and rodent control. Engage with the question of whether the right way to count this is in deaths averted or in animals never brought into existence, and with the further question of whether contracting cropland is a welfare gain at all if the lives it would have supported were worth living. Note also that the channel looks very different for pasture-raised cattle on land unsuited to crops than for grain-finished cattle.

4. The choice of measure. The thesis can be defended by counting individuals harmed, by counting total suffering, or by counting life-years at risk, and these do not rank the two meats the same way. State which measure the argument relies on and why the welfarist is entitled to it.

Aim for about fifteen hundred words. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; if you want to use rough orders of magnitude, present them explicitly as assumptions you are making rather than as data you are reporting, and indicate how much the argument would weaken if they were off by a factor of three or so.

Close with a short final section that separates what is established from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material. For each of the four points above, say whether your treatment of it reflects something close to consensus, or a live dispute, and in the latter case identify the opposing camps in terms of their positions rather than their names. Where your conclusion depends on a contested premise, say so plainly and describe what evidence or argument would settle it. Do not smooth over a disputed premise by writing as though it were agreed, even where that would make the argument more elegant.

If you conclude that the thesis cannot be sustained exactly as stated, say so, and argue instead for the strongest nearby version that can be — but do not simply reverse and argue for chicken.
