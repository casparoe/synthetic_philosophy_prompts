so I've been asked to hand in a one-page statement of where I've landed for a workshop on social epistemology, and I want a reality check before I do because I suspect I've stitched together things that don't quite go. here's the package:

1. In the Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch setup, the agent who joins a cascade is doing everything right. Herding is Bayes-optimal on her information, so conforming isn't an epistemic failing on her part.

2. What a cascade means is that the public record of actions stops transmitting information — after a couple of moves the herd's behaviour is basically a lock-in of whatever the early signals happened to be, so adding more deciders doesn't make the group better informed and can make it worse. Condorcet just doesn't apply once actions are observable.

3. But anybody who can tell that a cascade is underway should stop treating the crowd as evidence and fall back on their own private signal, since they know the crowd isn't saying anything anymore.

4. In the real setting (and in the Anderson–Holt experiments) people can see the whole history of prior choices. What they can't see is anybody's private signal, or whether the others were reasoning carefully.

5. So ordinary conformity is criticizable after all — its only excusable if you're blind to the fact that you're in a cascade, and we aren't blind to that.

6. Policy upshot I want to defend: make deliberation more transparent. Publish who argued what and why, show the reasoning trails and not just the tallies. The more people can see of each other's moves and grounds, the weaker the bad cascade dynamics get.

7. I also think a cascade's fragility is a feature: one decent piece of public news can shatter a wrong herd, and that's how the system self-corrects.

8. And I'm committed to individual rationality and collective epistemic failure being perfectly compatible — I don't think there's any contradiction in saying every herder is blameless and the herd is dumb.
