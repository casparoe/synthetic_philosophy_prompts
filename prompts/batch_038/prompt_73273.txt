I've been sorting my gut reactions about who should get compensated when things go badly, and I can't get them to line up. The distinction I keep reaching for I made up myself, so don't go looking for it in the literature: odds-setters versus odds-takers. If you had a hand in writing the terms of the risk, the outcome is yours; if you only accepted terms somebody else wrote, it isn't. That's roughly the spirit of the old option luck / brute luck split, but my verdicts don't behave the way that split says they should. Here's what I actually say:

- Del, hit by lightning on the walk to his mailbox, paralyzed from the chest down. Compensated, no argument at all.
- Marisol, takes a rig job for a 40% wage premium, sits through an orientation that spells out the injury rate in numbers, loses her hand in a blowout. Not compensated. She took the deal with her eyes open.
- Marisol again, same injury, except the orientation quoted half the real injury rate. Compensated in full.
- Nate, free soloing a route two grades above anything he's ever sent, falls. Not compensated.
- Tomás, forklift driver, takes the night shift at twice the injury rate because the day shift is full and he can't cover childcare otherwise. Injured. Compensated. I know this looks exactly like Marisol. I still feel the difference and I don't know if that's just sentiment.
- Priya, told plainly that an elective surgery carries a 30% chance of permanent nerve damage, goes ahead, gets the nerve damage. Not compensated, tho I feel queasy about it, because the outcome is exactly as bad as Del's and she's exactly as blameless as Nate isn't.
- Yvonne, quits a good job to care for her mother through four years of dementia, knowing precisely what it will do to her career and her retirement, ends up nearly broke at 68. Compensated.
- Ross, spent a decade cultivating a taste for vintage audio and single-origin coffee, can't stay satisfied on an ordinary income. Not compensated.
- Lena, content enough on ordinary food until a gut condition meant that reaching the same level now costs three times as much. Compensated.
- Going the other direction: I think a guy who wins big on lottery tickets he chose to buy should have some of it clawed back, but someone who gambles on a startup and hits should not. Both are good outcomes of risks they took.

What I want from you:

State the principle, or if you think two rival principles are each defensible, state both. Give each one named clauses, and tie every clause to the case in my list that forces it. I'd rather have one clean principle that tells me to drop a verdict than a five-clause contraption that saves all ten, but show me the contraption too so I can see what it costs.

Then run it on three cases I haven't decided yet: someone who was fully compensated for a brute-luck injury and then lost the entire payment at the track (compensate again?); a company town where the smelter is the only employer and everyone who stays takes the job; and a case you invent yourself that your principle handles worst, or that forces a clause you'd rather not have.

Format, please: prose for the principles first, then two tables, then a JSON object.

Table 1, columns: clause | what it says in one sentence | which of my cases forced it | what it costs you (ad hocery, or something it wrongly rules out).

Table 2, columns: case | my verdict | your principle's verdict | match? (yes / no / yes-but-warped) | note.

JSON object at the end, exactly these fields: verdict_to_drop (the one verdict of mine you think has to go, or "none"), why, replacement_verdict, simpler_principle (the name and one-line statement of the principle you'd pick if forced to choose), Tomás_and_Marisol (which way you resolved that pair), and predicted_verdicts (an array of three objects, one per undecided case above, each with fields case and verdict).
