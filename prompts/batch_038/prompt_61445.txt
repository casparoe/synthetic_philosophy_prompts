Ludwig Wittgenstein died in 1951, before the cognitive science of religion became a research program. Suppose he were alive to read the field in something like its current state: the hyperactive agency-detection account of our readiness to see intentional agents behind ambiguous events; the claim that the concepts which travel best are the minimally counterintuitive ones, ordinary in most respects but violating one or two ontological expectations; the theological-correctness findings, on which believers under time pressure or cognitive load describe a localized, embodied, sequentially attending agent even when their own tradition's official doctrine denies all three properties; the imagistic and doctrinal modes of religiosity, with dysphoric, rarely repeated rites held to produce identity fusion, alongside costly-signalling accounts of painful ritual as credible commitment; and the cultural-evolutionary argument that moralizing high gods facilitated cooperation among strangers, together with its recent embarrassments — the failure of religious-priming effects to replicate, and the chronological dispute over whether such gods precede or follow social complexity.

Reconstruct his response, and do so as argument rather than as pastiche. Specifically:

1. Sort these results into three classes: those he would treat as objections to religious belief, those he would dismiss as addressing causes where the question concerns grammar, and those his own method would positively welcome. Press hardest on the third class. He insists that philosophy must reckon with the natural history of the human animal, and that our concepts rest on shared ways of reacting and behaving that we did not choose and cannot justify from outside. Is there a version of this research that he would regard not as a threat but as his own project continued by other means? Say what such a version would have to look like, and whether the field's actual self-presentation permits it.

2. Test the Frazer comparison to breaking point. His remarks on Frazer object to replacing a perspicuous presentation of a practice with speculative causal and historical hypotheses, and to the developmental idiom that treats ritual as primitive error awaiting correction. Which of these objections transfers to the cognitive science of religion, and which does not? Note that much of this research claims no historical sequence and purports to explain persistence rather than to rank practitioners on a scale of advancement. Does that difference rescue it in his eyes, or is byproduct-talk the same progressivist disparagement wearing laboratory clothes?

3. Locate the field on the scale marked by his attitude to Freud — an explanatory mythology he found gripping while denying that its discoveries were what Freud took them to be. Would he grant this research the empirical standing he arguably withheld from psychoanalysis? If he would, what changes for the believer when the explanation is experimental and statistical rather than clinical and interpretive?

4. Take up the one result that may genuinely bite. If the words of a religious community carry two divergent concepts in the same speakers, and which one governs is settled by cognitive load rather than by training in the practice, what becomes of the claim that meaning is use within a form of life? Is this a fault line his framework had no room for, or exactly the surface-and-depth distinction he would have relished?

5. Address the evidential question directly. He denied that religious belief is the holding of something probable on evidence, describing it instead as something closer to a passionate commitment to a system of reference, so that believer and sceptic are not giving rival answers to a single question. This research claims to show that such commitment is generated by mechanisms indifferent to truth and is revised, if at all, unlike ordinary belief. Is that a confirmation of his description of the phenomenon, or the strongest available evidence against it — showing religious belief to be the quasi-empirical hypothesis he wanted to deny it was? Give your verdict and defend it.

6. Finally, say what he would make of the apologetic and anti-apologetic use of these results, and whether he could hold onto any principled distinction between explaining why a belief arises and showing it false, or whether that distinction is one he would let stand only until it became inconvenient.

Throughout, state which of his actual commitments you are extrapolating from, describing them rather than quoting, and mark clearly where you have gone beyond anything he wrote. His later work's hostility to theorizing may make a tidy systematic answer unavailable in principle; if you think that is so here, say it and explain what remains to be said anyway. Do not attribute quotations to him. If you include a short dictation-style passage, label it unmistakably as your own construction. No bibliography or reading list.
