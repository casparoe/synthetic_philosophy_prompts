I am drafting a position paper for a workshop on religion and public institutions, and before I circulate it I would like a rigorous audit of the package of commitments set out below. I am not asking for background on the arrangements I mention, since I have the facts; nor for a bibliography or reading list. What I want is an assessment of whether these views are jointly tenable, and if not, exactly where the trouble is and what it would cost me to fix it.

Here is the package. I have tried to state each item the way I would actually defend it, rather than as a slogan.

1. The Church of England should be disestablished. That the monarch is its Supreme Governor, that its senior bishops hold seats in the upper house of the legislature, and that its canons take legal effect only through parliamentary approval are all indefensible. My objection is expressive as much as material: an established church announces, officially and continuously, that one creed is the state's own and that everyone else is a tolerated guest.

2. Expressive harms are genuine harms. I do not accept that an arrangement can be vindicated simply by showing that it leaves non-adherents no poorer and closes no door to them. What the state says about who belongs is part of what citizens owe one another.

3. The German church tax is close to my ideal, and I would defend it against American-style separationists. The state collects a surcharge on income tax for recognized religious bodies, retains a collection fee, and remits the balance. Affiliation and formal exit are registered with the civil authorities, and recognized bodies hold the status of corporations under public law, which the Länder confer subject to criteria of permanence and loyalty to the constitutional order. On my view the state is administering, not adjudicating doctrine. The arrangement is voluntary in the only sense that matters, since exit is always legally open and never punished by the state, and it frees the churches from dependence on wealthy donors and on the humiliations of fundraising.

4. The state should never make determinations about which bodies are religions, how orthodox they are, or where one tradition ends and another begins. Classification is itself a theological act, and the state has no competence to perform it. This is a principled commitment for me, not a pragmatic rule of thumb.

5. France's separation of church and state was right in principle, and the exception preserved in Alsace-Moselle should be ended. I regard that exception as more intrusive than English establishment rather than less, since the state there pays the salaries of clergy of the four recognized traditions, participates in appointing their senior ministers, and provides denominational religious instruction in public schools. But the prohibition on conspicuous religious symbols worn by pupils in state schools, and the more recent legislation directed at what the French government calls separatism, overreach badly. The state has no business policing what individual children wear, and the argument that the school must be a neutral space is being used to burden precisely those least able to exit the situation.

6. The American ministerial exception is correct. A religious body must be free to select and dismiss the people who speak and teach for it, including on doctrinal grounds and on grounds of conduct that no secular employer could rely upon, and courts cannot review whether the body was right about its own theology.

7. State funding of faith schools in England should end. Public money should not support schools that give admission priority on religious grounds and teach a confessional syllabus, however good their examination results.

8. The exemption of churches from property taxation is not a subsidy and is unobjectionable. It places them in the same class as hospitals, universities, and charities; it removes a burden rather than conferring a benefit.

9. Religious bodies and individuals should receive accommodations from generally applicable laws where the burden on their exercise is serious and the state's interest can be served by other means.

10. Prison and military chaplaincy should be publicly funded. Where the state has itself removed a person's ability to attend worship, it acquires an obligation to make that worship possible. This is a custodial duty, not sponsorship of religion.

11. Methodologically: since strict neutrality is unattainable, the state should aim at even-handedness among worldviews rather than at a wall of separation.

What I would like from you:

(a) Identify the genuine tensions. I mean by this either pairs and clusters whose usual motivations cannot both be honored, or a concrete verdict in the list that my own stated principles cannot actually deliver. Rank them by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging, and for each say precisely which commitment would have to give and what I would lose by giving it up.

(b) I expect that at least one pairing that looks like a tension is not one, but I do not know which. Tell me which apparent conflicts dissolve on inspection and why. If a dissolution is only local, so that the reasoning used to dissolve it reappears as a problem somewhere else in the list, say so explicitly.

(c) Tell me which single revision to the package would clear the largest number of difficulties at once, and what its cost is.

Please take the items as I have stated them. If you think some commitment is better formulated in a way that avoids the difficulty, say so, but first tell me whether my formulation is workable as it stands. And do not soften the verdict for my benefit: if this is an incoherent package rather than a strained one, that is exactly what I need to hear before I present it.
