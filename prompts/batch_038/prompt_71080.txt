Six years ago I helped found what has since become a small political party in a mid-sized European city. We began as eleven people meeting in the back room of a library to argue about housing policy, and we spent our first two years as something that was barely an organization at all: a self-published journal, no membership list, no platform, no line. People wrote for it or read it or did neither, and nobody's standing depended on which. During that period we got exactly one thing done — a municipal ordinance on eviction notices that we had drafted ourselves — and I mention it because, in the six years since we registered as a party, with four thousand members, a paid staff of six, and one council seat, we have not matched it. I am not the leader. I chair the platform committee, which means I draft the positions the rest of the organization then rallies around.

The reason I am writing is an accumulation of small observations I can no longer dismiss as fatigue. First: twice in the last two years we have abandoned campaigns our own analysis said were winnable in favour of campaigns we knew we would lose, because the losing ones were better for recruitment. Nobody voted for this, and each time the reasoning seemed sound as it was happening. Second: our bylaws now contain a clause — which I helped write — requiring members to support publicly any position the party has adopted until the party itself reopens it, and the party reopens a position only if the executive committee agrees to reopen it. Third: when a member wrote a mildly sceptical column in a friendly local paper about our leader's refusal to endorse a strike fund, she was expelled; the letter said she had placed herself before the organization at the worst possible moment, and most members, including me at the time, thought that was fair. Fourth, and this is the one I cannot talk myself out of: I no longer form opinions before our meetings. I arrive trying to work out where the room will land, and by the time I speak I find that I agree with it. Two years ago I would have called that impossible; I used to be the person who argued against my own side for sport.

Two people I trust have given me incompatible readings, and I cannot decide between them. My closest colleague in the organization, who is considerably more effective than I am, says I am being precious: on his account every organization above a certain size ends up managed by a handful of people whose first concern is the survival and growth of the organization itself, this is close to a law of organizational nature rather than a moral failing, and the only alternative to what we have built is a scattering of sincere people with no capacity to do anything at all. A friend who left three years ago says something much sharper: that what we have become is structurally identical to what we founded the party to oppose, and that the resemblance is not an accident that befell the form but something the form produces reliably, in us as much as in anyone. I keep finding that her account explains things his does not — the ordinance, for instance, and why the campaigns we choose are the ones that recruit — but I cannot tell whether she is identifying a real feature of the world or simply flattering my vanity as someone who would rather be right than effective.

I have narrowed my choices to three: (a) stay and try to change the bylaws, principally by abolishing the public-support clause and by requiring the platform committee to record in writing whenever a decision is being made for recruitment reasons rather than on the merits; (b) leave and try to work as an independent — writing, testifying, doing slow unglamorous policy work with no organizational home; and (c) propose that we dissolve the party entirely and reconstitute as a non-electoral association organized around a journal, with no platform, no membership roll, and no line, which I have almost no hope of carrying but which I would want to have said out loud.

Please answer as a political philosopher who works across the history of political thought and the empirical study of parties and organizations, and think carefully before you begin: I am explicitly not looking for advice about burnout, boundaries, or how to have a difficult conversation with my colleague, and I would rather be told that my framing of the three options is confused than be answered within it. Start by identifying what kind of problem this actually is and what has been said about the form of organization I have built; I suspect there is a substantial tradition of argument holding that my friend who left is right and that the defect lies in the form rather than in the people who fill it, and I want that argument reconstructed as strongly as it can be, together with the best replies from those who think parties are indispensable and who regard the abolitionist impulse as a kind of purism that mistakes the ordinary costs of collective action for a moral catastrophe. Where you refer to particular thinkers or works, reconstruct the argument in your own words rather than quoting it, and flag any attribution you are not fully confident about. Please use exactly these sections, in this order: (1) "What kind of problem this is" — about 150 words, naming the structure of my situation and the debates it belongs to, in ordinary language before any technical vocabulary; (2) "The ordinary-cost view" — about 200 words, the strongest version of my colleague's diagnosis, including what evidence and reasoning support it and how much of what I have described it can absorb; (3) "The defect-in-the-form view" — about 300 words, the strongest version of my departed friend's diagnosis: why she would say the resemblance she describes is systematic rather than accidental, what she makes of the expelled member, of the bylaw clause, and of my changed habits of mind, and why she would predict that the two years of the journal produced the ordinance; (4) "Where the two come apart" — about 200 words, on what would discriminate between them, what a case or a piece of evidence showing one rather than the other would look like, and whether the ordinary-cost view can in principle explain everything on my list or is insulated from it; (5) "The three options" — about 250 words, assessing (a), (b) and (c), including whether (a) can do any work if the defect really is in the form, whether (b) is a real alternative or a self-consoling exit, and whether (c) is a serious proposal or a gesture; and (6) "My recommendation" — about 120 words, telling me plainly which of the three to pursue, what it is likely to cost, and what I should count as evidence within a year or two that the recommendation was wrong.
