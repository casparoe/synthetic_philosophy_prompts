Next week I present in our formal epistemology seminar, where I want to stake out a middle position on indicative conditionals, and my advisor has asked me to circulate a one-page statement of my commitments in advance so that the group attacks the package rather than my exposition. Since the whole session will turn on whether the package is coherent, I would like an audit before it goes out. A classmate who read an early draft offered only that I was trying to have it both ways, which is not actionable advice. My commitments are these:

(1) Adams's thesis in full generality: for every credence function I count as admissible, and every antecedent A with non-zero probability under it, the probability of "if A, C" equals the probability of C given A. I take that proviso seriously and I want the thesis stated once, for all admissible functions, not as a schema with exceptions. (2) Indicative conditionals express propositions: they can be negated, conjoined, disjoined, and embedded as the antecedent or consequent of further conditionals, and my credence in a compound is fixed compositionally by my credences in its parts; conditional sentences therefore denote elements of the same Boolean algebra as ordinary sentences. (3) Learning is always conditionalization: whenever I come to accept a sentence with certainty, my new credence function is my old one conditionalized on the proposition that sentence expresses, and this includes cases where the sentence is itself a conditional. I do not want a special-purpose update rule sitting alongside conditionalization. (4) I know Lewis's triviality results — the 1976 argument and the strengthened version a decade later that assumes less closure — and I think they are valid, but I do not think they touch me, for two reasons. First, the derivations seem to me to depend on reading conditional probability as a ratio of unconditional probabilities; I take conditional probability as primitive in the Popper–Rényi style, so the probability of C given A is not shorthand for a quotient, and the algebraic machinery should not get going. Second, the derivations seem to need the thesis applied to conditionalized credence functions — my function conditionalized on the conditional, and on its negation — that no agent could rationally occupy, and I am willing to restrict the admissible class accordingly. (5) Methodologically, I will not accept a solution that restricts a general thesis by syntactic category, for instance by saying the thesis holds only for unembedded, stand-alone conditionals; that is a restriction on sentences rather than on the phenomenon, and it abandons compositionality without an independent reason. I want these questions settled by asking which principles a compositional semantics actually needs. (6) I endorse the Ramsey test as a rule for accepting conditionals — to evaluate "if A, C" is to add A hypothetically to your stock of information and see whether C follows — and I read it as a rule about acceptance rather than as a claim about what, if anything, conditionals express. (7) Two case verdicts I am confident about. First, a senior colleague I trust without reserve asserts that if a certain manuscript is by Descartes then its watermark is a fleur-de-lis: I say it is rationally required to raise my credence in that conditional to 1, to leave my credence in its being by Descartes exactly where it was, and to let my credence in the watermark claim rise only by what follows from those two together. Second, in the card case — one card drawn from a well-shuffled deck, and the conditional "if the card is red, it is a heart" — I say the right credence in the conditional is one half while the material conditional deserves three quarters, which shows the material-conditional analysis is simply false rather than merely pragmatically misleading in the way Grice described.

Please audit the package rather than lecture me on the literature. I want: (a) the genuine tensions, ordered from most to least severe, with an explanation in each case of exactly which commitments generate the conflict and why it is not merely verbal; (b) the tensions that look real but dissolve on inspection, with the dissolution spelled out — I suspect that some of my unease about the fit between (6) and (1) belongs here, but I cannot tell; (c) an assessment of whether either of my two reasons in (4) actually blocks Lewis's argument, and if not, which assumption of the argument is doing the work I have misattributed to it; (d) whether my first verdict in (7) is deliverable from (1) through (3) and (5) taken jointly, or whether it is a symptom of a conflict you will already have raised rather than an independent one; and (e) the cheapest revision that restores coherence, what it costs me — in particular whether it collides with (5), and if so whether (5) should be reformulated rather than abandoned — together with your judgment about which single change would clean up the most. I am deliberately not telling you which of these I would be most reluctant to give up, and please do not try to infer it from the order or the length of my glosses: I want the ordering driven by the logic of the position, not by my attachments to it.
