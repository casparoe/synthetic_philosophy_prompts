Last night I spent about four hours losing an argument to a friend who does reinforcement learning for a living. The topic was embedded agency. His position — which I will paraphrase rather than quote, since he advanced it in half a dozen different formulations — is that once you grant hidden state, a nonstationary environment, and other agents inside that environment, there is no residual content left for the phrase to carry: the talk of an agent being part of the world it models is either a POMDP with extra steps or a mood. I could not produce a residue. I suspect the residue is real and that my failure was pedagogical rather than conceptual, and I now have an opportunity to test that suspicion.

I run a ninety-minute reading group for machine-learning graduate students: a dozen of them, fluent in optimization and RL notation, no philosophy, and at least two who will arrive inclined to agree with my friend. The session has a single idea, which is the assumption classical Bayesian decision theory smuggles in and which an agent that is part of its own world cannot satisfy — namely, that the true hypothesis about the world sits inside the agent's hypothesis space, and that the boundary between the reasoner and the thing reasoned about is fixed before reasoning begins. I want the plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

Specifically, I need:

1. The dependency order. What must be in place before the next piece is intelligible, and why; in particular, how far I can travel on decision-theoretic assumptions these students already accept before I need anything they would recognize as philosophy.

2. The carrying example. I propose building the entire session around a recommender system whose own recommendations change the users it is trying to predict. I want your honest verdict: does that example bear the weight, or does it collapse straight into the nonstationarity objection my friend already has a rebuttal for? If it backfires, say so and tell me what to use instead. Either way, state what the example must contain and what it must conspicuously lack.

3. A misconception map, with the point in the sequence at which each is headed off rather than merely contradicted. I expect at minimum: that this is just partial observability; that it is Gödelian incompleteness in a lab coat; that a sufficiently large model dissolves it; and that it is the map/territory distinction restated. I believe one of these four is half right in a way worth exploiting and the others are simply wrong, but I would rather you sorted them than take my word for it.

4. A single question I can put to the room at the end whose answers would distinguish students who have the idea from students who can recite it.

5. What I should refuse to explain at this level, and the sentence I should say aloud when I refuse.

I do not want readings, and the room will not have encountered the source material, so the whole thing has to stand on the example alone. Open with the one sentence you would want them to leave holding — the nearest thing this plan has to a bottom line — and keep the remainder under six hundred words.
