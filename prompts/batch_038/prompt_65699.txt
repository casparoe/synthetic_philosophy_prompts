I'm writing a seminar paper for a philosophy of mind course this term on the unity of consciousness, and I need a thought experiment built to order rather than another pass through the familiar demonstrations. Please construct one to the following specifications.

The case concerns a single patient who underwent complete commissurotomy for intractable epilepsy some years earlier, and it should be written in the register of a clinical case report: no implants, no reversible or pharmacological disconnection, no stipulated technology, nothing a neurologist could not in principle have encountered. Partway through the narrative, a dominant-hemisphere stroke destroys the language-capable side, leaving the patient aphasic and hemiplegic on the right, with the left hand and the disconnected right hemisphere intact.

Four constraints on how the case is built.

First, the evidence that the two hemispheres are leading separate mental lives must be non-linguistic. Nothing may turn on the disconnected right hemisphere producing articulate speech — a capacity most such patients lack, and whose presence in the few who appear to have it is itself contested. Pointing, drawing, object retrieval, unilateral learning, conditioned responses, and consistent left-hand behaviour are all fair game.

Second, do not simply re-stage the stock demonstrations: the word flashed to one visual field, the two hands fighting over the same object. I want new pressure on the unity view, not the cases everyone already argues about.

Third, the disunity must be diachronic rather than merely synchronic. In the months before the stroke each hemisphere must have formed and retained its own standing intention about the same course of action — one endorsed in speech and carried in the patient's social life, the other expressed only in what the left hand repeatedly does and undoes — and each must be ignorant of the other's. State explicitly which commissures you take to be sectioned and which spared, and show that what is spared cannot carry the relevant content.

Fourth, close off the hierarchical escape as well: the case must leave no unified set of higher-order endorsements from which both intentions are owned together. If you think this cannot be done without building in the conclusion, say so rather than fudging it.

The purpose, so that we agree on what counts as cheating: my paper argues that the refusal to multiply subjects in these patients is a metaphysical commitment about what constitutes a subject rather than a reading of the psychology, and I want a case that strips the one-subject view of its usual resources — shared information, cross-cueing, common autobiographical memory, a single higher-order will, and the speaking side's testimony that all is one — so that whatever is left has to be named. A case that stipulates its verdicts instead of generating them from the views is no use to me.

The scenario itself should run to no more than two paragraphs. After it, give me the verdict each of three positions delivers on three questions: how many subjects there were before the stroke, whether the survivor is one of them, and whose standing intention now governs the body. The positions are the many-subjects reading of the commissurotomy data; the view that there is one subject whose conscious field is disunified but who is not thereby multiplied; and the reductionist line Parfit takes on fission, on which the counting question is either empty or not what matters. Then tell me which single feature of the case does the most work, and run two removals: what happens if the two intentions are mutually known before the stroke, and what happens if the stroke destroys the silent hemisphere instead. Finally, give the unity theorist's best attack on the case and the most that theorist would have to concede.

Please commit rather than hedge. I want your own verdict on whether the case really delivers two subjects or whether the unity theorist escapes, the point in the argument where you are least confident, and an honest note on where the case stretches the neurology or misdescribes what is known about these patients. And if you think the brief itself is confused — that the third and fourth constraints cannot both be met, or that my characterisation of the three positions is wrong, or that a case of this shape can only be assembled by smuggling in its own conclusion — say so plainly at the outset instead of producing something agreeable.
