ok so our phil-of-mind reading group spent two weeks on Dennett's three stances and the "real patterns" idea — the one where a pattern counts as real if describing the system in terms of it buys you the predictions you want at a better information cost than the bit-by-bit physical description would. One of us turned up with an argument he says shows the criterion is self-defeating, and I've stared at it for three days without finding the exit. Please act as a philosopher of the special sciences who has actually thought hard about this literature, and think carefully rather than fast: before you diagnose, brainstorm two or three candidate break-points, rank them by how much of the argument they'd really sink, and then commit to one.

Here's the argument as he gave it.

(1) On the criterion, a pattern is real iff a description of the system in terms of that pattern is shorter than the bit-map description while preserving the predictions you want.
(2) Any description that keeps fewer details than the bit map is shorter than the bit map, no matter how gerrymanderred the details it keeps.
(3) So consider the property being a doorknob-or-containing-a-number-of-atoms-congruent-to-3-mod-7. A description attributing that property is shorter than the bit map, so by (1) and (2) it picks out a real pattern.
(4) There are indefinitely many hybrid properties like this, and which ones get picked out as patterns depends entirely on what we happen to describe and on our predictive interests — so the criterion makes the inventory of real patterns interest-relative and unbounded.
(5) Beliefs and desires are supposed to be among the patterns the criterion licenses, but it licenses them only by licensing everything in (3) and (4) as well.
(6) The point of the criterion was to give beliefs and desires a real-but-deflationary status: genuinely there, without buying into a privileged ontology or any special mental stuff.
(7) A criterion under which believing that it will rain has no better ontological standing than being a doorknob-or-3-mod-7 doesn't deliver realism, it dissolves it. So the criterion defeats its own purpose.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1) and (6) than (7), but I suspect that's just because I like them and I don't want you to take that as a hint. Three things I want out of you: name the step and explain precisely why it looked innocent when we read it aloud in the room; say what would have to be added to (1) to block the trivialization, and whether that addition smuggles back the naturalness or privileged-ontology commitment that (6) was trying to avoid; and tell me whether your diagnosis changes if the criterion gets run at the design-stance level — centers of gravity, tigers, immune systems — instead of at the intentional level, or whether the same move goes through there unmodified.
