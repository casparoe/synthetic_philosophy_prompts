A friend of mine — a former clinical epidemiologist who now sits on the coverage committee of a statutory health insurer — and I spent most of last night arguing about whether the therapy/enhancement distinction can bear the weight his committee puts on it, and we got nowhere, in part because we kept sliding between two different questions. His committee operates a two-step test. The first step asks whether the claimed intervention addresses a departure from species-typical functioning for a reference class matched on age and sex, which is roughly the baseline Norman Daniels defends in his paper on normal functioning and the treatment-enhancement distinction; the second asks whether correcting that departure improves the claimant's share of the normal range of opportunities. My friend thinks the test is sound in principle and that its failures are failures of application — bad reference classes, drift in what counts as typical, inconsistent treatment of prevention. I think the first step is doing no real work: either normal functioning is a statistical artefact of how the reference class is drawn, in which case it cannot by itself generate an obligation to fund anything, or it is already saturated with the evaluative judgment about which departures are bad for a person, in which case the distinction is a restatement of the conclusion rather than a ground for it. We agreed on one thing, which is that the committee's own reasoning is internally unstable: in some dossiers it measures the claimant against a population norm and in others against her own prior or projected level, and it never says which it is using. I want to see the argument written up properly instead of left as a dinner-table stalemate.

The document I want is the joint statement my friend and I could both put our names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, worked through against the actual dossiers on his committee's queue rather than against abstractions. Those dossiers are: prophylactic mastectomy for a woman carrying a high-penetrance breast-cancer variant with no malignancy and no symptoms; a lipid-lowering therapy for a man whose LDL sits at the population median but whose father and brother both infarcted before fifty; growth hormone for a child in the first percentile for height with no identifiable endocrine pathology; a wakefulness-promoting stimulant for a rotating-shift worker who has no sleep disorder and simply cannot stay safe on nights; preimplantation testing with selection of embryos not carrying a familial variant; cochlear implantation for a prelingually deaf four-year-old whose Deaf parents object that they are being asked to treat a culture as a deficit; and deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant depression in a patient whose family reports that he is also noticeably quicker afterwards. For several of these the committee's two steps come apart from each other and from common sense about what ought to be funded, and I suspect that divergence is where the real content of our disagreement lives.

So draft the statement. I want the claims both camps can sign — about what the coverage decision is, about what the distinction was recruited to do, about which of the dossiers the two steps classify differently and which they classify the same way, about what a health system is entitled to expect from a line it draws; then the residual disagreements, each formulated so that the side holding it would accept the wording, with no strawmanning and no collapsing my objection into his or his into mine; then the cruxes, meaning only those sub-questions whose settlement would actually move one of us, with a note for each of what evidence, case, or argument both of us would accept as bearing on it and what each of us predicts the outcome will be, recorded in advance rather than after the fact. Two cruxes I would insist on seeing addressed are whether the baseline is person-relative or population-relative, and whether that choice flips any verdict on the queue or only changes the reasons offered for the verdicts we already share. And if you conclude that some portion of the dispute is verbal — that one of us is using the distinction as a moral category and the other as an administrative test, or that we mean different things by normal — say which portion, and why the remaining portion is not verbal. Please write it as continuous prose, the way a jointly signed memorandum would read: paragraphs only, no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables, with the structure carried by the sentences themselves. I am not asking for a literature review, a bibliography, or suggestions for further reading; I want the document.
