I lead a team that builds multi-agent systems: orchestrations in which several autonomous agents pursue a common goal, decompose it, negotiate over subtasks, and commit to plans they then have to see through under partial observability and unreliable communication. I am completely fluent in that world — beliefs, desires and intentions as state; commitments as constraints on subsequent deliberation; plans that mesh or fail to mesh; mutual belief established by protocol; preconditions and postconditions; safety and liveness; deadlock and livelock; exception handling and re-planning; and the formal machinery of shared plans and joint intentions my field uses to reason about coordination. I also know the genealogy: the BDI tradition and the shared-plan formalisms in agent AI were built out of Bratman, and they argue with Searle explicitly. So the vocabulary I want you to translate into is not neutral with respect to the philosophy; it is a stripped-down, operationalized descendant of it, and I suspect that is exactly why I keep failing to see what the philosophers are still disagreeing about.

Here is the task. Take the three main accounts of collective intentionality — Bratman's planning account, on which a shared intention is a structure of interlocking, mutually responsive individual planning attitudes whose subplans mesh; Gilbert's plural-subject account, on which the parties form a joint commitment that pools their wills and generates obligations, entitlements, and a standing to rebuke that can be dissolved only by all of them together; and Searle's account, on which the we-mode is a primitive biological capacity that cannot be reduced to I-intentions plus mutual beliefs — and restate each one, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use. I do not want gestures at it. For each account I want the load-bearing claims rendered concretely: what the states and commitments are, what the protocol for entering into them would look like, which pathology the account is designed to rule out (agents merely acting in parallel, one party being duped, a plan adopted without the other's knowledge), and what exactly each account insists cannot be constructed out of simpler parts.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. For each of the three, tell me which claims translate cleanly into something I could implement or prove properties about; which translate only by distortion, and precisely what the distortion costs; and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Be explicit whenever you find yourself rendering a normative item — an obligation incurred by joint commitment, an entitlement to rebuke, the fact that a joint commitment cannot be rescinded unilaterally — as something structural, such as a lock, an invariant, a precondition, or a fault that gets raised. In each such case, say whether you have translated the claim or quietly replaced it with a different claim that happens to have similar behavioural consequences. Keep the translation honest throughout: do not adopt my framework's working assumptions, that everything is a state plus a transition function and that whatever cannot be operationalized is not real, and then report those assumptions back to me as though they were Bratman's or Gilbert's or Searle's.

Finally I want a verdict, and a defended one. Where the residue accumulates, is it a defect of my vocabulary — a genuine gap in expressiveness, something my framework cannot represent because it has no notion of a plural subject and no way to hold normative relations between agents — or is it evidence that the original claims were less clear than they sounded, that pooling wills and an irreducible we-mode are names for a job that never gets specified? Take a side and argue for it rather than splitting the difference. And answer one particular question, because it decides how I think about my own systems: when I treat mutual belief as satisfied once the relevant messages have been exchanged and acknowledged, am I capturing what these philosophers mean by it, or am I committing precisely the error they think the reductive programme commits? For context, my systems currently have no way to represent the situation in which one agent silently abandons a subtask and the others thereby acquire a legitimate complaint; my only available responses are to re-plan or to raise a fault.

Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would in a long email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables.
