Last night my friend Daniel and I finally agreed to write down where an argument we have been having for the better part of two years actually stands, rather than continuing to trade the same three moves at each other over dinner. He trained as a mechanism designer and now works at a competition authority; I am finishing a philosophy dissertation on the structure of individual rights. Our dispute concerns Sen's liberal paradox, and more precisely whether it is a genuine theorem about the incompatibility of independently plausible normative conditions or an artifact of one particular way of representing rights inside a social-evaluation framework. Our reading group has asked us to submit a document for its adversarial-collaboration session in three weeks, and we cannot get past the second paragraph, because each of us keeps rewriting the other's position in vocabulary the other refuses to sign. I would like you to draft that document for us, so that we have something concrete to fight over instead of a blank page.

The two positions, as fairly as I can state them, are these. Position A, which I will call the formalist reading, holds that the paradox is exactly what it appears to be: given unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, and a liberalism condition understood as each person being decisive over at least one pair of social states, the three cannot be jointly satisfied, and the honest responses are to restrict the domain of admissible preference profiles in a way that is principled rather than question-begging, or to qualify Pareto, or to accept the conflict. On this reading, a right that is to figure in a theory of social evaluation at all must be representable as a constraint on the social ordering, and the rival position does not answer the theorem so much as decline to state it. Position B, the jurisdictional reading, which is closer to mine, holds that a right is not a device for securing the satisfaction of the right-holder's preferences but a protected sphere of choice that constrains what others and the collective may do; that rendering liberalism as decisiveness over pairs of social states quietly converts a right into a welfarist claim to have one's ranking registered in the social ordering; and that the paradox therefore reveals a limitation of the modelling choice rather than a limit on liberalism. On this reading the correct fix is to change the object being characterized, not to prune the preference profiles. Please be careful to state each position in wording its own proponent would accept, including the parts of the self-description that sound tendentious to the other side.

I would like the draft to be organised as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: everything both of us could sign, including what the formal result does establish under the stated conditions (I want this stated crisply, because neither of us wants to be accused of denying the mathematics), what Sen's own two-person example generates, and where the two of us agree the tension actually arises. Then a disagreements table with the columns issue, Position A's formulation in A's own words, Position B's formulation in B's own words, whether the difference is substantive or merely verbal, and what would settle it. Then a section on the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one of us, each paired with a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance, before we know how the other will react. Finally, a short closing section listing the claims that would have been contentious between us a few years ago and that we now both accept, since my supervisor is sceptical of this whole genre and thinks such documents are only signable because everything interesting has been drained out of them; I want the draft to let us answer her directly, and to flag any agreed claim that looks like it was made agreeable by being emptied of content.

As raw material for the cruxes, three cases should be worked through explicitly, and I would like each side's verdict on each recorded separately rather than merged. The first is Sen's own example: a single copy of a book that must either be read by one of two people, read by the other, or disposed of unread; the first prefers that the second be made to read it, then that he read it himself, then that it go unread; the second prefers that it go unread, then that he read it himself, then that the first read it; and the planner makes each decisive over the pair of outcomes differing only in whether he himself reads. Both of us accept that this yields a cycle, so the interesting question is what each of us says the cycle shows. The second is a case I made up last month and Daniel has not yet answered: a right-holder who is genuinely indifferent between the two outcomes inside his protected sphere, or who simply has not formed a view, while everyone else holds strong preferences about which of those outcomes obtains. On the decisiveness picture there is no strict preference to transmit, so liberalism delivers nothing and Pareto appears free to decide the matter; on the jurisdictional picture the claim to choose is not forfeited by indifference, and someone must be assigned the decision, which raises the question of what the default is when a protected choice goes unexercised. The third is the case Daniel pressed on me: a right-holder who exercises a protected choice in a way that imposes large diffuse costs on many others. I say this is a dispute about the delineation of the sphere rather than about the logic of rights; he says my reply only shows that scope-determination is doing the real work and cannot be prior to the theory it is supposed to ground. I suspect, though I may be wrong, that a substantial part of our quarrel turns on the fact that we use the word right for two different things and that we may even agree about the outcome in the first case while disagreeing entirely about why it obtains; if so, please say which part of the dispute is verbal and why, and do not let that diagnosis bleed into the parts that are not.

Do not adjudicate between us, do not tell us who has the better of it, and do not write a history of why the debate has been stuck. The deliverable is a constructive text that both of us could put our names to, with the residual disagreement made exact. Where a claim can only be signed by one side with a qualification, show the qualification in the text rather than smoothing it away. Somewhere between fifteen hundred and two thousand words is about right for the reading group's packet, and it should read like something two people wrote together after a long, good-faith argument rather than like two alternating monologues.
