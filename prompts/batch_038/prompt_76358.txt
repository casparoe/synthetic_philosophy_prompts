I've been going back over the internal versus external reasons argument — Williams's line that you have a reason to do something only if you could reach wanting to do it by a route of sound deliberation from what you already care about, against the externalists who say reasons take no notice of your motivatonal set — and what strikes me is how rarely the two ever come apart. In the addiction counselling service where I work we've argued for years about the client who has no path whatsoever from anything he cares about to staying off heroin, and honestly both camps would give the same advice, the same criticism and the same file entry.

Hypothesis I'd rather you tried to break then agreed with: the debate has stalled because the two positions only genuinely conflict over a thin residual class — the agent who would still be unmoved after ideal deliberation — and everything else is common ground neither side notices they're sharing. Or is the real split about what reason-talk is for, explaining action on one side and licensing criticism on the other, with the overlap in cases just a symptom? Or is it that the deliberation idea is being made to do two jobs at once, a counterfactual about motivation in one place and a normative standard in another?

Tell me which diagnosis you'd defend, using what each side actually claims, and then what would count as progress on that diagnosis — a distinction, a case that separates them, a change of question — or why none should be expected. American English please, and exactly five numbered points of a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion.
