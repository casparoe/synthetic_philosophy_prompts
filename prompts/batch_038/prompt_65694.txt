I'm an ancient philosophy person — Aristotle's practical ethics, mostly — and this term I've let myself get talked into a bit of public-facing stuff. A journalist at one of those online culture magazines emailed me after a panel, we spoke for maybe twenty minutes, and she's now sent over her draft, a piece about why nobody ever changes their mind about anything. Most of it is fine and she quotes me accurately on the dialectic material. But there's one paragraph about the analytic debate over disagreement that I suspect she's assembled from podcasts and secondhand summaries, and I have until Friday to send corrections. My problem: I read Elga and Kelly once, fifteen years ago, never really read Christensen at all, and half the department is being renovated so my books and offprints are in boxes somewhere. She's clearly smart and she did do some reading, so it isn't garbage — I just can't tell which bits are fair shorthand and which bits are going to make an epistemologist wince.

Here's the paragraph as it stands in her draft:

"Philosophers have been arguing about this since the mid-2000s, and the debate has settled into two camps. The conciliators, above all Adam Elga and David Christensen, start from an idealized case: you and a colleague have gone over exactly the same evidence, you're equally smart and equally free of bias on the question, and you come out with opposite conclusions. Elga's equal weight view says that at that point you owe your colleague's assessment the same weight as your own, so you split the difference — if you were 80 percent confident, you now drop to 50, and if it's a straight yes-or-no question you simply suspend judgment. Christensen gets to the same place by a different route: the disagreement itself is higher-order evidence, evidence that you may have botched your reasoning somewhere, and evidence of that kind trumps whatever first-order reasons you thought you had. On either version the lesson generalizes, which is why the conciliators are happy to say that in contested domains — politics, religion, philosophy itself — hardly anyone is entitled to hold on to a belief once a well-informed opponent denies it. That's where the familiar complaint comes from, that the view is spineless, and it's worse than a complaint: it's self-refuting, since plenty of equally sharp philosophers reject conciliationism, so by its own rule the conciliator has to give up conciliationism. The steadfast camp, Thomas Kelly most prominently, answers that all that ever matters is the total evidence, so if you really have assessed that evidence better than your opponent did, the bare fact of their disagreement adds nothing you didn't already have. Because both sides accept the same setup and only disagree about what follows from it, the argument has been deadlocked for close to twenty years."

So — can you go through this sentence by sentence and tell me what holds up, what's a defensible simplification, and what's just wrong? The bits I'm least sure about are these, and they're hunches rather than knowledge: (1) whether "split the difference, 80 becomes 50" is really what the equal weight view licenses, or whether that's a cruder rule than anything Elga holds, and what the view actually tells you to do with the reasoning that got you to the 80 in the first place; (2) whether she's collapsed Christensen's higher-order-evidence story into Elga's, and whether "trumps" is too strong for what a conciliationist thinks higher-order evidence does; (3) the self-refutation line, which smells to me like a standard objection being dressed up as a knockout — I'd like to know what a careful conciliationist actually says back to it, and whether there's more than one distinct objection being mashed together in that sentence; and (4) the claim that both camps "accept the same setup," because I'd have thought a lot of the fight was precisely about the setup — whether there are any real epistemic peers outside arithmetic, whether these idealized cases are even coherently describable, whether steadfastness is one view or a family. Also tell me if the "deadlocked for twenty years" framing is fair or whether the debate has actually moved somewhere. Then, practically: rewrite the paragraph for her in something like her voice, keeping the punch but not getting anything wrong. Don't bother with citations or a reading list — I won't have time to chase anything down, I just want to know what to tell her before Friday.
