I teach political philosophy, and I have been asked to advise the clinical ethics committee of a mental health trust that is reviewing its advance-choice scheme. Before I put anything in writing to them, I want a rigorous reconstruction of what the scheme presupposes about freedom. My suspicion is that the document is philosophically committed in ways its authors have never noticed, and that some of its clauses are committed in mutually incompatible ways. I want that suspicion either substantiated clause by clause or refuted.

Here is the scheme, stated as the trust states it. Please take these features as given and run the analysis on the policy as I have described it, not on any real jurisdiction's statute book or case law.

(a) Patients who are currently well may record in writing what should happen to them if they relapse — including an instruction that they be admitted to hospital and treated even if, at the time, they object and resist.

(b) The document has force only if countersigned at the time of writing by the clinician responsible for that patient's care, who certifies that the patient had capacity then and was not acting under pressure from family or ward staff.

(c) At the moment of crisis the document does not bind. The duty clinician must decide whether acting on it serves the patient's best interests and whether it reflects what the patient would have wanted; a decision not to follow it need only be recorded in the notes, and requires nobody else's authorisation.

(d) A patient may use the document to authorise in advance things the trust might otherwise have to impose. They may not use it to require the trust to provide anything it does not already provide — no particular medication, no particular ward, no particular therapy.

(e) The document expires after two years unless renewed, and renewal requires a fresh capacity certification.

(f) The trust's own audit reports that patients holding such documents are compulsorily admitted rather more often than clinically comparable patients who hold none.

I would like the following, in this order.

First, reconstruct the conception of freedom the scheme as a whole presupposes. I am aware of the three standard candidates — freedom as non-interference, freedom as self-mastery or the rule of one's own better self, and freedom as non-domination — and I do not need them defined or attributed; reciting that triad earns nothing here. What I need is the argument for which of them the scheme cannot do without, and which it merely happens to be compatible with. Some clauses will be underdetermined, licensable by two or even three of the theories; say so explicitly rather than forcing a single verdict.

Second, identify the clause that does the most philosophical work — the one that, if deleted, would leave the scheme intelligible under a conception of freedom it is not currently intelligible under. Explain what turns on it.

Third, take clause (d) separately. The asymmetry it creates, between pre-authorising interference and pre-requiring provision, seems to me to settle a substantive question about whether freedom is a matter of the source of constraint or of the range of options one has. Say what it settles, and say which conceptions that forecloses.

Fourth, take clause (f) separately. Does a scheme that increases the incidence of interference while making each instance self-authorised leave its holders freer or less free than they were? The three conceptions should not give the same answer here; if they do, tell me why my expectation is wrong. Tell me also whether any of them can make sense of the trust's apparent complacency about the audit result, or whether the trust is simply not asking a question any of them would recognise.

Fifth, clause (c) looks to me like the scheme's bad faith: the document is solicited, certified and renewed on the strength of an idea about self-government that clause (c) then declines to honour. Assess that charge. If it holds, say what would have to change to make the scheme coherent — and say what new difficulty a genuinely binding document would create, since I expect the committee to reach for bindingness as the obvious repair.

Finally, tell me which conception of freedom, if the trust adopted it, would force the largest redesign, and sketch that redesign in three or four replacement clauses. Flag anywhere you think I have mis-stated the stakes.

Please write in British English, and keep it to the analysis; no reading lists and no bibliography.
