You are to work as a senior epistemologist whose research covers the logic of knowledge, evidential defeat, and contextualism, and who has taught the dogmatism paradox to graduate seminars for many years. Think carefully, and proceed through the stages below in order; do not skip ahead to the finished cases.

Background, stated in my own words so that we are reasoning from the same argument. The dogmatism paradox runs roughly as follows. Suppose S knows that p, where p is an ordinary empirical proposition. If S knows that p, then any future evidence against p must be misleading evidence, since evidence against a truth is misleading by definition. That conditional looks knowable a priori: it is a piece of reasoning, not an empirical discovery. So S can know in advance that whatever turns up against p will be misleading — and hence, the paradox concludes, S is licensed in advance to discount it.

Purpose of the exercise. I am assembling a take-home design assignment in which students test diagnoses of the paradox against cases rather than against intuitions about the argument form. So I need a minimal pair: Case A, in which S stands fast on p and discounts the incoming evidence, and this is the intuitively correct, even admirable, response; and Case B, in which S does exactly the same thing and is the paradigm of the epistemic vice the paradox seems to license. The verdicts must be ones a careful reader would reach independently on reading the cases; a pair that smuggles its target verdicts into the description of the situations fails outright.

Stage 1 — brainstorm before drafting. List five or six candidate features that might generate the contrast between A and B, and for each say in one or two sentences whether it is usable given the constraints below or whether using it would count as cheating, and why. Only then proceed.

Stage 2 — the constraints on the pair.

1. In both cases p is the same general sort of proposition: contingent, empirical, not a necessary truth, and not a proposition about S's own current mental states.
2. In both cases S knows p before the counter-evidence arrives, and the kind and strength of S's original warrant are matched as closely as the design allows.
3. In both cases S is in a position to run the paradoxical inference, so that the a priori premise is satisfied in both, not just one.
4. In both cases S subsequently receives evidence E that genuinely points against p. p is in fact true in both cases, so E is misleading in both. E must be matched in kind, apparent source-type, and force across the two cases.
5. The variable that drives the contrast must be a feature of S's epistemic position at the time E arrives. It may not be a feature of E's content, of the reliability of E's source, of the truth of p, or of the strength of S's original warrant.
6. S may not be handed an explicit defeater for this particular E in either case. No announcements that the lab has been falsifying results, that the informant has a grudge, that the instrument is miscalibrated, and so on. That is the cheapest way to build the contrast and it is disallowed.
7. The settings must be realistic: a workplace, a clinic, a field survey, a household, a school. No demons, no simulations, no predictive devices, no science fiction. The stakes and the practical consequences of believing p are to be matched across the cases, so that no pragmatic asymmetry is doing the work.
8. Each case is a single paragraph of at most about 150 words.

Stage 3 — the verification. Take three families of diagnosis: (i) Kripke's own, which turns on the relation between the a priori warrant for the conditional and the empirical warrant for p, and on what S is still in a position to know once E arrives; (ii) a contextualist diagnosis in the style of Lewis's Rule of Attention, on which making the possibility of misleading evidence salient changes what may properly be ignored, and hence what may properly be said to be known; (iii) a diagnosis that locates the failure in the step from knowledge to rational confidence, holding that receiving E changes what S's total evidence supports even though p is true and even though S in some sense still knows it. State each diagnosis in your own terms, flagging plainly anywhere you take my gloss above to be inaccurate or too coarse. Then run all three through both cases, saying for each whether it predicts the verdict the case is built to have, and which of them — if any — accommodates both halves of the contrast. Present this as a short table plus commentary. If none of the three gets both, say so rather than massaging the cases.

Stage 4 — post-mortem. Identify the single feature of the pair that is doing the work. Then remove it or reverse it and report what happens to the contrast, so that I can see the design is load-bearing in exactly one place. Then give the best attack available to a defender of whichever diagnosis comes out worst: denying knowledge in Case B, relocating the contexts, contesting the normative intuition in Case A, or arguing that the two cases are not in fact matched in the respect constraint 2 demands. Say which of these attacks you judge most damaging and why.

Format: use the stage headings; write in prose with the one table where requested. Do not quote from any text, do not give a bibliography or a reading list, and do not invent publication details. If you cannot satisfy a constraint, flag it explicitly and explain the trade-off rather than relaxing it silently.
