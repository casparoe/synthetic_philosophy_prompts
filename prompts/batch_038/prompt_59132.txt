I'm in the last stretch of an independent reading project on Bell's theorem, and the group I meet with has asked each of us to come next week not with a summary of the result but with an explicit criterion of our own for what counts as nonlocal — something we'd be willing to defend clause by clause. Trying to write mine down, I've discovered that my usage is a grab bag of reactions to particular set-ups. So I want to lay out the verdicts I actually have and see whether a principle can be extracted from them, or whether some of them have to go.

The set-up is the familiar one: a source emits pairs to two distant stations, each of which picks one of two measurements and records a ±1 outcome, and the resulting correlations exceed what any account can deliver in which each outcome is fixed by that station's own setting together with information available in the pair's shared past — a bound of 2 on the usual combination of four correlations, where quantum mechanics reaches 2√2. Versions closing both the locality and the detection loopholes have been run. My verdicts are these. (1) That experiment, as actually performed, is nonlocal. (2) Nothing in my verdict turns on distance: if the stations are three centimetres apart, or if both settings are dialled in before the particles leave the source, I say the same thing — still nonlocal, even though there's no longer any room for anything to travel between them. (3) A model in which each particle carries a complete instruction card and each station's outcome is a function of its own setting and its own card alone is local, even when the agreement between stations is perfectly rigid and looks uncanny. (4) Now suppose someone constructs a model that reproduces the quantum statistics exactly, including the 2√2, while preserving no-signalling and keeping the observable predictions Lorentz-covariant, and in which each station's outcome is still a function of its own setting and a shared variable — the catch being that the distribution over that variable shifts with the distant station's setting, and the construction only works because the shift takes exactly the values needed. I call this nonlocal, or at any rate not a genuine local account. It's the verdict I'm least sure of, since structurally it looks like (3). (5) Superdeterminism, where the early state of the universe fixes both settings and outcomes and screens the correlation off by that common past, I would not count as local — though I can't exactly call it nonlocal either; what I want to say is that it isn't an explanation at all. (6) Retrocausal models, where each station's choice acts backwards inside its own light cone onto the emission event so that the shared variable is a product of both settings, I call local. That feels right and I can't properly justify it, since (5) and (6) give up the same independence assumption and differ mainly in the direction of an arrow. (7) Bohmian mechanics with a preferred foliation is straightforwardly nonlocal, and I'm at peace with that. (8) Everett: the wavefunction evolves by a local equation, nothing done at one station changes the reduced state at the other, and yet the branch on which the two observers later compare notes is always the one where the statistics match. I call that nonlocal, because something has to line up, but I suspect I'm being unfair to it.

There is also an objection I keep getting and think is mistaken, and I'd like it confirmed or corrected: people say (1) cannot be squared with the no-signalling theorem, on the grounds that a genuinely nonlocal arrangement should let one station modulate the other's local statistics. I think the two sit together fine — but I notice that if I'm right, my notion of nonlocality is one that leaves no observable trace, which is an odd thing to be insisting on, and I'd like to know whether that worry dissolves or bites.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precise enough to be applied mechanically rather than by taste: Bell-style conditions on probability distributions, a criterion for which variables are permitted to screen off which correlations, a constraint on how much fine-tuning a model may contain before it stops counting as an explanation, or an explicit split between a theory's being nonlocal and an observed phenomenon's being locally inexplicable. For each candidate, tell me which of the eight verdicts it delivers, which clause is doing the work in each delivery, and what that clause costs. Then say what the leading candidate makes of three cases I have no view on yet: a Bell test whose settings are chosen from light arriving from very distant stars or quasars, so that any common cause of the settings and of the pair would have to sit extremely far back in the past; delayed-choice entanglement swapping, where a choice made after two particles have been irreversibly absorbed appears to settle whether they were ever entangled; and a three-party arrangement with perfect deterministic correlations, where the contradiction arrives without anyone having to accumulate statistics. Finally, be blunt about which of my verdicts a decent principle forces me to abandon. My hunch is that (4) and (6) cannot both survive; I'd rather be shown the mechanism than have the hunch confirmed.
