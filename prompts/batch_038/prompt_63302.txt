I'm putting together a seminar session on relational accounts of personhood and I need a case that runs against the grain of how these debates almost always go. Normally the ubuntu-style view comes out looking more generous than the individualist one — it counts the ancestors, the not-yet-born, people with advanced dementia, and the capacity theorist gets cornered. I want a fair case going the other way.

Build me a thought experiment in which a strong relational/communitarian account of personhood — the Menkiti-ish line on which personhood is something you achieve and can lapse out of, not something you have in virtue of your inner makeup — has to rank an actual human being as less of a person than a standard capacity account does. Same individual, full marks from the capacity theorist, short of personhood on the relational view.

The constraints are the point, so please respect them:

- Present-day and realistic. No feral children, no isolation tanks, no coma patients, no sci-fi. The person has to be ordinary, competent, and visibly functioning.
- They must be blameless. This can't be a case where they wronged their people and got cast out for it, and it can't be a case where they're simply antisocial or refuse to relate. If anyone is at fault in the story it's the community, or nobody.
- The relational shortfall has to be generated by the theorys own machinery — by what the view says personhood consists in — not by me stipulating that the person is lonely, disliked, or unrecognised. The verdict has to fall out, not be built in.
- Two paragraphs for the scenario itself, no more.

Then do the checking work:

1. Walk through what each account says about this person and exactly why, in the theory's own terms — including what the relational view says about how much moral consideration the person is still owed. I suspect it wants to seperate personhood-as-status from how-they-should-be-treated, and I want to know whether it actually can here.
2. Name the one feature of the case that's doing the work.
3. Take that feature away and tell me what happens to both verdicts. In particular: if the community later does take this person in, does their personhood snap back into place, and if so what does that imply about whether the relational theorist is tracking a real property of the person or just reporting a social fact about other people?
4. Give me the capacity theorist's best attack — I'm expecting something like the charge that this makes personhood hostage to other people's good behaviour, offered as a reductio rather than a cost — and then the relational theorist's best defence. One defence I want you to take up explicitly is the retreat to a modal version of the view, where status rests on the capacity to be part of communal relationships rather than on actually being in them. Does that move save the case, or does it concede the individualist's point and leave my scenario with no verdict difference at all?

Finally, flag anywhere you had to bend one of my constraints to get the thing to work. And if you think the specification is straight-up unsatisfiable — that a blameless, fully functioning person can't come out short on a relational view without cheating — say so and show me why, rather than handing me a fudged scenario.
