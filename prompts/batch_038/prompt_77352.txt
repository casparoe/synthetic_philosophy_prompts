I'm drafting a paper for a workshop in social epistemology and would like substantive comments on the middle section, where the argument actually moves. The paper's overall question is whether Kierkegaard's analysis of the press and "the public" can be carried over to algorithmic publicity, or whether the carry-over silently breaks. Below is roughly 800 words of draft prose. I have paraphrased rather than quoted almost everywhere, so please treat my attributions as claims to be checked, not as settled readings.

**Working title: Phantoms and Profiles: Kierkegaard on the Press and the Algorithmic Public**

**§1 Kierkegaard's public.** *Two Ages* (1846) is a review of a novel by Thomasine Gyllembourg, and it contrasts an age of revolution, in which passion forces decisions, with a present age that is reflective, passionless, and given to leveling. Kierkegaard writes that "the trend today is in the direction of mathematical equality, so that in all classes about so and so many uniformly make one individual." The public, on his account, is the distinctive category of this present age: it is not a people, not a generation, not a community, and not any determinate set of actual persons. It comes into being together with the press, because the press makes it possible for people who will never meet and who bear no responsibility to one another to take part, en masse and anonymously, in a discussion. Nothing is asked of them and nothing is at risk for them. What they constitute is therefore not an aggregate of individuals but an abstraction. I want to call that abstraction a phantom and to make the word precise: something that is nowhere present, yet that functions as the audience to which everything is addressed. Leveling is the process by which the concrete particularity of persons is dissolved into it; and, importantly for my argument, leveling is nobody's deed. It is not the work of a class or a faction but of reflection itself. As I read him, this is not simply to be deplored: leveling is the negative through which a single individual, taken up religiously, can be brought to stand on his or her own.

**§2 Phantom and profile.** The obvious move is to say that the recommendation feed is the press with better instrumentation, and that Kierkegaard anticipated it. I want to resist that. His public is a phantom: a false representation of something that could have been real. It purports to be an audience; it is produced by address; and it fails, because nobody who is addressed is present. The algorithmic public is a different kind of object. It is produced not by address but by measurement: a statistical aggregate constructed out of recorded behaviour that was never addressed to anyone and never intended as speech. It does not purport to be an audience at all. A profile, unlike a phantom, is not a misrepresentation; it is a representation of nothing, because there is no represented subject standing behind it. The consequence is that Kierkegaard's moral psychology loses its purchase. Leveling, for him, runs on envy, resentment, and the comfortable pleasure of judging without being judged. An optimizer has no envy. Whatever the feed does to us, it does not do it out of the motives his diagnosis assumes, so that diagnosis has to be rewritten rather than applied.

**§3 Pre-emptive leveling.** Kierkegaard's single individual is forged by decision: by taking a position in one's own name at some cost, with the possibility of being wrong and of being answerable for it. I want to claim his account has a structural precondition he never states. An environment must retain a certain amount of friction — cost to expression, delay before consensus forms, exposure of the speaker to consequences — for there to be anything at stake in endorsing a view. Remove the friction and the individual is not leveled; the individual is pre-empted, dissolved before it ever took shape. This is why I think the contemporary problem is not that people are being shouted down, or that too many voices are audible, but that endorsement has become free. Free endorsement cannot be owned, and what cannot be owned cannot be answerable.

**§4 The design paradox.** That claim generates a difficulty I do not yet know how to handle. If the individual is pre-empted by an environment with insufficient friction, then the remedy looks like a design task: reintroduce cost, delay, and attribution into the machinery of publicity. But Kierkegaard's own remedy is religious, and its point is that the single individual cannot be produced by any worldly arrangement — not by institutions, not by education, not by press reform. A platform that succeeded in manufacturing individuals would be producing, by engineering, precisely what he insists can only arise from a person's own relation to the absolute. So either his remedy is right and my diagnosis is merely aesthetic, or my diagnosis is right and his remedy is theologically overwrought. The paper currently asserts the second horn, and I suspect that is too quick.
