I'm a research engineer on a small team building an agent whose policy-selection core is a proof search over a verified kernel for first-order arithmetic, with a hard cap on prover time per decision — call it a fixed, standard bound *n*, known at compile time rather than discovered at run time. The agent is expected to run for about five years and is permitted to rewrite that core a small number of times, four or five at most, each rewrite of which we need to be able to audit afterwards. The part of the design that keeps coming apart is the Löbian obstacle to self-trust: by Löb's theorem, if a theory containing enough arithmetic proves that the provability of a claim implies the claim, then it proves the claim outright, so no such theory can license a general principle of trusting whatever its own proofs establish, and it certainly cannot certify a successor that reasons in a stronger or differently structured system. My complaint about the literature is that essentially every proposed fix seems to have been written with an idealized, unbounded reasoner in mind, and I'm not convinced any of them survive contact with a compile-time proof budget and a finite, tiny number of self-modifications.

Here is my working hypothesis, and I'd like you to say frankly if it is wrong. Once the search is capped at a fixed standard bound, the base theory ought to be able to establish the soundness of everything the capped search can actually produce, because the relevant existence statements are bounded and hence decidable rather than self-referential. If that is right, the Löbian structure drops out for our purposes, and what remains is an ordinary verification problem — trusting the kernel, and trusting the bound — plus a residual difficulty about unbounded iterated rewrites that we simply will not perform. If so, the ranking below should be driven by auditability and by whether an approach degrades gracefully when it cannot prove anything, rather than by whether it dissolves the self-reference at all.

Please evaluate the following eight candidate responses on two separate scales, each 0–10. Scale A is practical value for the deployment I described: bounded kernel, five-year horizon, at most a handful of rewrites, auditability required, and the failure mode we fear most is not "the agent proves something false" but "the agent freezes because it cannot prove anything." Scale B is how well the approach addresses the obstacle for the idealized unbounded reasoner it was designed for. Give a one- or two-sentence justification for each score. The candidates are: (1) fully certified rewriting in the Gödel-machine style, where no self-modification executes unless the machine proves in its own system that the new policy dominates the old; (2) escalating the base theory, moving to a system containing reflection principles for the weaker one and iterating them, so that trust in one's own proofs lives in the extended system rather than the base one; (3) the robust-reasoning or parametric bounded Löbness strategy, requiring proofs that hold across a family of assumptions about the agent's own future behaviour, indexed by a resource parameter, instead of a proof about one specific successor; (4) graded trust, replacing proof-based trust with a calibrated probability assignment over mathematical claims along the lines of logical induction or reflective oracles, so that successors are trusted to a degree rather than absolutely; (5) limited-resource reasoners, making the bound on inference effort an explicit part of the agent's self-model so that self-trust becomes a claim about what it can feasibly derive rather than what is derivable in principle; (6) asymmetric or Vingtian reflection, giving up on certifying oneself and designing each generation so that it need only certify the next one under a monotone improvement criterion, accepting convergence in the limit rather than at every step; (7) amortized certification, which is my own proposal and may be a bad one — prove nothing about the successor's general behaviour, verify the candidate rewrite against a fixed precomputed suite of decision problems by brute-force comparison, and require the successor to defer to the incumbent policy on anything outside the suite; and (8) no self-modification at all, freezing the policy and permitting updates only through an external, human-audited redeployment.

After the scores, please answer four follow-ups directly. Which candidate moves most between Scale A and Scale B, and what explains the move? Which are effectively disqualified by a fixed proof budget, as opposed to merely weakened by it? Does my hypothesis in the second paragraph survive your scrutiny, and if it fails, at exactly which step? And in one sentence, what does the obstacle actually reduce to once the reasoner is bounded? I'm not asking for references, a reading list, or attributions — I know roughly where these ideas come from — just your assessment of them.
