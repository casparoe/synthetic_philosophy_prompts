so i've been meaning to open a long post about when two descriptions turn out to be one description wearing different clothes — permutation invariance, gauge redundancy, the whole thing — and instead of the usual throat-clearing i want to open it with a poem about Max Black's two spheres. the universe with nothing in it but two exactly similar iron spheres, same diameter, same temperature, same everything, and the claim that you still have to admit there are two of them and not one. i've had this arguement with like four different people over the years and the poem is doing the arguing for me.

here's the form i want, and the form *is* the argument so please don't soften it: twelve lines, six couplets. in the first five couplets the two lines have to be word-for-word identical — same words, same order, same punctuation, nothing changed at all. a couplet whose lines are exact twins is doing precisely what the spheres are doing: the only thing keeping them two is where they sit, and where a line sits isn't a property of the line.

each of those repeated couplets should be a real attempt to tell the spheres apart that fails. so handle the relational escape route explicitly — anything true of one is true of the other, mirror-image descriptions included, because there's no landmark, no edge, no light source and nobody around with a left and a right to break the tie. and i'd like one of them to be about the temptation to smuggle an asymmetry in: a scratch, a speck of dust, a visitor who wanders up. the poem should notice that doing that isn't answering the question, it's just building a different universe. by couplet five the repetition should be starting to feel like a threat, like the two of them are sliding into one and the poem is losing its grip on there being two things at all.

then the sixth couplet breaks the rule. the two lines are identical except for exactly one word. that word is the only asymmetry in the whole poem and it has to be a word that carries no information whatsoever — a name, a bare "this", something of that kind, not an adjective or a number. no gloss on it, no explaining it, no "and the one on the left is called X". the reader should hit the last line and realize that keeping two spheres two cost a word that means nothing at all, and that whether you're willing to pay that is the entire dispute.

no jargon anywhere in the poem: don't use Leibniz, don't use Black, don't say indiscernible or haecceity or name the principle, and don't drag my physics analogy in either — that's my job in the prose. plain, present tense, a bit cold, no exclamation points, no title.

after the poem, two sentences max on which word you swapped in and why that one rather than a real descriptor. if six couplets turns out to be genuinely unwriteable, four plus the break is fine, but the rule stays: every line has an exact twin except the last two.
