Reading group on biological function and I've talked myself in to an argument that proves way more than I want it to. I can't find the bad step. Here's the chain:

(1) Any naturalistic account of function has to explain malfunction — how a thing can have a function it isn't performing. An account that can't say what's wrong with a failing heart isn't an account of function.

(2) On a causal-role account, the function of a part is what it actually contributes to some capacity of the containing system, and which capacity we care about is fixed by our explanatory interests.

(3) If (2) is right, a part only "malfunctions" relative to a contribution we've already chosen to single out, and since any part plays indefinitely many causal roles in indefinitely many containing systems, there's no principled way to privilege one. So malfunction collapses into "isn't doing the thing I decided to focus on."

(4) So causal-role functions can't ground real malfunction. Fine, go etiological: a trait's function is the effect its ancestors were selected for having.

(5) But then a trait's function is fixed entirely by its history, and stays fixed no matter what the trait is doing now or what it's worth now.

(6) Case. A lab lineage we've been propogating for maybe 400 generations. Gene g's product was originally selected for breaking down sugar A. A came out of the medium centuries of bacterial generations ago; g is retained but under no detectable selection either way, basically drifting along. The enzyme is still made, still folds, still binds. By (4)+(5) its function is still to metabolize A, and in this medium it never does. So the lineage is permanently malfunctioning — while being perfectly fit and with nothing whatever going wrong inside it.

(7) Now spike A back into the medium for a single transfer. The enzyme does exactly what it was selected to do. It is functioning properly, in a body where doing so is worth nothing.

(8) So the etiological account gives malfunction with nothing wrong, and proper function with nothing going right. Malfunction has come apart from any normative fact, in exactly the way (3) said the causal-role account's does.

(9) Whichever account you take, "function" is just doing double duty for "does this" and "did that," and the normativity that everybody says functional talk carries is something we project onto it.

(10) Therefore there's no naturalistically respectable notion of biological function; function talk in biology is at best convenient shorthand for causal-role description plus history.

I refuse (10). I want to be able to say the heart's function is to pump blood and that this one is failing at it, full stop, without a disclaimer about my explanatory interests. Something has to give somewhere between (1) and (8) and I can't see where. My hunch is (5) is doing something sneaky, but if (5) is false I don't know what makes an etiological account etiological — and honestly (1) looks load-bearing too now that I stare at it, since I'm not sure Cummins ever signed up for the requirement I'm imposing on him.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? I want the cost priced for each of the camps — the causal-role people, the selected-effect people (Wright, Millikan, Neander, that wing), and whatever the organizational/self-maintenance view has to offer here — plus the option I haven't considered, which I guess is denying (1) and saying malfunction was never the test. If someone's escape route depends on a move that only works for the (6) case and not for real ones like vestigial organs or newly recruited genes, say so. And please mark clearly what's actually settled among people who work on this versus what's still live — I'd rather know that "function pluralism dissolves it" is a contested position than have it handed to me as the consensus.
