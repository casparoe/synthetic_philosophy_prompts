ok so i just got in from a four hour argument with my friend about takeoff speeds and my head is still going and I want to get the shape of it down as a poem before it evaporates — but I want the form to *carry* the argument instead of just describing it, because everything I've tried so far just turns into two people making their cases in verse.

here's the conceit. five stanzas, and each one is exactly half as long as the one before: 16 lines, 8, 4, 2, 1. 31 lines total, not one more. Each stanza is one doubling, and the length of the stanza is the time that passes before the next one — so the poem is literally a series where the intervals keep halving, and the whole thing fits in a finite page.

the argument it's staging: her side is that the world has drag in it, that making the model smarter doesn't make the fab or the power line or the wet lab or the regulatory clock any faster, that an experiment takes as long as it takes, so a doubling of capability buys you strictly less than a doubling of research speed and the curve bends over instead of blowing up. my side is that the halving is exactly the thing that compounds, and that once the gap between doublings is measured in days the slow parts of the world never get a turn, they're just still waiting around when it's already over.

structure it like this. give stanza one the room to be leisurely: an ordinary tuesday, scenery, hedges, "on the other hand," her case made generously and at full length, no strawman — she should sound like she's winning. then let the compression do the damage. by the four-line stanza there aren't enough words left to qualify anything. by the two-line stanza the poem physically can't hold both positions at once, and that's the real point, the running out of slack *is* the argument, not something the argument says about. run the lines into each other with enjambment early on, then cut the enjambment away as it goes so the last two stanzas land like doors closing.

and the single final line is the crux. i want it genuinely ambiguous between "the last step of a series that got here smoothly" and "the thing that arrives with no warning," because that's exactly where we got stuck and kept talking past each other. make that last line hers. one completely ordinary word, the kind of thing you say about dinner. the poem should end with the explosion not happening — or at least not being announced.

also: don't write the limit. the series converges one line-length past the end of the poem, and everything after that is still in there unwritten, infinitely many more doublings packed into less time than the last stanza took up. the poem stops one step short and never mentions it. if it works, the reader should feel the page running out rather than be told about it.

no chrome, no sci-fi vocabulary, no singularity, no rhyme scheme turning it into a jingle. free verse, plain diction, the kind of language two people actually use at a kitchen table at 1am. and don't let me win it — i honestly dont know that i did.
