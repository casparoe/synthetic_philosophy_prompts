I am a senior at a public high school, and for my school's capstone seminar I am writing an extended research paper on Henry Sidgwick's work as the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, which he helped found in 1882. My paper is not about Sidgwick's moral philosophy but about his method in these investigations: the sequence of judgments he actually reached on particular cases. I have assembled that sequence from secondary histories and from summaries of the Society's Proceedings, but I have hit a case that Sidgwick never ruled on, and I want help reasoning about what he would have said. One note before I begin: please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations. My school's database access is limited, and a list of titles I cannot obtain would not help me. I want the reasoning, and I would rather you work from the material I supply than reach for sources you cannot check either.

Here is the track record I have compiled. Where I am relying on a summary rather than the original report, I have said so.

1. The slate-writing medium Henry Slade, London, 1876, several years before the Society existed. Sidgwick and the physicist William Barrett attended sittings. What Sidgwick recorded, in a letter of the period, is essentially a complaint about the conditions: Slade wrote on a closed double slate and on a slate Sidgwick was partly holding, but he never kept the slate continuously in view after Sidgwick had inspected it. Sidgwick's verdict was trickery. Other respectable observers, including a German professor of physics and astronomy and a well-known naturalist, came away convinced the phenomena were genuine. Sidgwick treated the disagreement as being about whether the conditions excluded ordinary explanation, not about what anyone saw.

2. The Theosophical phenomena of the mid-1880s. Sidgwick accepted the Society investigator's finding of deliberate fraud and allowed it to be published, notwithstanding protests from the accused movement's supporters.

3. The demonstration arranged in the mid-1880s in which a young amateur, performing under an assumed name, reproduced the slate-writing effects of a famous professional medium exactly, and then showed the sitters how he had done it. Several sitters had signed a joint statement that the effects could not have been produced by trickery, and a number of them resisted withdrawing that statement even after the method was demonstrated in front of them. Sidgwick's verdict, so far as I can tell, was that the episode settled nothing about spirits and a great deal about witnesses: multiple careful, educated observers in séance conditions could not detect ordinary sleight-of-hand, and their signed testimony should therefore be given close to no weight.

4. The Boston trance medium Leonora Piper, whose English visit began in 1889 and whose case the Society pursued for years. She produced no physical phenomena, only purported communications from the dead through trance speech and writing. Sidgwick did not call her a fraud. He also refused to grant survival. The explanation he preferred was that she was acquiring information from the minds of living people, including the sitters and those connected to them. He was willing to leave that hypothesis unexplained in turn; it was enough for him that it did not require the dead to act.

5. The Society's mass statistical survey of hallucinations, reported in 1894 with Sidgwick chairing the committee. The survey counted how often a vivid apparition of a person coincided with that person's actual death, and found the coincidence far more frequent than chance would predict. Sidgwick accepted the statistical result. He nonetheless drew no conclusion about the cause and did not treat the excess as evidence that the dead appear to the living. This is, I think, the single most important item in my list: it is the one case where he conceded a robust, quantified anomaly and still declined the inference his colleagues wanted.

6. The Italian physical medium Eusapia Palladino, at a series of Cambridge sittings in 1895. Sidgwick agreed with the finding that she had freed a hand or a foot and produced the effects himself. But he did not treat detected fraud as disposing of the whole performance, the way the Society's American investigator did; he asked what remained once the identified tricks were subtracted, and judged the residue too thin, too entangled with the trickery, and too dependent on conditions nobody could reproduce, to be accepted. Two senior colleagues disagreed with him openly and publicly. I may be slightly off on a detail here, since I am working from a secondary account, but the shape of his response is what matters for my purposes.

7. The laboratory thought-transference experiments of the same decades, in which a sender and a receiver were placed in separate rooms and the target was chosen by a third party. Here Sidgwick was prepared to treat something like direct thought-transference as at least a live possibility, and I read him as more sympathetic to these experiments than to anything done in a séance room.

The case I cannot resolve is the one that began after Sidgwick's death in 1900 and occupied the Society for the next two decades: the so-called cross-correspondences. Briefly, and again from secondary accounts: a number of automatists in England, Italy, India, and America, working independently, produced handwritten scripts in semi-trance and attributed them to three deceased founders of the Society. Taken one at a time, the scripts are nearly worthless as evidence. An individual script offers a Greek word, a scrap of verse, a small drawing, a date, or an obscure classical allusion. The force of the case is supposed to emerge only by comparison: a fragment written by one automatist is completed, answered, or explained by a fragment written days or weeks later by a different automatist with no access to the first script, sometimes on another continent, and the interlocking fragments build up into sustained themes drawn from classical literature. Crucially, the scripts repeatedly announce this method themselves. They claim the communications have been deliberately split, on the grounds that any single item could be explained away as information picked up from a living mind or as the automatist's own buried reading, whereas an intricate design distributed across many minds, many places, and many months could not be so explained. Some of the automatists were unpaid, respectable, and unknown to one another; one was a classical scholar married to a Cambridge classicist, while others had little or no classical education, which is where the argument is meant to bite. There are no physical phenomena whatever: nothing moves, nothing is touched, no room is darkened, and there is no opportunity for sleight-of-hand, so the entire case rests on documents written and dated at the time and compared afterward. The standard objection is that the scripts number in the thousands, that the automatists' unconscious minds had absorbed a good deal of classical literature from their own education, from the sitters, and from one another, and that the interlocking pattern was imposed after the fact by scholars who went looking for it and who were themselves devoted to the deceased men purportedly sending the messages.

What I would like from you:

First, state the rules that could generate the whole of the track record above. I want at least three, formulated precisely enough that I could check each item in the list against each rule, and I want you to say explicitly how each rule handles the two hardest items, the statistical survey, where Sidgwick conceded an anomaly and refused an inference, and the trance medium, where he conceded the person's honesty and still refused survival. If a rule cannot handle those two, it is not one of his rules.

Second, say which of those rules come apart on the cross-correspondences. I suspect that they do, because this case seems to sit in a category Sidgwick never faced: not a séance and therefore not touched by his contempt for séance testimony; not a repeatable experiment with a third party choosing targets; but a large, contemporaneously documented body of records from unpaid people, structured precisely so as to block the living-telepathy escape he used on the trance medium. For each rule you identified, state what it predicts he would say, and whether that prediction is fraud, self-deception, genuine anomaly with the wrong explanation attached, or genuine anomaly with survival established.

Third, give me your best single guess at his verdict, with the reasons you prefer your favored rule over the alternatives. I want you to say how much weight his own reported hope that survival might turn out to be true should carry in that prediction, and whether it pushes him toward accepting a case like this or toward being harder on it than his colleagues were.

Fourth, and this is the part I most need: describe the modification of the cross-correspondence case that would flip his verdict under the rule you favor. For example, would it matter if the allusions had been recorded and sealed by a neutral party before publication rather than compared afterward by sympathizers; if the automatists had been chosen for having no classical education; if the fragments had been solicited on demand rather than arriving unsolicited; or if the whole procedure could be repeated on request? Tell me which of these changes would move him, which would not, and what that difference reveals about the rule he was actually running.
