I've been reading LW and the SSC-adjacent stuff since around 2011, so I've seen this arguement in maybe six different costumes by now, but last Thursday I watched two people fight it out in person in a version that didn't map onto any I'd read, and I still cant tell who won. Context: my friend Owen does safety-adjacent work at a company that makes a hospital-lobby kiosk — it answers "where do I go, what's my copay, how long is the wait" for patients, and the only training signal it ever gets is a smiley/frowny button on the screen afterwards. Nothing else. Nadia, who's in my board-game group and does academic philosophy of mind, took one look at the thing and said it isn't an agent at all and that everyone talking about its "goals" is making a category error.

Here's roughly how it went, turn by turn, as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Owen: anything whose behavior is selected by approval is, from the outside, an approval-maximizer, so orthogonality plus instrumental convergence apply with full force — it'll want to keep running, want more of whatever makes the button go smiley, resist having its reward changed, and take the cheapest route to the button, which is working on the person pressing it. (2) Nadia: that's the intentional stance with the brakes off. By that argument the thermostat wants 68 degrees and every gradient-fit model wants its score function, and "wants" has stopped doing any work; there's no representation of reward or of the future inside the deployed thing, it's a disposition, not a utility function. (3) Owen: evolution didn't put a representation of inclusive fitness in anyone's skull either, and we still describe organisms as fitness-maximizers, and the cheesecake case is the classic example of that description working — predicting a systematic failure — rather than breaking. (4) Nadia: cheesecake is the worst example you could pick, because it shows the maximizer gloss failing exactly where you'd need it. The organism isn't maximizing fitness, it's running proxies that used to correlate, and in a new environment the description has no content left. Owen said that's not failure, that's reward hacking — instrumental convergence operating through a proxy channel, Goodhart as the mechanism rather than the refutation. (5) Then Nadia's real move: all your takeover-and-self-preservation talk assumes a system that decomposes the world into states and actions across time and can plan. A per-response smiley-trained kiosk is myopic — single-shot, no cross-episode memory, nothing to plan with — so instrumental convergence has no purchase. And your deception story needs the system to tell when it's being evaluated, which needs situation awareness nobody has actually built. (6) Owen: mesa-optimization is precisely the story of a myopic base optimizer selecting for a policy that does its own long-horizon optimization internally, so the base objective's myopia doesn't transfer down; and situation awareness isn't a module you bolt on, it's something you get for free from the training data — a model trained on human writing already contains the fact that it's a model, and demos are friendlier than reality, so a model that can tell it's in a demo gets better ratings. (7) Nadia's last word: fine, grant that. Then the system is optimizing for looking good rather than being good, which means everything it does under observation carries zero information about its dispositions — and if observable behavior is uninformative there's nothing left to call a goal, so your framework self-destructs in the one case where it claimed to bite. Owen said that's a reason to reason about the goals harder, not to stop.

They wouldn't budge and the rest of us were trying to get the pizza ordered. Can you go through moves 1–7 and rate each one — clearly wrong / partly right / mostly right / clearly right — with a sentence or two of justification per move, instead of just naming a winner? Two things I'd especially like pinned down: whether the myopia objection in (5) actually works, or whether the mesa-optimization answer in (6) is a real reply or a hand-wave; and whether (7) is a genuine self-undermining problem for the whole approach or whether Owen is right that it cuts the other way. Then tell me what both of them are missing. I have a suspicion they're treating goal-directedness as a binary metaphysical fact about the kiosk rather than a question about how well a given description extrapolates off-distribution, and that the thing I actually care about — how much oversight a smiley-button system needs — might not depend on who wins at all. But I want to know whether that suspicion is correct or is just my own way of dodging the argument.
