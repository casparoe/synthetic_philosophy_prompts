Our NHS foundation trust is redrafting its clinical governance framework, and two of the proposed changes seem to me to turn directly on questions that the philosophical debate about moral luck addresses.

First, the trust currently grades the seriousness of a clinical incident partly by reference to the harm the patient actually suffered, so that two clinicians who make an identical deviation from protocol may face quite different sanctions depending on whether the error happened to be intercepted in time. The draft replaces this with what it calls a harm-blind matrix: grading depends only on the nature of the deviation and on how foreseeable its consequences were, with the realised patient outcome excluded altogether.

Second, the trust's appraisal scheme weights two traits it labels diligence under load and composure, which in practice separates staff who appear temperamentally unflappable from those who do not; separately, the draft justifies retaining the anonymous near-miss reporting line on the grounds that a reporter should only ever be assessed on what lay within their control.

Please describe the most important implications of moral luck — and in particular of the distinction between resultant luck and constitutive luck — for these two proposals. In doing so, consider whether a harm-blind grading matrix genuinely escapes the difficulty it is meant to resolve or merely relocates it elsewhere in the framework, and whether the trust's appeal to control in defending its reporting line sits comfortably with the way the same document evaluates temperament. Organise your answer around the implications you judge most consequential rather than around a survey of the debate, and write throughout in British English.
