I am in the second year of a doctorate in environmental philosophy. My chapter concerns the moral standing of insects, and most of my reading this term has been in agronomy rather than ethics. Two features of pest-control practice have come to look to me like philosophical documents, and I should like help reconstructing what they presuppose. I am not after a survey of who has said what — no reading list, please — but the implicit theory.

The first is the action threshold of integrated pest management. The decision to treat is reached by comparing the value of the crop that will be lost at a given pest density against the cost of controlling it, and treatment is applied only where the density is expected to pass the point at which damage outweighs expense. On that ledger the insects killed by the treatment do not appear at all: they are neither a cost nor a loss, and the only harm registered is harm to yield.

The second is resistance management. Where crops have been engineered to express an insecticidal protein, growers are required as a condition of planting to set aside a proportion of their land to a non-engineered variety, so that a reservoir of susceptible insects survives and mates with any resistant survivors from the treated fields. The policy assumes that resistance alleles begin rare and largely recessive, and that a maintained refuge keeps them rare. Label and extension literature speaks of resistance as a natural and expected development in insect populations, of susceptibility as something to be conserved, and warns that once resistance is established it is very hard to reverse; a grower who sprays indiscriminately is criticised for imposing costs on neighbours and on later growers.

We therefore require farmers to preserve the pest, at their own expense, in order to keep our capacity to kill it working. What does that practice presuppose? In particular:

1. Is the refuge ledger the same theory of value as the threshold ledger, or a rival one? Does treating susceptibility as a resource to be husbanded concede that the deaths of insects carry a weight the threshold denies, or is the refuge wholly instrumental — valuable only because the capacity to kill is valuable to us?
2. What does it presuppose about the unit that counts: the individual, the population, the gene, the generation? And does its intergenerational logic resemble that of climate ethics, where we conserve for descendants who have interests, or is it structurally different because the beneficiary of this conservation has none?
3. Susceptibility is handled as a common-pool resource that the individual grower over-exploits. What account of fairness between contemporaries, and between generations, does that require? Can a strict instrumentalist supply it, or must the practice borrow something like a duty to preserve options?
4. Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and mark the places where it is underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same rules.
5. If insects were recognised as welfare patients and their deaths entered the ledger, would the refuge requirement become more urgent, or would the framework cease to make sense?
