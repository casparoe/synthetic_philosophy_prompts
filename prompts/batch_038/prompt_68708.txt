I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of economics, and this term we have spent three weeks on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem in the version where individual preferences and the social preference are all defined over a common set of lotteries on a common consequence space, all satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and the social preference satisfies Pareto indifference with respect to the individuals — so that social utility is a positively weighted sum of individual utilities, with equal weights if an anonymity or indifference condition is added. Nobody disputes the proof. What the class cannot settle is what the theorem establishes, and the argument goes in circles because students are rejecting different things and cannot tell which disagreement is which. I would like a decision tree that sorts a reader from "I accept the theorem" to a named position about its philosophical import.

Please build it to the following specification.

1. Begin with the space rather than with the names: list the questions that generate the tree and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the actual positions within it. No node may contain the name of a position or of a person, and every node must be phrased as a question a reader could answer from the statement of the theorem plus their own intuitions, without knowing where the answer leads.

2. The first two or three splits must not be about which axiom to drop. They must be about the status of the conclusion: whether the quantity the theorem represents is a moral good, an aggregate of individual well-being, a mere representation of a social preference ordering that carries no moral weight of itself, or something else again. Only after the tree has separated readers on that axis should it branch into premise rejection.

3. One node must turn on invariance: whether each individual utility function in the representation may be rescaled by its own positive affine transformation, and if so what survives of the weights and of the sum. This is the node at which the reader who says the theorem is empty and the reader who says it settles utilitarianism part company, and I want the tree to make the parting explicit.

4. One node must turn on separability across persons, and it must keep distinct two moves that my students constantly conflate: giving up the sure-thing principle for the social ordering in order to make room for a concern with the distribution of well-being, and giving up anonymity in order to make room for unequal weights. A further node should separate evaluating lotteries before the uncertainty resolves from evaluating states of affairs after it resolves, where those two can come apart.

5. One node must ask whether the veil of ignorance is doing independent argumentative work or merely encoding the weights that were going to be assumed anyway.

6. Aim for no more than eight nodes and between ten and fourteen leaves. Each leaf should carry the name of the position that occupies it, or the label unoccupied where no one does, together with one sentence on what it costs to stand there — the premise surrendered or the consequence that has to be swallowed. At minimum the leaves should accommodate: the reading on which the theorem is a utilitarian theorem; the deflationary reading on which it is only a representation result about social preference; the reading on which it delivers weighted welfarism and leaves the weights to a separate argument; a prioritarian position reached by abandoning separability; a position that rejects Pareto indifference itself; a position that denies that the satisfaction of preferences is what is good for people; and the Rawlsian response to the veil-of-ignorance move.

7. Flag the pairs of leaves your tree cannot actually separate rather than papering over them — in particular, any two leaves that generate exactly the same social orderings over lotteries and differ only in how those orderings are described. For each such pair, say what additional question would be needed to pull them apart.

8. For every unoccupied cell, diagnose it: is the combination incoherent, is it coherent but unattractive, or has it simply not been explored?

9. Close by placing four things my students actually said. For each, say at which leaf the speaker belongs, or say that the tree cannot accommodate the remark and explain what is confused about it.

   - "The theorem proves that what one ought to do is maximize the sum of individual utilities, so anyone who denies that is denying a theorem of decision theory."
   - "Because each person's utility function can be multiplied by any positive number and shifted by any constant, the weighted sum is not invariant under those rescalings, and so the theorem says nothing whatever."
   - "I accept every premise and I am a prioritarian. Since my view is not among the theorem's conclusions, the theorem must be wrong."
   - "The theorem shows that behind the veil of ignorance rational self-interest just is utilitarianism, which is why the equality of the weights is not a moral assumption but a piece of decision theory."

Present the tree as an indented outline with the branch labels visible, keep the whole thing to roughly the length of a two-page handout, and do not include a reference list or bibliography — names of positions and of views are welcome, citations are not.
