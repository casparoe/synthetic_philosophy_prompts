Acting as someone who works at the intersection of the philosophy of biology and the economic theory of signaling, please help me unpick a slogan. I sit on the selection committee for a competitive research fellowship, and our application is deliberately onerous: a thirty-page research proposal plus a week of unpaid assessment tasks. When I questioned this at last week's meeting, a colleague waved the objection away with the handicap principle — "a costly signal is an honest one; if it were cheap, everyone would fake it."

Take that sentence apart. Lay out, as a menu rather than a single gloss, the distinct precise claims it could be making, from the near-definitional to the substantive, and for each say whether it is true, false, or true only under conditions you specify: what counts as a cost and on whom it must fall, whether cost is being offered as necessary or as sufficient for reliability, whether the honesty at issue is a property of every token signal or only of the equilibrium average, and how signals kept honest by expense differ from those kept honest by physical constraint or by the receiver's retaliation. Brainstorm the candidate readings before writing them up, and do not collapse ones that are doing different work.

Then tell me which of those readings, if any, actually supports my colleague's use of the slogan to defend our application process, and what would have to be true of applicants and of the tasks themselves for that support to hold. I am not asking for a policy recommendation — I want the disambiguation and your verdicts.

Finally, flag where your own analysis is most likely to fail: which reading you may have mis-stated or wrongly classed, and what consideration would move you to a different verdict about whether the slogan does any work for his argument at all.
