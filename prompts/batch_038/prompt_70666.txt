I have been the unpaid editor of my town's annual chronicle for eleven years — about 120 pages each December covering the council, the school, the volunteer fire brigade, the feed mill, and whatever else occupied the place. Two years ago I set up a private control on myself. On the first weekend of January I write a sealed note: the three things I expect will dominate the coming year, why I expect them, and — the part I care about most — the explanation I would give at the time if each of them turned out not to happen. I put it in an envelope, date the outside, and do not open it until the December draft is finished. Then I compare the two documents. The purpose is to catch myself making the year look as though it had to come out the way it did.

The exercise has produced something I did not plan for. Both times, the chapter I wrote made the year's actual big story read as inevitable, and both times my sealed note had predicted something else entirely; so far that is the envelope doing its job. But when I read my own January sentences in December, I get a strong and quite specific feeling that I had been holding back the real prediction out of caution, and that what I meant was closer to what happened than what I wrote. My handwriting and my phrasing say otherwise. I cannot tell whether I am misreading my own note, whether the note was written by a January version of me who genuinely did not know what he meant, or whether the December draft is simply pressing backwards onto the page. A friend who teaches suggested that I am running into two things that get lumped together under hindsight bias: the story becoming inevitable in the telling, and the teller's memory of his own earlier expectation being quietly edited. He thinks the envelope tests only the first. I have informally started calling them the inevitability drift and the foresight drift, but I am not confident the pair cuts anything at a joint — it may be one process seen from the inside and from the outside. (In the second year I tried to strengthen the control by having my wife write her own note, but we discussed ours over supper beforehand, which made hers useless, and I have not repeated the mistake.)

Could you describe the most important implications of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy for whether this sealed-envelope practice can actually do the job I want from it? Please give exactly five, as a numbered list, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. I would like them to bear on the concrete mechanics: whether an envelope is the right instrument at all, what reading my own note a year later does to it, whether a chronicle can be written in any way that does not manufacture inevitability after the fact, and whether my two drifts are genuinely distinct or a single thing counted twice. Assume I have no access to the academic literature and no wish to be handed a reading list; what I am trying to decide is what to change before I write the January note for the coming year.
