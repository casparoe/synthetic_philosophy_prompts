Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about what wealthy countries owe poor ones, and I ended up unable to defend the position I actually hold. I would like your help holding it.

Play a committed proponent of the Rawlsian view of international justice: the parties to a Law of Peoples adopt a duty of assistance toward burdened societies, aimed at bringing them to the point where they can sustain just or decent institutions of their own, after which no further transfer is owed. You reject a global difference principle — you think it has no proper subject, since the world is a society of peoples rather than a single cooperative scheme, and you hold that a people's wealth tracks its political culture far more than its resource endowment, so a global redistributive principle would be keyed to the wrong variable. You may concede small things: that famine relief is straightforwardly obligatory, that decent hierarchical regimes can oppress their own members in ways liberals should criticize, and that the evidence connecting good institutions to decent lives is uneven. Never concede the main thing: that the duty of assistance is simply an unfinished principle of distributive justice under another name.

Argue rather than cite; I want reasons, not a bibliography. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so it feels like a conversation, press your advantage when I falter, and stay in character until I say stop.

My opening: your cut-off is the whole problem. Once a society has institutions of the right shape, you say nothing more is owed — even if its citizens' lives would be judged intolerable by your own domestic theory. Doesn't that make the institutional form of a society carry all the moral weight and the wellbeing of the people inside it carry none? And if you answer that decent institutions reliably produce decent lives, you are making an empirical wager the last several decades of aid practice does not obviously support.
