You are to act as a philosopher of action and moral responsibility who has also spent years designing undergraduate seminar exercises — someone who knows Galen Strawson's Basic Argument not as a slogan but as a live piece of dialectic, and who has watched students talk past it. I teach a sixteen-student upper-level seminar titled Agency, Character, and Blame; we meet once weekly for eighty minutes. Next week's session concerns the impossibility of ultimate moral responsibility. My students have so far read a competent contemporary compatibilist treatment of free will, and the class is now firmly convinced that the debate is a choice between determinism and indeterminism: three of them are libertarians of a rather crude sort and expect to win by pointing at quantum mechanics. I want an activity, run before any lecture and before the argument is named, that makes them feel the regress inside their own judgments rather than hear about it from me.

The core mechanic I have in mind is a layered dossier on a fictional agent, disclosed to four-person groups one layer at a time, with two separate scores recorded after each disclosure. Please use or revise this seed case: R., twenty-seven, who at a party corners a man who has just mocked him in front of a room full of people and breaks his orbital bone. Layer one gives only the act. Layer two gives the proximate mental state — he knew what he was doing, no weapon, no coercion, no psychosis, he stayed to help. Layer three gives the formation — a father who punished crying, an older brother who taught that the only usable currency in the house was force. Layer four, which should make the scores rebound, gives the self-knowledge — R. had told a friend that afternoon that he should leave when he felt cornered, that he knows what he is like, and that he has been working on it since a court-ordered anger-management programme two years ago. Layer five gives the origin of that self-knowledge and of the disposition to take the programme seriously rather than scoff at it: the arrest was a matter of which precinct, the seriousness a matter of temperament, both unchosen. Layer six is what I am calling the randomness card: a neuroscientist testifies that an undetermined fluctuation in the relevant circuit is what tipped R. from restraint to striking, and the groups must decide whether that raises, lowers, or leaves untouched their scores. After each layer, groups record two numbers on a visible chart: how much blame R. deserves, and how appropriate it would be for the court or for the victim to hold him accountable. I expect the first column to collapse and the second to stay stubbornly high, and I want that divergence to be the hinge of the debrief. I would also like a closing round in which each group must nominate the single fact in the dossier that makes R. responsible and then state what made him the sort of person to whom that fact was available.

Think carefully, and before drafting anything, spend a short paragraph brainstorming what the felt shape of this regress ought to be, which mechanics produce it, and which plausible alternatives you rejected and why. Then deliver: the complete dossier text layer by layer, ready to print; the two-column scoring sheet; a minute-by-minute timeline for eighty minutes; the debrief, which should map each layer and each scoring column onto a specific step of the argument, make the independence of the whole thing from determinism visible through the randomness card rather than asserted, and bring in by name the two standard escapes — self-forming actions and responsibility without ultimacy — at the point where the students have already half-articulated them; an honest list of where this will break in the room the first time I run it, with what to say in the moment; and a frank statement of which feature of the argument the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply myself. Two constraints. I have run staged-case exercises before and twice watched a group slide into a criminal-justice-policy debate within ten minutes; one of my students writes op-eds on sentencing reform and will want to convert the conclusion into an argument for abolition, and I need a way to hold that line without shutting her down. And the students should not encounter the argument, or its author, until the debrief.
