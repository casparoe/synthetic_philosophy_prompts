I am writing the final exam for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar and want a single essay question on the Libet experiments that cannot be answered by reciting the standard trio — the readiness potential, the reported time of the conscious urge, and the veto reply. Please build the question around the following argument, which should appear verbatim as part of the prompt handed to students:

(1) An action is free only if conscious agency is what settles whether it occurs.
(2) On the stochastic-accumulator reinterpretation of the readiness potential, the slow negative drift is not an unconscious decision but subthreshold neural noise approaching a threshold; the moment subjects report as the conscious urge marks the crossing of that threshold rather than an event preceding it.
(3) A veto is itself an event with an onset, and its onset is preceded by the same kind of subthreshold accumulation, so appealing to the veto relocates the problem instead of solving it.
(4) Therefore the veto reply fails, and the experiments threaten free will more deeply than Libet himself concluded.

Students should be required to do three things: identify at least one premise a defender of free will can reject without disputing the neuroscientific data; specify a concrete neural or behavioural signature that would distinguish a veto amounting to a genuine exercise of agency from a veto that is merely one more accumulating process, which they may do by drawing on the neuroimaging distinction between endogenous inhibition of an already-initiated movement and simple non-initiation; and say whether the regress generated by (3) is vicious or benign.

Deliver the question as students would see it, a model answer of about 700 words that commits to a definite verdict on (4) rather than weighing the options evenly, and a marking scheme out of 25 with the allotted 75 minutes stated. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain, and should include a short list of errors that cost marks — among them treating premise (2) as a friendly correction that thereby rescues free will, assuming that the unreliability of clock-report judgments can only damage one side of the debate, and confusing the ability to refrain with the ability to initiate. Do not request citations or a bibliography anywhere in the question, the model answer, or the marking scheme.
